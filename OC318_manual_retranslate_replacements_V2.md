1. Seif number
2. Source block
3. Note number or source heading

## Seif 2, Tur note 5

Replacement English:

(8) Because it grows and continues growing. This refers to fruit whose ripening was not yet complete, as explained by the Beit Yosef. But where its ripening was complete, we do not say that it continues to grow. Otherwise, why would the Gemara in the beginning of Beitzah need to forbid produce attached to the ground because one may go up and detach it, or because of muktzeh? See Tosafos there, who were pressed on this point. The Gemara should have said simply that it is forbidden because it grows and continues growing. Rather, it must be that once its ripening is complete, it no longer continues growing, and preparation can be effective. This is also implied by the wording of the Orchot Chaim, as cited by the Beit Yosef. See Tosafos in Bava Kamma 69a, that what grew becomes nullified in the majority. See also siman 320:2. In Beitzah, Tosafos 37b implies that this is not muktzeh even according to R. Yehuda, for an animal also grows and continues growing, yet it is permitted to slaughter it on Yom Tov. See Tosafos there.

## Seif 2, Tur note 16

## Seif 3, Mechaber and Rama

Replacement English:

(c) Just as it is forbidden to cook with fire, so too it is forbidden to cook with derivatives of fire. ## Seif 3, Shulchan Aruch K'pshuto note 1

Replacement English:

(17) Introduction to the seif. There is no Torah prohibition of cooking except through heat from fire. The prohibition includes both cooking directly with the heat of fire, such as roasting or cooking on a stove, and cooking on something that was heated by fire and remains hot, which is called toldos ha'or, such as sand upon which a fire had burned. By contrast, the Torah did not forbid cooking in the sun. This seif explains that cooking directly in the sun is permitted lechatchilah, but the Sages forbade cooking with something heated by the sun, called toldos chamah, such as cooking on sand that became very hot from the sun, because it resembles cooking with toldos ha'or. Cooking in the sun itself is permitted even rabbinically, but cooking with toldos chamah is rabbinically forbidden. Cooking with electricity, such as an electric oven or electric stovetop, has the law of fire and is forbidden by Torah law.

## Seif 4, Tur note 1

Replacement English:

(3) “A dish that was fully cooked,” etc. This is the opinion of Rashi, who explained the statement in the beginning of Shabbos 18a that one may place wool into the dye pot close to Shabbos. The Gemara asks that perhaps he will stir it, meaning that perhaps he will turn it over in the pot, and with regard to cooking, stirring is considered cooking. This shows that there is cooking after cooking.

In chapter Chavit, 145b, the Mishnah teaches that anything that came into hot water before Shabbos may be soaked in hot water on Shabbos. Meaning, if it was cooked before Shabbos, one may soak it on Shabbos in hot water so that it will soften. This implies that there is no cooking after cooking.

One must distinguish and say that the case in chapter Chavit deals with a dry food, as is stated there regarding the chicken of R. Abba, meaning that he would cook it and soak it for many days in hot water until it dissolved, and would eat it for healing. But with a cooked food that contains broth, there is cooking after cooking.

The Rosh wrote: It seems to me to bring proof from the beraita in chapter HaMevi, which states that one person brings the fire, another brings the wood, etc., and all are liable. Even though the one who placed the water into it already became liable for cooking, the one who stirs it is also liable for cooking. This shows that there is cooking after cooking, even though it had not cooled. One can reject this proof by saying that until it has reached maachal ben Drusai, anyone who advances its cooking is liable, but after it has reached maachal ben Drusai, perhaps there is no cooking after cooking.

It is reasonable that before it has reached maachal ben Drusai, anyone who advances its cooking is liable. After it has reached maachal ben Drusai, if it cooled and he brought it back to a boil, he is liable, as Rashi explained in the beginning of Shabbos regarding the concern lest he boil it, meaning lest he boil a pot that had cooled when he wishes to insulate it, and then he boils it over the fire and is found to be cooking. The Gemara’s question, “let us be concerned lest he stir it,” is because of dyeing.

The conclusion of the Rosh, from what he writes “it is reasonable,” appears to be that after it has reached maachal ben Drusai there is liability only if it cooled and he brought it back to a boil. It also appears that after complete cooking, there is no further cooking even if it cooled.

But the Tur writes here that even while it is still boiling, cooking can apply to it, and if it cooled and he brought it back to a boil, even if it was fully cooked, this follows Rashi’s opinion. Therefore the Tur brings the distinction between a food with broth and a dry food. According to the Rosh, this distinction would not be necessary, because he holds that once something is fully cooked there is no cooking after it. The Mishnah of “anything that came into hot water” would not be difficult, since it deals with something fully cooked. Rather, the Tur certainly holds like Rashi, and this is how the Shulchan Aruch rules here.

It seems to me that the Rosh also holds this way. He mentioned liability when it cooled only with regard to Torah law, that whenever it cooled one is liable by Torah law. For even where it is fully cooked, if it cooled there remains additional cooking. But when it is still boiling, there is a distinction: if it only reached maachal ben Drusai, one is exempt but it is forbidden, while if it was fully cooked, it is permitted even lechatchilah. This refers to placing it near the fire, but directly by the fire itself, it is obvious that it is forbidden in every case, even if it is mitzamek v'ra lo. See the end of this siman, seif 15.

## Seif 4, Tur note 22

Replacement English:

(24) If it cooled: This means that it is no longer yad soledet bo, even though it is still somewhat warm. But if it is still yad soledet bo, even though it cooled from its boiling state, cooking no longer applies to it, because it is still included in the category of hot, and there is no cooking after cooking. This is specifically with regard to returning it to an oven that has been swept of coals, or where the coals have dimmed. But returning it to an oven that has not been swept, and certainly placing it directly on the fire, is forbidden according to everyone, even if it is fully boiling, as stated above in siman 253. All of this is the opinion of the Mechaber. But the Rama later in seif 15 writes in his gloss that as long as it has not cooled completely, the custom is to be lenient and not treat it as cooking. See there.

## Seif 4, Tur note 37

Replacement English:

(39) That it is forbidden to soak it: This applies even in a kli sheni. The same is true if something would become fit to eat merely through rinsing with hot water, in which case rinsing is also forbidden, as stated above regarding salted fish.

Regarding making tea on Shabbos, the poskim explain that one must be very careful because there is a concern of cooking. One should therefore prepare the tea essence before Shabbos by pouring boiling water from a kli rishon onto the tea leaves, and preferably by fully cooking them before Shabbos. ## Seif 4, Tur note 45

Replacement English:

(32) One may rinse it with hot water on Shabbos. Meaning, one may pour water over it from a kli rishon, because according to this view pouring from a kli rishon does not cook. The Mechaber’s words in seif 10, that one may not pour from a kli rishon onto spices, refer specifically to finely ground spices, where there is special concern that pouring cooks them. ## Seif 5, Tur note 1

Replacement English:

(6) “If one cooked it afterward,” etc. The Tur wrote this in the name of R. Eliezer of Metz: even though there is no cooking after cooking for a dry item, nevertheless there is cooking after roasting. His proof is from Pesachim 41a regarding the korban Pesach: if one roasted it and then cooked it, he is liable. Whose view is this? It is R. Yossi’s view, who says that one fulfills the mitzvah with a wafer soaked in water on Pesach, but not with cooked matzah, because it is no longer called bread. Even though it had already been baked in an oven, once he cooked it afterward, he removed it from the category of bread. So too, regarding Shabbos, there is cooking after baking.

Ra'avyah disagreed with him, because in chapter Keitzad Mevarchin the Gemara brings that statement of R. Yossi regarding blessings, that over boiled vegetables one recites shehakol and not borei pri ha'adamah as initially, just as with matzah according to R. Yossi. The Gemara concludes there that R. Yossi said this only because one needs the taste of matzah, and that taste is absent. The Beit Yosef praised Ra'avyah, saying that his question is strong, for we rule that its blessing is borei pri ha'adamah. If so, the rejection that one needs the taste of matzah remains according to halacha. Tosafos also write there in chapter Kol Sha'ah that one may reject the proof because there one needs the taste of matzah, and it is not comparable to the korban Pesach, just as the Gemara rejects it in chapter Keitzad Mevarchin regarding blessings.

I am not worthy to decide between great mountains. Nevertheless, it appears to me proper to explain the words of R. Eliezer of Metz as follows. He holds like R. Yerucham and R. Yona above in seif 1 regarding mitzamek v'ra lo, that once it has been fully cooked, there is no further cooking. We already explained his reasoning nearby: where further cooking improves it, there is cooking, since one benefits from a new taste created by the melacha of Shabbos; but where further cooking worsens it, there is no such cooking.

So too here we may say this reasoning. Even according to the attempted rejection that R. Yossi’s law is because we need the taste of matzah, that only helps reject the proof from roasting the Pesach and then cooking it, because there the Torah decree requires roasting and the matter does not depend on taste. Therefore one may say that the name “roasted” was not removed from it. But regarding matzah, where we require the taste of matzah because we need “bread of affliction,” Tosafos can reject the proof from Pesach in that way.

However, regarding Shabbos, that rejection does not help. Even if you say that there the issue is the taste of matzah, here too we follow the taste of the food. Since the taste changes through cooking, we say that he intended this on Shabbos. This is no better than mitzamek v'yafeh lo, where there is cooking even after cooking; all the more so after baking. This is what we have written according to Tosafos’ view, that even regarding Pesach one can reject the proof because we require the taste of matzah. But according to the other view, the proof of R. Eliezer of Metz remains even stronger.

## Seif 15, Biur Halacha note 1

Replacement English:

And it is dry, etc., opposite the fire: See above in the Mishnah Berurah, where he brings the opinion of the Rama and the Magen Avraham’s explanation of his view. Regarding a roasted item after cooking, the Beit Meir questions how the Rama can simply permit placing it opposite the fire. ## Seif 16, Tur note 1

Replacement English:

(20) In a place where it is yad soledet bo. The Beit Yosef brings that the Mordechai wrote like the Baal HaTerumah, meaning the stringent view cited here. Rabbeinu Yechiel of Paris brought a proof to permit it from chapter Kirah: a woman may anoint her hand and warm it opposite the fire, and then anoint her child. Plain olive oil is congealed, and nevertheless it is permitted. Even so, it is good to be careful not to place it so close to the fire, but rather at a distance where it will not become yad soledet bo.

The Beit Yosef wrote that it appears according to the Baal HaTerumah that it is forbidden only in a place where it becomes yad soledet bo. This is not correct if the reason is nolad, because then it should be forbidden even where it does not become yad soledet bo. Rather, one must distinguish between the concern of cooking and the concern of nolad. Regarding cooking, the issue is a place where it can become yad soledet bo. Regarding nolad, the concern can apply even without full cooking, depending on the case.

As for the proof from oil, where the Gemara permits warming the oil on one's hand, that case is different because it is done in an unusual manner. Therefore, the same reasoning may apply to fat on an inpanada. The primary concern is not always cooking, but also nolad, and the practical ruling depends on whether it is placed where it can become yad soledet bo and whether the act is considered normal cooking or an unusual manner.
