(1) Who prayed, etc. Mincha was instituted corresponding to the afternoon Tamid. The Torah-level time for slaughtering it begins after six and a half hours, namely half an hour after midday. They ordinarily delayed its offering until nine and a half hours so that private offerings could first be brought, since no offering may be brought after the afternoon Tamid. The exception was Erev Pesach that fell on Erev Shabbos, when the Tamid was slaughtered at six and a half hours. Therefore, the primary initial time for Mincha is from nine and a half hours onward, corresponding to the time when the Tamid was ordinarily offered. Nevertheless, if one prayed from six and a half hours onward, he fulfilled his obligation, since the Torah-level time for the Tamid begins then. Some Rishonim permit this even initially. In any event, if one wishes to eat or travel, or can now pray with a minyan but will have no minyan if he waits for Mincha Ketanah, all agree that he may initially pray from six and a half hours onward.
(2) From six and a half hours onward he fulfilled his obligation. This implies that before then he does not fulfill his obligation even after the fact. So write Magen Avraham, Derech HaChaim, and Birkei Yosef. Some Acharonim maintain that although one may not initially pray during the half hour immediately after midday, if he did so he should not repeat the Amidah. Know also that the statement that, according to the Sages, one may pray until night is not literal. Approximately a quarter hour before tzeis hakochavim is certainly bein hashemashos according to all, as explained below in siman 261, and one should not pray Mincha then. See the second gloss below for the practical ruling.
(3) Which extends until the end, etc. The period of Mincha Ketanah is two and a half hours, from nine and a half hours until the end of twelve hours. Half of that period, plag hamincha, is therefore one and a quarter hours.
(4) These hours are calculated, etc. All the foregoing calculations, whether for Mincha Gedolah, Mincha Ketanah, or plag hamincha, use proportional daytime hours. The day, whether long or short, is divided into twelve equal portions, each called an hour, and these hours determine the relevant Mincha times. The poskim dispute how the twelve daytime hours are measured. Some count from alos hashachar until tzeis hakochavim. According to this view, Rabbi Yehudah permits Mincha until one and a quarter hours before tzeis hakochavim, while the Sages permit it until night, as explained above. The Mechaber rules without qualification this way here and below in siman 443. Others count the twelve hours from sunrise until sunset. According to this view, Rabbi Yehudah permits Mincha only until one and a quarter hours before sunset, while the Sages permit it until sunset.
(5) One who follows one authority, etc. Each view entails both a leniency and a stringency. Regarding Mincha, Rabbi Yehudah is stringent, permitting it only until plag hamincha. Correspondingly, he is lenient regarding Maariv, since from plag onward the time is treated as night for purposes of reciting the Maariv Amidah. This applies only to the Amidah itself, for all agree that the time for the evening Krias Shema is at tzeis hakochavim, as stated below in siman 235. The Sages are lenient regarding Mincha, permitting it until evening, and correspondingly stringent regarding Maariv, which may not be prayed before nightfall.
(6) Always according to one view. He should not sometimes follow one view and sometimes the other, because the practices contradict one another, even on different days. Certainly, on the same day he may not pray Mincha after plag in accordance with the Sages and then pray Maariv before tzeis hakochavim in accordance with Rabbi Yehudah.
(7) At that time. Meaning, only before plag hamincha.
(8) Until night. Meaning, until bein hashemashos, which is of doubtful nighttime status.
(9) Before sunset. Meaning the end of sunset, which according to the Mechaber in siman 261 occurs shortly before tzeis hakochavim. Even then, however, one should not pray Maariv until actual tzeis hakochavim, since beforehand it is only doubtful night, as stated below in siman 235. The wording here is not exact regarding this brief interval.
(10) From then onward he fulfilled his obligation. Before plag hamincha, however, he does not fulfill his obligation even after the fact. Since the poskim dispute the exact time of plag hamincha, one may rely on the lenient view and need not repeat the Amidah even if he prayed one and a quarter hours before sunset.
(11) In pressing circumstances, etc. Even one whose regular practice is to pray Mincha after plag may, in pressing circumstances, pray Maariv during that period. This leniency applies only if on that day he prayed Mincha before plag. If on that same day he prayed Mincha after plag, he may not pray Maariv before night, because the two practices directly contradict one another on the same day. This applies to an individual. If a congregation has prayed Mincha and it would be difficult to gather them again for Maariv after they disperse, so that communal Maariv would be lost entirely, the Acharonim permit them to pray Maariv immediately after Mincha. See below, siman 235, seif 1.
(12) Mincha afterward. Meaning that even on another day he should not pray Mincha after plag, since he consistently treats that period as the time for Maariv, even if on that particular day he intends to pray Maariv only after tzeis hakochavim.
(13) Or in pressing circumstances, etc. Since it is a pressing circumstance, he may initially pray Mincha during the period in which he has until now regularly prayed Maariv. As explained in seif katan 11, he must then be careful not to pray Maariv until after tzeis hakochavim, unless he is praying with a congregation that would be difficult to reassemble after everyone returns home.
(14) Meaning until tzeis hakochavim. This is not literal, for approximately a quarter hour before tzeis hakochavim is certainly bein hashemashos according to all, and one should not pray during that period. Rather, it means close to that time. So states Darchei Moshe in siman 232. Know that although the Mechaber and Rama imply that, according to our practice of praying Maariv after tzeis hakochavim, Mincha may be prayed even after sunset until close to tzeis hakochavim, many poskim disagree and hold that Mincha may be prayed only before sunset. Therefore, initially every person must ensure that he prays before sunset, finishing the Amidah while the sun is still visible. It is preferable to pray on time individually than afterward with a congregation. After the fact, one may rely on the lenient view and pray after sunset until a quarter hour before tzeis hakochavim, but he must pray as early as possible so as not to enter the doubtful period of bein hashemashos. Once stars are visible, the time for Mincha has certainly passed, since this is a sign of night, as explained in siman 293 in Mishnah Berurah and Biur Halachah. This leniency applies only after the fact or in a case of great necessity. Initially, one certainly may not delay Mincha until after sunset, and all the more so must take great care not to delay until close to tzeis hakochavim. Chazal said that in Eretz Yisrael they would curse one who prayed at the dimming of the sun, lest the time suddenly pass.
