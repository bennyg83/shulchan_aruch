In one house. See Mishnah Berurah, which explains that this means in one room. So write Magen Avraham and the other later authorities, for moving from room to room, even within one house, constitutes a change of place. This is also implied by siman 273 and by seif 1 here; see the Tur and Beit Yosef there, who assume this plainly. If, however, he can see his original place, or he intended this at the time of the blessing, one may be lenient, as explained above in seif 1. Accordingly, if one ate fruit or drank beverages that do not require an after-blessing in the place where they were eaten, and during the eating entered another room from which he cannot see his original place, all agree that he may not continue eating there without a new blessing. Even the gloss below, which is lenient regarding fruit and beverages, agrees to this. Even if he returns to his original place, he should ideally recite a new blessing. Although this rule is stated by several later authorities, it is extremely difficult to observe, and it appears that the general practice is not to do so. I therefore examined the sources of the law and found that it is not explicit. One who examines Pesachim 101a, Tosafos s.v. aval, and the Rosh there, will see that this conclusion follows only their reading of the Gemara regarding Kiddush, “but from place to place.” According to them, Kiddush in the place of the meal and change of place have the same law. If, however, the reading is, “but from corner to corner,” we are not compelled to say that the Gemara equates moving from corner to corner with moving from room to room merely because both are under one roof. Indeed, if we are stringent from room to room, we should also have to be stringent from corner to corner, for otherwise what proof does the Gemara bring from Rav Anan, whose case involved moving from the roof to the ground floor? It follows that when the Gemara later permits moving from corner to corner with respect to change of place, movement from room to room is included as well. Only moving from one house to another is called a change of place, in accordance with the Gemara's formulation, “They taught this only from house to house.” According to this reading, one must distinguish between the law of change of place and the requirement that Kiddush be in the place of the meal, as Maharam Chalavah, a student of Rashba, explains. For Kiddush, the actual place of the meal is required, because the location of the bread is determinative, and therefore even moving from one corner to another is prohibited. With respect to change of place, however, the law depends upon a complete uprooting, and moving from corner to corner is not an uprooting. This reading, “they inferred from it, etc., but from corner to corner not,” appears explicitly in Rabbeinu Chananel, Halachos Gedolos, Rif, and Maharam Chalavah. Even one who wishes to reject this proof and argue that the Gemara explicitly permitted only movement from corner to corner must concede that no explicit prohibition of moving from room to room appears either. If he infers from the end of the passage, one may infer from its opening, which says that the law applies only from house to house, thereby excluding room to room. Beit Yosef understands Rambam as agreeing with Tosafos and the Rosh, namely that Kiddush in the place of the meal and change of place have the same law, and that just as one is stringent from room to room regarding Kiddush, so too regarding change of place, with leniency only from one corner to another within the same room. Maggid Mishneh in chapter 29 of Hilchos Shabbos, however, clearly understands Rambam in the opposite manner. In his view, Rambam distinguishes between Kiddush in the place of the meal and change of place during a meal, and with respect to change of place even movement from room to room does not constitute a change. He too reads the Gemara regarding Kiddush, “they inferred from it, etc., but from corner to corner not,” following the version of the Geonim, and explains that “from corner to corner” refers to moving from one end of a house to the other even though several rooms divide them. He proves this from the Geonim. This also resolves the Gemara's proof from Rav Anan, whose case involved moving from the roof to the ground, for “corner to corner” there likewise means moving from one room to another. It is possible that all the Geonim who had this reading understood the sugya this way. Rambam therefore permits moving from one corner to another for Kiddush because this is not the “corner to corner” of the Gemara. Consequently, when the Gemara says regarding change of place that only movement from house to house is prohibited but movement from corner to corner is permitted, it necessarily includes movement from room to room, which is also called “corner to corner.” Rambam likewise copied the Gemara's wording here, permitting movement from corner to corner within one house. According to this explanation, he necessarily means even from room to room, since he initially prohibited only movement from house to house. It is also highly precise that Rambam changed his wording: regarding Kiddush he says “from one angle to another,” whereas here he says “from corner to corner.” It emerges that according to Maggid Mishneh, Rambam holds that moving from room to room is not a change of place. The same appears from Halachos Gedolos, Rabbeinu Chananel, and Rif according to their reading. We also find that Rashi explicitly holds that moving from a house to its upper story is not a change of place, and the same would apply from room to room. Rashi reads “but from place to place not” in both passages, and therefore necessarily distinguishes between Kiddush and change of place during a meal. Or Zarua likewise cites his view as practical halachah. Therefore, although it is proper initially to be stringent in accordance with the later authorities and not enter another room during the consumption of foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place, unless he had this in mind when reciting the blessing, one should not protest against those who are lenient, for they have authorities upon whom to rely. In any event, after the fact one certainly should not require a new blessing, for the matter remains doubtful and in cases of doubtful blessings we are lenient. If he can see his original place, one may certainly be lenient even initially, as explained at the end of seif 1 in Mishnah Berurah.
And he ate foods that require a blessing, etc. This means that he initially ate foods whose after-blessing must be recited in the place where they were eaten. At the place to which he subsequently went during the meal, however, even if he drank wine, where the case is that he had initially recited the blessing over the wine during a bread meal, or drank water, he need not recite another blessing. Since he must in any event return to his original established place, he is regarded as still standing in the middle of the meal. Magen Avraham in the name of Tosafos and other authorities.

Without a blessing. This wording implies that Rama holds that when one leaves he must first recite Bircas HaMazon. Yet one who examines Ran will see that Ran says this only according to his own approach, under which uprooting oneself from one's place is considered the end of the meal and therefore requires a new initial blessing afterward. Accordingly, he writes that one should also recite Bircas HaMazon before leaving, even though he could return and recite it later, because we fear that he may forget, as in every completed meal, in accordance with siman 184. According to the present opinion, however, leaving is not considered the end of the meal, since he may return and continue eating without any new blessing. From where, then, is there a basis to require Bircas HaMazon initially before he leaves? On the contrary, reciting it in the middle of the meal would constitute an unnecessary blessing. I later found that Rabbi Akiva Eiger raises this point in his novellae. It appears, however, that Rama's source is Tosafos and the Rosh. According to their view, cited in the gloss, a change of place never requires Bircas HaMazon retroactively according to any opinion, because it is only comparable to a diversion of attention, which requires merely a new initial blessing. Nevertheless, as practical advice, lest he delay until he becomes hungry and thereby lose the opportunity to recite Bircas HaMazon, they write that he should recite it when leaving his place. Gra likewise indicates that this ruling derives from Tosafos. It would therefore appear that the same concern applies even according to our ruling like Rabbeinu Chananel. Yet one may argue that this applies only according to Rav Sheshes, who holds that by strict law leaving requires a new initial blessing because it is considered a diversion of attention. Since he has in any event diverted his attention from eating, we say that he should also recite Bircas HaMazon before leaving lest he delay until he becomes hungry. According to the view that no new initial blessing is required upon returning to a bread meal, since leaving is not considered a diversion of attention, there is likewise no reason, even as practical advice, to recite Bircas HaMazon when leaving, for it is inappropriate to recite it in the middle of the meal. At first glance this may be proven from the Baraisa itself, which says that if he left an elderly or ill person at the table, he need not recite a blessing when leaving or an initial blessing when returning, even as practical advice. The reason must be that since there was no diversion of attention, a blessing is not appropriate. The same should apply according to our ruling that leaving is never considered a diversion of attention. This proof may be rejected, however, because where an elderly or ill person remains he will presumably not delay very long, whereas if no one remains we fear that he may do so. It is possible that even the first Tanna of Rabbi Yehudah agrees to this, and that they dispute only whether a new initial blessing is required upon returning. Still, the second Baraisa explicitly supports the first Tanna, according to whom the practical halachah is that even when leaving no blessing is required, necessarily because he is considered to remain in the middle of the meal. After further study, Rama's words find support in Kol Bo and Baal HaMaor. Baal HaMaor infers from the first Baraisa's case of going to greet a groom and from the second Baraisa's case of going to the synagogue or beis midrash that uprooting oneself was permitted only for a mitzvah, but not for a discretionary matter. Moreover, Baal HaMaor implies that even Rabbi Yehudah, who is lenient when some companions remain, is lenient only for a mitzvah; otherwise one may not leave his place, lest he continue onward and fail to return to recite the blessing in its proper place. Yet even Kol Bo does not state that he should recite Bircas HaMazon before leaving, and Baal HaMaor likewise says only that one may not leave for a discretionary matter lest he continue onward. Perhaps their intention is that he should finish his meal and recite the blessing, not that he should recite Bircas HaMazon in the middle of the meal, leave for a discretionary purpose, and later return to finish eating, thereby causing an unnecessary blessing. This may also be Baal HaMaor's meaning when he writes, “until they recite the blessing.” Perhaps Rama likewise means that he should finish the meal and recite Bircas HaMazon. This requires study. If, however, he estimates that he may remain away for a considerable time, it is reasonable that he should recite Bircas HaMazon beforehand. This is especially proper if he did not leave any companions behind. There is no concern for an unnecessary blessing, because one may combine the Mechaber's view, stated without qualification in accordance with Rambam and the Geonim, that whenever one leaves the doorway of the house he must recite a new blessing upon returning.
Because we are concerned, etc. See Mishnah Berurah. Although Baal HaMaor implies that regarding foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place one may initially leave even for a discretionary purpose before reciting the after-blessing, according to the reasoning of Tosafos and the Rosh, who are concerned that he may delay, several later authorities write that this concern applies even to foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place. This is especially so because in that case his eating is terminated by leaving, since he will in any event require a new initial blessing when he later resumes eating.
Passing. Kol Bo writes this. Baal HaMaor, however, does not imply that the mitzvah must be time-sensitive; he writes only that one may not leave for a discretionary purpose. This is especially so according to Tosafos and the Rosh in the chapter Kisui HaDam, who explain that the requirement of a retroactive blessing is merely practical advice lest he delay.
