And likewise if they were. Kesef Mishneh asks that this is all the more obvious, since regarding drinking or eating fruit all agree that a new blessing is required. Why, then, does the Rambam say “and likewise”? He answers that this teaches us not to say that the eating in the first clause refers to foods that do not require an after-blessing in their original place. See there. This is strained, for the Rambam should have stated explicitly in the first clause that they were eating bread. It appears to me that the plain meaning is that he teaches that even when they were drinking, if some of the group remained behind, they need not recite another blessing, as stated in the gloss. This is also implied by all the poskim who rule like Rav Sheshes, that drinking and eating follow the same law. It is likewise implied by Rif and Rabbeinu Yerucham, and is stated explicitly in Shiltei HaGibborim, unlike Bach, who disagrees and gives a strained explanation of the gloss to Semak. It appears to me that Semak’s words are straightforward. The gloss refers to Semak’s statement that if some remained behind no new blessing is required, and explains that this applies to all foods, even those that do not require an after-blessing in their original place. For foods that do require an after-blessing in their original place, even if no one remained behind, no new blessing is required. This is clear. It is also implied by the Gemara. Were it obvious, as Bach writes, that according to Rav Chisda there is no distinction between leaving some members behind and leaving none, why did the questioner need to infer from the wording “they moved”? The Ran and Ramban were forced into a strained explanation of that inference. He should simply have challenged Rav Chisda from the distinction between leaving some behind and leaving none. Necessarily, even Rav Chisda recognizes a distinction, as Rama writes in the name of Semak and Or Zarua.
In one house. Meaning, in one room. See the end of seif 8 in the gloss.
He must recite a blessing. Since he cannot see his original place. See seif 3. It appears to me that if he is in one room, even if a high object stands in the middle, such as the back of an oven, so that he cannot see his original place, he need not recite another blessing. Since the entire area is enclosed by walls, it is considered one place. See Beit Yosef.
Foods that require. In such a case, even if he went elsewhere during the meal and drank wine or water, he need not recite another blessing, because he returns to his original fixed meal. Beit Yosef; Tosafos.
And there is no distinction. However, when some members of the group remained behind with respect to other foods, this helps only if he returns to his original place. If he eats elsewhere, he must recite another blessing, since there he is considered an individual. This is also implied by the Gemara, which states that those who heard Kiddush in the synagogue must recite another blessing over the wine in their homes, even though they left guests eating in the synagogue.
For we are concerned that he may forget. According to this reason, if he is invited to eat elsewhere, it is permitted, since this concern does not apply, and this is the widespread custom. Bach, however, writes that it is permitted only if, when reciting HaMotzi, he intended to leave. Otherwise, he may not leave, because ideally he must recite the after-blessing in his original place, as stated in siman 184. This is also explicit in the Ran, and Rama writes likewise in responsum 1. See there. Nevertheless, prior intention is effective, as stated at the end of seif 1.
