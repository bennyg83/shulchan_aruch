Seif 7. If a person sleeps temporarily during his meal, this is not considered an interruption. Note: See Magen Avraham, who writes that he must nevertheless wash his hands again because there was a diversion of attention, and that this is also implied by the Tur. He brings proof from the eve of Pesach, and so forth. In my humble opinion this is not clear, for there is room to distinguish based on Tosafos, Chagigah 21a, s.v. ha'onen umechusar kippurim. Tosafos cite Rabbeinu Yitzchak that a diversion of attention invalidates only in a case similar to the one there, where the item remained in his possession and he consciously diverted his attention from it. Where it was no longer in his possession, such as when it was lost, a diversion of attention does not invalidate it. Rabbeinu Elchanan brings proof from the chapter Gid HaNasheh, where the Gemara states that limbs found in the Temple courtyard are presumed to be from burnt-offerings, while pieces are presumed to be from sin-offerings. This implies that a burnt-offering is sacrificed and a sin-offering eaten, and that they are not invalidated through diversion of attention. Since I have mentioned this, I will first explain Tosafos' words, which appear exceedingly difficult. First, their citation from Gid HaNasheh is not the primary location of the teaching, but only an incidental reference. Its source is the seventh chapter of Shekalim, where the Mishnah concludes that animals found in Jerusalem are presumed to be peace-offerings, and that in both cases their form must be allowed to decay before they are taken out to the place of burning. This appears to state the opposite of Tosafos, who say that the burnt-offering is sacrificed and the sin-offering eaten. My esteemed friend Rabbi Tzvi Hirsch Schor of Zamosc asked me this question, and I answered that Tosafos are correct but their text contains an error. In Pesachim 34a, Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish dispute the law of diversion of attention. One holds that it creates a disqualification of impurity and therefore requires decay of form, while the other holds that it is an intrinsic disqualification and does not require decay of form. The Gemara raises several objections there, but does not challenge from the Mishnah in Shekalim, which requires decay of form for a found offering. Why not? It was surely subject to diversion of attention, and if diversion of attention is an intrinsic disqualification, why require decay of form? This necessarily proves Rabbeinu Yitzchak's position that when an item is lost it is not considered a diversion of attention. This is a clear proof. I then examined the Yerushalmi in its original place and found that it raises this very issue. Rabbi Elazar said in the name of Rabbi Elazar that an item from which attention was diverted requires decay of form. Rabbi Hoshaya said that our Mishnah proves this, for it says that its form should decay and it should be taken out to the place of burning. Rabbi Yose rejects the proof: “Indeed, you cannot eat it, for perhaps its form has already become spoiled; therefore one must say that its form should decay and it should be taken out to the place of burning.” Korban HaEdah explains correctly that the Yerushalmi rejects the proof because the Mishnah's requirement of decay of form is not due to diversion of attention, but because perhaps the offering had already become disqualified. Yet his explanation that diversion of attention does not apply in the Temple courtyard is strained. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak, the matter is readily understood: a found item is not regarded as having been subject to diversion of attention. Thus Rabbeinu Elchanan's proof must be from the seventh chapter of Shekalim, and the words “from the chapter Gid HaNasheh” are a scribal error. The intended proof is from the Yerushalmi there, which says regarding limbs presumed to be burnt-offerings and pieces presumed to be sin-offerings that the reason they require decay of form is the possibility that their form had already changed. This implies that absent that concern the burnt-offerings would be sacrificed and the sin-offerings eaten. Why were they not disqualified because of diversion of attention? Necessarily, in such a case there is no diversion of attention. Whatever the precise correction of the text, this is the meaning of the proof. Accordingly, the same principle should apply to handwashing. A diversion of attention exists only when a person consciously turns his mind away, such as when he converses with another person or recites Hallel and the Haggadah, which is the case brought by Magen Avraham from the Gemara. Sleep, however, resembles an item that was lost, for it is not voluntary and is comparable to circumstances beyond one's control. Even though it is not a complete accident, the loss of an item is also not necessarily beyond one's control, since a paid guardian is liable for it, yet it is still not considered a conscious diversion of attention. Therefore, if sleep overtook him without his intending to sleep, this should not be regarded as a diversion of attention. One may distinguish, however, that if he deliberately lay down to sleep, this is voluntary and is considered a diversion of attention, and the proof of Magen Avraham from the eve of Pesach applies. If he did not lie down intending to sleep, but sleep overtook him, it appears that there was no diversion of attention. Since the obligation of handwashing is Rabbinic, the halachah follows the lenient view in a case of doubt concerning handwashing. This appears correct to me as practical halachah. Regarding the preceding seif, where prayer does not constitute an interruption, I wrote in the margin of my Shulchan Aruch: If he had no time whatsoever to wait, even for a moment or two, see Taz below, siman 271, seif katan 4, who implies that in such a case it is an interruption and he must recite a new blessing. According to Magen Avraham, seif katan 10, however, it is evident that even then he need not recite a blessing. See there and examine carefully.
