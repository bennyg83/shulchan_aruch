Since he recited a blessing on one tree, etc. Even if he cannot see his original place, it is permitted so long as the area is enclosed by partitions. Magen Avraham. This implies that if it is not enclosed, even moving from one tree to another within the same garden is forbidden, specifically where he cannot see the original place. If he can see it, it appears permitted, as above regarding one who ate on the east side of a fig tree. The Taz, however, writes that moving from tree to tree is worse, and even if he can see the original place it is forbidden when the area is not enclosed. This does not appear correct.
On which he recited a blessing, etc. Above in seif 2, concerning one who ate on the east side of a fig tree, the case is where he did not initially intend to move, as explained there. According to my conclusion that one should be stringent that foods of the Seven Species require the final blessing in their original place, even without prior intent it would be permitted for us to move. Yet the Levush writes that the other fruits are considered like a new decision, which implies an actual diversion of intent requiring a new blessing for any food. In Orchot Chaim, as cited by the Beit Yosef, this reason is not mentioned. It implies that the issue is not a new decision but solely a change of place. The Beit Yosef and all the later authorities also somewhat imply this, as I wrote, because the fig tree itself separates the areas and makes them like two places. I later found in Kesef Mishneh, chapter 4 of Hilchot Berachot, that the explanation is that he originally intended to finish his meal on the east side and afterward decided to finish on the west side, not that he decided to eat more than he had originally intended. Thus the case does not concern a true new decision. This requires study.
