(Seif katan 3) And likewise, etc. Everyone agrees, etc. Pesachim 101a teaches that a change of place requires a new blessing. Rav Chisda said: This was taught only regarding foods that do not require an after-blessing in the place where they were eaten. Foods that require an after-blessing in their place do not require a new blessing. Why? Because he returns to his original fixed setting. Rav Sheshes said that both categories require a new blessing. Rif and Rambam rule like Rav Sheshes, that even foods requiring an after-blessing in their place nevertheless require a new blessing after a change of place. Kesef Mishneh objects: Since the first clause teaches, “They were sitting to eat,” it presumably refers to bread, and nevertheless requires a new blessing. Although according to Rav Chisda bread requires its after-blessing in its place, as stated in seif 5, we nevertheless rule like Rav Sheshes.
Accordingly, the latter clause, which speaks of a group reclining to drink and eat fruit, must also require a new blessing even according to Rav Chisda, since such items do not require their after-blessing in the place where they were consumed. Although Rambam holds that anything requiring the blessing Mei'ein Shalosh must be blessed in its place, it is possible that “drinking and fruit” refers to wine and fruit of the seven species. In any event, bread is certainly included, for all poskim agree that its after-blessing must be recited in its place.
Lest we say that the eating in the first clause refers, etc. The latter clause therefore teaches “wine and fruit,” meaning even fruit of the seven species. Rambam follows his own view that these too require their after-blessing in the place where they were consumed, and nevertheless, if one leaves, he must recite a new blessing, in accordance with Rav Sheshes. In my view, etc. Even regarding drinking and fruit that are not of the seven species, which according to all opinions do not require an after-blessing in their place, leaving some members of the group behind is nevertheless effective. This is unlike Bach, as explained below.
And as Rama writes in the gloss. This is unlike Bach, who disputes Rama and explains the passage differently. Rama's gloss is based on Hagahos Semak, and Bach was therefore compelled, according to his approach, to interpret it differently from Rama. In my view, however, the words of Semak support Rama. Semak, as cited in Beit Yosef, Bach, and Taz, writes: “A group who were reclining and left their place to go elsewhere, if they left some members of the group behind, they do not require either an after-blessing, namely Bircas HaMazon, or a new HaMotzi.” Hagahos Semak comments: “This applies even to foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place. For regarding foods that do require an after-blessing in their place, even if they left no one behind, a change of place does not require a new blessing beforehand.” Beit Yosef writes that Semak's language is imprecise, since his reference to Bircas HaMazon and HaMotzi implies that he is discussing bread, whereas Hagahos Semak then applies the passage to foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place. Bach raises a further objection: How can Hagahos Semak say that foods requiring an after-blessing in their place need no one left behind, when the Baraisa explicitly says that the exemption applies specifically where some members remained? Magen Avraham resolves both objections from Pesachim. The Gemara challenges Rav Chisda from a Baraisa: Members of a group who were reclining to drink and left to greet a bridegroom do not require an after-blessing when leaving, and upon returning do not require a new blessing, provided that some members remained there. If none remained, they require an after-blessing when leaving and a new blessing upon returning. Since the Baraisa says that they “left,” it implies that it discusses foods requiring an after-blessing in their place. Yet it makes the exemption depend on leaving some members behind, contrary to Rav Chisda. The Gemara answers that the Baraisa follows Rabbi Yehudah, whereas Rav Chisda follows the Sages. According to Bach, who assumes as a matter of logic that leaving some members behind is ineffective for foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place, Semak's ruling that leaving some behind is effective must refer to bread, and Semak would therefore rule like Rav Sheshes, not Rav Chisda. Hagahos Semak would then indeed be difficult, for it states that foods requiring an after-blessing in their place need no members left behind, while the Baraisa, which necessarily refers to bread, requires precisely that some remain. Magen Avraham instead explains that Hagahos Semak follows Rav Chisda, and that Semak itself likewise rules like Rav Chisda. Regarding bread and all foods requiring an after-blessing in their place, no members need remain. Semak's rule is needed for foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place: although Rav Chisda agrees that a change of place ordinarily interrupts their meal, he also agrees that if some members remain, no new blessing is required. This resolves Beit Yosef's objection as well. Beit Yosef understood Hagahos Semak as limiting Semak's statement specifically to foods not requiring an after-blessing in their place, and therefore objected that Semak's references to Bircas HaMazon and HaMotzi do not fit. Yet Hagahos Semak says “even” regarding such foods, which shows that it agrees that Semak refers to foods requiring an after-blessing in their place and merely adds that the ruling applies even to other foods. Hagahos Semak therefore means as follows: Semak certainly refers to bread, as shown by his mention of Bircas HaMazon and HaMotzi, but he states the case of leaving some members behind in order to teach a general solution that is effective even for foods not requiring an after-blessing in their place. For foods that do require an after-blessing in their place, leaving anyone behind is actually unnecessary, because Semak rules like Rav Chisda. This also explains the word “even” in Hagahos Semak and resolves Beit Yosef's objection. Bach's objection from the Baraisa also falls away, since the Baraisa follows Rabbi Yehudah, as Rav Chisda explains, and Rabbi Yehudah agrees with Rav Sheshes. Rav Chisda, however, follows the Sages. Therefore, foods requiring an after-blessing in their place do not require anyone to remain, while even foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place are exempt from a new blessing when some members remain. This is unlike Bach, and the entire passage is now understandable.
For according to Rav Chisda there is no distinction, etc. Meaning, regarding foods requiring an after-blessing in their place, no new blessing is required even if no members remained. Regarding foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place, leaving some members behind is ineffective according to both Rav Chisda and Rav Sheshes, according to the approach under discussion.
The inference from the words “and they left” means that the Baraisa concerns foods requiring an after-blessing in their place. Ran was therefore forced into his explanation, etc. The proof is not from the word “left” alone. Rather, the Gemara means: Since it states that they left, and then teaches in the latter clause that when they leave they require an after-blessing, this shows that it concerns foods requiring an after-blessing in their place. If so, it is understandable that immediately before leaving they must recite Bircas HaMazon, since one may not leave before reciting the blessing in its proper place. But if the Baraisa concerned foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place, why would they require an after-blessing when leaving? Leaving one's place is no greater than forming a new intention, which requires only a new blessing before what one will subsequently eat, not an after-blessing on what one already ate. This follows the opinion cited by Rama in seif 2 and is also the view of Ramban, who adds further explanation there.
It necessarily follows that even according to Rav Sheshes there is a distinction: For foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place, leaving some members behind is effective. Were it not for the inference from “and they left,” there would have been no difficulty for Rav Chisda. One could have said that the Baraisa distinguishing whether some remained concerns foods not requiring an after-blessing in their place, while the Baraisa stating generally that a change of place requires a new blessing, which Rav Chisda limits to such foods, refers to a case where no one remained. The Gemara therefore infers from “and they left” that the Baraisa concerns foods requiring an after-blessing in their place, and nevertheless requires that some members remain. One cannot answer that Rav Chisda's statement that foods requiring an after-blessing in their place need no new blessing also applies only where some remained, because an Amora must state such a limitation explicitly. More fundamentally, the Gemara assumes logically that where some members remain there is no distinction between foods requiring an after-blessing in their place and those that do not. Even foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place are protected by leaving some members behind, unlike Bach. Rav Chisda's distinction applies only where no one remained.
