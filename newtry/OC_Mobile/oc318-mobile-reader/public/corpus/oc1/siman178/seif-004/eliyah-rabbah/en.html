[4] Or even if he did not leave, etc. This implies that even regarding foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place, if some companions remain behind he need not recite a new blessing. Rama writes this in the name of Semak and Or Zarua. Bach, however, argues at length that according to all opinions, regarding foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place it makes no difference whether some companions remain. He explains Semak differently; see there at length. Taz writes likewise, whereas Magen Avraham agrees with Rama and resolves Semak's words with difficulty. In my humble opinion, Semak can be explained readily and Rama's ruling upheld. Bach's explanation is astonishing, for he writes that Semak rules like Rif, who makes no distinction between foods that require an after-blessing in their place and those that do not. Yet Semak explicitly writes, in words Bach himself cites: “If one ate bread in one place and then ate bread in another place, he must return and recite Bircas HaMazon, but need not recite a blessing before eating in the second place. With wine and fruit, however, he must recite a blessing before eating.” Semak therefore clearly follows the view of the Rosh and the “some say” opinion, which distinguishes between foods that require an after-blessing in their place and those that do not. This also confirms Rama's interpretation. Otherwise, once Semak distinguishes between the two categories, why does he state the law of leaving some companions behind? Necessarily, he teaches that even regarding foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place, if some companions remain, no new blessing is required. Nachalas Tzvi likewise implies this and derives it from the Gemara. Semag also appears to hold this view, for he too records the law of leaving some companions behind even though he rules like the Rosh. Bach questions Semag in seif 2, but in my humble opinion the matter is as I explained. Rama also cites Or Zarua, whom Bach does not mention, yet Bach states that all authorities agree that there is no distinction. Sefer Zikaron of Rabbi Yitzchak HaKohen, in the chapter Keitzad Mevarchin, likewise implies Rama's view, and I found it explicit in Bircas Avraham, pages 73 and following. There is also proof from Pesachim 102a. Regarding the Baraisa of leaving some companions behind, the Gemara asks: “Who is the Tanna of uprootings? Rabbi Yehudah.” The term “uprootings” is difficult. Rashi and Rashbam explain with difficulty that the Tanna of the Baraisa, who requires a blessing even for foods whose after-blessing must be recited in their place, is Rabbi Yehudah. Yet this does not explain why the term “uprootings” is needed. It appears that the Gemara thereby indicates that the entire Baraisa is not limited to Rabbi Yehudah, but can follow even the Sages regarding foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place. Only its use of the term “uprootings,” which implies foods requiring an after-blessing in their place, follows Rabbi Yehudah, as Rashi explained earlier. It is thus evident that even regarding foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place, one need not recite a new blessing if some companions remain. This is also implied by Shulchan Aruch's wording, copied from Rambam, chapter 4 of Hilchos Berachos. After distinguishing whether some companions remained, Rambam continues, “Likewise, if they were reclining to drink or to eat fruit,” which implies that the distinction concerning companions applies also to fruit, which all agree does not require an after-blessing in its place. This resolves Kesef Mishneh's question why Rambam needed to write “Likewise, if they were reclining.” One might alternatively say that Rambam teaches that even foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place require a retroactive blessing, contrary to Ran in Arvei Pesachim, who holds that according to all opinions no retroactive blessing is required. Kesef Mishneh, however, explains that one might have understood Rambam's earlier reference to eating as speaking of foods that do not require an after-blessing in their place, and he therefore teaches that the same law applies to drinking wine and eating fruits of the seven species, which do require an after-blessing in their place. This is astonishing, for Rambam's earlier reference to eating certainly concerns foods requiring an after-blessing in their place, since he writes that the person “ended his meal,” a phrase used in the Gemara and all the authorities concerning bread, and further writes that upon returning he recites HaMotzi, which clearly means a bread meal. Rambam also does not specify wine or fruits of the seven species. The fact that leaving companions behind is effective applies when he returns to his original place. If he eats in another place, he must recite a blessing because he is then like an individual. Magen Avraham, siman 210, writes that if one ate less than the required measure, went outside, and returned, he must recite a new initial blessing even over bread, because he was not yet obligated in an after-blessing. If he then ate an additional amount less than the required measure, the amounts combine for the after-blessing, provided that he did not remain away longer than the time required to eat a pras.
