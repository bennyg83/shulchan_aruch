This poses a difficulty to Rama’s ruling in seif 1 that prior intention helps only within one house, whereas between two houses, according to the first opinion, one must recite an after-blessing before leaving. Why is the present case different? Necessarily, even the first opinion, which follows Rav Sheshes, agrees that if one intended to eat elsewhere, no new blessing is required, because this is not considered a change of location. This is contrary to Rama’s gloss. Yet there remains a difficulty from the rule below concerning moving from one garden to another, where prior intention does not help. It appears to me that even the first opinion distinguishes between bread and fruit regarding prior intention to eat elsewhere. Since bread creates a fixed meal, prior intention to eat there is effective and no new blessing is needed. Regarding fruit, prior intention is ineffective because the two acts of eating are not joined. This contradicts Rama’s earlier gloss. There is no difficulty, however, from the Shulchan Aruch’s statement in seif 2 that when they return they must recite another blessing, because there the case is not that they eat in the second place; rather, they return to finish the meal in the first place. If one eats in the second place and had intended to do so, no new blessing is required. Rama’s requirement that both places be in one house is therefore difficult. His proof from siman 273 is not comparable. There, one does not eat at all in the place where Kiddush is recited but intends to eat only in the second place, so the two are considered one place of the meal only if they are in one house. Here, however, one eats in the first place as well. If he intended to continue eating elsewhere, the places need not be in one house. This appears correct to me. As practical halachah, since we rule like Tosafos, Rashbam, and the Rosh, whom we generally follow, the law is as follows. Ideally, a person should not leave a meal at all before Bircas HaMazon, unless a passing mitzvah requires it, such as when the time for prayer has arrived. In that case, even if he did not originally intend to leave, our practice is to do so. At a seudas mitzvah, such as a wedding feast, people commonly eat a little in their homes and then go to the wedding feast to finish the meal, provided that they originally intended to go there. Travelers likewise recite the after-blessing where they finish eating. If a person went elsewhere without intending to eat there and then returned to his meal, if he had eaten something requiring an after-blessing in its original place, as described in seif 5, he need not recite another initial blessing, and certainly need not first recite Bircas HaMazon. If he ate fruit or drank wine, left, and returned, he must recite a new initial blessing over what he drinks upon returning. If he diverted his attention from eating when he left, then in every case he must recite a new blessing over what he subsequently eats. Regarding fruit in a garden, the matter depends on whether it is one garden. If it is one garden, prior intention helps for everything in the garden. It appears that even one garden, if it is not enclosed by a surrounding wall, is treated like moving from one garden to another. Beit Yosef writes this in explaining Rambam’s case of eating on the east side of a fig tree, as stated in seif 2. Although he later writes that the rule depends on seeing the original place, here, since one wishes to eat from a second tree, the law is like moving from one garden to another, and seeing the original place does not help. This appears correct to me.
