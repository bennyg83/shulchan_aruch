(Seif katan 11) The threshold, etc. And Sefer Tanya, etc., and so it appears from the Gemara. See Beit Yosef. Meaning, the source of this ruling is Pesachim 85b. We learned in a Mishnah: From the doorway inward is considered inward, both regarding lesser sanctities, which may be eaten throughout Yerushalayim but not outside Yerushalayim, and regarding the most sacred offerings, which may be eaten only within the Azarah. From the doorway outward is considered outward. Rav said: The same applies to prayer. The Gemara then raises a contradiction between the implications of the Mishnah. The first clause says that from the doorway inward is inward, implying that the doorway itself is outward. The latter clause says that from the doorway outward is outward, implying that the doorway itself is inward. It answers that in the gates of the Azarah, the doorway itself is regarded as inward, because all the gates of the Azarah were sanctified with the sanctity of the Azarah except the eastern gate, namely the Gate of Nikanor. This was for the sake of a metzora who had healed and had acted in accordance with the Torah and the mitzvah. On the eighth day he had not yet completed his atonement and was forbidden to enter the Azarah, yet he needed to insert his hands into the Azarah so that blood from his guilt offering could be placed upon his thumbs. Since the Gate of Nikanor was not sanctified, he could stand in the gateway and insert his hands. In doing so he did not violate the prohibition of partial entry, because inserting part of his body into the Azarah is not called entry. The other gates of the Azarah, however, were sanctified, and therefore the doorway itself is regarded as inward. By contrast, none of the gates of Yerushalayim were sanctified, so that metzoraim, who were sent outside Yerushalayim and forbidden to enter the city, could stand in the gateways to protect themselves from the sun and rain. Beit Yosef writes: “It would appear at first glance that regarding combining for a davar shebikdushah, the doorway itself is regarded as inward. For if, generally, gateways were inward regarding sacrificial law, the gates of the Azarah would not have required sanctification. Likewise, regarding the gates of Yerushalayim, were it not for the metzoraim they would have been sanctified for this reason. Rabbeinu Yerucham, however, writes regarding prayer that the doorway itself is regarded as outward. The Rambam likewise writes in chapter 9 of Hilchos Korban Pesach, regarding the prohibition against taking the Korban Pesach outside its company during its consumption, that the doorway itself is regarded as outward. It appears that we derive prayer from there, in accordance with Rabbeinu Yerucham.” These are the words of Beit Yosef. Therefore Shulchan Aruch writes here, without citing any dissenting view, that the doorway is regarded as outward. Although Beit Yosef found it more logical that the doorway should be regarded as inward for prayer, since the gates of the Azarah were sanctified and because a reason was needed for why the gates of Yerushalayim were not sanctified, as explained above, he nevertheless set aside his own view in favor of Rabbeinu Yerucham, especially since he found support from the Rambam. Had Beit Yosef seen Sefer Tanya, which disagrees, he would certainly have cited its view, and perhaps would have ruled accordingly, since that is what appears from the Gemara.
