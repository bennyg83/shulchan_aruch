(Seif katan 10) And the thirteenth year, etc. This also appears from the end of Eruvin, where a complete year is required. Meaning, it appears there in accordance with Rama's gloss, from which Magen Avraham raises his question. The Torah permits one who sold a house in a walled city to redeem it within a year. If he does not redeem it within a year, it becomes permanently vested in the buyer and never returns to the seller. If the year is intercalated, the seller has thirteen months. The same applies here. Although one might distinguish that there the verse says “complete,” thereby including the added month, nevertheless Beit Yosef derives our case from there. See there. Magen Avraham's statement concerning the thirteenth year is an additional proof, for during thirteen years there were several leap years, and in each leap year the child is given thirteen months for that year. His statement that in the thirteenth year he becomes bar mitzvah in Adar I follows from this reasoning.
Her virginity returns. If she was violated before three years had passed from the day of her birth, her virginity returns, but if she was violated after three years, it does not return. If she was born at the beginning of Adar and, in her third year, was violated at the end of Adar, her virginity does not return. If beis din subsequently decided, in the period when months were sanctified by observation, to intercalate the year, so that she was still within her first three years, her virginity returns.
He states the usual case. Meaning, most years are not leap years. Eliyah Rabbah and Responsa Shevus Yaakov, siman 9, write, however, that Magen Avraham evidently did not examine the actual responsum of Mahari Mintz, the source of this ruling, for he writes explicitly to the contrary. Mahari Mintz states: “It appears obvious to me that if one child was born in Adar I of a leap year and another was born in Adar II, and their thirteenth year is also a leap year, we would certainly say that the child born in the first Adar becomes bar mitzvah in Adar I, and the child born in the second Adar becomes bar mitzvah in Adar II. The end of the law follows its beginning, each according to his own month.” Responsa Shevus Yaakov concludes that this is the established custom among all halachic authorities.
And one who was born, etc. This is implied in Nedarim, where the Mishnah teaches: If one says, “This item is forbidden to me for this month,” when Rosh Chodesh of the following month arrives, it becomes permitted. The Gemara asks that this is obvious, and answers that the ruling was needed for a full month. Meaning, the month during which the vow was made had thirty days, and therefore the following Rosh Chodesh had two days. The Mishnah teaches that even the first day of Rosh Chodesh is permitted, since people call it Rosh Chodesh and vows follow ordinary speech. This implies that were we not following ordinary speech, that day would by law belong to the preceding month, as it is the thirtieth day of that month.
There are differing opinions regarding how the first day of Rosh Chodesh should be written in a get. The question is whether it should be written as the thirtieth day of the preceding month or as Rosh Chodesh of the coming month. The view that one should write Rosh Chodesh reasons that documents too follow ordinary speech, as the Ran writes. See Beit Yosef there. In any event, since the first day of Rosh Chodesh belongs to the preceding month and is its thirtieth day, one born then was not born on Rosh Chodesh Kislev but at the end of Cheshvan. Therefore, in his thirteenth year, even if Rosh Chodesh Kislev consists of only one day, he becomes bar mitzvah at the end of Cheshvan, namely on the twenty ninth of Cheshvan.
Until twenty nine days of Cheshvan have passed. Meaning, although in siman 53, seif katan 13, he writes that once the beginning of the day on which one was born arrives, we say that part of the day is regarded as the whole day and he is thirteen years and one day old, this does not apply here. Although he was born at the end of Cheshvan and in his thirteenth year the twenty ninth is the final day of Cheshvan, he must nevertheless first live through twenty nine days of Cheshvan before becoming bar mitzvah, just as twenty nine days of Cheshvan had passed before he was born. According to this, the same ruling applies to Rosh Chodesh Teves. If one was born on Rosh Chodesh Teves in a year when Rosh Chodesh Teves consisted of two days, while in his thirteenth year it consists of only one day, the same rule applies. See Eliyah Rabbah.
