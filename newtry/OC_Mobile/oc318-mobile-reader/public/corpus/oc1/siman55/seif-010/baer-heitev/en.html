Leap year. The Magen Avraham wrote that this is not specific, but the same applies if the thirteenth year is a leap year: one born in the first Adar becomes bar mitzvah in the second Adar, as written in the gloss; see there. But he did not examine the source of the ruling, for the Rama's gloss is from Teshuvos Mahari Mintz, siman 9, who wrote plainly and ruled that even if one was born in a leap year in the first Adar and becomes bar mitzvah in an ordinary year, he becomes bar mitzvah after one who was born in the second Adar. Likewise, one born in Adar in an ordinary year and becoming bar mitzvah in a leap year does not become bar mitzvah until the second Adar. Nevertheless, he wrote in these words: it appears obvious to me regarding one born in a leap year in the first Adar and another later born in the second Adar, and the thirteenth year was also a leap year, then we would certainly say that the one born in the first Adar becomes bar mitzvah in the first Adar, and the second in the second Adar; this one in his month and that one in his month. Teshuvos Shevus Yaakov, part 1, siman 9. This is also the plain practice among all decisors, and the gaon Rabbi David Oppenheim, of blessed memory, agreed to this as practical halacha, unlike the Magen Avraham. So too wrote the Eliyah Zuta, and so ruled the Peri Chadash; see there. The Magen Avraham further wrote: one who was born on the first day of Rosh Chodesh Kislev, and when he becomes bar mitzvah Cheshvan is deficient and Rosh Chodesh Kislev is only one day, he does not become bar mitzvah until Rosh Chodesh, for the year is not yet complete for him until twenty-nine days of Cheshvan have passed, since we require thirteen complete years; see there. This too resulted from not seeing the responsum, for there this law is explained explicitly; see there. See siman 568, seif 6, regarding yahrzeit, and see the Taz in Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 306.
