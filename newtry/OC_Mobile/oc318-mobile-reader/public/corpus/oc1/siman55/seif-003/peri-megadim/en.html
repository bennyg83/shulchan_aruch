It does not. See Taz, who inclines to say that although there was no minyan when the preceding section was recited, if a minyan arrived afterward they may recite Kaddish, and he cites siman 53. At first glance, Kaddish is not recited for Yishtabach alone, but only for the preceding verses, as I wrote in letter 1. If so, this ruling is explicit. Even were we to say that Kaddish may also be recited for Yishtabach, because it contains language resembling verses, nevertheless, since the reason given is that one should not interrupt between Yishtabach and Kaddish, the more basic reason should have been that a minyan is required at the time of Yishtabach. This indicates that it is not required. As for what he writes in the name of Rama, that one may wait before reciting Yishtabach, I do not know how long this waiting may continue. Certainly, after a lengthy interruption it is not appropriate to recite Kaddish for material studied long before. His comments on Levush require study. He apparently understood Levush to mean that there is no obligation to recite Kaddish after Aleinu because Aleinu contains no verses, or because it is recited as supplication. According to that understanding, Levush would concede regarding Tehillim or aggadah. Yet one who examines Levush sees from his conclusion, “Rather, the custom is to recite it because of the mourner's Kaddish that follows,” that his earlier words must also be understood differently. At Maariv, the Sages obligated the recital of the blessings after Krias Shema, and Kaddish consequently follows them. It therefore suffices if ten were present either for the blessings or for Kaddish. Aleinu, however, is not itself obligatory. His opening words may likewise be understood this way. See there. See Magen Avraham 69:4 and 234:1, who disagrees and holds like Levush. It appears there that even for Tehillah at Minchah, which one is obligated to recite three times daily, ten must be present during Tehillah. This obligation is not as absolute as the obligation of the Maariv blessings; rather, one is merely assured of reward for reciting it, even though it is obligatory. Moreover, one may sometimes discharge the obligation in the morning by reciting Tehillim that includes Tehillah. See Eliyah Rabbah here, letter 3. According to this, what is the Taz's proof from Kol Bo for his position? Rabbi Amram's proof that Yishtabach should be recited standing was so that there be no interruption, even when ten are already present. How does this prove anything from Ashrei at Minchah, where ten are not yet present? One may answer that Ashrei has the status of an obligation to recite it twice, and therefore the only possible reason is to avoid an interruption. Understand this. Regarding his statement that Pesukei DeZimrah was not mentioned, I do not understand its connection to what precedes. Rama in siman 53 explicitly states that one should wait before Yishtabach. One may say that Yishtabach is then recited and the Kaddish refers to Yishtabach. There is no need to elaborate. The proper course is that afterward they should recite three verses in the presence of the minyan and then recite Kaddish. Eliyah Rabbah 581:13 questions the words of Kol Bo, and his view there appears to accord with Levush.
Now I have seen some who, at Minchah, recite Vayedaber and Ashrei, and only afterward take the tallis, inspect the tzitzis, and recite the blessing. Who permitted them to make such a lengthy interruption between Ashrei and Kaddish when they could put on the tallis before Ashrei or before Vayedaber? See Beit Yosef in siman 53 regarding Kol Bo, and examine the discussion there.
