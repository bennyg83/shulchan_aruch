Such as the sukkah of gentiles. In the Gemara we say that with ganbach there is a deficiency because they are not subject to the obligation, and the deficiency of rakbash is because they are not fixed, though they are subject to the obligation because they are Jews. In the Gemara we say: provided it is roofed according to its law. What does "according to its law" mean? Rashi explained: what does it come to teach us? If it means that its shade must be greater than its sun, that is obvious; because it is deficient in other matters, since it was not made for the sake of sukkah, should it become valid even with invalid materials? R. Chisda said: it means that he made it for the shade of a sukkah. Rashi explained: this "according to its law" that he said means that it is well roofed, so the matter is evident that its original making was for shade and not merely for privacy. Although we do not require a sukkah to be for the sake of the festival, we do require it to be for the sake of sukkah, and shade is what is called sukkah, for it shelters from dryness. The Ran wrote: "and it is made for shade," meaning that it is not made for dwelling or storage. End quote. It appears that even according to Rashi, if he made it for dwelling, it is invalid, for that is like a house in which one lives all year. Rashi speaks where it is known and evident to all that it was not made for dwelling, for they called it a sukkah, namely a temporary dwelling; but regarding that temporary dwelling there is uncertainty whether he entered it because of shade from dryness or because he sometimes wants to do something private there. About this Rashi wrote to explain R. Chisda's words, that we require proof that it was made for shade from dryness, namely that we see it is well roofed, which one does not do for mere privacy. The Tur wrote: Rabbeinu Tam explained "according to its law" as meaning that it should not be thickened like a house; this follows his view that it is invalid when rain cannot fall into it. End quote. This is difficult for me according to Rabbeinu Tam's explanation: how is the questioner's difficulty answered, when he asked what "according to its law" means? Is it not obvious that whatever generally invalidates a sukkah must be stated to invalidate here? It is understandable according to Rashi, for it teaches that although we do not know whether it was made for shade or privacy, nevertheless since it is well roofed we say the matter proves it was made for shade. But according to Rabbeinu Tam, what does it teach? It appears that this itself teaches, according to Rabbeinu Tam, that these sukkot have an invalidity because they are made to protect from rain, and they make them for this purpose; from this we learn that there is an invalidity in making any sukkah to protect from rain, for there is no invalidity here except this.
It is "make it" and for this reason. This is difficult according to Rabbeinu Tam's explanation that it is invalid when rain cannot fall, for in a stack of sheaves rain cannot fall. One can say that the invalidity because of rain is only rabbinic, so it not be like a house, while here the invalidity is Torah law. That which is valid when the stack first had a handbreadth of empty space, as stated afterward, even though rain cannot fall, appears to me to be because the rabbis prohibited the case where rain cannot fall only when it resembles a house. This is unlike a stack, which does not resemble a house at all; therefore it does not invalidate even rabbinically when rain cannot fall, as appears to me.
And he hollowed it out afterward. Meaning: not only if he hollowed it afterward from above downward and left the original hollow as it was, where it is obvious that it is valid; but even if he hollowed it afterward above, in which case the roofing in the first hollow is no longer present at all, and the roofing that is now made should have the problem of "make it," etc., it teaches that nevertheless it is valid, because at first everything above already had the name of roofing, except that now he thinned it and made it fine [Ran].
And one should not make the roofing before, etc. It seems to me that the reason is that it is written, "the festival of Sukkot you shall make," meaning the roofing: at the time you make the roofing, it must be made for shade, meaning as a tent. Anything that has no walls but only a roof is not called a tent, as stated regarding Shabbat in siman 315. If afterward he makes the walls, this is invalid because of "and not from what is already made." An explicit proof appears to me from the Mishnah regarding one who trained a vine over it, etc., where we say that he must shake it. The Rif wrote that this is so that an act be done for the sake of sukkah, and we do not say that cutting them is their making. Rashi and the Ran explained that if he roofed with attached material and afterward cut it, the cutting does not help to remove the invalidity of "make it and not from what is already made," for this is what "Sukkot you shall make for yourself" implies: when you make it, it must be fit for a sukkah. We see that at the time of making it must be fit for sukkah, and not by joining an act that will come afterward, namely cutting. The same applies here, and it is all the more so from there. There, at least the name of roofing and tent is upon the roofing, yet since it is invalid, combining the later act with it does not help. All the more so here, where at the time the roofing is made it has no name of roofing or tent at all, but only afterward, through making the walls, will the roofing be called a tent; certainly it does not help and it is "not from what is already made." Therefore it does not help above in siman 626, seif 2, to make a sukkah under attached material or under a house and remove it afterward, and it is not considered already made only because there is valid roofing there and it is a complete tent, but there is an external impediment from the attached material above it. In that case there is no invalidity in the act itself. This is unlike here, where at the time of making it is worse than invalid, for the name of tent is not upon it at all; it is obvious that later making walls cannot repair it by making it a tent for the sake of sukkah. One should not object from Rashi's words in the first chapter, page 16, on the Mishnah of one who lowers walls, where Rashi wrote: from above downward, meaning he began to weave the wall near the roofing and wove downward, etc. This implies that the roofing was already there and nevertheless it is valid. Rashi did not intend that there, but only spoke of the place of the roofing and called it roofing as it is before us. Even if you say the roofing was already there, perhaps it was in a manner where it is valid to make the roofing first, namely that there was first a handbreadth, as written here afterward. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote based on that case of lowering walls that we mentioned, that there is no invalidity in making the roofing first, and made this only a stringency at the outset to make the walls first. This does not appear to me. Even after the fact, a sukkah whose roofing was made first is Torah-invalid, as appears clear to me.
And if he made a handbreadth, etc. Meaning that he first made a wall one handbreadth wide along the entire length of the sukkah, placed the roofing on it, and afterward completed the walls. This is valid, for it is like hollowing out a stack and adding to the tent that existed at the outset.
