It is forbidden even, etc., because of muktzeh. Even Rabbi Shimon agrees regarding this type of muktzeh.
It is forbidden to move them. Since they are unfit for eating, they are regarded as muktzeh. Because they are forbidden for consumption due to the prohibition of the day, they are also forbidden to be moved, as stated by Rif and Rosh. Yam Shel Shlomo disagrees at length and writes that the prohibition against moving them is because they are muktzeh; therefore, from the second night onward they may be moved even though they remain forbidden for consumption. None of his proofs is conclusive. What he writes from Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel is contradicted by his own gloss there in siman 5. His proof from produce that left its permitted area, which may be moved as stated at the end of siman 405, is not comparable, because there it may be eaten if it returns to its original area. An item that has no possibility of permitted consumption may not be moved, as stated in the gloss at the end of seif 4. This is also implied by Beit Yosef, siman 586, in the name of Hagahos Ashri and the poskim, and by Rashba, siman 847. Yam Shel Shlomo further explains the words of Rif and Rosh, that because the item is forbidden for consumption due to the prohibition of the day it is forbidden to move, as answering the objection that neveilah is forbidden for consumption yet may be moved; its prohibition is not caused by the prohibition of the day. In my view, however, the reason is that neveilah is fit for dogs, whereas one does not intend to throw this item to dogs. It is therefore forbidden like tevel, as stated in siman 308, seifim 31 and 32. Tosafot likewise write on Beitzah 6b that the item must be prepared and standing ready for use. See what I wrote in simanim 446 and 513.
For the time needed for it to be done. This is so that he will not benefit from melachah performed on Yom Tov. One may infer from the Gemara and the poskim, who discuss a non-Jew who brought a gift, that if he does not bring it gratuitously, but the Jew fixed a payment with him before Yom Tov to bring fish, and the non-Jew delayed and brought them on Yom Tov, they are permitted immediately that evening. If the item is neither attached to the ground nor in need of trapping, it is permitted even on Yom Tov, even if brought from outside the techum. After all, if a non-Jew made a garment on Yom Tov, it may be worn where a fixed payment was agreed upon, because the non-Jew acts for his own benefit, as stated in siman 252, seif 7. This is certainly so where he merely brought it from outside the techum. Even those who are stringent there are stringent only regarding full melachah, but they agree regarding techum. Likewise, after full melachah it is permitted after the first day of Yom Tov once the time needed to perform it has elapsed. This is also implied by Beit Yosef, siman 517, who cites Mordechai concerning the incident of the turnips of Mechoza and the rams mentioned in the third chapter of Eruvin. Jewish shopkeepers told the non-Jews before Yom Tov to bring them, and fixed the price; the non-Jews delayed and brought them on Yom Tov. Raavan writes similarly. If so, how did Rava permit them by saying that they came for the non-Jews? Rather, the meaning is that the non-Jews acted for their own benefit because the price had been fixed before Yom Tov. See siman 247. One may nevertheless argue that this resembles a non-Jew who lights a lamp on Shabbos, which may not be used even where a fixed payment was agreed upon, because he made it with the intention that the Jew use it on Shabbos. Shoes, by contrast, were not made specifically for Shabbos use. Food may therefore resemble a lamp. This requires study, for perhaps a lamp is different because it must be used now, since tomorrow it will be gone, unlike food.
It is permitted immediately. This applies even to the person for whom it was brought, because one of the two days is certainly a weekday.
And some are stringent. Their concern is that if it were permitted on the second day of Yom Tov, one might tell a non-Jew to bring it on the first day so that he could eat it on the second. According to this reason, it is permitted immediately after the first day to another person, because we do not decree that one might tell a non-Jew to bring it so that his fellow could eat it; a person does not sin for no benefit to himself. According to the first view, however, the reason is that one may not benefit from melachah performed on Yom Tov. Another person must therefore also wait the time needed for it to be done, after which it is permitted according to all opinions. Yam Shel Shlomo rules this way. Semak, however, rules that even another person may not use it until after the second day of Yom Tov. Bach rules likewise, and this is also Rama's position except in a case of need.
To be lenient for others. Meaning, when that other person invited guests, it is permitted for him because we rely upon all the poskim who rule unlike Semak. This is what Terumas HaDeshen intended, and Yam Shel Shlomo writes likewise, contrary to Beit Yosef's understanding; see there. Nevertheless, Beit Yosef's ruling may also be correct. It is forbidden specifically for members of the recipient's household, but if he invited guests after the gift had been brought, they may be regarded as other people. Bach writes that Maharil was uncertain whether invited guests have the status of household members. Knesses HaGedolah cites Radbaz, part 2, siman 253, as ruling that even guests invited incidentally may not eat it in the recipient's house, though it is permitted in their own homes. If they depend upon him for their food throughout that Yom Tov, it is forbidden in all circumstances. In my view, where he invited guests who are not residents of that city, one may be lenient after the first day of Yom Tov once the time needed for it to be done has elapsed, because we may rely upon the first view. See siman 353, end of seif 1. It is possible that the host himself may also eat with them, since it would be disrespectful for him not to eat with them; see siman 168, seif 5. Perhaps even Radbaz was stringent only with an item brought from outside the techum, which is forbidden on Yom Tov to the person for whom it was brought but permitted to others, in which case guests are regarded as household members. Here, however, we may rely upon the first view, as explained. If he already has some of that same kind, the case is not considered one of need.
It was brought on the second day of Yom Tov. If it was brought at the beginning of the night, there is no concern that it was trapped that day.
On a Yom Tov adjacent to Shabbos. Although they are two distinct sanctities, as stated in siman 416, seif 1, even items that fell or were trapped on their own are forbidden on the second day because of preparation, as Tosafot write. This preparation is D'Rabbanan, as Beit Yosef writes in the name of Rashba. According to Maharshal, however, fruit detached on Shabbos that cannot be eaten raw constitutes preparation according to the Torah, as stated in his name in siman 507, seif 2. Fish, however, are D'Rabbanan according to all opinions, because they are not considered a significant preparation, as stated at the end of siman 513. See what I wrote at the end of seif 3.
For the time needed to travel. If it was brought by someone riding a horse, he need wait only the time required to ride there, as Knesses HaGedolah writes in siman 321.
