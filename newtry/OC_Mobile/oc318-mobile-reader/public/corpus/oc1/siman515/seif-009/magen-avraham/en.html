It is permitted even, etc. The non-Jew is not bringing his own property, and therefore we do not impose a decree lest the Jew tell him, “Bring it for me,” as Beit Yosef writes in the name of Rashba. It appears to me that in this case all opinions permit it, because the prohibition against benefiting from melachah performed on Yom Tov does not apply when he does not benefit from the melachah itself. This is specifically implied regarding fruit whose species is not attached to the ground. If its species is attached, a seal is required, because we are concerned that the non-Jew exchanged inferior produce for superior produce that he detached that day. The same applies to fish, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, siman 118. See what I wrote in siman 516. Knesses HaGedolah writes in siman 325 that this applies specifically to a gift. If one sent an agent to his fellow to purchase meat and send it to him, knowing that it would need to be brought on Yom Tov, it is forbidden, for it is as though he told the non-Jew to bring it on Yom Tov. Terumas HaDeshen, simanim 225 and 226, writes that the leniency applies only when the non-Jew was delayed. If the sender knew that he would travel on Shabbos, it is forbidden even when sent as a gift. This is also implied by Yoreh De'ah, siman 98, which rules that one who intentionally nullifies a prohibition may not permit it to the person for whom it was nullified.
And specifically where he had it in mind, etc. I shall present the source of this ruling, from which the law will become clear. Beit Yosef writes in the name of Rashba that the permission for an item brought from outside the techum applies specifically to produce belonging to a non-Jew. Produce belonging to a Jew is forbidden to everyone because it is muktzeh. Since it was outside the techum, its owners consciously set it aside. Property belonging to a Jew requires preparation, whereas property belonging to a non-Jew does not. At the end of the siman, Beit Yosef cites from Rashba the law of a Jew who sent a gift. Rama therefore distinguishes here by requiring that the recipient had it in mind. This is difficult. The question presented to Rashba concerned an ordinary case of a Jew who sent a gift, and Rashba should therefore have answered that it is permitted only where the recipient had it in mind. It is also difficult that if the item were muktzeh because it was outside the techum, having it in mind would not help, as stated in siman 498, seif 3. Furthermore, at the end of siman 405 we rule that produce that left the techum may be eaten by the residents of the city to which it was brought, as Rashi writes. That teaching concerns Yom Tov, when muktzeh applies, as Ran and Maggid Mishneh explain, yet it is permitted. One may answer that there the produce was within its owner's techum at twilight and therefore was not set aside. All Jews may eat it with him. It is obvious that if a person brought an item with him from outside the techum, his fellow may eat it with him, and Raavan writes likewise. This is also the reason that property belonging to a non-Jew does not require preparation. A non-Jew does not consciously set anything aside, and since it is prepared for its owner, it is prepared for everyone. Here, however, the owner had already set it aside consciously, while the recipient knew nothing of it, and therefore it is forbidden. Levush appears to rule similarly. Nevertheless, the matter requires study. It should be no worse than ownerless produce that came from outside the techum, which may be eaten even though the recipient had not had it in mind, as stated in Eruvin 45b. Ran writes similarly at the end of Beitzah regarding an animal belonging to a fattener. Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 2, writes that where an item was brought from outside the techum there is no issue of muktzeh; see there. Thus, even though a Jew sent it, there is no muktzeh. His reasoning is that only with animals located outside the techum do we say that the owners consciously set them aside, because they did not bring them within the techum before Yom Tov. Here, however, the owners did not consciously set it aside. It resembles one who transferred ownership of portions to others through a third party, as stated in siman 397. Although ownership cannot be transferred through a non-Jew, there is nevertheless no muktzeh, for it is no worse than finding ownerless produce, as explained above. This is also implied by siman 586, seif 22. According to what I wrote in siman 498, that Rabbi Shimon permits it, none of this analysis is necessary. See siman 495, seif 4.
