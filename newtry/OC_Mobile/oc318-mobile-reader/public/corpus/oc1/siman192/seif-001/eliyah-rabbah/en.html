And through His goodness we live, etc. The Bach writes that Maharshal justified the custom, since this was the practice throughout these lands. Nevertheless, it is difficult to maintain a custom not mentioned in any written source, that the one leading should say 'and through His goodness we live.' Therefore the gaon Maharal of Prague decreed that it should not be said, and from then onward this became the common practice. Magen Avraham writes that if one does say it, he is not required to repeat anything, because Maharshal justified the custom and the Levush likewise records it. I am astonished at all these great authorities because I found in Baal Halachot Gedolot, chapter Three Who Ate, the explicit wording that the leader says, 'Let us bless Him from whose food we have eaten and through His goodness we live.' This clearly implies that the leader says it. The Taz also discusses the matter at length and agrees with the Bach not to say it. He adduces proof from Tosafot on folio 50, which mentions that with ten one says 'and through His goodness we live,' implying that with three one does not. Tosafot's words are at first surprising, because the phrase 'through His goodness we live' is not otherwise the topic there. But with respect to the Taz, it is difficult to explain Tosafot his way. If the phrase cannot be said with three, why would it be said with ten? On the contrary, with ten Hashem's Name is mentioned explicitly, so there is less chance anyone will mistakenly think the leader is blessing the host. With three, where Hashem's Name is not mentioned, there is greater room for such an error unless he adds 'and through His goodness we live.' It therefore seems to me that Tosafot's statement is a greater novelty: even with ten, where there is no possibility of error, the leader still says 'and through His goodness we live.' This too is difficult for the great authorities cited above. Another possible reading of Tosafot is that even with ten one must specifically say 'and through His goodness we live' and not 'from His goodness.' See there and note this well.
[Levush] Blessed is He, etc. Knesset HaGedolah cites Maharshal, Mateh Moshe, and Lechem Chamudot that one should not say 'Blessed is He and blessed is His Name' at all, even in a zimun of ten. The Shulchan Aruch also omits it. The Bach writes that it should be said only when making a zimun of ten. Malbushei Yom Tov, however, writes that since the Tur and Rokeach include it, this must have been their received text and the Gemara merely abbreviated the formula. This implies that he holds it should be said even with three, retracting what he wrote in Lechem Chamudot. Rabbi David Abudarham and Maharan write likewise, and this is the custom. The Shelah, folio 83, also agrees it should be said. Yet his citation is surprising: he attributes this to Kol Bo, siman 25, and to Orchot Chaim. I examined Kol Bo itself and found no mention of it. Mateh Moshe, siman 346, even cites Kol Bo as holding it should not be said. Know that even the poskim who agree to say it mean only during zimun. When one recites Birkat HaMazon alone, everyone agrees he begins 'Blessed are You, Hashem' and does not say 'Blessed is He and blessed is His Name.' The reason seems to be what Darkei Moshe writes: the phrase is said to create a separation between the zimun formula and Birkat HaMazon, which is irrelevant for an individual. It also appears to me that only the leader says it, not the other two participating in the zimun. Abudarham's wording supports this, since he says the leader 'adds to them, Blessed is He, etc.' Netivot Olam, folio 41, also makes this clear. The Shelah writes that when the leader responds, 'Blessed is He from whose food we have eaten and through His goodness we live,' the diners should answer Amen before he begins Birkat HaMazon, because at that point the zimun formula is complete. He says he saw great authorities act this way, and the Gemara states that if someone enters when they are responding 'Blessed is He from whose food we have eaten,' he answers Amen. Or Chadash writes that according to this, Darkei Moshe's reason just given falls away. According to what I wrote, however, only the leader says 'Blessed is He and blessed is His Name' to separate the zimun from Birkat HaMazon. There is no difficulty from the Levush in siman 198, who writes that Amen is not answered there, because that siman explains that the case of one who enters is different. Lechem Chamudot and all the later authorities cite this, and the Taz rules accordingly. I believe this is the common practice. I later saw that Mateh Moshe writes that the custom is indeed to answer Amen, although he says the Rama in siman 198 appears to compare this to other blessings. In my humble opinion the Rama's words prove nothing because they refer to one who enters. The Levush himself follows the Rama there and nevertheless holds that the regular participants do not answer Amen. Mateh Moshe's proof from Abudarham is also not a proof; see there.
