And there is one who permits use for a matter of sanctity. See the Taz, who discusses this at length and concludes that even according to this view, this means specifically in a casual manner, but fixed learning is forbidden according to everyone. See Eliyah Rabbah and Chemed Moshe, who disagree with him, and their view is that according to this opinion even fixed use is permitted. The later authorities answer his difficulties; see Yad Ephraim, Maamar Mordechai, and Chemed Moshe. If so, it is possible to say that the first opinion, which forbids, applies specifically if the learning was in a fixed manner, but not in a casual manner. As for the proof that the Magen Avraham brought in s.k. 2 from what the poskim wrote, that even to eat near them is forbidden, the Chemed Moshe already wrote that this proof is not decisive, for perhaps they spoke of a fixed meal. What he wrote further, that so is proven from siman 678, is also no proof from there, for a Shabbos meal is certainly fixed and not casual. In truth, the view of the Rosh is known, that casual uses are permitted; only regarding examining coins, when one brings his hands near the Chanukah lamp, it is forbidden because it is degrading to them, but in general it is permitted. Regarding the Taz's difficulty, see Chemed Moshe. The Maharshal also ruled this way. Therefore, in my humble opinion, in our case, where we are in doubt regarding casual learning, one cannot bring proof to be stringent from what the Magen Avraham brought in s.k. 3, s.v. 'and even a use,' etc., and so the Maharshal ruled, for the Maharshal wrote explicitly that specifically examining coins is forbidden because one brings his hands near the lamp, but other casual uses are permitted. End quote. Therefore, in my humble opinion, even if we rule in practice like the conclusion of the Magen Avraham in s.k. 2, that casual uses are forbidden even when his hands are not near the lamp, unlike the view of the Beis Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Maharshal, whose words they copied as practical law, when we also combine with this that it is a use of sanctity and in a casual manner, such as learning before them casually, it is possible that according to everyone one need not be stringent; this requires investigation. Know further that even to learn in a fixed manner, perhaps one need be stringent only for the measure explained above in siman 672, se'if 2, but not after that. That is, even if we wish to be stringent there unlike the view of the Mechaber, that is only for an optional matter, but not for a mitzvah matter; this requires investigation. It is better, if it is a weekday and he has no other lamp by which to learn, that he extinguish it after the measure has ended and then relight it.
So that if one uses, etc. See the Magen Avraham, who wrote: nevertheless, it is forbidden to use near them, etc. See the Pri Megadim, who explained that even opposite the light of the additional lamp or the shamash, it is also forbidden ab initio. But one who examines Rabbeinu Yerucham, cited in the Beis Yosef, does not find this implied; rather, opposite the shamash, when he places it by itself a little away from the lamp, it is permitted to use by it. So too the Machatzis HaShekel wrote in explanation of the Magen Avraham's words; see there. Afterwards I found in Eliyah Rabbah that he too agreed in practice that it is permitted to use opposite the shamash. See Shaarei Teshuvah at the end of s.v. 'the shamash'; his view is also as we have written.
It will be by the additional light. According to the Bach, this is as its plain meaning: only opposite it is it permitted to use, but opposite all of them together it is forbidden, since a Chanukah lamp is among them. So too the view of the Ran is proven, as the Pri Chadash wrote here, and the Gra in siman 264. According to the Rama at the end of the se'if, since there is a weekday lamp among them, there is no longer degradation of the mitzvah when he uses opposite all of them, since the additional lamp or the shamash is there, and its light alone would have sufficed for his use, so the Chanukah lamp does not benefit him. According to his words, that which is written, 'it will be by the additional light,' means that the additional light also helps him for his use, and therefore it is permitted. So too the Magen Avraham and the Pri Chadash agreed to be lenient. However, the Pri Chadash concluded that if the weekday lamp is not sufficient for his use, meaning something that requires great examination and for which he also needs the light of the Chanukah lamp, in such a case there is certainly no room to be lenient. But the Magen Avraham concluded that according to the words of the Milchamos, one should not use opposite all of them in our case, for another reason: so that people should not say he lit them for his own need. But opposite the shamash alone one need not be stringent, as we wrote above. This is unlike the case there regarding a mixture, where this concern does not apply, as we wrote there in the name of Machatzis HaShekel; see there.
And then it is permitted, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, s.v. 'it is permitted.' Although the Magen Avraham himself wrote in s.k. 4 in the name of the Milchamos that even though there is a shamash, one should use only the light of the shamash and not the light of the Chanukah lamp, that is because the Chanukah lamp is burning for its mitzvah, and therefore recognition is needed that it was lit for the sake of the mitzvah; if he uses near them, people will say that he lit it for his own need, for sometimes one lights several lamps. But here, regarding a mixture, when he lights them he does not light them for the sake of the mitzvah but for his own need. A permitted lamp is needed only because we do not permit using the forbidden lamp alone; and since there is one permitted lamp, it is no longer called deriving benefit from a forbidden item. This is from Machatzis HaShekel. See Chemed Moshe, who agrees in practice with the Rama to be lenient when it became mixed after it went out, unlike the above law of the Milchamos; see there.
