And even though the oils, etc. This means that with a Shabbos lamp it is forbidden because perhaps it will go out and he will go back and relight it. With a Chanukah lamp, we also should have been concerned even on a weekday that perhaps it will go out and he will be negligent and not fix it; therefore it teaches us that there is no concern about this, for we hold that if it went out, he is not bound to it. Furthermore, we should have been concerned regarding a Chanukah lamp on Shabbos Chanukah that perhaps it will not burn well and he will tilt the lamp, as we are concerned with a Shabbos lamp. Therefore it teaches us that this is not a concern, since it is forbidden to use its light; if so, he does not care that it does not burn well.
If he does not put in the lamp, etc. For otherwise there is a concern that he might tilt that Chanukah lamp in order to use it after the measure of lighting, when it is permitted to benefit from it, as written above.
Or to count them by its light. In the Beis Yosef in the name of the Rosh, he wrote about this that even a degrading casual use, such as examining coins, is forbidden, because his hands are near the lamp in order to examine them carefully. So too the language implies, for it says, 'opposite the Chanukah lamp,' and it did not say, 'it is forbidden to examine coins by its light.' End quote. The Beis Yosef wrote: It is implied from his words that a casual use that is not degrading, meaning one that does not require his hands to be near the lamp, is permitted, and I do not know why our teacher did not write this. End quote. I am astonished that the Beis Yosef wrote specifically 'that is not degrading,' for even if it is degrading, like the example of examining coins, wherever he does not need to bring his hands near the lamp it is permitted. He should not have mentioned degradation regarding the use at all, but only degradation to the lamp when he brings his hands close to it. This indeed appears in the rulings of the Maharshal, who wrote as follows: Even the casual use of examining coins is forbidden, since he brings his hands near the lamp, but other casual uses are permitted. End quote. But I have difficulty with the words of the Rosh: what forced him to write 'degrading'? What was difficult to him? He came only to answer why Rav Asi had to say that examining coins is forbidden, since it was already ruled that all use of its light is forbidden. About this he answered that the earlier statement referred to fixed use, where one who sees it says that he lit it for that use and not for the sake of the mitzvah, but not to casual use; therefore Rav Asi teaches that even casual use is forbidden just like fixed use. Why, then, did he need to mention 'degrading' and that his hands are near the lamp? Furthermore, what degradation is there in examining coins? It appears to me that one word is missing in the Ashri, and it should read: 'and even a casual use that is not degrading, such as examining coins,' etc. For the reason they stated in the Gemara about this is degradation of the mitzvah, and this applies even when the use is not degrading, since regarding sukkah decorations they also say there that the prohibition is because of degradation of the mitzvah, that one may not benefit from them. So too in siman 674 it is forbidden by means of a splinter because of degradation of the mitzvah, and even to light from one lamp to another there is an opinion that it is forbidden if placement performs the mitzvah. So too he wrote in the remazim, where he did not mention 'degrading.' And since the Tur did not write 'opposite the Chanukah lamp,' but wrote that it is forbidden to use its light, this implies that even if his hands are not near the lamp it is forbidden, just as the Rosh inferred from Rav Asi's language, for he did not say 'by its light.' Therefore, in every manner it is forbidden to use its light, even if his hands are not near the lamp, as is implied by the Shulchan Aruch.
And there is one who permits use, etc. This is the author of the Ittur, cited in the Tur. This is difficult for me: the reason it is forbidden to use its light is so that it should be recognizable that it is a mitzvah lamp, as Rashi explained in the Gemara. If so, why is it permitted to learn near it? One who sees will think that he lit it not for the mitzvah of the Chanukah lamp but to learn. Furthermore, why did they permit on Chanukah the oils that are invalid for a Shabbos lamp, because we are not concerned that he might tilt, since it is forbidden to use their light? We should still be concerned that he might tilt for the sake of learning, which is permitted. One cannot answer that learning by lamplight is forbidden even by a Shabbos lamp because perhaps he will tilt, unless another person is with him to remind him, as above in siman 275, and therefore here too there is no concern since another person is with him. This is not so, for if so, why did they invalidate on Shabbos oils that are not drawn after the wick? They should have permitted them when another person is with him. Rather, it is clear that regarding invalid oils, another person with him does not help, because one cannot be careful not to tilt. If so, here too, with a Chanukah lamp, it should be invalid because perhaps he will tilt in order to use it for a mitzvah use. Therefore it appears that even the author of the Ittur permitted mitzvah use only in a casual manner, for in such a case the recognition of Chanukah is not diminished, since everyone knows that it was not lit for a casual use. Also, we are not concerned that he might tilt the invalid oils on Chanukah, because we do not decree regarding casual use. What the Beis Yosef wrote in the name of Shibolei HaLeket, that it is permitted to read in a book, also means only in a merely casual manner. If so, the Shulchan Aruch was brief here where he should have explained that 'there is one who permits use for a mitzvah' means specifically casual use, but not fixed learning. And here another person being with him does not help, for even on a weekday it is forbidden, because there would be no recognition that it was made for the sake of Chanukah.
So that if one uses, etc. Do not say that he still has benefit from the Chanukah lamp, for certainly there is greater light when there are many lamps. Here the prohibition against using the light of the lamps is only because it would not be recognizable that they were lit for the sake of Chanukah, or because of degradation of the mitzvah, as above; and here neither reason applies, since there is a weekday lamp near them, as appears to me.
For it is an item that is counted. It is written there in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 103, that although when they are sold they are sold by weight, nevertheless a mitzvah item is a counted item. Since they became mixed after they were lit for the mitzvah, and now they are counted items, they remain in their status, even though regarding weekday use they would not be a counted item. He brings proof from what Tosafos wrote in chapter Gid HaNasheh, folio 100: If you will ask, why do we say in chapter HaArel that a piece of an impure sin-offering that became mixed among one hundred pure pieces becomes nullified? They answer that consecrated items are not called fit to be honored, since they are fit only for kohanim, and they are accustomed to eat from one another without holding a debt of gratitude. Thus, even though in general it would be fit to be honored, nevertheless we follow the present state even leniently; all the more so here stringently. End quote. I am very astonished at this great man, for he wrote imprecisely. On the contrary, from there is proof to the opposite, for Tosafos wrote there and in chapter HaArel that we follow what it will be after the nullification. Therefore, there in chapter HaArel, Yevamos folio 81, in the latter clause regarding a sin-offering that became mixed with non-sacred meat, it does not become nullified, because after it is nullified it is fit to be honored with it. But in the first clause, where it became mixed with pure pieces of sin-offering, even if it becomes nullified it is not fit to be honored, because honor for kohanim does not apply in the Mikdash; therefore it becomes nullified, for it is only 'anything whose way is to be counted,' etc. End quote. Meaning: we hold that only something whose way is to be counted everywhere is not nullified. Here regarding Chanukah, if we say that the Chanukah lamp becomes nullified in the majority of weekday lamps, it will not be a counted item, for for weekday use they are not used by count. This is similar to the first clause, that if an impure piece became mixed among pure pieces, it is indeed nullified, as written. However, it appears to me that for another reason it is not nullified. There in Terumas HaDeshen, the question was where the lamps were placed and burning, and one Chanukah lamp became mixed among two shamash lamps. Regarding this I say there is a distinction: if the Chanukah lamp was fixed in its place and two shamash lamps became mixed with it there, it is not nullified, since we hold that something fixed is like half and half. But if the Chanukah lamp was moved from its fixed place and became mixed elsewhere among the shamash lamps, then it is indeed nullified in the majority. I also saw in the rulings of the Maharshal that he wrote as follows: It appears that one need not add one lamp as a shamash, as is the custom of Ashkenaz, since he has a lamp on his table, and their enactment is their ruin; I discussed this at length. Therefore, if a Chanukah lamp became mixed with two shamash lamps, one is nullified in two. End quote. His reason is not known to us. In my opinion, he intended what I wrote, that there is ruin through the shamash lamp. It appears to me that his intent is that they will benefit from the light of the shamash and come to benefit from the other Chanukah lamps; or perhaps he meant that one benefits from the added light from the Chanukah lamps. I have already written that there is no prohibition on account of this.
Rather, he should light from the mixture, etc. It appears to me that the reason of the Rav in this is that he holds that the lamps are not stricter when they are placed in a mixture than when they are burning for their mitzvah. Since at the time of lighting there is no prohibition if there is another lamp near them, even though there is greater light from them, nevertheless it is permitted for the reason I mentioned immediately above. Therefore, now as well, certainly the only prohibition is to use each lamp by itself. About this he wrote nearby that even one in a thousand is not nullified, meaning to take one of them by itself. With other dry mixtures, one dry item is nullified in two and it is permitted to eat each one separately, but here, because it is a counted item, even in a thousand it is not nullified to light one lamp by itself. This appears to me to be the reason for the Rama's words. But I have no way to settle his words, for certainly he is discussing extinguished lamps, and in that case even the Terumas HaDeshen agrees that it is nullified in the majority. In Terumas HaDeshen he mentioned there that they are placed and burning, and then they have the status of a counted item according to his view; but if they are extinguished, the importance of being counted has departed from them. This law is explicit in what is written at the end of siman 677, that leftover oil that became mixed on the eighth day with other oil is nullified in sixty. Consequently, if it is a wax candle, its law is like dry in dry, and it is nullified in the majority, unless we say that he is discussing the day of Chanukah, when it became mixed and is then fit to be joined with others to light it on the second night; therefore it is a counted item. But I have already written that there is no counted item here, and there is no foundation here to forbid at all.
