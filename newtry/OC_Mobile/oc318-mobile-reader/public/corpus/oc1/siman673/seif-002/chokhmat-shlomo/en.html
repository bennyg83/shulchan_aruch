Kindling performs the mitzvah; therefore, if it went out before its time passed, he is not required to tend to it, etc. Note: the language is astonishing, as Turei Zahav wondered, and we already wrote several ways in our novellae. Now it seems as follows, based on what Beis Yosef wrote to resolve why in siman 672 they wrote that leftover oil in the Chanukah lamp may be benefited from, while in siman 677 he ruled the opposite. Beis Yosef answers that if it remains from the measure needed for lighting, it is forbidden to use, but if more than the required measure remains, one may benefit from it. At first glance it seems possible to add flavor to this matter: since we hold that nothing whose mitzvah has been performed remains subject to me'ilah, and for this reason the ash of all burned items is permitted, here too, if it burned for the required measure and more remains, its mitzvah has been performed, and therefore it is permitted for benefit. But if the required measure remains, then its mitzvah has not been performed, and therefore it is forbidden for benefit. However, the fact that Beis Yosef did not write this reason appears to be because we hold that kindling performs the mitzvah, and if it went out he is not required to tend to it. If so, immediately upon kindling the mitzvah was performed, and even if the required measure remains, its mitzvah has been performed. He therefore had to give another reason for this. See what I wrote about this regarding a Torah scroll that wore out and was erased, whether one may write mundane text on it, in a responsum to the holy community of Botishan. In any event, according to this one may say as follows. Generally, we find many times that they forbade keeping prohibited items lest one come to a mishap, as in the first chapter of Pesachim regarding making it into perfume; see there. If so, here too it would have been proper to say that if it went out within the time, he is required to relight it immediately, since it is forbidden for benefit and there is concern that perhaps one will come to a mishap with it. But one may say that in a rabbinic matter they were lenient. Yet we find several matters where they strengthened their words like Torah law. This is when there is one rabbinic level; but with two rabbinic levels, they certainly did not strengthen it like Torah law. According to this, one may say that there are two meanings of if it went out, he is required to tend to it. The one the Talmud debates is from the law of the Chanukah lamp itself: whether he is obligated to relight it as a Chanukah lamp. A practical difference would be whether he must bless again. If it were from the law of the Chanukah lamp, he would have to bless, as with tefillin when the knot snapped and the like. So too it appears from Rashba, brought by Beis Yosef in this siman, who wrote that if it went out unintentionally, he need not bless, for we hold that if it went out he is not required to tend to it. This implies that if he were required to tend to it, he would have to bless. About this the Talmud debates, and this law does not depend on whether kindling performs the mitzvah or placement performs the mitzvah; therefore it does not resolve from this. We hold that if it went out he is not required to tend to it, and he is not obligated to relight it because of the law of the Chanukah lamp. Nevertheless, for another reason it would have been proper to require him to tend to it, since at any rate it is forbidden for benefit and requires burning, and there is concern for a mishap lest he forget and benefit from it. But for this reason he would not need to bless, since it is only because of concern for a mishap, similar to what we find in siman 434, that on a search after Pesach one does not bless, since it is only because of concern for a mishap; see there. However, this would be if we held that placement performs the mitzvah. If so, its mitzvah has not yet been performed, since it did not burn the required measure. If this were Torah law, it would be forbidden for benefit by Torah law; automatically, even with the rabbinic Chanukah lamp, they would have strengthened it like Torah law to relight it immediately, so that one not come to a mishap. But since kindling performs the mitzvah, immediately when he kindled it, its mitzvah was performed. If so, even by Torah law it would be permitted for benefit. Thus it is not comparable to Torah law, and wherever the equivalent does not exist in Torah law, we do not say that they instituted it like Torah law, as stated in Gittin 64. Therefore, although the Rabbis were stringent and forbade the oil that remains from the required measure, nevertheless since it is two rabbinic levels, we should not be stringent to be concerned for a mishap, because the body of the prohibition is rabbinic and also its mitzvah has been performed. Therefore if it went out he is not required to tend to it even because of concern for a mishap, and it is permitted to keep it for the other days. This is well understood; examine it. Now, the explanation of the distinction is as follows: if kindling performs the mitzvah, it is considered that its mitzvah has been performed; but if placement performs the mitzvah, then even though we hold that if it went out he is not required to tend to it, it is not considered that its mitzvah has been performed. If kindling performs the mitzvah, in truth he kindles only at the beginning, not every moment. Perforce, the enactment is based on the rule that anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing. Since at the beginning he performed the main mitzvah, we say afterward regarding the rest that anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing. If so, at the beginning of the lighting his entire mitzvah was performed, since it was fit for mixing. But according to the one who says placement performs the mitzvah, one may say that the mitzvah is the whole time of its placement, for it remains placed constantly, and he performed all of its placement when he placed the oil. If so, one may say that the mitzvah is the entire time of its placement. This is unlike kindling, where he did not kindle all the oil, only a little, and perforce the rest works through the rule of fit for mixing. But with placement, he placed the vessel, and the mitzvah is throughout its placement. The rule that if it went out he is not required to tend to it is only because the Merciful One exempts an unavoidable circumstance and they did not obligate him to trouble himself and rekindle. In this case, although the Merciful One exempts an unavoidable circumstance, at any rate there is no fulfillment of a mitzvah and its mitzvah has not been performed; therefore it is forbidden for benefit by law. This is clear and correct, and this must be said to understand the Mechaber's intent, since he made them depend on one another; examine carefully. There is another way to resolve this. One may say that the reason whether if it went out he is required to tend to it depends on whether kindling performs the mitzvah or placement performs the mitzvah. For according to the one who says kindling performs the mitzvah, it is difficult: he only kindles for a moment, and the rest burns on its own. Perforce, the intent is as I wrote in the first chapter of Bava Kama regarding one's fire being because of his arrows, that what burns at the beginning is considered as though he kindled all of it immediately. According to this, the later measure of how much it should burn is not the main mitzvah, but an appendage of the mitzvah. Regarding an appendage of the mitzvah, we say that anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing. But according to the one who says placement performs the mitzvah, the main mitzvah is that it rests there in its place. If so, the whole time of its placement is the main mitzvah, and with the main mitzvah we do not say that anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing, as Rashbam wrote in Bava Basra 81b; see there. Therefore, if it went out, he is required to tend to it. But at first glance it is difficult: even though anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing, that is only after the fact, as written in Menachos that if he did not mix it, it is valid. But ab initio there is a mitzvah to mix. If so, here too, ab initio why should he not be required to relight it? Perforce, the reason is not because of anything fit for mixing, but because this was the enactment from the beginning. If so, there is no distinction between whether kindling performs the mitzvah or placement performs the mitzvah. However, one may say that since by Torah law, regarding a meal-offering, even if he did not mix it, it is valid, then if by Torah law it is valid after the fact, it is proper to say regarding the Chanukah lamp, which is rabbinic, that it is permitted ab initio. Thus one can properly say that it depends on anything fit for mixing. But this depends on the dispute in Gittin 65: Rav Chisda holds that anything the Rabbis instituted they instituted like Torah law, while another opinion holds that we say they instituted like Torah law only in a matter that has an equivalent in Torah law, but in something that has no equivalent in Torah law, they did not institute it like Torah law. Now, since we hold that a minor, even if he can distinguish a pebble and throw it, etc., cannot acquire on behalf of others, perforce we hold like Rav Chisda, according to the Gemara's answer that partnerships in alleyways are rabbinic. If so, in something that has no equivalent in Torah law, they did not institute it like Torah law. Therefore it is well understood. The Talmud's question whether kindling performs the mitzvah or placement performs the mitzvah follows Rav Chisda, that in every matter they instituted it like Torah law. If so, one cannot say that the reason if it went out he is not required to tend to it is because anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing, for ab initio he would still be obligated to kindle, as with a meal-offering. Perforce, it does not depend on anything fit for mixing, but rather this was the enactment of the Sages from the beginning. Therefore the Talmud does not resolve from this. But we hold that there is a distinction between whether something has an equivalent in Torah law or not. If so, Chanukah, which has no equivalent in Torah law, was not instituted like Torah law. Therefore one may say that this is the reason that if it went out he is not required to tend to it: because anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing, and since by Torah law it is valid after the fact, it is permitted rabbinically ab initio. Thus this properly depends on whether kindling performs the mitzvah, and it proves that kindling performs the mitzvah. This seems to me in my humble opinion, with the help of Heaven, and it is well understood; examine it.
