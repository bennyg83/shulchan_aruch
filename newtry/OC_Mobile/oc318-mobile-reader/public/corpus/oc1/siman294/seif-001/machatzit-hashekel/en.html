In Chonen HaDaas, etc., the widespread custom, etc. This excludes the view that one should not say both 'Atah Chonen' and 'Atah Chonantanu,' because that would be repetitive, and that one of them suffices. Even among those who use only one formulation there is a dispute: some say one should begin with the ordinary blessing, 'Atah Chonen l'adam daas,' and then continue into the distinction between sacred and profane, so as not to alter the fixed text established by the Sages. Others say it is preferable to begin only with 'Atah Chonantanu.' The Levush writes to say both, but would say 'v'chonenu me'itcha' twice. The Bach cites and rejects this. Therefore Magen Avraham brings the Bach's formulation: say both 'Atah Chonen' and 'Atah Chonantanu,' but say 'v'chonenu me'itcha' only once at the conclusion. The Bach explains that this is not repetition. First we praise Hashem for granting knowledge to humanity generally, including worldly wisdom, in which Israel and the nations share. Then we praise Him for granting us, Israel, an additional gift: knowledge of His Torah, 'which He did not do for every nation.' Finally we petition, 'Grant us from Yourself,' for both kinds of wisdom, worldly wisdom and Torah wisdom.
And if he forgot, etc. This requires study from the original enactment. Berachos 33a states that when the exiles returned in the days of Ezra they were poor, so Havdalah was instituted in prayer. When they later became wealthy, it was instituted over a cup. When they again became poor, they fixed it in prayer, while also declaring that one who recites Havdalah in prayer must still recite it over a cup.
If so, at the beginning of their return, when Havdalah was instituted only in prayer and not over a cup, one who forgot and neither prayed the proper Amidah nor recited Havdalah would have had to pray Shacharis and include Havdalah in the compensatory prayer, because he had not yet recited Havdalah at all. This is analogous to our law that Havdalah over a cup may still be recited until the end of Tuesday, as explained below in siman 299.
This also appears from the Gemara. There it asks: one who recites Havdalah over a cup, must he also recite it in prayer? Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak answers by an a fortiori argument from prayer: prayer was the primary enactment, yet the Sages said that one who recites Havdalah in prayer must also recite it over a cup. All the more so one who recites it over a cup, which was not the primary enactment, should still recite it in prayer.
See Yoreh De'ah, siman 341, regarding an onen on Motzaei Shabbos, who neither prays nor recites Havdalah that night. On Sunday after burial, although he is exempt from a compensatory Maariv, he must nevertheless recite Havdalah over a cup. Siman 299 explains that the time for Havdalah extends through Tuesday.
