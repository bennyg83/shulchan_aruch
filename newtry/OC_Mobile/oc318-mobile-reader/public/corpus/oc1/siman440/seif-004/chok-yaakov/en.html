Although the custodian accepted liability. Rosh writes at the beginning of Pesachim, in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah, that because the principal money belongs to the owner and the custodian merely lent him space to guard the owner’s property, the obligation of removal rests upon the owner. The Mechilta states: Just as “your house” means something in your domain, so “your borders” means something in your domain. This excludes a non-Jew’s chametz in a Jew’s possession, which is in the Jew’s house but he cannot remove because it is not his. It also excludes a Jew’s chametz in a non-Jew’s possession, which belongs to the Jew but is not in his domain. Rosh explains that the Mechilta must concern a non-Jew who lent money to a Jew upon the Jew’s chametz. Taz finds two serious difficulties in Rosh. First, if the Mechilta concerns a loan, then the parallel case of a non-Jew’s chametz with a Jew must also concern a loan. Why should the Jew then not be required to remove it? Any ordinary loan secured by collateral leaves the collateral under the lender’s liability. Second, if a custodian’s house is considered the owner’s house because the custodian lent him space, then when a non-Jew deposits chametz with a Jew, it should be regarded as the non-Jew’s house and the Jew should not be required to remove it. Because of these questions Taz adopts another approach. In my opinion, Rosh’s words are entirely correct. The first question is no question at all. The rule that collateral is ordinarily under the lender’s liability applies when the borrower is a Jew, because of the laws of custodians, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 72. Collateral belonging to a non-Jew is different, because the Jew has no law of a custodian over it, as explained above in seif katan 8. Tosafos writes this explicitly in Shevuos under the heading “A custodian of a lost object.” The second question is likewise no difficulty. Where the owner is present and is obligated in removal, the duty rests upon him. Where the depositor is a non-Jew, who has no obligation of removal, or where the Jewish owner is not present, the duty falls upon the custodian because the chametz is in his domain, as explained in siman 443. This is also Levush’s view. Olas Shabbos challenged him without basis. His challenge from the Talmud likewise disappears upon examination. Certainly, where the owner is present, the obligation of removal rests upon the owner. Taz also rules this way. See below, siman 443, and my responsum there.
