A Jew who deposited, etc. The Rosh writes in the first chapter of Pesachim: Some say that when a Jew deposits chametz with a non-Jew, the custodian is obligated to destroy it, even though it belongs to the depositor, because it is not in the depositor's domain. They bring proof from the Mechilta, which teaches: Why does the Torah say, 'in your houses'? Since it says, 'throughout your borders,' I might understand the words literally. The Torah therefore says, 'in your houses.' Just as your house is in your domain, so too 'your borders' means in your domain. This excludes chametz belonging to a non-Jew in the domain of a Jew, which is in his house but which he cannot destroy, and it excludes chametz belonging to a Jew in the domain of a non-Jew, which belongs to him but is not in his domain. Rabbeinu Yonah writes that the obligation rests upon the depositor, and he brings proof from the verse, 'If it is stolen from the man's house,' which excludes the house of the Temple treasury. I did not understand his proof, nor did he explain the Mechilta. It appears to me that since the custodian lent his house for the safekeeping of the depositor's property, it is called the depositor's house. The Mechilta, however, deals with a non-Jew who lent money to a Jew against his chametz. End quote. According to this explanation, the first clause of the Mechilta, concerning a non-Jew's chametz in a Jew's possession, must likewise involve a loan. Why, then, is the Jew not obligated to destroy it? We rule that whenever a Jew bears responsibility for chametz, he must destroy it, and all the more so where he lent money against it, for ordinarily the collateral is the lender's responsibility. I also find it difficult that according to this explanation, since lending one's house to a depositor causes it to be called the depositor's house, the same should apply when a non-Jew deposits chametz with a Jew, and it should be called the non-Jew's house. Why, then, does the Gemara rule that if a non-Jew deposited chametz with a Jew, the Jew must destroy it, whereas if the Jew designated a house for him, he need not destroy it? According to the present approach, even without designation it should already be regarded as the non-Jew's house. It appears to me that Rabbeinu Yonah's view, that the depositor is obligated to destroy it, can be explained from the discussion on folio 5. The Gemara says that this works well according to the opinion that something which causes monetary liability is treated like money, for that reason a Jew is obligated to destroy chametz for which he bears responsibility. But according to the opinion that it is not treated like money, what can be said? The Gemara answers that here the case is different because the verse states, 'It shall not be found.' We see that the Torah creates a special rule here, departing from the general principle that something which merely causes monetary liability is not treated like money, and treats it as money for the laws of chametz because of the words, 'It shall not be found.' According to the opinion that something which causes monetary liability is not treated like money, the phrase 'in your houses' limits this special rule. The special rule applies only in your houses and not in the house of a non-Jew. Thus, the Torah did not distinguish between a Jewish house and a non-Jewish house in every case. Rather, it limited only the novel rule involving chametz belonging to a non-Jew for which a Jew bears responsibility. Since this is a novelty, we apply it only to the extent expressly stated. By contrast, where the chametz actually belongs to a Jew, no novelty is involved, because it is truly his property. The distinction does not apply, and the chametz is forbidden even in a non-Jew's house. According to the opinion that something which causes monetary liability is treated like money, namely R' Shimon in the chapter Merubah, no special rule is derived from 'It shall not be found.' Consequently, when the Torah excludes a non-Jew's house through the phrase 'in your houses,' the exclusion applies to all cases, both actual property and property that merely causes monetary liability, since they are equivalent. Therefore, actual chametz belonging to a Jew in a non-Jew's house is exempt. Rabbeinu Yonah therefore properly obligates the depositor, because he rules that something which causes monetary liability is not treated like money. The Tanna of the Mechilta, however, holds like R' Shimon, who says that it is treated like money. Accordingly, there is a leniency for the depositor when the chametz is in the custodian's house. If actual chametz belonging to a Jew in a non-Jew's house is exempt, then chametz belonging to a non-Jew in a Jew's house is likewise exempt, because liability requires both disadvantageous factors together. The Ramban writes this in his Torah commentary as the practical halacha.Based on this, in my humble opinion, Rabbeinu Yonah's proof from the chapter Merubah is readily understood. We rule that something which causes monetary liability is not treated like money, for the Gemara expounds, 'If it is stolen from the man's house,' but not from the house of the Temple treasury. Even if the person who consecrated the item bears responsibility for it, the thief is exempt from double payment because he stole consecrated property. Were something which causes monetary liability treated like money, the thief should be liable, since the person who consecrated it bears responsibility. We must therefore conclude that we rule that it is not treated like money. Here, however, the Torah creates a special rule through the words, 'It shall not be found,' and that special rule applies only 'in your houses,' not to chametz belonging to a non-Jew. This also properly explains the Ran's difficulty in the first chapter, in the Baraisa concerning a non-Jew who entered a Jew's house with dough in his hand. The Ran writes: The Rambam, however, writes in chapter 4 of Hilchos Chametz that a Jew who deposited chametz with a non-Jew violates the prohibition of bal yera'eh. I do not know his source, because when the Gemara later says that he violates the prohibition, it means only rabbinically. Otherwise, why does it say, after Pesach? End quote. According to what I have written, the Rambam's ruling is clear. He rules that something which causes monetary liability is not treated like money. Therefore, a Jew is liable for his own chametz even in a non-Jew's house, because the exclusion of 'in your houses' does not refer to such a case, as explained. This is very correct, with God's help. It is obvious that this discussion concerns the obligation when the depositor is present. If he is not present, the custodian must act as explained in siman 443, seif 2. See what I wrote there.
