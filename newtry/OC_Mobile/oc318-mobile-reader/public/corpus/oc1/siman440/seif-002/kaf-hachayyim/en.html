If one is liable for it, etc. For example, a Jew said to the non-Jew, 'If it is stolen or lost while in my possession, I will pay you,' and the non-Jew secured this undertaking through an act of symbolic acquisition so that the Jew could not retract. The same applies even without such an acquisition if the governing law requires the Jew to pay once the Jew explicitly made this undertaking when accepting the deposit. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 9. Chok Yosef, subsection 1, however, writes that liability means liability under the governing law, because it is irrelevant whether the Jew would be liable under Jewish law; the non-Jew will certainly bring the claim under the non-Jew's law. See there. Birkei Yosef, subsection 1, writes likewise. It appears to me that everything depends upon the circumstances. If it is common for a non-Jew to bring such a claim before a Jewish court, and the Jew is liable under Jewish law, the Jew must eliminate the chametz. If it is not common for the non-Jew to bring such a claim except under the governing law, the Jew need eliminate it only when liable under the governing law.
