(1) If it is, etc. Scripture states, “Leaven shall not be found in your homes,” implying that even leaven that is not entirely his may be forbidden according to the Torah. This refers at least to a case in which he accepted responsibility for it, through which Scripture treats it as his. An ordinary deposit for which he bears no responsibility may be retained, as another verse states, “It shall not be seen by you,” from which the Sages expound: you may not see your own chametz, but you may see that of others.
(2) He is responsible for it. This refers to one who explicitly accepted responsibility and performed a formal act of acquisition, for otherwise the laws of guardianship do not apply to property of a non-Jew, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 301, seifim 1 and 4. If the law of the government makes a guardian liable even without an explicit stipulation, the chametz is considered within the Jew’s domain from the time he becomes its guardian, even without a formal acquisition and even if he did not expressly accept responsibility for theft, as explained below.
(3) Anywhere in his domain. Even if he rented or lent the non-Jew a separate place for the chametz within his house or courtyard, this does not help when the Jew accepted responsibility, because rental does not transfer ownership and the chametz remains in the Jew’s house. Nevertheless, if he retained it until after Pesach, one may rely on the poskim who hold that when a distinct place was designated for the chametz, he did not transgress bal yera’eh even though he accepted responsibility. They require that the chametz be available under the Jew’s control like an ordinary deposit that he may move wherever he wishes. When a separate place is designated, it is not considered available to him, and after Pesach the chametz is permitted in eating and benefit. A designated place means a house, room, or corner assigned in the non-Jew’s presence by rental or loan. If the Jew first accepted the deposit without designating a place and afterward assigned one without the owner’s knowledge, it does not help, since the non-Jew may want his chametz to remain under the Jew’s general care. Even a partition does not help in that case.
(4) He must destroy it. When the time for destruction arrives, if the non-Jew is not present so that it can be returned to him, the Jew must arrange for its removal. The later authorities dispute whether nullification and sale are effective, since the substance of the chametz is not his. Many hold that they do not help, while many are lenient. See Sedei Chemed, Chametz U’Matzah, siman 5, section 33. According to Magen Avraham, seif katan 1, he may deposit it with another non-Jew, or sell the house in which it lies to a non-Jew who accepts responsibility for theft and loss. The proper course is to do both, selling the house and the chametz.
(5) To destroy it. If he failed to destroy it and Pesach passed, many poskim prohibit it in eating and benefit like a Jew’s chametz that remained through Pesach. If the non-Jewish owner comes afterward, the Jew may return the chametz to him, and the benefit gained by discharging his debt is not a concern. Where there is reason to fear that the non-Jew will sell it back to a Jew, causing him to violate the prohibition of chametz that remained through Pesach, one can argue that it should not be returned. Other authorities are lenient in the fundamental ruling and hold that the Sages penalized only chametz belonging to a Jew, not chametz belonging to a non-Jew that was deposited with a Jew. One may rely on them at least to permit benefit.
(6) Even if he deposited it again, etc. Since he accepted responsibility, it is treated as his chametz, and resembles a Jew who deposits his own chametz with a non-Jew, which is forbidden as explained in seif 4. Even if the second non-Jew accepted all the responsibility that rested upon the Jew, the Jew’s responsibility toward the first owner has not been removed. Some are lenient because the chametz itself is not his, it is not in his home, and the second non-Jew accepted corresponding responsibility for theft and loss. According to this view, if the Jew sold the room in which the chametz lies to a non-Jew, it is likewise permitted when that non-Jew accepted responsibility for theft and loss. Peri Megadim.
(7) Another non-Jew. This means someone other than the owner. If he returned the chametz to its owner, it is permitted, for this is merely accepting responsibility for a non-Jew’s chametz while it is in the non-Jew’s house.
(8) Some say, etc. Ideally one should be concerned for this view. Therefore, if the owner is present, the chametz should be returned to him. If he is absent, the Jew should sell the chametz and also nullify it. Although many poskim hold that sale and nullification do not help for chametz whose substance is not his, one may rely on the lenient opinions in this situation. If the sixth hour has already arrived and it can no longer be sold, as explained in siman 443, one may rely on the first view. The Jew need not destroy it completely, but may retain it until the owner comes to the city. If the owner comes during Pesach, he should return it immediately to avoid the possible Torah prohibition involved in retaining it according to the stringent view. He must take care not to touch it with his hands, as explained in siman 444. After Pesach it is permitted even if it was not returned to its owner.
(9) Some disagree. Since the Jew did not accept responsibility and is exempt both under Torah law and under the law of the government, the chametz is not considered his merely because the non-Jew can compel him. Nevertheless, the primary ruling follows the first opinion. See above, seif katan 4. If he failed to destroy it and Pesach passed, one need not be stringent, and it is permitted even for eating.
