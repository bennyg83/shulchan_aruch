If he is liable for it. This is Tur’s wording. Rambam, however, writes in chapter 4, “if he accepted liability,” and Rashi at the beginning of Pesachim also explains it this way. This implies that liability must be expressly accepted, and that absent an express acceptance he is treated as not having accepted liability. Levush likewise writes this. Ran, cited by Beit Yosef, however, explains that this does not mean that he expressly accepted liability. Rather, once the non-Jew deposited it with him, he is treated as though he accepted liability, just as Bava Kamma states that if one says, “Bring your ox into my domain,” he becomes liable. Bach and my brother-in-law in Eliyah Zuta likewise write that the law follows what is explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 291: ordinarily he is treated as having accepted liability unless he said, “Place it before me,” unlike Levush. That is indeed the law where one Jew deposits with another, as explained there in Choshen Mishpat. Yet siman 301 there states that where a non-Jew deposits property, the laws of custodians do not apply and the Jew is exempt from payment, because Scripture says “his fellow.” Rambam derives this from the Mechilta, as Beit Yosef explains there. Accordingly, our case must concern one who accepted liability, meaning that a formal acquisition was made from him, as Beit Yosef explains there. Alternatively, even without a formal acquisition, if he expressly stipulated and accepted liability, then although strict law would not obligate him to pay, because the law and custom of the kingdom make him liable, he must remove it according to all opinions, even according to the dissenting view below, as Maggid Mishneh cited by Beit Yosef explains. Likewise, according to the alternative opinion below, even liability for negligence alone requires removal. Therefore, according to the opinion in Choshen Mishpat that although the laws of custodians do not apply to a non-Jew’s property, one is nevertheless liable for negligence, he must remove it here. According to the first opinion, however, which requires liability for theft and loss, a non-Jew’s deposit does not require removal unless the Jew expressly accepted such liability.
For theft and loss. See Tosafos in Bava Metzia, chapter HaOmanin, and in Shevuos, chapter Shevuas HaDayanim. They write that according to Rashi, removal is required only where he accepted liability even for unavoidable accidents. See Shach to Choshen Mishpat, siman 72, who writes that many poskim disagree with Rashi and labors to reconcile Rashi’s position. See also Beis Levi, who challenges Shach.
Wherever it is, it is in his domain. Even if he designated a separate house for it, he must nevertheless remove it. It is proper to be stringent initially, as Beit Yosef writes in the name of Rashba. The later authorities conclude that initially one should be stringent even where a separate house was designated, but after the fact one need not be stringent. See seif katan 4.
He must remove it. If he transgressed and did not remove it, Magen Avraham writes that if the non-Jew comes after Pesach, the Jew may return the chametz to him, just as one who stole chametz and retained it over Pesach may say, “Your property is before you.” He nevertheless suggests that it may be forbidden to return it, lest the non-Jew sell it to another Jew, who would then stumble through chametz that remained over Pesach. His words imply that it is obvious to him that this chametz is prohibited after Pesach. In my opinion, that is not obvious at all. Since the chametz belongs to the non-Jew who deposited it and is returned to him intact, although the Jew sinned by failing to remove it, this is still chametz belonging to a non-Jew that remained over Pesach and is permitted for benefit. I later found explicit support for this in the Jerusalem Talmud, Pesachim, chapter Kol Shaah, halachah 2: A non-Jew deposited his chametz with a Jew. Rabbi Yonah permits it and Rabbi Yose prohibits it. Rabbi Yonah said, “It is the non-Jew’s chametz; the Jew is the one who transgressed concerning it.” The Jerusalem Talmud concludes like Rabbi Yonah, that it is permitted for the reason I gave. Even if this remained a doubt between the disputing opinions, chametz that remained over Pesach is prohibited only Rabbinically, and we would rule leniently, as explained below in siman 449. Furthermore, he is not selling the chametz to the non-Jew but merely returning the non-Jew’s own property and saying, “Your property is before you.” Even if there were some basis to forbid a Jew from eating it, the custodian is not obligated to pay the non-Jew and purchase the chametz from him merely so that another Jew should not possibly stumble through it later. We need not be excessively stringent because perhaps the non-Jew may sell it to a Jew.
Even if he redeposited it. This implies that even if the first non-Jew deposited it with the Jew under the Jew’s liability, and the Jew then redeposited it with another non-Jew who accepted liability, the Jew must nevertheless remove it. Since it is treated as his own, it resembles chametz belonging to a Jew that was deposited with a non-Jew. This is implied by Shiltei HaGibborim at the beginning of Pesachim and by Rama’s unqualified wording. This is unlike Magen Avraham, who writes without proof that if the second non-Jew accepted liability, no removal is required. In my opinion, the ruling is as I have written.
In the possession of another non-Jew. Magen Avraham writes that Rama’s precision, “another non-Jew,” implies that if the Jew redeposited it with the original non-Jew, then even though liability remains upon the Jew, he need not remove it. This resembles one who accepts liability for a non-Jew’s chametz while it remains in the non-Jew’s house. Even those who disagree with Rabbeinu Tam concerning a designated house disagree only where the designated place is in the Jew’s home. Where it remains in the non-Jew’s house, all agree that it is permitted. Although the final law is correct where the Jew accepted liability for chametz that always remained in the non-Jew’s house and never entered the Jew’s domain, that differs from our case. Once the Jew accepted the deposit, the chametz is treated as his own for purposes of removal. If he later redeposits it, even with the original non-Jew, it resembles a Jew who deposits his own chametz with a non-Jew. What difference is there between the original non-Jew and another? Rama mentioned another non-Jew because he wished to teach that even if the second non-Jew accepts liability, the Jew must still remove it, as explained in seif katan 5. With the original non-Jew, if that non-Jew again accepts full liability, the chametz has effectively been returned to him completely, because the Jew then has no remaining liability or authority over it. A second and primary explanation is that Rama spoke in the ordinary manner. Why would the Jew give it back to the original non-Jew as a deposit? If he returns it, presumably he returns it completely. Otherwise it is merely exchanging one burden for another for no purpose.
Only a shomer chinam. My brother-in-law in Eliyah Zuta concludes that initially even a shomer chinam should remove it. After the fact, if he forgot to remove it, one may rely upon the first opinion that removal is unnecessary unless he is liable for theft and loss.
And some disagree. The view of the major poskim follows the first opinion, and that is primary. If he is liable because of statutory law or the law of the kingdom, then even according to the dissenting opinion he must remove it, as Raavad and Maggid Mishneh explain in Beit Yosef.
