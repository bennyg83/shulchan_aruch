The Jew is liable when he tells the non-Jew, “The house is before you; place it wherever you wish.” If, however, he designates for the non-Jew a specific corner within his house in which to place the chametz, he need not remove it, even if he accepted liability for it. Since he lent the non-Jew a specific place, it is regarded as though the chametz is not in the Jew’s domain, and as though he is guarding a non-Jew’s chametz in the non-Jew’s house. This applies when the place was provided without payment. If the Jew rents the place to him for payment, it is forbidden because he profits from an item from which benefit is prohibited. Nevertheless, after the fact, if he accepted the payment, it is permitted for him to benefit from it. This is from Maharam Yaffe. Even if he later deposited it with another non-Jew, etc. This is from Hagahos Alfasi at the beginning of Pesachim. It appears obvious that if the non-Jew originally placed his chametz in another non-Jew’s domain and the Jew accepted liability for it there, the Jew need not remove it. Beit Yosef writes that if the Jew designated a corner for the non-Jew, he need not remove it, so this case is certainly no more stringent, since the chametz was placed in the non-Jew’s domain from the outset, even though the Jew accepted liability. Many proofs may be brought for this. If the Jew bears no liability, he is not obligated to remove it, but he must place before it a partition ten tefachim high. Even when a specific place was designated for the non-Jew, one should be stringent and require a partition ten tefachim high. Maharitz writes as follows: With due respect to his Torah scholarship, it appears to me that no proof can be brought from that case. There, the owner of the chametz is a Jew. Therefore, there is also a concern in the opposite direction. If the robber does not tell the owner, “Your property is before you,” but instead pays him the value of the chametz, the owner will derive benefit from chametz that remained in existence over Pesach, since he will receive compensation from the robber for it. We therefore say that the robber may tell him, “Your property is before you. Take your chametz.” One might argue that the robber should fulfill the mitzvah of removing chametz, and after destroying it he should become liable to pay, as in a case where the object was lost. There would then be no concern that the owner benefited from the chametz through receiving payment, since the chametz would no longer exist. The answer is that the robber does not wish to do so. Instead, he tells the owner: “The mitzvah of removing the chametz is before you. You fulfill the mitzvah, and your property is before you. I do not wish to pay you money and thereby cause you to benefit through money.” He therefore honors the owner of the chametz with the mitzvah of removing it. That case is unlike ours. Here, if the Jew returns the chametz to the non-Jew, the mitzvah of removing chametz will not be fulfilled. The Jew will benefit by returning the chametz because he will thereby avoid liability to pay. In truth, however, such benefit is not prohibited. Consider this carefully.
