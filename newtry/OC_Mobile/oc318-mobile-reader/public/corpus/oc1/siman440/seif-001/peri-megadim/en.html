One may answer that because Rambam reads that an item which can cause monetary liability is regarded as money, the same should follow by reasoning in a case of unavoidable loss. Otherwise, according to the view that it is not regarded as money, from where is this law known? The same applies to seif 4, since the item itself is returned. Taz also asks that in the case where the chametz is located in the Jew’s city, he should sell it. This is difficult, because it does not belong to him and he cannot sell it. His suggestion that the Jew give it to another non Jew is insufficient nowadays, and certainly according to Rama. One may explain that in the first arrangement the Jew bears liability even for unavoidable loss, whereas the second non Jew accepts liability only for theft. Certainly, according to Rama’s wording, even if the second non Jew accepts all the liability borne by the first, it remains forbidden. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 1; with Hashem’s help, this will be explained there. One who examines Maggid Mishneh to chapter 4, halachah 4, of Hilchos Chametz U’Matzah, and Lechem Mishneh, will see that they resolve this. Even though the chametz was held under compulsion, the case was that the authorities gave the Jews flour to bake into bread. As long as the bread remained in a Jew’s house, he bore liability even for unavoidable loss. If they returned it, however, the authorities would accept it back. The instruction to remove it from their houses therefore meant that it should be returned to them. Raavad understood Rambam to mean that the authorities gave them bread to guard and that they could not return it to the army. He therefore asks how Rav Nachman could say, “Bring it out,” as explained there. Rava’s proof from Rav Nachman may still be valid even though one might say that there the Jews were liable even for unavoidable loss. Perhaps the authorities imposed liability only if the bread was lost through a preventable occurrence, in accordance with Rashi’s opinion in Bava Metzia, chapter HaOmanin. See Bach and Chok Yaakov, seif katan 2. The expression, “he accepted liability,” implies that the Jew accepted it willingly and not through coercion. The first occurrence of the word “not” is a scribal error. One may say that Rambam speaks specifically of theft and loss, but not negligence, as we will explain, with Hashem’s help. The coercer imposed liability only for theft, not for unavoidable loss, so the proof is certainly valid by an a fortiori argument.
Now, the Mechaber cites the first opinion without qualification, that liability applies specifically to theft and loss, but not negligence, unlike Rambam and Ri. Regarding coercion he reaches no clear conclusion. Therefore, after Pesach one may regard the matter as a doubt and rule leniently, whereas during Pesach it is a possible Torah prohibition. If the coercer imposes liability only for negligence, then during Pesach there is a compound uncertainty, especially because the Mechaber cites the first opinion anonymously. Nevertheless, one should not deliberately create a compound uncertainty by accepting the deposit initially. See Magen Avraham, siman 467. Levush’s words may be understood accordingly. See there.
I was asked about non Jews who place flour in a Jew’s house and pay him a fixed weekly rental, while the Jew bears liability for it during Pesach. How should they act? I answered that the Jew cannot sell the flour because it is not his. This is not comparable to the principle that one may acquire a benefit for a person in his absence and appoint him as an agent. There is no solution unless the depositor gives him permission to sell it, or he deposits it with another non Jew who accepts liability, as Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 1, and rents the place to that non Jew. The payment would not then constitute wages for guarding chametz. See siman 450; here there is even more room for leniency. With Hashem’s help, this will be explained further in Magen Avraham. Our flour is made from soaked wheat and is full chametz. Nullification does not help because the flour does not belong to the Jew.
