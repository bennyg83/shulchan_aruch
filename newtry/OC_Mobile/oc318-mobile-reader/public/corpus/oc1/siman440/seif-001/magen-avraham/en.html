In the possession of another non-Jew. Meaning one who is not the owner, because the chametz is regarded as belonging to the Jew. It is therefore comparable to one who deposits his chametz with a non-Jew. This is the language of Shiltei HaGibborim. This implies that if he redeposited it with its original owner, he does not violate the prohibition. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 72, seif 3, that even if the first custodian redeposits it with its owner, the second is a shomer chinam while the first remains liable for theft and loss. Nevertheless, this is like a Jew accepting liability for a non-Jew’s chametz while it remains in the non-Jew’s house, which is permitted, as Tur writes in the name of Rabbeinu Tam. Although we do not rule like Rabbeinu Tam where the Jew merely designated a corner in his own house for the non-Jew, all authorities agree that it is permitted where the chametz is in the non-Jew’s house. If the Jew deposited it with another non-Jew and that second non-Jew accepted liability, it appears to me that it is permitted. Some authorities permit even a Jew’s own chametz deposited with a non-Jew who accepted liability. Although we do not rule that way, regarding chametz belonging to a non-Jew it should be permitted. The second non-Jew must, however, accept liability for theft and loss. The fact that the first non-Jew may claim against the Jew, while the Jew in turn claims against the second non-Jew, is irrelevant. Wherever a Jew was obligated to remove the chametz and transgressed by failing to do so, if the non-Jew comes after Pesach the Jew may return the chametz to him. Chametz that remained over Pesach is prohibited only as a penalty, and no penalty was imposed in this case. Choshen Mishpat, siman 363, similarly rules that one who robbed chametz and retained it over Pesach may say, “Your property is before you.” If it was lost, he must pay its value. Thus he benefits by returning the chametz and nevertheless is permitted to do so. The same applies here. It is possible that even if the non-Jew comes during Pesach, one may allow him to take it, because regarding items prohibited for benefit one may say, “Your property is before you,” as stated in Bava Kamma 98. Nevertheless, one might say that it is forbidden to give it to the non-Jew for another reason. Perhaps the non-Jew will later sell it to a Jew, who will then stumble through chametz that remained over Pesach. Hagahos Maimoniyos writes similarly at the end of Hilchos Chametz. See siman 467.
