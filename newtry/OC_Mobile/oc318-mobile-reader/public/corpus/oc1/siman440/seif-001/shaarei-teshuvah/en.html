Nullification and declaring the chametz ownerless do not help for chametz belonging to others for which he accepted liability. These acts do not remove him from the prohibitions of bal yera’eh and bal yimatzei. His only remedy is to burn it, designate a separate house for it, or erect a partition ten tefachim high before Pesach. Shaagas Aryeh writes in simanim 79 and 80 that if he failed to make this remedy of designating a house or erecting a partition until Pesach had already begun, the chametz is forbidden for benefit after Pesach. If, however, he made the remedy on Erev Pesach after the sixth hour, then although even regarding a non-Jew’s chametz for which he accepted liability there is a positive commandment of tashbisu from the sixth hour onward, and he consequently violated that positive commandment, the chametz is nevertheless permitted for benefit after Pesach. He further writes that regarding avoiding the Torah prohibitions of bal yera’eh and bal yimatzei, it is sufficient to remove the chametz from his domain even during Pesach. Regarding the positive commandment of tashbisu, however, removing it from his domain is ineffective even according to Torah law until he actually destroys it. See simanim 83 and 84. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, siman 41, which defends the ruling of that living lion, the great authority, against the challenge of another great authority in the introduction to Mekor Chaim. See also Beit Ephraim, siman 37, concerning one who sold his chametz. During Pesach a member of his household entered the cellar, removed the spigot from a barrel of whiskey, poured some into a vessel, and hid it so that he could drink it after Pesach. After Pesach, when the Jew repurchased the chametz from the non-Jew, the owner discovered the vessel of whiskey. There is reason for concern that by lifting it, that person acquired it and accepted liability for it. Based upon several grounds for leniency explained there at length, Beit Ephraim concludes that it may at least be permitted for benefit, though not for drinking, since there is no substantial financial loss. For added certainty, it should be mixed with twice its quantity of permitted material and sold in that state. In a case of substantial loss, it may possibly be permitted even for drinking through mixture with twice its quantity. See Beit Ephraim, siman 38, concerning a non-Jew who stole chametz from a Jew before Pesach and, during Pesach, threw it at night in front of the Jew’s house. The Jew refused to take it, and others took it to a fortress. After Pesach they returned it to him. There is room to permit it, but only for benefit and not for eating. See Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 77, concerning one who collected a debt from a non-Jew through an officer’s order. During Pesach the officer came and seized everything found in the non-Jew’s possession toward the debt, including grain. After Pesach Jews testified that this grain had been taken from the bottom of a pit and had been washed. The non-Jew agreed to take it back and pay money in exchange. In a case of substantial loss, Noda BiYehudah permits returning it to the non-Jew and accepting the money, provided the grain showed no visible splitting and provided the creditor did not realize when he received it that it had been washed. The acquisition was then made in error. The grain itself, however, should not be permitted even under these circumstances. See there.
