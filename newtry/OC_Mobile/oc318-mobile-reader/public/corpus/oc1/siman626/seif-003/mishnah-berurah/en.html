(16) The thin beams - That are called latis.
(17) It is valid - Even to sit beneath the beams themselves, although they are invalid s'chach, for they were not fixed into the roof for the sake of shade but in order to place the roof tiles upon them. Nevertheless, since he performed an act and removed the roof tiles for the sake of making a sukkah for shade, it is as though he performed an act on the beams themselves and prepared them for the sake of sukkah, and they became valid. But if he did not remove the roof tiles in order to make a sukkah there, but it had previously been built without a roof, then since he performed no act on the roof for the sake of making a sukkah for shade, the beams or rafters are actual invalid s'chach. Their law is as written in se'if 1 regarding a tree, namely that wherever the valid s'chach is not so abundant that, if the amount shaded by the thin beams were removed from it, the sukkah's shade would still be greater than its sun, it is invalid. Some later authorities are stringent about this and hold that in every case we require that even if what is opposite the beams were removed, its shade would remain greater than its sun. In truth, with ordinary sukkahs like ours, where people usually cover the whole sukkah, in every case its shade will certainly remain greater than its sun, and it is valid. However, in order to satisfy all doubts, one should see that there is a space of three tefachim between each of the thin beams. All this is specifically when he makes a sukkah below, beneath the roof. But if he places s'chach on top of the latis, even though they are within less than three tefachim of one another, it is valid. We do not say lavud here to combine all the beams so that they should be considered one wide beam of invalid s'chach more than four tefachim wide, which would invalidate the sukkah as explained in siman 632, because valid s'chach lies between them and they do not combine.
(18) Beneath roofs that are made, etc. - Meaning, even while they are closed, and this is not called make it, and not from what was already made, as above in se'if 2 in the gloss. Know that the Bach's view is to be stringent in this, that one must open the roof doors before placing the s'chach. The Magen Avraham, Eliyah Rabbah, and other later authorities agreed. If he forgot and placed the s'chach while the roofs were closed, he must open the roofs and shake all the s'chach, meaning that he should lift each piece of wood by itself and put it back down for the sake of shade, then lift the next piece of wood and put it back, and so on with all of them. If he placed s'chach over the ceiling and afterwards removed the ceiling, it is invalid according to everyone, because the s'chach on the ceiling initially did not have the name sukkah, since there were no walls here. Therefore removing the ceiling does not help to validate the sukkah.
(19) And to close and reopen them - This is permitted even according to the stringent authorities mentioned above, since it was initially made validly, meaning that he opened the roof before placing the s'chach. Although while it is closed the sukkah is called invalid, as the Rema wrote at the end of his words, nevertheless this is not included in make it, and not from what was already made; it is like one who spread a sheet over it and then removed it.
(20) Hinges, etc. - For it is like an opening. If they have no hinge, it is permitted through a non-Jew. If all year they were fixed with nails, so that opening them is forbidden according to the Torah, and he forgot to open them until Yom Tov began, whether it is permitted to open them through a non-Jew when he has no other sukkah on the first night of Yom Tov, because of shevus in a place of mitzvah, and likewise whether it is permitted to tell a non-Jew to place s'chach for him on Yom Tov when he has no other, the Peri Megadim is uncertain. He concluded: Heaven forbid that I rule leniently; all this requires examination. See what the Bikkurei Yaakov wrote about this. If it is still bein hashmashos, it appears that one may certainly be lenient through a non-Jew.
(21) That one should not sit, etc. - For it is like sitting in a sukkah built inside a house, as the Gra writes. One must be careful that the roofs open well, so that they stand upright level with the walls and are not somewhat tilted over the s'chach. If they are somewhat tilted, even if the tilt is not large enough to invalidate the sukkah, nevertheless one must be careful not to sit in that place where the roof slopes and leans over the s'chach. The Peri Megadim wrote at the end of siman 644 that a sukkah is exempt from mezuzah. It implies that a room of the whole year, where one lives constantly, and during Sukkos he removes the roof, is nevertheless exempt from mezuzah during Sukkos, for seven days are not called permanent. If one has two rooms, one inside the other, and he goes from his room to the sukkah, the doorway of the sukkah requires a mezuzah because of his room. If he goes from the sukkah to his room, in a manner where the doorway is from the sukkah room, one can say that he is obligated in mezuzah on the doorway in the sukkah that opens to the public domain and the like, for it is like a gatehouse.
