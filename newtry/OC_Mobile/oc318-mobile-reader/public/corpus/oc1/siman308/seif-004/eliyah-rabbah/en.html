[8] Whose function is permitted, etc. The same applies to a utensil used for both prohibited and permitted functions (Shiltei Gibborim).
[9] [Levush] Nevertheless, etc. Shelah, p. 136, warns that even God fearing people often fail to notice this rule. Taz asks how people carry knives in their sheaths hanging from their belts on Shabbos and go with them to the synagogue, where there is no need for them. One cannot say this is an unusual manner of carrying, since seif 1 prohibits even knives inserted in a sheath with other knives. Taz answers that the prohibition applies only when the knife is not needed at all that day. If he will use it that day and carries it so it will be ready when needed, it is permitted. According to what I write below in seif katan 2 regarding knives in a sheath, the earlier case may concern moving the knife by itself. One can also distinguish that there he moves the sheath directly by hand, whereas here it hangs from his belt.
[10] Sacred writings, etc. Likewise cups, bowls, bottles, a fig cake mold, a pot scraper, and a small table knife (Shiltei Gibborim, p. 144). Rambam's commentary to the Mishnah, chapter Kol HaKelim, somewhat implies that cups and bowls are not equivalent to sacred writings, and Kol Bo, p. 33, likewise implies this. Tosafot on 123b, however, supports Shiltei Gibborim. This requires study, and the later authorities did not note it. A Megillat Esther appears to have the status of sacred writings.
