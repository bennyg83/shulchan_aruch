(Subparagraph 5) Undiluted vinegar, he is exempt. It seems to me this is specifically when it bubbles, etc., as written in Yoreh De'ah siman 123. It is explained there that if a non-Jew touched our vinegar, it is not forbidden. Rabbenu Tam wrote that it specifically requires that it bubble, etc.; if not, it is forbidden through a non-Jew's touch because of doubt, for we are not expert in what is vinegar. There are some who call it vinegar, while some people recite kiddush on that wine. But if it bubbles, etc., then it is certainly vinegar. According to this, the Magen Avraham's intent here in writing that he is certainly exempt is necessarily specifically when it bubbles, etc. But if it does not bubble, it is a doubt. This is forced, however, since in any event he would be exempt because of doubt. One cannot say there is no practical difference for us between liability and exemption, since in any event there is a prohibition, because there is a practical difference regarding a sick person, as the Taz wrote in subparagraph 1. According to this, here too there is a practical difference with a sick person who needs to drink vinegar: if possible, it is better to give him vinegar that bubbles, where there is certainly no liability, rather than vinegar that does not bubble, where in any event there is a possible liability. But what seems more correct to me is that the Magen Avraham holds that with vinegar that does not bubble, he is certainly liable, and not merely because of doubt. This is from his own reasoning, without proof: since on Yom Kippur one is liable for the pleasure of his throat, if it is not so strong that it bubbles, certainly he has pleasure and it is proper that he be liable. So too he wrote above in siman 204, subparagraph 13, in these words: when it does not bubble, he certainly has pleasure and needs to recite a blessing, end quote. He needed proof from Yoreh De'ah siman 123 only that when it bubbles there is no doubt, for it is certainly called vinegar and harms the one who drinks it, and he is certainly exempt if he drinks it on Yom Kippur.
And in the Gemara it implies, etc. And they pressed to explain this, etc. This is because in Yoma 81 Rabbi and the Rabbis disagree about one who drinks vinegar on Yom Kippur. The Rabbis exempt, and Rabbi makes him liable because it restores the soul.
Rav Gidel taught: the law is not like Rabbi. The next year everyone went out and drank vinegar diluted with water. Rav Gidel became angry and said: Say that I said after the fact, meaning if he drank, he is exempt; did I say at the outset? Say that I said a little; did I say a lot? Say that I said undiluted; did I say diluted? This is our reading. According to this it implies that regarding what he first said, "say that I said after the fact," he then said, "say that I said a little," meaning that even after the fact, when we say he is exempt, that is specifically a little, but a lot, even after the fact, makes him liable. Therefore the Beis Yosef wondered about the Tur for not explaining that with a lot he is liable. He wondered even more about the Rambam, who wrote the opposite, that undiluted vinegar, even if he drank a lot, he is exempt; see there.
And the main view is as the Bach wrote, etc. Their reading was the reverse of our reading, and this was their reading: Say that I said undiluted; did I say diluted? Say that I said a little; did I say a lot? Say that I said after the fact; did I say at the outset? So is the reading in the Rosh. According to this, the explanation is as follows: "Say, etc.; did I say diluted?" And he added, "Say that I said a little," meaning: although I did not say diluted, nevertheless even with diluted there is a side to be lenient, namely specifically a little. The Bach wrote that "a little" means less than a cheekful, but a lot, no. He then added further, "Say that I said after the fact, etc.," meaning that all the leniencies I said, whether undiluted and a lot, or diluted and a little, are specifically after the fact, if he drank then he is exempt; did I say at the outset? For even undiluted and a little is forbidden at the outset. These are the Bach's words.
And so it implies in the Rosh, chapter 6 of Berachos, siman 23. There we learned in a Mishnah: over vinegar one recites Shehakol. The Rosh asked: the Talmud compares the law of a blessing to Yom Kippur, and anything for which one is exempt on Yom Kippur if he ate it, one does not recite a blessing over it. But regarding vinegar we rule on Yom Kippur like the Rabbis against Rabbi, that if one drank vinegar he is exempt. Why then did we learn that one recites a blessing over it? And why did he not answer that the Mishnah, that one recites a blessing over it, speaks of vinegar that is not strong? See what the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 204, subparagraph 13. He answered, in one answer, that the Mishnah in Berachos speaks of one who has pain in his teeth and whose remedy is vinegar, and he put much vinegar into soup and drinks it for healing, and the vinegar is primary. The Lechem Chamudos wrote that for this reason he needed to say the vinegar is primary, for if the soup were primary, he would recite over the soup its proper blessing and it would exempt the vinegar. In any event, through the soup he has pleasure from the vinegar and must recite a blessing over it, end quote. This implies that without soup, even a large amount, etc., for the Rosh wrote that he put in much vinegar, and even so he required that he put it into soup for it to be fit for a blessing. The same applies regarding Yom Kippur, for their laws are equal, as above. However, this proof can be rejected: perhaps what the Rosh wrote, "and he put in much vinegar," does not mean that the quantity of vinegar was large, but rather that it was more than the soup, for this is the way of drinking it for healing. Rather, the Magen Avraham's proof is that if one recites a blessing over a large amount of plain vinegar, why did he need to establish it as a case where he put it into soup and had tooth pain? He should have answered more simply that it speaks where he drank a large amount of undiluted vinegar.
