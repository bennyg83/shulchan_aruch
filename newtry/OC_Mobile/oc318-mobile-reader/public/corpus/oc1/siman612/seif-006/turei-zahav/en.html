Until he loathes his food, he is exempt. But at the outset it is forbidden, as we say in the Gemara regarding vinegar: after the fact, yes; at the outset, no. Regarding foods that are not fit, the Tur wrote in the name of the Raavyah that there is not even a prohibition, while the Rambam wrote that one gives him lashes of rebellion for this. In my humble opinion there is no dispute here, as I will write with Heaven's help. The words of the Raavyah require examination: he says there is no prohibition with foods that are not fit, but we say in the Mishnah that with foods that are not fit he is exempt, which implies exempt but forbidden. One cannot say that it means exempt and permitted, for the Gemara says about what the Mishnah taught, that if one drank brine or muryas he is exempt: but vinegar would make him liable? Whose view is the Mishnah? It is Rabbi's, for it was taught: Rabbi says vinegar restores the soul. Rav Gidel taught: the law is not like Rabbi. The next year everyone went out, diluted vinegar, and drank it. Rav Gidel heard and became angry. He said: say that I said after the fact; did I say at the outset? Say that I said a little; did I say a lot? Say that I said undiluted; did I say diluted? We see that the exemptions here regarding Yom Kippur are only after the fact. One cannot say that the Raavyah permitted only less than the measure, and this is what he calls "a little," for he stated plainly that if one ate foods that are not fit, there is no prohibition, implying even a large amount. There is another difficulty from what the Beis Yosef wrote about the Tur, who brings the words of the Raavyah without dispute, implying that he agrees, yet nearby regarding liquids he writes that if one drank liquids that are not fit, namely brine or muryas and undiluted vinegar, he is exempt but forbidden. It is obvious that there is no distinction between foods and liquids regarding not being fit. The Beis Yosef wrote to answer this difficulty that the Raavyah spoke of things that are entirely unfit, while brine or muryas are things eaten through dipping. I am astonished at that holy mouth: since he entered into this distinction, he should have said that the Raavyah and Rambam do not disagree either, for the Raavyah speaks of things entirely unfit, such as dirt. I can bring proof from what is stated in Yoreh De'ah siman 238: one who swore not to eat and ate dirt is exempt. This is Rava's statement in Shevuos, chapter 3, and the reason is that he did not consider it eating at all. There it means exempt and permitted, for since dirt is not included in the oath, why should it be forbidden at the outset? Even if you say that in a case where there is a reason to forbid at the outset it is nevertheless forbidden, here on Yom Kippur the matter depends on settling the mind, and certainly eating dirt does not settle his mind. The Rambam, who wrote regarding foods that are not fit that one gives him lashes of rebellion, means things like bitter herbs or foul resins, which the Rambam mentioned explicitly in chapter 2 of the Laws of Resting on the Tenth, for at least the name of eating applies to them. So too with undiluted vinegar, and about this Rav Gidel said: after the fact, yes; at the outset, no. Therefore the Beis Yosef should not have made a dispute between the Raavyah and the Rambam.
