(Seif katan 6) It is permitted, etc. This is not comparable to chametz, etc., which one may not touch lest he eat it. It appears that "there" means in the Bach, siman 611.
For according to the Ran and Maggid Mishneh. The Magen Avraham brought them above, siman 611 seif katan 2, for they hold that for this reason they permitted trimming vegetables from Minchah time onward: so that one would not need to prepare everything at night and be distressed. They permitted this specifically from Minchah time onward, because it is close to eating time and resembles one who has bread in his basket, and we do not decree lest he come to eat from it. But before Minchah it was not permitted, for we decree lest he eat.
According to this, it is proven that according to them, unlike Rashi's explanation of trimming vegetables, which the Magen Avraham also brought above in siman 611, there is no proof from there, it is forbidden to touch foods on Yom Kippur lest one eat from them. The Bach wrote that according to this, the teaching in Yoma 77b, "the school of Menashe taught: Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says a woman may rinse one hand in water and give bread to a child," and Abaye said this is because of Shivta, must be explained as follows. Rabbeinu Tam explained that in their days a harmful spirit named Shivta was common, which would choke a child if bread were given to him by one who had not first washed his hands, even if he had washed in the morning. Rabbeinu Tam wrote that this harmful spirit is not found among us, and so wrote the Tur in siman 613. It follows that a woman may touch food; one must say that there it speaks of a child unable to take the bread himself, and therefore it was permitted for the woman to give it to him.
And this is forced. Meaning, it is forced to establish the teaching of the school of Menashe specifically with such a child. If so, that teaching implies that we do not decree lest he eat.
A Kohen Gadol may offer while an onen. Meaning, although an ordinary kohen may not offer when he is an onen, a Kohen Gadol is biblically permitted to offer even when he is an onen. But since even a Kohen Gadol is nevertheless forbidden to eat sacrificial meat while he is an onen, R. Yehudah holds that rabbinically even offering is forbidden for a Kohen Gadol who is an onen, as a decree lest he eat. The Gemara asks: what is different from Yom Kippur? Meaning, R. Yehudah said that another wife is prepared for the Kohen Gadol before Yom Kippur lest his wife die, because we require "and he shall atone for himself and for his household," meaning his wife. And so that he not have two wives, for "his household" implies specifically one household, he divorces before Yom Kippur the wife he already has, stipulating with her that if that wife dies on Yom Kippur, the divorce takes effect retroactively from before Yom Kippur; and the other woman he now betroths conditionally. See the Gemara at length regarding the condition. The difficulty is: what does preparing another wife help? In any event, when his first wife dies on Yom Kippur, he will be an onen because of his wife's death.
If so, learn from this that we do not decree since everyone does not eat. Another difficulty: why did it not ask simply, what is different that on every Yom Kippur, etc.? Meaning, if everyone is forbidden to touch foods because of concern lest he eat, then why is the Kohen Gadol permitted to offer on Yom Kippur; we should decree lest he eat. Perforce one must say that it is impossible otherwise, for he must offer, since the Merciful One commanded it. If so, even when he is an onen and there is an added eating prohibition because of aninut, what of it? Why should we decree, since in any case regarding every Kohen Gadol we cannot be concerned lest he eat? And if because he is preoccupied due to his aninut there is greater concern that he will forget and eat, the Magen Avraham holds that the Gemara had not yet raised the reasoning of preoccupation until afterward, when it answered, "is he not preoccupied?" as will be explained below.
Rather, certainly on Yom Kippur we do not decree. Meaning, even for everyone; and the questioner also considered that we are not concerned for any person that he will eat through forgetfulness.
Rather, this is the explanation: we are concerned, etc. Meaning, if so, why did R. Yehudah forbid a Kohen Gadol who is an onen to offer lest he eat, since we are not concerned for forgetfulness? Therefore the Magen Avraham wrote that we are not concerned that he will eat through forgetfulness; rather, we are concerned that through error he will eat, because he sees that he offers while an onen, etc. Therefore the Gemara asks from Yom Kippur, when he offers while an onen. According to the Magen Avraham, one must say that the question is not that he may eat on Yom Kippur, for we are not concerned for forgetfulness. Rather, since he sees that he offers while an onen, he will know that a Kohen Gadol who is an onen may offer, while R. Yehudah forbids him to offer while an onen. The questioner's question is not at all that we should decree lest he eat, for in truth on Yom Kippur we are not concerned for eating. Rather, R. Yehudah stated generally that an onen may not offer, yet on Yom Kippur he perforce offers while an onen. To this it answers: on Yom Kippur, since everyone does not eat, he too will not eat. Meaning, R. Yehudah prohibited an onen from offering only where the decree lest he eat applies, namely on a weekday; but on Yom Kippur, where "lest he eat" does not apply, he may offer. And if through offering on Yom Kippur as an onen he will know that a Kohen Gadol who is an onen may offer, and will therefore err and eat also on a weekday when he is an onen:
On a weekday he has a distinguishing sign, for it is forbidden to offer. With this one can somewhat resolve Rashi's words there. On the question, "Does aninut take effect upon the Kohen Gadol? But he divorces her," meaning if she died on Yom Kippur, he divorced her retroactively by the condition explained there at length, as above. If so, why did it initially ask according to R. Yehudah that on Yom Kippur he offers as an onen when his wife dies, as above; he is not an onen at all even if she dies. The Gemara answers: although aninut does not take effect, is he not preoccupied? Rashi explained: For with sacrificial meat we require joy and greatness, as it is written, "I have given it to you for distinction," in the manner that kings eat; end quote. The simple understanding is that if he eats, although aninut did not take effect upon him, there is nevertheless a prohibition in that eating because he is preoccupied and not in joy. Based on this, Tosafot Yeshanim in the responsum of the great Chacham Tzvi wondered about Rashi: why did Rashi need to seek an eating prohibition because joy is required, and where did the Yom Kippur eating prohibition go? See there. According to the Magen Avraham's words it works well, for certainly the first questioner's difficulty was not that we should decree that through his offering he would eat on Yom Kippur, as above. Rather, the difficulty was: how could R. Yehudah state generally that a Kohen Gadol who is an onen may not offer, when on Yom Kippur, according to him, he perforce offers as an onen, as above? To this the Gemara asks: but aninut does not take effect upon him. It answers that he is nevertheless preoccupied. Rashi then came to explain: what of that, since he is still not an onen? Therefore Rashi wrote that with sacrificial meat, even regarding its eating, we require joy and greatness, all the more so regarding its offering. If so, just as R. Yehudah forbade a Kohen Gadol who is an onen to offer, he should likewise forbid a Kohen Gadol to offer when he is preoccupied and not in joy.
