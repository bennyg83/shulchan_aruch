And if. See Taz and Terumas HaDeshen, siman 1. Regarding the three hours mentioned in siman 233, Levush calculates seasonal hours from sunrise until the beginning of sunset, which resolves the matter somewhat. See also Magen Avraham there, seif katan 3. Reciting Krias Shema with its blessings is not considered a blessing in vain; see Beit Yosef. Regarding Taz’s challenge to the Mechaber, the Mechaber clearly holds that the essential time begins specifically at tzeis hakochavim. One recites Krias Shema in the synagogue with its blessings without intending to fulfill the obligation, and at tzeis hakochavim he intends to fulfill it, exactly in accordance with Rabbeinu Yonah. Levush also implies this. The blessings were not instituted specifically for Krias Shema, as shown by the fact that we do not recite “Who sanctified us with His commandments and commanded us regarding Krias Shema.” Nevertheless, I do not understand why such a condition should detract or cause harm. He can stipulate: if the halachah follows those Tannaim who hold that its time begins before tzeis hakochavim, let this reading fulfill the obligation; otherwise, I will recite it afterward at its proper time. This is not comparable to adopting two contradictory practices. Regarding the suggestion to rely on the Krias Shema recited before sleep, one can answer that ideally the obligatory Krias Shema should be recited before eating. Although he is not subject to the severe censure mentioned by Chazal because he already recited Krias Shema in the synagogue, as explained in letter 2 and in Magen Avraham, seif katan 4, ideally he should still recite the obligatory Krias Shema immediately before eating, as Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 2. See Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 3.
Know that according to the opinion that the time for Krias Shema begins at tzeis hakochavim, one certainly does not fulfill it beforehand. If one is unsure whether he recited it at the proper time, but knows that he recited it in the synagogue, must he repeat it? In our novellae at the beginning of Berachos we were uncertain. It may be that bein hashmashos is not a doubt here at all, but rather that he certainly did not fulfill the obligation because it is not yet the time of lying down. This may explain the Mishnah’s wording, “from the time the kohanim enter to eat their terumah.” There too, bein hashmashos may not be a doubt, but the sky must be cleared of the sun’s light. See Tosafos Yom Tov there. Yet Shabbos 35, regarding Rabbi Yose’s bein hashmashos, implies somewhat that the kohanim remain forbidden because of doubt. It may be possible to explain that the known Torah status of terumah treats a doubt as certainty with respect to prohibition, although lashes are not administered, as explained in Toras HaAsham. The same would apply to Krias Shema. Since bein hashmashos is not the time of lying down, there is no double doubt and he must repeat it. Still, according to us there are several Tannaim who hold that the time begins earlier, which may create a double doubt. Yet perhaps no Tanna allows it as early as the case under discussion. Regarding the Krias Shema before sleep, one might argue that only the first section is recited, whereas according to the opinion that two sections are Torah-level, our doubt remains. The Yerushalmi at the beginning of Berachos seeks to prove that one who is uncertain whether he recited Krias Shema must repeat it from the Baraisa that one who read before the proper time did not fulfill the obligation, although that case is not one of doubt. See Chiddushei HaRan at the beginning of Berachos, and our longer discussion there; this is not the place for it. Regarding Rashba’s statement that Chazal were lenient concerning prayer, Beit Yosef cites him in siman 233. This requires study. One can understand such leniency regarding Maariv, which is optional and Rabbinic, but Krias Shema is Torah-level. Perhaps those who pray Minchah until evening and at times recite Krias Shema while it is still broad daylight hold that the time for Krias Shema begins at plag haminchah, and Chazal were likewise lenient regarding Minchah because of the burden on the congregation. Mentioning the Exodus from Egypt at night is effective, and reciting the section of tzitzis in the synagogue may fulfill that obligation even though it was recited while still day. We have written elsewhere that the exposition “all the days of your life, including the nights” may not be a full derivation. With Hashem’s help, this will be explained further in Hilchos Pesach regarding the Haggadah.
