(Seif katan 8) One may not, etc. For the Gemara challenges the statement that one may not greet his fellow with “shalom” before praying.
But did we not learn that between the sections one may greet out of respect? The Gemara answers that the prohibition concerns one who rises early to go to his fellow’s doorway. The difficulty is: What was the Gemara’s question from the Mishnah concerning between the sections? That case is different, because he has already recited some of the blessings, since he is standing between the sections. It must therefore be that whatever is forbidden by the essential law remains forbidden until one has prayed the eighteen blessings of the Amidah. What is permitted here concerns only that which the poskim added as a stringency.
But regarding his question that the Shulchan Aruch’s rulings contradict one another. Meaning, here Beit Yosef permits after one has recited some of the blessings, whereas in seif 3, regarding attending to one’s affairs, Beit Yosef forbids it unless one has already prayed the eighteen blessings of the Amidah. Merely having recited some blessings does not suffice. Yet the Gemara implies that the two laws are equivalent, since in both cases it uses the phrase “before he prays.”
Just as regarding attending to one’s affairs, “before he prays” means before the Amidah, so too the phrase “before he prays” stated regarding greeting another should also mean before the Amidah. What, then, is the difficulty? He himself writes that the greeting mentioned in the Gemara, namely rising early to go to one’s fellow’s doorway, is indeed prohibited until one has prayed the Amidah.
In the Shulchan Aruch: Some say that even when one encounters his fellow, etc. “So that he will take heed, etc.” These are the words of Raavad, who writes: “So that he will take heed, etc.; because he alters his wording, he will remember.” I therefore have a doubt. In their days the normal custom was to greet with “shalom,” so when one said “Good morning, master,” it constituted an alteration and thereby reminded him. Today, however, everyone normally says “Good morning, master” rather than greeting with “shalom,” so there is no alteration. It is therefore possible that according to Raavad, nowadays one should not say “Good morning, master.”
