Or. See Taz. One who studies Beit Yosef will see that he too maintains that setting out on a journey is forbidden because it is included in attending to one's affairs, for there is no greater instance of attending to one's affairs than this. His difficulty is how, in seif 8, Rashba permits one to pray while traveling; see Tur. He answers that there the person is compelled, because the caravan will not wait for him, and for this reason he was permitted to leave beforehand. Taz, however, answers that from dawn the principal time for prayer has not yet arrived, since it begins at sunrise. Perhaps even after the eastern horizon becomes illuminated it is still not the principal time. He may therefore leave before praying, and this is not considered attending to his affairs. Such activity is forbidden only once the principal time has arrived, just as seif 2 rules concerning greeting. Magen Avraham, seif katan 6, disagrees. Since in a compelled situation one may pray from dawn, dawn is properly called the time for prayer in such a case. Rashba permitted the compelled traveler to leave and pray on the road. According to this, when one is not compelled, greeting and attending to one's affairs are forbidden from dawn. According to Taz, it appears that greeting is also permitted before sunrise. Yet what is the Gemara's question from the law that one may greet between the sections of Krias Shema, since the exceptionally scrupulous also recite Krias Shema before sunrise? One must say that before the eastern horizon becomes illuminated it is not called the time for prayer, but once it becomes illuminated, since after the fact one has fulfilled his obligation, it is properly called the time for prayer. This would necessarily mean that the time when one can recognize his fellow at a distance of four cubits is at least equivalent to illumination of the eastern horizon. See siman 58, seif 1, regarding this.
