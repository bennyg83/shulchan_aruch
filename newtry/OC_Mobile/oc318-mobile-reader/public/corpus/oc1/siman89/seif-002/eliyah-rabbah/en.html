[4] Some business, etc. Lechem Chamudos, page 12, writes that Levush wisely wrote that he passes by there, because it is forbidden to attend to business before prayer, and they did not wish to formulate the ruling with reference to people committing a prohibition. Nachalas Tzvi, however, does not mention Lechem Chamudos and writes that Levush altered the language unnecessarily, for the authorities carefully wrote “some business” to teach that only actively engaging in business is forbidden. His intention is to distinguish between “some business” and “business.” It appears more correct to distinguish that actively engaging in business is forbidden, whereas merely inspecting the business is permitted, because simple observation is not prohibited. Perhaps this is also Nachalas Tzvi's intention if his words are corrected. Study this carefully. The phrase “to pass by there” means that even though he arises early to go to his fellow's doorway, since he does so for business and not to greet him, he may say, “May my master's morning be good.”
[5] Rather, to greet him, etc. Even if he does not arise early to go to his doorway, but merely walks in order to encounter him, it is forbidden.
[6] Even without, etc. Rather, he is passing there in any event, as Levush writes. It appears that the same applies if he happens to encounter him on the road; bowing is forbidden. This is also implied by the Shulchan Aruch and is the ruling of Bach. Lechem Chamudos and Nachalas Tzvi discuss at length the source for the Shulchan Aruch's ruling that bowing is forbidden even when one did not arise early to go to his fellow's doorway. In my humble opinion, it may be explained as follows. There are two interpretations of the Gemara's statement that it is forbidden to greet someone when one arises early to go to his doorway. According to one interpretation it means an actual greeting of shalom, while according to the other it refers specifically to bowing, as is evident from Beit Yosef. According to the interpretation that it means an actual greeting, it appears that bowing is always forbidden.
[7] There is no concern, etc. Levush explains this as permitting one to go to his fellow's doorway and greet him with shalom. Malbushei Yom Tov writes that Levush was imprecise, because the absence of concern was said only regarding bowing when one did not go to the doorway. Olas Tamid likewise challenges Levush for permitting bowing even when one arose early to go to the doorway. In Eliyah Zuta I wrote that they did not read Levush carefully, for he writes “and greet him with shalom,” implying that bowing remains forbidden. His permission to greet with shalom even when one arose early to go to the doorway rests on the interpretation that the Gemara's prohibition concerns bowing. According to that interpretation, an actual greeting with shalom is permitted even when one arose early. This also resolves the question raised by Nachalas Tzvi and Olas Tamid on Berachos 14a; see there. There is no need to elaborate. All this is unlike Taz. For the reason I mentioned, it appears that after reciting the blessings one may also bow if he merely encounters the person, and Bach rules likewise. Piskei Tosafos writes that in practice one should be stringent and prohibit all these acts also once the time for the evening Krias Shema or prayer has arrived, but before Musaf or Minchah they are permitted.
