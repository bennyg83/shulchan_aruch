And if. See Taz and Bach, who note that the Shulchan Aruch appears to contradict itself. Here it states that after reciting some of the blessings one may proceed, implying that one may even greet a person at his doorway with shalom. Levush writes this explicitly. If so, the Gemara's statement in Berachos 14a that one may not greet another with shalom before praying must mean that reciting the blessings is sufficient. Certainly, then, regarding attending to one's affairs, the ruling there should likewise be that the blessings suffice. Terumas HaDeshen, responsum 18, proves that “before he prays” means before the Amidah; otherwise, what is the Gemara's question from greeting between the sections of Krias Shema? If so, since in seif 2 the Mechaber did not accept this difficulty, why in seif 3 does he specify the Amidah? The answer is that bowing alone, without greeting with shalom, is permitted after one has recited the blessings, even at the person's doorway. Certainly, a greeting with shalom alone, which is more lenient than bowing, is permitted. It is difficult to say that after the blessings, shalom is more stringent than bowing alone, as Levush writes. A greeting with shalom accompanied by bowing, however, as was customary in their practice, remains forbidden even after the blessings until one has prayed the Amidah. The same applies to attending to one's affairs, which is comparable to greeting with shalom and bowing. Levush's words are therefore well explained. Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 7, appears to explain that according to these authorities, the primary meaning of “shalom” is bowing alone. On the road this is forbidden before the blessings, and at the person's doorway bowing alone is forbidden even after the blessings. A greeting with shalom alone, however, is permitted after the blessings even at the doorway, because the Gemara's primary reference to shalom means bowing. According to this, the first opinion, which forbids a greeting with shalom alone when one arises early to go to the doorway, would forbid it even after the blessings, as may be inferred from the law between the sections of Krias Shema. The first opinion should therefore have been presented as the stringent view and the second as introducing a leniency. From Nachalas Tzvi, however, it appears that the permission after the blessings applies only on the road and refers only to bowing alone, also only on the road. At the person's doorway it remains forbidden after the blessings, and even a greeting with shalom alone is forbidden there after the blessings. According to this reading, the second opinion introduces no leniency either. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 5. The second opinion, which forbids a greeting with shalom on the road, is seemingly only a custom enacted so that one will direct his attention to prayer, rather than a strict law. See Peri Chadash and Levush. It may, however, be a strict law. These authorities maintain that greeting on the road is permitted after one has recited the blessings, for the passage concerning greeting between the sections is discussing such a case. The Gemara does not answer that greeting is forbidden only before the blessings because the phrase “before he prays” specifically means before the Amidah, as Terumas HaDeshen writes and as the wording “until he prays” implies. It therefore answers that the prohibition concerns going to the person's doorway. See Darkei Moshe in the second edition, and review the matter here.
If. If one arises early to go to his fellow's doorway and greets him with shalom in another language, the matter requires consideration. See siman 84. It is reasonable that descriptive Divine titles, such as saying, “May the Merciful One have mercy upon you,” or “May the Gracious One show you favor,” and the like, are permitted here even according to the view that they may not be uttered in a bathhouse, as discussed in siman 84, because here he is blessing him with one of Hashem's names. Perhaps the same applies if he says, “May the One whose name is Shalom grant you peace.” This too requires study.
