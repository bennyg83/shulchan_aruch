And if he prayed from dawn, etc. Dawn is the point at which light begins to shine on the eastern horizon, approximately one hour and one fifth of an hour before sunrise, as Rambam writes in the first chapter of Hilchos Berachos. It is not, as some mistakenly think, the morning star, which appears much earlier, as stated in the Yerushalmi, first chapter of Berachos. The Mechaber's wording implies that before the eastern horizon becomes illuminated, even if dawn has risen, one has not fulfilled his obligation even after the fact. This also appears from Rabbeinu Yerucham. Magen Avraham and Peri Chadash maintain that once dawn has risen one has fulfilled his obligation after the fact, because it is already day. Peri Megadim questions this. Although regarding the underlying law he appears to agree with them that, on a Torah level, once dawn has risen it is considered day for every matter even before the eastern horizon becomes illuminated, and that for this reason in pressing circumstances one may ideally pray immediately from dawn, nevertheless, outside pressing circumstances he inclines to prove from Rambam that regarding prayer one has not fulfilled his obligation even after the fact merely from dawn. More than this, Eliyah Rabbah, Mateh Yehudah, and the Gra in Shenos Eliyahu all agree that until the eastern horizon becomes illuminated one has not fulfilled his obligation even after the fact, because it is still included in night until the east becomes illuminated. Whenever the poskim write that something may be done from dawn, they mean when the eastern horizon has become illuminated. Likewise, the Mishnah in Megillah 20a, which states that all acts performed from dawn are valid, means after the eastern horizon has become illuminated. I later found that Magen Gibborim also inclines to rule this way, in accordance with Eliyah Rabbah. It is surprising that he did not mention that the Gra also ruled this way and brought proof from the Yerushalmi; see Shenos Eliyahu. This has practical consequences for many Torah laws. Accordingly, the ruling below in seif 8 that in pressing circumstances one may ideally pray from dawn also means when the eastern horizon has become illuminated. The Mechaber relied upon what he wrote at the beginning of this siman, that it must specifically have become illuminated, for an ideal ruling in pressing circumstances cannot be more lenient than an after-the-fact ruling outside pressing circumstances. This also resolves Peri Megadim's question from siman 58, seifim 3 and 4. Likewise, the rulings above in siman 58, seifim 3 and 4, regarding Krias Shema apply only after the eastern horizon has become illuminated. See Biur Halachah there, where I brought proof from Rashba that he too agrees with Eliyah Rabbah, the Gra, and Mateh Yehudah. In practice, one must certainly take account of all these great authorities regarding every mitzvah whose time is during the day. If one performed it before the eastern horizon became illuminated, he must perform it again after illumination. Regarding prayer, however, there may be room to distinguish. Outside pressing circumstances, it appears that one should be stringent even after the fact, in accordance with Peri Megadim. This also appears from Derech HaChaim, who records as practical law the view of Bach and Eliyah Rabbah that it is insufficient for dawn merely to flash like a point; the entire eastern horizon must become illuminated. In pressing circumstances, however, if one prayed before the eastern horizon became illuminated, perhaps after the fact one may rely on Magen Avraham, Peri Chadash, and Peri Megadim and not pray again because of the concern of a blessing in vain. This requires study. Ideally, one must certainly be exceedingly careful about this, even in pressing circumstances. All this concerns Krias Shema and prayer. When one must also put on tefillin, he must wait a little longer, until he can recognize his fellow at a distance of four cubits. If he puts them on earlier, he should do so without a blessing; when the proper time arrives, he should touch them and recite the blessing, as stated above in siman 30, seif 3.
He has fulfilled his obligation. This means after the fact, as I wrote in Mishnah Berurah, and many later authorities rule likewise. Peri Chadash disputes the Shulchan Aruch and maintains that once the eastern horizon becomes illuminated, this is the ideal time for prayer; only the exceptionally scrupulous pray before sunrise. However, according to what we wrote above, that before the eastern horizon becomes illuminated one has not fulfilled his obligation even after the fact, it follows from all the poskim that even after illumination one should not ideally pray, because this time is still included in dawn. In practice, none of this makes a difference for us, because Peri Chadash's statement concerns only the essential mitzvah of prayer. Since every Jew is careful ideally to join redemption to prayer, how could one ideally pray immediately upon daybreak? He must first recite Krias Shema and its blessings, whose time does not begin until he can recognize his fellow at a distance of four cubits, as stated above in siman 58. After that time, however, perhaps one should not protest against those who follow the lenient practice. See Bach and Derishah regarding Rabbeinu Yerucham's view, and see Biur Halachah above in siman 58. Nevertheless, for the most preferred fulfillment, all agree that one should not pray the Amidah before sunrise.
And after noon, etc. — and see in Mishna Berurah regarding the half hour after noon. And after this half hour, for everyone he should no longer pray the Shacharit prayer, even if what he did not pray until now was under duress — and all the more so if intentional; and one who prays his blessings in vain according to several poskim — rather he should pray first Mincha and afterward [the Amidah of] eighteen for the sake of Shacharit to complete it; and this is when the nullification of the prayer was initially under duress and not intentional, as will be explained below in siman 108.
