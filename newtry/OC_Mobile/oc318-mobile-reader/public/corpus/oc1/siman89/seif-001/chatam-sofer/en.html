In Magen Avraham, marginal reference 20. Gloss: Here is the general rule for this calculation. If you calculate twelve hours from dawn until the emergence of the stars, with forty mil during that period according to everyone, each mil is eighteen minutes. Rambam, however, calculates twelve hours from sunrise until sunset alone. If he held that there are five mil from dawn until sunrise and likewise from sunset until the emergence of the stars, only thirty mil would remain from sunrise until sunset. Distributed over twelve hours, each mil would be twenty-four minutes. This is what Tosafos Yom Tov writes in his name in the third chapter of Pesachim. The truth, however, is that Rambam follows the opinion that there are four mil from dawn until sunrise and likewise from sunset until the emergence of the stars. Thirty-two mil therefore remain from sunrise until sunset; distributed over twelve hours, each mil is twenty-two and a half minutes. This is the primary calculation, and Chok Yaakov rules likewise in siman 459. Rambam writes regarding prayer that the period from dawn until sunrise is one and one-fifth hours. This may be explained according to Tosafos in Pesachim, page 94a, s.v. Rabbi Yehudah. Although the period from the beginning of sunset is four mil, the final three quarters of a mil, after the sun has already entered the thickness of the firmament, is called bein hashmashot. See also the language of Tosafos in the chapter Bameh Madlikin. If so, the same applies at dawn: the first three quarters of a mil from the beginning of the sun's emergence are entirely unsuitable for prayer. Three quarters of a mil is close to eighteen minutes. When this is subtracted from four mil, approximately one and one-fifth hours remain. Rambam, in the third chapter of the laws of chametz, may also hold as follows. The Talmud states that Reish Lakish gave the Amoraim before him the measure of leavening as the distance from Migdal Nunaya to Tiberias, which is a mil. See Ran concerning Migdal Nunaya and Tiberias; this distance can be measured. If you would think that this measure is the shorter mil of twenty-two and a half minutes, then Ulla and Rava in Pesachim, page 94, who hold that a mil is twenty-four minutes, would disagree with Reish Lakish. In such a case the Talmud should have stated that they disagree with Reish Lakish. Rather, the distance from Migdal Nunaya to Tiberias is also twenty-four minutes, and Reish Lakish calls it a mil because he taught that one must also set out early and continue until dark, like the Rabbis in the chapter Mi SheHayah Tamei. Although regarding the general measure of a mil we do not rule in accordance with him, they do not disagree regarding the measure of leavening: it is the travel time from Migdal Nunaya, namely twenty-four minutes, or two fifths of an hour. See there carefully and examine this. I have now found, however, an ancient work called Kaftor VaFerach, cited by Tosafos Yom Tov at the beginning of chapter 7 of Shevi'it. See it carefully in Beit Yosef, Orach Chayim, at the end of siman 10, and in that work, chapter 11, page 68b, s.v. “Let us return to Jerusalem,” etc. He writes: “From Jerusalem to Azazel is approximately three hours.” These are his words. That rabbi saw, knew, and measured with great precision. Three hours are 180 minutes, and the distance is ten mil according to Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Yose in Yoma, page 67b. Necessarily, then, a mil is no more than eighteen minutes, contrary to Rambam; empirical observation and the senses cannot be denied. Perhaps one may answer that the Land is called “the land of the deer,” so that its dimensions can contract. The same must then be said regarding what he writes there in siman 72, that the width of Eretz Yisrael amounts to only, etc.; there too, “the land of the deer” is written concerning it.
