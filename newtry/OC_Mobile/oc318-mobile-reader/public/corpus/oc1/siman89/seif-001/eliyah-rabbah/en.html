[1] When the eastern horizon became illuminated, etc. This applies specifically when the entire eastern horizon has become illuminated; it is insufficient for dawn merely to flash at a single point. Bach writes this. Magen Avraham, however, writes that even if the eastern horizon did not become illuminated at all, one has fulfilled his obligation, because according to the strict law the daily offering may be brought from dawn. The requirement that the eastern horizon become illuminated is only a safeguard lest one err, as stated in Yoma, chapter 3, 28b. In my humble opinion, the strict law itself requires the eastern horizon to become illuminated. This also appears from Rambam at the beginning of Hilchos Berachos. They went up to the roof to look because of the possibility of error, and this likewise appears from the Gemara there. Study it carefully. This is also unlike Minchas Kohen, page 3. All the poskim who mention dawn presumably mean that the eastern horizon has become illuminated, because that is what dawn signifies. Furthermore, when a safeguard is enacted lest one err, even after the fact the act is invalid, as explained in siman 233. Although Magen Avraham writes that here error is possible only when moonlight rises, this is incorrect, for he overlooked Bartenura there, who writes that even on Yom Kippur, when moonlight is not a concern, we fear that another similar error may occur. Rambam, in his commentary to the Mishnah, writes that an error in fact occurred on Yom Kippur. The Gemara there on page 29 requires study.
[2] He has fulfilled his obligation, etc. This implies that after the fact it is permitted even outside pressing circumstances, while in pressing circumstances it is permitted even ideally, as stated below at the end of this siman. Maadanei Yom Tov, Nachalas Tzvi, and Lechem Chamudos at the beginning of the chapter Tefillas HaShachar rule likewise. Maharshal writes that during the days of Selichos one should be careful, because people commonly begin before sunrise, and this is improper. Magen Avraham writes regarding this that perhaps they relied on the fact that it would burden the congregation to go home and then assemble again, similar to what Tur writes in siman 235 regarding Maariv. Nevertheless, ideally one should see to it not to arise so early. It appears to me that the cases are not comparable. For such a short interval they can wait in the synagogue and need not go home, unlike the case of Maariv.
[3] After midday, etc. Bach concludes that the time for Shacharis extends until Minchah Gedolah, which is one half hour after midday. From the time of Minchah onward, one prays Minchah twice. Magen Avraham disputes this, because in truth the time for Minchah begins immediately after midday, as stated in siman 233. Indeed, this is implied in Yoma 28b, and Rashi explains this way in Parashas Bo. The reason we rule that the time for Minchah begins one half hour after midday must be that the Sages were concerned that people might err. Sanhedrin, chapter Hayu Bodkin, requires study. I have seen, however, that Derishah and Taz also rule like Bach. This also appears to be the view of Kesef Mishneh in chapter 3 of Hilchos Tefillah, Raavan in siman 167, Tzeidah LaDerech, and Rekanti. They apparently maintain that since it is forbidden to pray Minchah until one half hour after midday, as Rashi clearly implies on page 26, that interval is treated as though it is not yet the time for Minchah, and one may pray Shacharis. If one intentionally allowed the proper time to pass, however, it appears to me that one should rule like the Shulchan Aruch and Levush. According to the flow of the Gemara, it appears that after one third of the day, an intentional delay renders the prayer forbidden and its blessings are in vain. Raavan likewise writes this. It is therefore sufficient for us to be lenient until midday and no further. The Shulchan Aruch's wording, “and he prayed,” appears to mean that one may likewise initially pray after four hours if he erred or negligently allowed the time to pass, and certainly if he was an onen. See siman 71.
