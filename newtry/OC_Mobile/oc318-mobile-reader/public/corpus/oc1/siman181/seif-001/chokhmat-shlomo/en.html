Seif 1. Mayim acharonim are obligatory. Note: See Magen Avraham, seif katan 1, who cites Beit Yosef in siman 158: “The first waters are a mitzvah, and the final waters are obligatory.” What practical difference follows from this? See what he writes there. In my opinion, the distinction may be explained based on Rambam, Hilchos Shabbos, chapter 5, regarding lighting a Shabbos lamp. Rambam writes that it is obligatory, meaning that it is not optional, such that one may choose whether to light, nor is it merely a mitzvah that one is not obligated to pursue in order to perform. Rather, it is an obligation that one must actively pursue to fulfill. His words are cited by Tosafos Yom Tov in the second chapter of Shabbos. It follows that the term “mitzvah” indicates that although one is indeed commanded to perform it, as Rambam already excluded the possibility that it is optional, nevertheless one might still say that a person is not obligated to pursue it. By contrast, the term “obligation” means that one must pursue it in order to fulfill it. The same applies here. We rule above, in siman 163, that if water is unavailable within one mil behind him or four mil ahead of him, he may eat without washing his hands. This proves that he is not obligated to pursue water beyond those distances, because washing before bread is only a mitzvah. Mayim acharonim, however, are obligatory, and one must pursue them. Therefore, if he is able to obtain water even more than one mil behind him or four mil ahead of him, he must return or proceed in order to obtain it. This is why the first washing is called a mitzvah, for one need not pursue it beyond the prescribed distance, while mayim acharonim are called obligatory, because one must pursue them. This is clear. See also Magen Avraham, beginning “The Tur wrote,” where he cites Rabbeinu Yerucham and states that the Tur’s teachings are not arranged in a fixed order. Note: See siman 270, in the laws of Shabbos, where I cited Bach, who holds that the Tur’s teachings are arranged in a fixed order. This requires study, for these statements appear contradictory.
