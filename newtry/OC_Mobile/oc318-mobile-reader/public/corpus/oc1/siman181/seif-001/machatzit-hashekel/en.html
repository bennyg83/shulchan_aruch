Magen Avraham writes: Beit Yosef, etc. The implication is that he takes mayim rishonim, etc. First, Orchos Chaim explains that for this reason one must take mayim acharonim in its case and forgo the later meal: the obligation of mayim acharonim has already fallen upon him, while the obligation of the third meal has not yet arisen, so he must fulfill the obligation presently incumbent upon him. Accordingly, in Magen Avraham’s case, where the obligation of mayim rishonim has now arisen but that of mayim acharonim has not, he should take mayim rishonim. Furthermore, otherwise Orchos Chaim could have stated the practical difference simply: one wishes to eat and has enough water to wash only once, so he must forgo mayim rishonim because of mayim acharonim and eat without the first washing. This requires study. If so, what is the greater stringency, etc.? Each time, he should perform the obligation presently incumbent upon him and defer the other. If mayim rishonim is incumbent now, it defers mayim acharonim; if mayim acharonim is incumbent now, it defers mayim rishonim. One must therefore say that it is forbidden, etc. Accordingly, there is indeed a greater stringency in mayim acharonim than in mayim rishonim: mayim rishonim is sometimes deferred because of mayim acharonim, as in the case of Orchos Chaim concerning the third meal, whereas we never find that mayim acharonim is deferred because of mayim rishonim. See siman 163, that if there is no water within four mil ahead or one mil behind, one may eat without washing by wrapping his hands in a cloth.
The obligation is not so strong, for it is only because of filth and is not as stringent as mayim rishonim, which is because of the concern of terumah, as above at the beginning of siman 158. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that they pour the cup, etc. See siman 166. The Mishnah at the beginning of Eilu Devarim states: Beis Shammai says one washes the hands and then pours the cup, while Beis Hillel says one pours the cup and then washes. It discusses mayim rishonim. One version of the Baraisa explains Beis Hillel’s reason as the requirement that the blessing immediately follow washing; if one washed first, pouring would constitute an interruption. Nevertheless, the poskim disagree in siman 166 whether one must avoid interruption between washing and Hamotzi, because some hold that we do not follow that version of the Baraisa, but the alternative version, which gives a different reason for Beis Hillel. The general statement that the blessing must immediately follow washing refers to mayim acharonim. Thus, regarding mayim acharonim all agree that the blessing must immediately follow washing, without interruption. At least the Baraisa proves that pouring the cup is considered an interruption. Therefore, for mayim acharonim all agree that one must pour first and then wash. However, from the order of the Tur, etc., meaning that he first arranged the laws of mayim acharonim in this siman and only in siman 181 arranged the law of the cup, it appears that washing should precede pouring. One must say that he agrees with what Magen Avraham writes at the beginning of siman 166: specifically mixing wine with hot water, which requires concentration not to add too much or too little, is considered an interruption, but ordinary pouring is not.
Nevertheless, the Gemara appears to support Rabbeinu Yerucham, as explained above, for it implies that every pouring constitutes an interruption.
Seif katan 1: But, etc. Yam Shel Shlomo permits it and explains that mayim acharonim comes to remove filth. Therefore, when one washes inside the vessel, although the water removes the filth from his hands, the water containing the filth then adheres again to his hands. This differs from the intermediate washing, namely between cheese and eating meat, which comes only to remove food adhering to his hands. Washing inside a vessel helps there, because in any event the food leaves his hands and does not adhere again.
