This requires further study — whether if one prayed in his heart he has fulfilled his obligation. At first glance it appears he has, even though we rule that thought is not equivalent to speech, as written in siman 62 and siman 185 — prayer is different, since we derive it from the verse “and to serve Him with all your heart” — what service is in the heart? This is prayer. If so, the essential requirement depends on the intention of the heart, and the Holy One, blessed be He, knows one’s thoughts. Yet the Gemara implies he has not fulfilled his obligation, for otherwise the Sages should have enacted that one who had a seminal emission may meditate on the prayer in his heart, since he is permitted to meditate — given that we rule thought is not equivalent to speech. Rather, one must conclude that meditating alone does not fulfill the obligation, for the lips must articulate the words, since the voice arouses [the Divine attention] above, as is known. And what is written in siman 94 that a sick person may meditate in his heart — that was said as an additional accommodation, but he does not thereby fulfill the obligation of prayer. And so is implied in the poskim, since in the Gemara on daf 15b it is stated that if one recited Birkas HaMazon in his heart he has fulfilled his obligation, and the same applies to other blessings. And the Rosh wrote in the chapter of what is [permitted on] Shabbos that this refers to one who articulates with his lips, since thought is not equivalent to speech — and he likewise ruled in siman 185. If so, he holds this is a general principle encompassing all matters — for otherwise one could distinguish: granted, Krias Shema has “v’shinantam” written about it, which is not the case with Birkas HaMazon, which has “u’verachta” — and even when one meditates he is also blessing the Holy One, blessed be He, since He knows one’s thoughts. Rather, one must conclude there is no distinction, and so the Gemara implies. This requires further study.

In accordance with the Beit Yosef, in Birkas HaMazon, the Zohar agrees with the Tosefta that one should not make [the prayer] audible to his ears. But there is no proof from there, as one can say the Zohar’s intent is that another person should not hear his voice — as the Rama wrote in siman 141. Nevertheless, it is obvious that if one did not make it audible to his ears, he has fulfilled his obligation. The Bach wrote that even cantors should not raise their voices beyond what is necessary for the congregation to hear, but his words are not conclusive. The Ari did not make his voice heard even during Pesukei D’zimra, except on Shabbos when he raised his voice slightly [Kavanot].
