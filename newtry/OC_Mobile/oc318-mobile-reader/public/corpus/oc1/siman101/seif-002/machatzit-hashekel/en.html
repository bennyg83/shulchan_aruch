Seif katan 2, "And he shall not," etc. The text should read siman 62 and siman 185. This refers to Bircas HaMazon, for one who recited Bircas HaMazon only in his heart did not fulfill his obligation.
Otherwise, they should have ordained, etc. We rule that while Ezra's ordinance remained in force, a baal keri was forbidden to pray. Nevertheless, when the time for Krias Shema arrived, the Gemara on page 20 states that he would think it in his heart, since Krias Shema is D'Oraisa. Although thought is not equivalent to speech and does not fulfill the obligation of Krias Shema, Chazal nevertheless instructed him to think it so that the entire world would not be occupied with accepting the yoke of Heaven while he sat idle. Regarding prayer, however, they did not permit even thought. According to Magen Avraham, the opposite should be true: although prayer is Rabbinic, Chazal should have obligated him in it even more than in Krias Shema, because if thought is not equivalent to speech, thinking would involve no prohibition, while still fulfilling his obligation of prayer. The passage concerns one who articulates the words with his lips; the expression "in his heart" is not precise.
For he stated a general rule. Meaning that the requirement stated regarding Krias Shema, that one articulate it with his lips, does not apply only to Krias Shema but to all matters. Even regarding prayer, one might have argued that because Scripture states "to serve Him with all your heart," thought should suffice, as stated above. If this is not a general rule, perhaps thought would also suffice for Bircas HaMazon for another reason, as Magen Avraham writes, since Scripture says "you shall bless," and thinking may also be called blessing Hashem because He knows thoughts. The Rosh must therefore hold that the requirement of speech stated regarding Krias Shema is a general rule applying equally to Bircas HaMazon and prayer. Magen Avraham's statement that the reason regarding Krias Shema is the verse "you shall teach them" is difficult, for the Gemara derives it from "Hear, Israel," meaning: make your ears hear what you articulate with your mouth.
