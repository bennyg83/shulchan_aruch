[3] Making it audible to his ears, etc. Bach rules that after the fact one has fulfilled his obligation even if he did not make it audible to his ears. This follows Beit Yosef's conclusion that the halachah requires one ideally to make it audible to his ears. I saw, however, in Beit Yosef's own Bedek HaBayis that he writes: "Nevertheless, the Zohar cited in siman 141 agrees with the Tosefta that he should not make it audible to his ears." Rama writes in siman 141 that there is no concern even if one makes it audible to his ears, because reading along with the Torah reader is no stricter than prayer, as above in siman 101. Beit Yosef's words imply that initially one should not make it audible to his ears, and Nachalas Tzvi and Levush there explain it this way. It also appears that Rama did not see Bedek HaBayis, in which Beit Yosef retracted his ruling regarding prayer as well. Perishah, who questions the apparent contradiction in Shulchan Aruch, likewise overlooked it. As a matter of practical law, however, there is no proof, because Perishah and Olas Tamid explain the Zohar as meaning only that others should not hear him, and this requires study. Magen Avraham writes that it requires study whether one who prayed only in his heart fulfilled his obligation. Although regarding Krias Shema we rule that thought is not equivalent to speech, prayer may differ because it is service of the heart. Yet the Gemara implies that he did not fulfill his obligation, for otherwise Chazal should have ordained that a baal keri mentally recite the prayer, since thought is permitted because it is not equivalent to speech. It follows that thought does not suffice. He further argues that the poskim rule that one who recited Bircas HaMazon only in his heart did not fulfill his obligation. They must therefore regard this as a general rule, for otherwise one could distinguish Krias Shema, regarding which Scripture states "you shall teach them," from Bircas HaMazon. This requires study. It is surprising, because their proof from Bircas HaMazon may be that if thought sufficed, why was a baal keri instructed merely to think the words rather than articulate them? The reason thought is not equivalent to speech regarding Krias Shema is the verse "you shall teach them." It also appears to me that the Mishnah in the chapter Elu NeEmarin proves this, for it teaches that Bircas HaMazon may be recited in any language. If thought sufficed, the concept of language would not apply, as explained in siman 62. Prayer cannot be proved from this even though the Mishnah likewise teaches that prayer may be recited in any language, because that statement may teach that one may request his needs even in Aramaic. Magen Avraham's argument that Chazal should have ordained mental prayer for a baal keri also appears questionable. On the contrary, if thought fulfills the obligation, his thought would be like articulated speech and would be forbidden to a baal keri. This, however, makes the Gemara's question from the prohibition against thinking prayer to Krias Shema difficult. Examine this carefully. Perhaps because prayer is Rabbinic, Chazal exempted a baal keri entirely. Rambam at the beginning of chapter 5 of Hilchos Tefillah also implies that thought fulfills the obligation, although according to him one likewise fulfills Bircas HaMazon through thought, as explained below in siman 185. This requires study.
