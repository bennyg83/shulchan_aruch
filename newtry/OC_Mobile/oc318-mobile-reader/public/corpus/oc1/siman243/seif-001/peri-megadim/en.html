In my humble opinion, this requires study. One who examines the Ran at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah will see that where a contractor is engaged for the entire year to harvest one's field, even though the Jew does not benefit specifically from the work performed on Shabbos, it nevertheless appears to ordinary people as though he is a daily laborer. Even if they know that he is a contractor, they do not distinguish between the cases when the non-Jew does not receive a share of the profits, and they may consequently come to permit an actual day laborer. Although this resembles a decree upon a decree, since the Sages supported it with the verse, “No work shall be done through them,” it is treated like a Torah prohibition for this purpose, as we will explain, with Hashem's help, in letter 3. For this reason, the case in Moed Katan 12a involving contracted work outside the techum is permitted where the public does not see it. A Torah scholar knows that a contractor is permitted because he labors for his own benefit. The Maggid Mishneh there and Tosafos, however, imply that a contractor does not appear to people like a daily laborer. The only concern is that they may suspect that he actually is a daily laborer, as Magen Avraham writes in siman 244, seif katan 5. A practical difference arises where the arrangement is publicly known and the local custom is to hire workers only through annual contracting, as the Tur writes. See letter 2, where this will be explained, with Hashem's help. According to the Ran, it is forbidden for him to harvest the Jew's field; according to the Maggid Mishneh and Tosafos, it is permitted. A furnace or mill is different, for contracting there is prohibited by the law itself because the Jew profits from the work. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 2. See also siman 243, Magen Avraham, seif katan 3, where this will be explained, with Hashem's help.
