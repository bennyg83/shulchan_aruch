1) [Seif 1] The time for the Mussaf prayer, etc. In Sephardic communities the custom was to say LeMoshe Tzivisa, while in Ashkenazic communities the custom is to say Tikanta Shabbos, arranged according to the reverse alphabet from tav to alef. This alludes to redemption, as the verse states, 'I will whistle to them and gather them.' From Tikanta through meSinai it contains twenty-one words, corresponding to the passages VeVayom HaShabbos and Olas Shabbos, which contain twenty-one words. Tur. Shaar HaKavanos, folio 73d, likewise writes that one must follow the Ashkenazic custom and recite Tikanta Shabbos, etc. At the end, the five final letters are added in the words meSinai nitztavu tzivui pe'aleha kara'ui, followed by, 'May it be Your will, Hashem my God and God of my forefathers, that You bring us up,' etc. These embody the mystical illumination of the twenty-two letters in the supernal mind, from below upward, according to the mystical meaning of the statement in Idra Naso: 'The letter tav inscribes a mark for the Ancient One that has no equal,' as explained by us in its place. These are his words. Sephardic communities likewise now recite Tikanta, because the Arizal instructed this and gave an esoteric reason. Although Pri Etz Chaim, gate 18, chapter 20, writes that one should say Tikanta with the letter kuf, we possess only what Shaar HaKavanos records, arranged by Rabbi Shmuel Vital in the name of his father, Rabbi Chaim Vital. There it is written Tikanta with the letter kaf, as stated above, and this is the common practice.
2) Shibbolei HaLeket writes: Tikanta is an expression of arrangement, like 'Who arranges hearts.' Ratzisa Korbanoseha is explained by some as follows: every offering is accompanied by the words, 'and a goat for atonement,' whereas no goat is mentioned with the Shabbos offering. The prayer therefore says, 'You desired its offerings,' because they are offerings that require no atonement. Others explain Ratzisa Korbanoseha to mean that an offering does not become acceptable until a Shabbos has passed over it, as the verse states, 'From the eighth day onward it shall be accepted,' and seven days cannot pass without a Shabbos. Tzivisa Peirusheha Im Sidurei Nesacheha is explained by some as referring to the verse, 'Command the Children of Israel,' where the Shabbos offering is specified: You commanded its explicitly stated offerings together with the arrangements of the lechem hapanim. The Aramaic translation of sheish hamaareches, 'six in each arrangement,' is shis sidrin. Nesacheha is related to the term kesavos hanasech, 'the tubes through which it shall be poured.' Me'angeha LeOlam Kavod Yinchalu refers to the aggadic teaching in the Pesikta of Basi LeGani: Through the merit of three matters, the Jewish people delight before the Holy One, blessed is He, through the merit of Shabbos, through the merit of tithes, and through the merit of Shabbos, as it is written, 'Then you shall delight in Hashem.' To'ameha Chaim Zachu refers to those who honor it with foods and delicacies. Some have support for tasting their cooked food on Friday. These are his words. Beis Yosef. In Shacharis we say lo nesato velo hinchalto in the masculine because it refers to the preceding words, 'and the seventh day,' and yom is masculine. In Mussaf, however, the prayer refers to Tikanta Shabbos, and Shabbos is feminine, so we say korbanoseha and peirusheha. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2.
3) There: Immediately after the Shacharis prayer. Its time begins in the morning because it corresponds to the Mussaf offering, whose time began immediately in the morning. Since its time is in the morning, it is proper to pray it immediately after Shacharis. Tur.
4) One who feels weak and wishes to taste something on Shabbos morning to strengthen his heart should preferably pray Shacharis and Mussaf consecutively at home with a minyan of ten, recite Kiddush, and eat his meal. He should afterward go to the synagogue to hear the Torah reading and haftarah. There is no objection to his praying Mussaf before the Torah reading, as Chikekei Lev, volume 1, siman 5, writes; see there. Whenever he can adjoin Shacharis and Mussaf and recite them consecutively, it is proper, as is evident from the present ruling that Mussaf should be prayed immediately after Shacharis. Yafeh LaLev, paragraph 1. See, however, Shaar HaKavanos, folio 73c and d and folio 74b, which gives an esoteric reason that the Torah reading must occur after the repetition of Shacharis and before the silent Mussaf; see there. Therefore, absent great necessity, one should not postpone the Torah reading until after Mussaf.
5) If a person is about to pray Mussaf with the congregation and is called to perform a bris milah, but having the congregation wait for him would burden them, he should not postpone the communal prayer for the bris unless there is concern that the bris will otherwise be delayed beyond its proper time. Mahari Ayash in Beis Yehudah, Orach Chaim, siman 48; Rav Yaakov Castro in Hagahos HaTur, paragraph 3; Birkei Yosef, paragraph 8; Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 5. Shaarei Teshuvah writes there that when the bris is performed in the synagogue and many people are waiting until afterward, the mohel should not pray Mussaf with the congregation and make them wait for him to finish, because of the burden upon the congregation. It is not respectful to have them sit and wait for him until he completes the communal prayer.
