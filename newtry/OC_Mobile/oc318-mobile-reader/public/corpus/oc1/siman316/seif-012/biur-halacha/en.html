To catch them. This does not literally mean catching them, for even if they did not rebel it is forbidden to handle them because they are muktzeh, as above in siman 308. Rather, it means trapping them by bringing them into a narrow place where they are considered trapped and ready to be taken. Alternatively, "to catch them" means to lead them by their necks and sides without lifting them from the ground, which we permitted above in siman 308:39-40, as Ma'amar Mordekhai explains.
And some say, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote that this refers to a large house that has the measure of trapping. The reason is that this opinion's proof is from the Mishnah that one who traps a domesticated wild animal or bird in his possession is exempt, and that exemption is like other Shabbat exemptions, namely forbidden, as stated in Hagahot Maimoniyot and as the Gra writes. The Mishnah must speak of a large enclosure, for otherwise even if it were not in his possession he would be exempt. This is also evident from the Rama, Hagahot Maimoniyot, Sefer Yereim, and Peri Megadim, who writes that even according to the Rama's stringency it is in a large courtyard. The words of the Olat Shabbat and Taz are imprecise. See Magen Avraham s.k. 26: at first he wanted to explain Hagahot Maimoniyot as forbidding on Shabbat even in a small enclosure, and he asked that its words contradict each other; but according to his conclusion, Hagahot Maimoniyot is not speaking of a small enclosure at all, even initially, as Levushei Serad explains. The Rama intends to be stringent even regarding geese and chickens, as is evident from Darkhei Moshe. Regarding the main law, whether the halakhah follows the Mechaber or Rama, we wrote in Mishnah Berurah that the later authorities rule like this "some say." In truth it is proper to rule this way, for it is evident from many Rishonim: the Rambam in Hilkhot Shabbat chapter 10 writes explicitly that for geese and chickens one is exempt, and his rule at the beginning of Hilkhot Shabbat is known, that "exempt" means forbidden; likewise the Ramban in Shabbat, even according to his later answer, holds that at least on Shabbat it is forbidden; so too the Raavan cited in Shiltei HaGibborim and the Agudah in HaOreg, cited by the Magen Avraham. The Rokeach, Rabbenu Yerucham, Yereim, and Hagahot Maimoniyot also incline to stringency, though in those four sources geese and chickens are not explicit and one could say they discuss wild animals and birds raised in one's home and made tame, on which the Rabbis decreed that trapping applies, unlike geese and chickens, which are domestic and return to their coop at night, like a cow or horse, where trapping does not apply at all. The Meiri cites such a view in Perek Shemonah Sheratzim, and the Gra inclines this way. The Rosh in chapter Ein Tzadin implies that geese and chickens that return to their coop at night and whose food is upon you are not subject to trapping at all, and he writes that this is also the view of the Baal HaItur. The Rashba in chapter Ein Tzadin also writes that "exempt" here means exempt and permitted. Apart from these, all are stringent as above. Therefore it is correct to be stringent lechatchilah like the majority of poskim, except where the chicken is not prone to slip away from the hand, as I wrote in Mishnah Berurah in the name of the Chayei Adam.
That it is forbidden, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, s.v. "to trap," where I wrote "and according to this..." Even if the birds were inside the cage and the cage opened, one must be careful not to close it, since he intends thereby to trap them so that they not go outside. So too it appears in Zekhor LeAvraham in the name of Machazik Berakhah: opening and closing a chicken coop on Shabbat is forbidden, but one may feed the birds in the coop on Shabbat through a non-Jew, and if the non-Jew closes it himself we have no concern. It appears that this speaks where the house is large or the coop stands in a courtyard; if the house is small, it is permitted to close the coop. Even in a large house one can feed them in a way that does not fully open the coop so that they could escape.
To trap. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote that if his intent is only to prevent damage, the reason for leniency is that it is a melakhah she-einah tzerikhah legufah, and most poskim rule like Rabbi Shimon. This is like the Mechaber's ruling in seif 7, that if he is occupied with it so it should not bite him, it is permitted, because he does not need the essence of trapping, as Rashi explains on Shabbat 107b. Here too that is so, and only a rabbinic prohibition remains. There, it is entirely permitted because in a case of injury the Sages did not decree, whereas here it is merely monetary loss. Moreover, since this is an animal not lacking trapping, the issue is itself only rabbinic; where there are two rabbinic factors, perhaps one may be lenient in a case of loss. This resembles the ruling of Masat Binyamin, siman 8, that if one fears theft and there is loss, one may be lenient to push a chicken into a house, which is trapping in an unusual manner, where trapping itself is only rabbinic, such as when it is accustomed to come home. We may also add the Gra's view, who rules like the lenient opinions regarding geese and chickens in one's possession that return to their coop at night. However, regarding new birds that are not accustomed to return home, one must be careful in every way, because if he puts them into a narrow cage to guard them from leaving, this is a Torah prohibition of trapping according to the later authorities, who write that Torah trapping applies to them. Even if he puts them in only so they not damage, which is melakhah she-einah tzerikhah legufah, there is at least a rabbinic prohibition according to most poskim. See further what we wrote regarding the law of the later authorities, which has practical effect here as well.
A wild animal or bird in one's possession. The law I wrote in Mishnah Berurah regarding buying a new bird was first innovated by the author of Masat Binyamin, followed by all the later authorities, from Beitzah 24. The Gemara explains that geese and chickens are not subject to trapping, even though one says "bring a trap and we will catch it," because they return to their coop at night, meaning they come by themselves to the coop and are easy to catch there, and are therefore considered already trapped, as Rashi explains, even if caught outside. From this he derived that if the bird is not yet accustomed to the house and will not return to its coop at night, trapping applies to it, namely when catching it outside; if inside, it depends whether the house is large or small. At first glance, according to this, if one has no coop in the house for chickens and the house is large so that it cannot be reached in one lunge, then they are not trapped there, and even a bird accustomed to come home would be subject to Torah trapping when caught outside. This is difficult. Rather, the Gemara's intent may be that because it is accustomed by itself to return home in the evening, it is not considered lacking trapping. "To its coop" is the usual case. Every animal or bird in one's possession is included, even without an actual coop. Rashi's phrase that it is easy to catch there is not limited to a coop; the same applies when it comes to a house to which it is accustomed. This explains why the Mishnah says that one who traps a wild animal or bird in his possession is exempt, and does not distinguish whether he has a coop. The Gemara's initial question from geese and chickens was about trapping them outside, as I wrote. Chayei Adam also mentioned only the condition that it returns home in the evening. Know further: if one bought a bird recently and it has not yet been accustomed to leave and return to the house, because they did not let it go outside, and if it leaves it will not know how to return by itself, but inside the house it is accustomed to come to its coop at night, it appears obvious to me that inside the house it has the law of an animal or bird in one's possession and is not subject to Torah trapping; but if it happens to go outside, it has the law of other new birds that are not accustomed to return home.
I also bring here what Chemed Moshe asked on the Masat Binyamin and Magen Avraham regarding a new bird: he argues that it still comes to a coop at night and its food is upon a person; if it does not come to this coop, it will come to another. This is not difficult, for even they agree that if one bought a new bird from someone nearby in the same city, there is no Torah trapping for whoever catches it outside. Although it is not accustomed to its new house, it is accustomed to return to its old coop if released, and is considered already trapped. The Mishnah's law that one who traps an animal or bird in one's possession is exempt certainly does not speak only of the owner; rather, since it is in some person's possession it is considered already trapped for all. They excluded only an ox that rebelled. Thus our case too is considered already trapped if it has a place to which it is accustomed to return. The Masat Binyamin and Magen Avraham spoke only where he bought it in a distant place, so that it will not recognize its former place and return on its own. I wrote this to awaken the reader's heart, though it is still not clear for practice. I find it difficult, according to this law of the later authorities, why the Tosefta in Shabbat chapter 13 distinguished between geese and chickens and dovecote pigeons; it could have distinguished within geese and chickens themselves, between those accustomed to come to him and those newly bought. One can answer that it preferred to teach a greater novelty regarding dovecote pigeons: even though they too return to their coop at night, as stated in Beitzah, one is nevertheless liable.
