Seif 1. A minor who eats nevelot — the court is not commanded to separate him. I will write here several new notes with Hashem's help; see Rashba in his novellae on Yevamos that even for Torah prohibition the court is not commanded to separate him, and it is permitted to give him rabbinic prohibition by hand; from this one should examine Ra'avad's explanation in tractate Demai chapter 2 mishnah 4 and chapter 5 mishnah 1, second explanation, from selling to minors lest minors eat something not tithed — challenging his explanation: for the accepted view that a minor who eats nevelot — the court is not commanded — if so permitted to give rabbinic prohibition by hand, and doubtfully tithed produce is only rabbinic — why make them a remedy; one cannot answer that since its root is d'oraisa they were stringent — behold we see the opposite, that they feed the poor doubtfully tithed produce and guests of doubtfully tithed produce and other rabbinic prohibitions — certainly forbidden to give the poor; if so proven they were lenient even regarding other rabbinic prohibitions; likewise one blesses on every rabbinic mitzvah, and on rabbinic matters one does not bless because most ignoramuses tithe — if so all the more if other rabbinic prohibitions are permitted to give them, doubtfully tithed produce is permitted; strained to say Ra'avad disagrees with Rashba and holds that even if the court is not commanded to separate, it is forbidden to give rabbinic prohibition by hand — strained; Rashba's words are reasonable and necessary there — it requires study. We learn in Yevamos 114a: R' Yitzchak the smith lost his key, etc. — Rashi s.v. "and to carry him" there they jest and it was Shabbat — end. I was asked: what did Rashi innovate — behold the Gemara explicitly states he lost the key on Shabbat. Strained to say the Gemara does not read thus; but it appears Rashi teaches: apparently one could explain it was Yom Tov as Rashi Beitza 17b s.v. "to acquire shevut on Shabbat" — intent is Yom Tov; behold even in the language of the Sages Yom Tov is also called Shabbat; and on Yom Tov carrying was permitted only through "from within"; and in any case some need is required, but without any need forbidden even on Yom Tov; therefore one could explain it was Yom Tov; if so difficult why the Gemara challenges from here on a minor who eats nevelot — one could say R' Yitzchak agrees regarding a minor who eats nevelot that it is not that the court is commanded to separate — we derive from "say" and "you shall warn the adults," etc., and likewise every prohibition; unlike Yom Tov where only a positive commandment as Tosafos Beitza 12a — we do not say revert the prohibition; therefore one could say the court is not commanded to separate; why compare this to that — therefore Rashi explained it must be Shabbat per his view that we say "from within" even without need on Yom Tov; if one cannot explain Yom Tov, necessarily Shabbat — therefore it challenges properly that we learn a minor who eats nevelot — the court is not commanded — as I wrote, correct and fits well. Regarding the law of a minor who eats nevelot see also in my Torah commentary year 5601 Parashat Beshalach what I wondered from the Mekhilta brought in Yalkut Parashat Ki Tisa on "and the children of Israel shall keep the Shabbat" — "perhaps even minors" — "teach those with understanding" — see there and examine; see here in Magen Avraham what he wondered on Rokeach; see in Avnei Miluim volume 2 siman 82 what he wrote; see my responsum in my Yoreh De'ah compositions 45 siman 17 — see there and examine. We learn in Bechoros 35 another incident, etc., and it is necessary: if we taught non-Jew he does not habituate, but minor who habituates — perhaps not; and if we taught minor because he is not confused with an adult, but non-Jew who is confused with adult — perhaps not — necessary; Rashi there s.v. "if we taught non-Jew" — they permitted the first firstborn through him because one cannot say that one will habituate to teach to inflict blemish on sacrifices — a non-Jew's habit is irrelevant, for he is accustomed to prohibitions — end Rashi; Ra'avad likewise; Tosefta wondered there: I wonder at them — blemish on a firstborn is forbidden to him, and the sugya only said he does not habituate; I could explain his habit is nothing because the prohibition is not for him — so Rambam appears to explain; after apology his words are not compelled — Rashi's intent plainly: what we fear everywhere regarding a minor lest he habituate is that he will do so even when adult — therefore it fits for a minor not accustomed to prohibitions that if he does one prohibition we fear he will think there is no prohibition and do when adult; but for a non-Jew if we fear lest he habituate when he converts — perhaps even when he converts he will habituate and do thus; with this Rashi wrote well on a non-Jew no concern lest he habituate even when he converts, for he is accustomed to all prohibitions in his gentile state — if he converts as he will separate from all prohibitions he will separate from this too and not learn permission from what he did in gentile state when he did all prohibitions; therefore no concern lest he habituate; Rambam who explains a non-Jew's habit is nothing — he holds like those who say a minor who eats nevelot — the court is commanded to separate, as Tur seems from his rulings, though his rulings contradict — but in his commentary he certainly holds like those who say a minor who eats nevelot — the court is commanded; therefore since the court must separate from his prohibition, what we fear regarding a minor lest he habituate is that he will still do so in his minority — thus also the court must fix that he not come to this; and for a non-Jew the concern lest he habituate in gentile state and when he converts — we do not fear he will habituate in gentile state; with this Rashi and Ra'avad explained well that in gentile state there is no prohibition — a non-Jew's habit is nothing; but Rashi and Ra'avad hold like those who say a minor who eats nevelot — the court is not commanded — if so to make a remedy lest he habituate in minority otherwise we have no remedy, for the court is not commanded; necessarily what we say lest he habituate is in adulthood; and for a non-Jew too the intent is lest he habituate when he converts; with this his habit is a word and he had to explain no concern of habit since he is accustomed to all prohibitions — fits well. When I was on this I wondered there, in the sugya why the second needs-parsing: if we taught minor because he is not confused with adult — why not remain in the first needs-parsing, since the mishnah explicitly states they saw they permitted him and went and tied other firstborns' tails and forbade — if so one could say if we taught minor, one could say for this they forbade if they did much from concern lest he habituate — that in one time they did not concern lest he habituate, but since they did many times they concerned lest he habituate — therefore forbade; but for a non-Jew where habituation does not apply, one would think even if he does many times it would be permitted; therefore it teaches even for non-Jews if he does many times they forbade — it requires study — examine well. (There) in the gloss: a minor who struck his father, etc. — see Taz and Magen Avraham what proof they brought; I too will answer my share. Proof from explicit mishnah in Kerisot daf 16b: "I said to him: not if you spoke in minority that although they are not in them now, they are in them after time; will you say regarding Shabbat that it is not in it now and not after time" — end. If we explain plainly that if he comes upon them later he is liable — difficult what relevance — because if when he matures he will be liable as in minority, behold in this act he cannot become liable, and between minority and maturity body changed — from maidenhood to maturity we say body changed, all the more between minority and maidenhood; and if the intent is as plain and how could R' Eliezer think R' Eliezer did not know she would be liable in maturity; if it implies nevertheless after the fact we follow — what did R' Akiva innovate and in what view do they dispute; therefore it appears R' Akiva's intent is as Gra and Rama above: in maturity he is liable a sacrifice for what he did in minority — if so proven even in minority it is not complete permission; therefore R' Eliezer holds a minor who sinned is completely exempt in maturity — therefore he learns from minority; R' Akiva holds a minor is liable a sacrifice when he acts in maturity — therefore he rejects "although not in them now, in them after time" — after time comes sacrifice for what he already did — proven not complete permission — fits well:
