Seif 1, if a minor is eating neveilot, the court is not commanded to separate him. I will record here several new notes, with the help of Heaven. See the Rashba in his novellae to Yevamot, who writes that even for a Torah prohibition the court is not commanded to separate him, and one may give him a rabbinic prohibition directly. From this one must examine the explanation of Rav Ovadiah Bartenura in Demai, chapter 2 mishnah 4 and chapter 5 mishnah 1 according to his second explanation, that produce is sold to minors so that the minors should not eat something untithed. This is difficult according to his explanation, for according to the view we rule like, that when a minor eats neveilot the court is not commanded to separate him, it is permitted to give him a rabbinic prohibition directly. Demai is only rabbinic, so why should we be concerned to enact a remedy for them, if it may be given to them directly? One cannot say that since its root is from the Torah they were stringent, for we see the opposite: the poor may be fed demai, soldiers' billeting may be fed demai, and other rabbinic prohibitions certainly may not be given to the poor. Thus it is evident that with demai they were more lenient than with other rabbinic prohibitions. Likewise, one recites a blessing over every rabbinic mitzvah, yet over demai one does not bless because most amei ha-aretz tithe. If then one may give minors other rabbinic prohibitions, all the more may one give them demai. It is forced to say that Rav Ovadiah Bartenura disagrees with the Rashba and holds that even if the court is not commanded to separate him, one may not give him a rabbinic prohibition directly; but this is difficult, and the Rashba's words are reasonable and compelled there. This requires study; examine carefully.
Now, we learn in Yevamot 114a: Rav Yitzchak Nappacha lost a key, etc. Rashi explains, s.v. "and they shall carry it": they should play there, and it was Shabbat, end quote. I was asked by one person what Rashi is adding, since the Gemara explicitly says that a key was lost on Shabbat. It is difficult to say that Rashi did not have that reading in the Gemara. Rather, it appears that Rashi teaches that one might otherwise have explained that it was Yom Tov, as Rashi explains in Beitzah 17b, s.v. "lemiknei shevita beshabbata," that the meaning is Yom Tov. Thus even in rabbinic language Yom Tov is called Shabbat. Although carrying on Yom Tov is otherwise permitted, it is only permitted by the principle of "mitokh," and in any case some need is required; carrying for no need at all remains forbidden even on Yom Tov. Therefore it would have been possible to explain that it was Yom Tov. But then the Gemara's challenge from here to the law of a minor eating neveilot would be difficult, for one could say that Rav Yitzchak agrees regarding a minor eating neveilot, where there is a prohibition and we derive that adults are warned concerning minors from "emor ve-amarta," and the same applies to every prohibition; whereas on Yom Tov, according to Tosafot in Beitzah 12a, it is only a positive command, since we do not say that it returns to the original prohibition, and perhaps the court is not commanded to separate him from a mere positive command. Therefore Rashi explains that it was necessarily Shabbat, for Rashi follows his own view that "mitokh" applies on Yom Tov even where there is no need at all, so it cannot be interpreted as Yom Tov. It was necessarily Shabbat, and the Gemara properly proves that when a minor eats neveilot the court is not commanded to separate him. This appears correct in my humble opinion; examine well. Regarding the law of a minor eating neveilot, see also my work on the Torah from the year 601, parashat Beshalach, where I wondered from the Mekhilta cited in the Yalkut on the verse "And the children of Israel shall keep the Shabbat": might it include even deaf-mutes, fools, and minors? The verse says, "to know," meaning one who has knowledge. See there and understand. See the Magen Avraham here, who wondered about the Rokeach; see Avnei Miluim part 2, siman 82, what he wrote about this, and what I wrote in a responsum in my work on Yoreh De'ah, Mahadura 5, siman 17. Now we learn in Bekhorot 35: once there was another incident, etc., and the Gemara says a tzrichuta: if it had taught us only a non-Jew, one might say that he will not come to continue the practice, but a minor, who may come to continue it, perhaps not; and if it had taught us only a minor, one might say because he will not be confused with an adult, but a non-Jew, who may be confused with an adult, perhaps not. Rashi explains that if it had taught us only a non-Jew, that they permitted the first bekhor on his account, there is no concern that he will come to continue and learn to place a blemish in consecrated animals, for the habitual practice of a non-Jew does not concern us, since he regularly acts with prohibitions. Rav Ovadiah Bartenura explains similarly. The Tosafot Yom Tov wondered about them there, saying that placing a blemish in a bekhor is a prohibition for him too, and the sugya says only that he will not come to continue; he suggests that the meaning is that his habitual practice is nothing, because the prohibition does not apply to him, and that this seems to be the Rambam's interpretation. In my humble opinion, with due respect, his words are not compelled. Rashi's intent is simple: wherever we are concerned with a minor lest he come to continue, the concern is that he will do so even when grown. This is understandable for a minor, who is not accustomed to prohibitions, so if he performs one prohibited act there is concern that he will think it permitted and do so as an adult. But for a non-Jew, if we were concerned that he might continue after conversion, Rashi properly writes that there is no such concern, since while a non-Jew he is habituated in all prohibitions; when he converts, just as he will separate himself from all prohibitions, he will separate from this too, and will not learn permission from what he did as a non-Jew. The Rambam, who explains that the habitual practice of a non-Jew is nothing, appears to hold like the view that if a minor eats neveilot the court is commanded to separate him, as the Tur in this siman suggests from his rulings, though his rulings contradict each other. But in his commentary one can certainly say that he holds like the view that the court is commanded to separate a minor eating neveilot. Since the court must separate him from his prohibition, the concern with a minor lest he continue means that he may continue while still a minor, and the court must also prevent that. Therefore, with a non-Jew too, the meaning is that we are not concerned that he will continue while still a non-Jew, because for him there is no prohibition at all. But Rashi and Rav Ovadiah Bartenura hold like the view that the court is not commanded to separate a minor eating neveilot. If so, we need not enact safeguards lest he continue while a minor, and the concern must be about adulthood. Therefore, with a non-Jew too, the concern would be that he might continue after conversion; and in that respect the habitual practice of a non-Jew is indeed significant, so Rashi had to explain that no such concern applies, since he is habituated in all prohibitions. This works well; examine carefully. While on this sugya, I found it difficult why in the second tzrichuta the Gemara says that if it had taught us only a minor, because he will not be confused with an adult, etc.; why did it not retain the first formulation? Since the Mishnah explicitly states that they saw that the first had been permitted and went and tied the tails of other bekhorot, one could say that if it had taught us only a minor, I would have thought that there they prohibited only when he did so repeatedly because of the concern that he may continue: although after one time they were not concerned, repeated practice creates concern. But with a non-Jew, where continuation is not a concern, perhaps even if he does so many times it remains permitted; therefore it teaches otherwise, that even with non-Jews, if they do this several times, it is prohibited. This requires study; examine carefully.
(There) in the gloss: and a minor who struck his father, or who transgressed other prohibitions in his minority, etc. NB: See the Taz and Magen Avraham, who wrote proof for this, and I too will add my portion. It appears to me that there is proof from an explicit Mishnah in Keritot 16b, where it says: "I said to him: no; if you said this concerning minors, who although they have no liability now, have liability after time, will you say it concerning Shabbat, which has no liability now and no liability after time?" End quote. If this is explained simply, that if one has relations with them later he is liable, it is difficult: what relation does that have to this case? Because if one comes upon her in adulthood he is liable, should that make him liable for coming upon her in childhood? In any event, this act can never lead to liability. Moreover, the transition from minority to adulthood is considered a change of body; in Ketubot chapter 3 they say that between na'arut and bagrut it is considered a bodily change, and all the more from minority to na'arut. Further, if this is the simple meaning, what did Rabbi Eliezer think? Did he not know that she will become liable in adulthood? And if he thought that nevertheless we follow the present state, what did Rabbi Akiva teach him, and in what principle do they disagree? Rather, it appears that Rabbi Akiva's intent accords with the Hagahot Ashri and the Rama cited above: in adulthood he is obligated in atonement for what he did in minority. It is therefore evident that even in minority it is not a complete permission. Rabbi Eliezer holds that a minor who sinned is entirely exempt in adulthood, and therefore he derives from minority; but Rabbi Akiva holds that a minor requires atonement when he becomes an adult. Therefore he rejects the comparison: although they have no liability now, they do have liability after time, meaning that after time atonement comes for what he already did. This proves that it was not fully permitted. This works well; examine carefully.
