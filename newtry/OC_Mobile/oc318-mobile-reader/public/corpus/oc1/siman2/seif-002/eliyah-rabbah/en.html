[2] Put on the right shoe, etc. Tosafos write in Shabbos 61a, and Nachalat Tzvi wondered at Tosafos and had to explain that Rabbi Yochanan would put on the left shoe first — hence with our shoes that have no laces one should put on the left first, see there. I find this difficult, for it is explicit earlier in the Talmud: Rabbi Yochanan said to Rav Shemen “Bring me my shoes”; he gave him the right; [Rabbi Yochanan] said, “You have made it a wound.” If he put on the right first, it would be hard [to object] — perhaps [Rav Shemen] gave him the wrong shoe. My father my teacher resolved: Rabbi Yochanan asked him “Bring me my shoes,” meaning both shoes so as to put on the right and then the left before tying the right; Rav Shemen brought only the right, which [Rabbi Yochanan] considered as putting on and tying the right first — therefore he said “You made it a wound.” The main difficulty, in my humble view, is resolved by saying Rabbi Yochanan who put on the left first did not know the baraita or held the halakhah is not like that mishnah; but as we conclude that one who fears Heaven fulfills both, we say that for donning one always honors the right first, and for tying one honors the left first. Olas Tamid wondered: but Rav Ashi said “I saw Rav Kahana was not strict,” and the halakhah follows Rav Ashi everywhere — until here. I found in a homiletical work in the name of Maharal of Prague: Rav Kahana cannot disagree with the baraita; moreover Rav Ashi did not say “Rav Kahana says” in a way that implies a dispute with Rav Nachman — he only said “I saw Rav Kahana was not strict” to mean he did not trouble himself to be exact when the left came to hand first, because he held Rabbi Yochanan and the baraita spoke for saintliness; in the usual case Rav Kahana too put the right on first — until here. His words are sound; were I not afraid I would say Rav Ashi also agrees with Rav Nachman that one who fears Heaven fulfills both honors — one matter for the right and one for the left — but he was not strict to honor tying specifically on the left; sometimes he put the left on first and tied the right first, and he did not accept Tosafos’ view to honor tying specifically left and donning specifically right. With this explanation all the difficulties of Beit Yosef, Bach, and Taz on the Tur are settled: the Tur omitted the law of our shoes without laces and of removing shoes because he held tying left first is not absolute — rather one honors one matter on the right and one on the left; therefore with our shoes and with removing shoes, when one cannot fulfill both, there is no need to be strict at all — therefore the Tur omitted them — examine this well.
