[1] [Levush] Whether it is an artisan's knot, etc. This is Rosh's view. He explains that the sandal cases are not contradictory: the case involving liability concerns Arab sandals whose knots are permanent, while the case that is exempt but prohibited concerns straps they themselves tie. Olat Shabbat asks why the second case must involve a non-artisan knot; since it is not permanent that alone should suffice. He answers that an artisan's knot creates liability even when not permanent. This is incorrect, because then why must the first case be established as Arab sandals? Its permanence would be unnecessary if artisan workmanship alone created liability. Rather, there was never a difficulty. Establishing the first case as an artisan's work means that such knots are ordinarily permanent, and establishing the second as tied by the wearers means they are ordinarily temporary. But if it is actually temporary, even an artisan's knot is permitted according to Rosh. Rashi, Tur, Semag, Rabbenu Yerucham, and Shiltei Gibborim in chapter Elu Kesharim explicitly imply this.
[2] A permanent knot is prohibited. This implies prohibition without biblical liability because it is not intended to remain forever. The same is the meaning of the following alternative opinion: even if intended only for one day it is called permanent with respect to rabbinic prohibition. This latter view is primary. Nevertheless, Taz's analysis is not compelled.
[3] How so, etc. This refers back to a permanent knot that is not an artisan's knot, for which one is exempt.
[4] One should be careful, etc. Since according to Rif and Rambam an artisan's knot is initially prohibited, and we are not expert in identifying such knots, any knot tied very tightly may be considered an artisan's knot. Therefore we are careful even when it will be untied that same day, because two knots form a strong knot. Shiltei Gibborim, the source of this rule, says this explicitly. Olat Shabbat did not examine it carefully.
[5] Nevertheless, in a place of distress, etc. Olat Shabbat says this is incorrect because according to Rosh there is concern for a biblical prohibition. That is not so. According to Rosh it is initially permitted even without distress, as I explained above. It therefore appears one may be lenient for any genuine need so long as the knot is intended to be untied that same day. Rabbenu Yerucham, part 14, writes that even when there are many tied ends and one can only with difficulty remove and replace the garment by loosening some while leaving others tied, it is nevertheless initially permitted to tie and untie them all because they are knots made with intent to untie them every day. The same applies to a head covering used to bind the hair, even if at times the hair can be removed with difficulty without untying the knots.
[6] At one end, etc. The same applies when one ties the middle of a torn strap in order to make it whole, since the knot is intended to remain permanently (Shiltei Gibborim).
