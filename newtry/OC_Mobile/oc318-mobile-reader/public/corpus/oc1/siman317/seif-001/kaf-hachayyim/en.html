1) [Seif 1] One who ties a permanent knot, etc. The general rule contains three levels of law. According to Rif and Rambam, whose view the Shulchan Aruch follows without qualification, if it is a permanent knot, meaning one intends it to remain tied forever, and it is also a craftsman's knot, one is liable to bring a sin offering both for tying and untying it. If it is a permanent knot but not a craftsman's knot, one is exempt but the act is forbidden. If it is neither a craftsman's knot nor permanent, it is permitted initially. Rosh and Tur, whose view is stated by Rema in the gloss, hold that the law does not depend upon professional craftsmanship at all. The essential distinction is that one is liable to bring a sin offering for a knot made to remain forever. If he does not intend it to remain forever, the matter depends upon a dispute. Some say that if he does not intend to untie it for seven days, he is exempt but the act is forbidden, whereas if he intends to untie it within seven days, it is permitted initially. Kol Bo holds that it is permitted initially only when he intends to untie it that same day; beyond that, one is exempt but the act is forbidden. The words of the author and Rema are explained accordingly. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 1. Each matter will be further explained below in its place.
2) There: One who ties a permanent knot, etc. The reason is that tying a permanent knot is one of the primary labors, resembling the tying of threads that broke in the Mishkan curtains. Rashi at the beginning of the chapter Eilu Kesharim. It appears to me that although today we do not bring a sin offering, the author's distinction between an act for which one is liable to bring a sin offering and one for which he is exempt but that remains forbidden has practical relevance to disqualification as a witness. If one deliberately transgressed an act for which he is liable to bring a sin offering, he is disqualified as a witness by Torah law. If a woman was betrothed in his presence, she does not require a get. If he transgressed an act for which he is exempt but that remains forbidden, he is disqualified only rabbinically, and a woman betrothed in his presence requires a get, as Magen Avraham explains concerning witnesses disqualified rabbinically. Another practical difference concerns public announcement, because those disqualified as witnesses rabbinically are not disqualified until an announcement is made, as stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 34; see there. There are also many practical differences in cases of uncertainty. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 1. One may further say that it matters regarding repentance. If one transgressed, repented, and asks how to rectify his act, distinctions exist between inadvertent and deliberate transgression and between Torah and rabbinic prohibitions, as will be explained below at the end of siman 334, with the help of Heaven.
3) There: And it is a craftsman's knot, etc. Both elements are required: it must be a craftsman's knot and a permanent knot. When only one is present, one is exempt but the act is forbidden. This is the view of Rif and Rambam. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 2. According to Rosh and Tur, however, one is liable for any permanent knot, as stated below in the gloss.
4) There: Such as a camel driver's knot. They pierce a camel's nose, place a leather ring through it, and tie it so that it remains there forever. When one wishes to tether the animal, he ties a long strap to that ring and secures it with the strap, sometimes untying it. The first knot, namely the ring in the nose that remains there forever, is called a camel driver's knot. Gemara and Rashi at the beginning of the chapter Eilu Kesharim.
5) There: And a sailor's knot. This too is like a ring made from a stick or strap in an opening at the front of a ship, and this knot remains in place. When one wishes to secure the ship, he ties a strap to that ring and secures it with it. When he wishes to release the ship, he unties and removes the strap. The first knot, namely the permanent ring at the front of the ship, is called a sailor's knot. Gemara and Rashi there.
6) There: Among the knots of a shoe's straps, etc. This is the knot made by the shoemaker when he inserts the strap into the shoe and ties it so that it cannot come out. It remains permanently. Rashi there, page 112a.
7) There: But one who ties a permanent knot that is not a craftsman's knot is exempt. The same applies conversely, because both elements are required. The author's words, and likewise Rif and Rambam, imply that even if a knot can be untied with one hand, one is liable when it is a craftsman's knot and permanent. Shibolei HaLeket, however, writes in the name of the Tosefta: One is not liable for any permanent knot that can be untied with one hand, nor for a nonpermanent knot that cannot be untied with one hand. These are its words. It appears that this follows Rabbi Meir, who states this view in the Gemara, but the Sages disagree, and we rule like the Sages. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 3. Nehar Shalom, paragraph 1, writes the same.
