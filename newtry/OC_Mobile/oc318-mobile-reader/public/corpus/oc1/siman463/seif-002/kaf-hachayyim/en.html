3) [Se'if 2] That is prepared from oil and salt, etc. We do not prohibit it because salt has the status of water and is mixed with the oil, since the amount is small, its form has changed, and it lacks the strength to act together with the oil. Actual water, however, creates a concern in any amount. It is also possible that a large amount of salt creates a concern. Ran, chapter Kol Sha'ah; Beis Yosef, siman 462; Taz in this siman, subsection 1; Magen Avraham, subsection 1; Chok Yaakov, subsection 2; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2; Chok Yosef, subsection 2; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 4. Ran's statement that salt is like water implies that even salt produced by boiling water, whose law is like water as stated above in siman 462, subsection 75, is permitted when present only in a small amount. Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes likewise in the cited subsection. Ritva in his novellae to Pesachim, however, writes: 'Vatika is permitted, and we establish that it contains oil and salt because fruit juice does not cause leavening. Some say this refers to salt quarried from a mountain, whereas salt made from water has the status of water and is prohibited. Others say that even salt quarried from a mountain dissolves into water, but here the salt is present only in the small quantity needed to impart flavor, and the Sages were not concerned. Others explain that this discusses every type of salt, but that the flour and oil were first thoroughly cooked and the salt was added afterward. The food is then like something baked and cannot become chametz. This last explanation appears correct to Ritva.' These are his words. Mishnah Berurah cites this in Biur Halachah and writes that in practice it is proper initially to follow Ritva and add the salt only at the end, after the food has been cooked.
