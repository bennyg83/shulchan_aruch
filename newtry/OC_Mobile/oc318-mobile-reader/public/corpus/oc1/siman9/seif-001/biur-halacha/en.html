Sheep. See Mishnah Berurah, where it states “or rams.” All the later authorities write likewise, and Baer Heitev's statement that ram's wool is exempt is a scribal error. Mishnah Berurah further writes that the wool of a ewe born from a goat is not considered wool at all and exempts only its own kind. Beit Yosef writes this. See Bechoros 17a and Divrei Chamudos there. Rambam, however, implies that when he says this wool is invalid for tzitzis, he means it is invalid even for techeiles. Divrei Chamudos proves otherwise by asking: can dyeing it techeiles make it worse? This can be answered simply. Its quality of techeiles is lost, leaving it only as ordinary white wool. Indeed, the Gemara's language implies that the name “wool” has not entirely departed from it. His further proof from the statement, “Whoever places techeiles,” is also not compelling, for not every occurrence of the word techeiles must refer to ordinary tzitzis. Beit Yosef can nevertheless be reconciled. Such wool does not exempt garments of other materials because the Gemara's comparison may also teach: just as linen must be unchanged, so too wool must be unchanged. According to this, it should still exempt a garment made from the wool of a ewe born from a ewe, even where the father was a goat, because the name wool remains upon it. This requires study. Regarding reishis hagez, the wool of a ewe born from a goat is certainly exempt. Chullin 137a concludes that the wool must be fit for standing in service, and Bechoros concludes that this wool is unfit for making techeiles, and therefore unfit for the Kohen's belt. If so, however, why does the Gemara ask only concerning reishis hagez whether the teaching of the academy of Rabbi Yishmael applies? That question concerns only a goat born from a ewe. It should instead have asked that the wool is unfit for techeiles, which would apply also to a ewe born from a goat. This matter can be discussed at length, but this is not its place. The simple answer is that the Gemara wished to ask even before it knew the later statements. Peri Megadim writes a surprising ruling, that even a ewe born from a ewe, if its father was a goat, has “changed wool” and is not considered wool, even according to the view that paternal lineage is disregarded. I do not know his source. His ruling would also create a major leniency regarding kilayim. In my humble opinion the reverse is clear. Perhaps his text contains an error, and one should not rely upon it regarding kilayim. Gra in Yoreh Deah 298 maintains that even the wool of a ewe born from a goat is considered wool, though lashes are not administered for it; see there. Regarding wool blended with other materials, see Mishnah Berurah. Peri Megadim questions Magen Avraham's implication that the majority always determines the law when two materials are blended. Peri Megadim argues that where the two materials have entirely different names, such as wool and silk, or camel wool and linen, whether the majority governs is disputed. Examination of Tur and Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah 299, however, explicitly supports Magen Avraham. They rule that if wool and linen became mixed, one brings another material, blends it with them, and thereby nullifies one of the two. Beit Yosef explains that this derives from the Yerushalmi: if wool and linen were blended, one adds a litra and a little more of camel wool and nullifies whichever prohibited material is the minority. Thus, even if there were two litra of wool, one litra of linen, and only a litra and a little more of camel wool, it would be permitted. According to Peri Megadim, camel wool could never nullify the sheep's wool unless it exceeded the sheep's wool itself. I later found, with Hashem's help, that Rabbi Akiva Eiger in Yoreh Deah 299 cites Tashbetz in agreement with this conclusion.
Only D'Rabbanan. If one took a woolen tallis that lacked the minimum measure and sewed onto it a piece of another material to complete the measure, it is exempt according to the Torah according to the Mechaber, because an identifiable item is not nullified by a majority. If the woolen portion itself has the required measure, it is obligated according to the Torah. If it has the Torah measure but most of the tallis is another material, it would appear that even according to the Mechaber it remains obligated according to the Torah, because an identifiable item is not nullified by a majority. Magen Avraham in siman 10, seif katan 5, however, implies that we follow the principal material of the garment. All this is from Peri Megadim. Regarding garments woven from wool and other materials, with wool threads and other threads mixed in both warp and woof, Artzos HaChayim concludes that the majority determines the law. If most threads are wool, the garment is obligated according to the Torah even according to the Mechaber; otherwise its obligation is D'Rabbanan. Nevertheless, it appears preferable to use woolen tzitzis. Eliyah Rabbah also implies that only where the warp and woof are of separate materials do we not follow the majority, because warp or woof alone is not considered a garment. If linen is mixed with other materials, Artzos HaChayim and Eliyah Rabbah leave unresolved which material should be used for its tzitzis.
