[3] Only garments of their own material, etc. Levush explains that the verse says “the corner,” with the definite article, implying that the threads must be of the material of the corner. Rashi on Shabbos 27a does not explain it this way. Rather, the word “the corner” is entirely superfluous, since the verse already says “upon the corners of their garments”; it should have said only, “They shall place upon the tzitzis a thread of techeiles.” See Tosafos, Menachos 39b, s.v. “Even.” Olas Tamid cites Knesses HaGedolah in the name of Radbaz that a tallis whose warp is wool and woof is silk, or the reverse, is exempt according to the Torah and obligated D'Rabbanan even if most is wool. He further cites Maharam of Rothenburg that such a tallis has no remedy, and leaves the matter requiring study. I examined Radbaz, responsum 199. His reason is that Hagahos Maimoniyos writes in the name of Maharam that wool and linen exempt other materials only when used together, but wool alone does not. Therefore the garment has no remedy: wool tzitzis alone do not exempt it, and silk tzitzis do not exempt the woolen warp. Olas Tamid was not precise in leaving this unresolved, because he himself rules in this siman that we do not follow Hagahos Maimoniyos and that wool alone exempts every material. The garment therefore has a complete remedy through woolen tzitzis. Shulchan Aruch and Levush likewise rule without qualification that wool exempts all materials, clearly rejecting Maharam's view, as Beit Yosef explicitly states. Moreover, Hagahos Maimoniyos's citation of Maharam is astonishing. Maharam of Rothenburg, responsum 444, explicitly writes that linen tzitzis do not help a silk tallis because they are not the material of the corner. The Torah said “a thread of techeiles,” meaning wool, but did not say ordinary tzitzis. The verse “You shall make twisted cords for yourself from them” refers to two techeiles threads together with two white threads. Where both cannot be placed together, as in our time, the tzitzis must be either wool, because the verse says “a thread of techeiles,” or the material of the garment, because “the corner” teaches that the threads must match the corner. Maharam thus clearly maintains that wool exempts every material. His statement that wool and linen exempt only together concerns linen: linen exempts only its own material, or another material when wool and linen threads are used together. In our time linen helps only a linen garment, but wool exempts every material. This responsum also clarifies Tur's citation of Semak that one should avoid linen tzitzis on a silk garment. Semak evidently takes Maharam's concern into account that linen does not exempt other materials, whereas wool certainly does because of “a thread of techeiles.” Tur therefore also rules that linen exempts linen, based upon “the corner,” meaning the material of the corner. Beit Yosef and Bach offered many strained explanations of Tur's words, leaving room for this explanation. The practical conclusion is that the garment described above should specifically have woolen tzitzis. Silk tzitzis do not exempt it, even if most of it is silk, because warp without woof, or the reverse, is not a garment. If wool and silk fibers were themselves blended and woven, the majority governs: if most is wool it is treated as entirely wool, and if most is silk it is treated as entirely silk. A tallis blended from linen and silk, or linen and another material, has no remedy.
[4] Levush: And white tzitzis, etc. This does not mean that they required this; rather, it says that doing so is permitted. What he writes; that likewise two white woolen threads together with two techeiles threads may be placed in a linen garment; means that since wool is required for the techeiles, white wool is also valid. For this reason it is all the more permitted, as explained below according to the view that specifically this case is permitted. As for what he writes that in the present time, when we have no techeiles, a partial positive commandment is not strong enough to override the prohibition of shaatnez, I do not know why he did not state the reason based on Reish Lakish in Menachos 40a: wherever you find a negative and a positive commandment together, if you can fulfill both, it is preferable; if not, the positive commandment comes and overrides the negative. Here too, in the present time when we have no techeiles, we can fulfill both, and therefore the positive commandment does not override the negative. He himself writes this reasoning in the name of the 'some say.' One should not object that, according to this, the first view should permit this even when techeiles is available. One can answer that it holds that since techeiles must be inserted, what accompanies it is different; so too it is implied in the Gemara. Regarding the words, 'And some say that even, etc.,' Malbushei Yom Tov writes: 'I do not know who these “some say” are. On the contrary, the question was resolved in the Gemara that even linen in wool is permitted.' I have now found this explicitly in Beit Yosef, siman 11, page 14b, in the name of the Rambam, who rules this way and explains the reason: we do not accept that the aforementioned question was conclusively resolved. This is astonishing regarding the gaon Malbushei Yom Tov. Regarding the words, 'And some say that also, etc.,' I wrote in Eliyah Zuta the following words of Malbushei Yom Tov: 'This is the rule of Reish Lakish. It appears to me that there is no dispute here. Rather, the essential Torah law follows the first view, as Rachavah says in the Gemara, but we say that one should not do so because of Reish Lakish's rule, since it is possible to fulfill both. He should not have presented this in the name of “some say,” but should have said: “This is the Torah law, but the Sages, of blessed memory, said that also in this case, etc.”' In my humble opinion, he correctly intended the first interpretation that Beit Yosef explains there in accordance with the Rambam, where it is explicit that the statement concerns Torah law. Yet all of Beit Yosef's discussion there escaped Malbushei Yom Tov's attention.
