But one who is in mourning. The Rosh writes: A baraisa teaches that during the first three days a mourner may not go to another mourner's house, but afterward he may go. Tishah B'Av is likewise treated like mourning after the first three days, and the mourner may go to the synagogue with the rest of the congregation, who are all like mourners. Beit HaBechirah writes that since the mourning of Tishah B'Av is for an ancient calamity, it is no more stringent than mourning after the first three days. This requires study. If the mourner himself is within his first three days, he may not visit another mourner even if that other mourner is beyond three days. Why, then, should Tishah B'Av be different? Conversely, if he is beyond his own first three days, he may visit even a mourner who is within three days, since there is greater reason to permit visiting one whose grief is intense. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes explicitly that only a mourner who is beyond three days may go to the synagogue; see there. It is known that his rulings are based upon the Rosh. The Tur, however, rules without qualification, implying that even within the first three days it is permitted, and this also appears from the Rosh in chapter 3 of Moed Katan. The explanation must therefore be as follows: Tishah B'Av is treated like mourning after three days. Just as an ordinary mourner may go out after three days, so too on Tishah B'Av one may go to the synagogue even during the first three days, because everyone is in mourning. This requires study. Terumas HaDeshen objects that at night a mourner may go out when necessary, as stated in Yoreh Deah 393. One may answer that this applies when he goes alone, whereas here, since he goes together with a group of people, it would be forbidden were it not for the reason that all of them are mourners.
