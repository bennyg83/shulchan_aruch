And one who is left-handed. The Mishnah Berurah says even one who became so through habit, following Magen Avraham from Mordechai. Although Magen Avraham cites dissent, the end of his next paragraph shows his legal conclusion follows Darkei Moshe; Derech HaChaim agrees. Dagul MeRevavah says all agree one made left-handed by Heaven has that status. The same logic plainly applies if his right palm was amputated, as stated in Mishnah Berurah. Pri Megadim, siman 651, doubts this and does not clearly accept Dagul MeRevavah, but we may rely on the latter, especially since Darkei Moshe treats even acquired habit as left-handedness. Since one whose left arm was amputated but retains kibores is fundamentally obligated to place there, the converse follows: one whose right hand was amputated but retains kibores must now put tefillin there after learning to act with his left, because the right has become his left.
He places it on his left. See Mishnah Berurah from Chut HaShani. Shaarei Teshuvah claims Baal HaItur, cited in Beis Yosef, siman 651, holds that reversed placement is valid. This is unconvincing. Baal HaItur says lulav is 'no worse than tefillin': since a left-handed person places tefillin on his own left, which is everyone else's right, taking lulav that way is at least valid after the fact. For tefillin themselves, placing on his right (everyone else's left) certainly does not fulfill the obligation, since his left has the status of an ordinary person's right. Chut HaShani later explains Baal HaItur this way with proofs.
Some say the hand with which he writes, etc. The unqualified wording of Shulchan Aruch and the poskim implies any writing determines this; he need not be capable of writing Torah scrolls, tefillin, or mezuzos, despite the verse's plain 'you shall write them.' If he cannot write proper script but only mnemonic marks or figures, called numerals, it is uncertain whether this counts as writing. See Rambam, Hilchos Shabbos 11:10, concerning two symbols, and Maggid Mishneh. In such a case it is certainly better to rely on the Gra and most Rishonim, who follow the first opinion, as Artzos HaChaim discusses at length.
And this is the custom. Magen Avraham says that under this view even one who writes equally with both hands places on every person's left, although performing all other acts with his left. Bach disagrees: one who writes right but does all else left is ambidextrous; one who writes left and does all else right places on everyone's left. Magen Avraham concludes we have only Shulchan Aruch's ruling, and many later authorities follow him. Artzos HaChaim upholds Bach, while the Gra fully follows the first view and entirely disregards writing, so one who writes right and acts left places on the right. Nevertheless, popular practice can be justified according to all views. Even the first view treats someone as ambidextrous if the writing hand can also perform other acts, though the other hand does them more easily; since the other cannot write at all, each hand has a distinct advantage. This differs from the Mishnah Berurah's case in paragraph 25. Shaarei Teshuvah teaches this logic regarding lulav, where everyone treats writing merely as another individual act; the same therefore applies here even to the first view and Gra. One who writes right but more easily performs other acts left places tefillin on every person's left.
