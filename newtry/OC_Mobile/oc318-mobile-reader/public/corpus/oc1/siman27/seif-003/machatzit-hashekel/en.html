Seif katan 3: One whose arm is amputated, etc. Yet this requires study, for the ruling follows no view. According to Tosafos, everyone agrees that one who still has an upper arm is obligated. If the authorities dispute this case, the difficulty is even greater, for the first Tanna exempts only one who has no arm, but agrees that one who has an upper arm is obligated.
Others obligate him even if he has no arm. If so, the Or Zarua, who exempts even one who has an upper arm, follows no opinion. His text read, "he has only an upper arm, etc.," which implies that according to this version the first Tanna exempts even one who still has an upper arm. The Or Zarua ruled like the first Tanna.
I was astonished, etc., as in chalitzah. The questioner reasoned that he should place the tefillin on his right arm, like a left-handed person who places them on his right. The answer was that an amputation is worse than left-handedness. Chalitzah proves this. Some hold that since chalitzah must be performed with the right foot, a left-handed man performs it with his left foot. But if his right foot was amputated, he does not perform chalitzah at all, and we do not say that he should perform it with the left. The same applies to tefillin. Moreover, tefillin require the weaker hand. For a left-handed person, his right hand is indeed the weaker hand. But if his left arm was amputated, why should his right be called the weaker hand? On the contrary, since he has no other hand, all his strength and activity are in the right hand, and it certainly is not the weaker hand. This is the substance of his words.
Surely the Talmud, etc. He should not rule from his own reasoning, especially since he did not cite the words of the Gemara at all. The Sifrei to Parashas Vaeschanan, cited in Yalkut Shimoni to Parashas Bo, remez 222, states: "Upon your arm means the left, etc. Rabbi Nasan, etc. Rabbi Yose HaChoram, named for his place as Tosafos explain, says: We find that the right is called an arm, etc. What then does the verse teach by saying upon your arm, etc.?" The same appears in Menachos 37a. The Sifrei adds to Rabbi Yose's words and concludes that the verse was needed because he would place the tefillin on the right.
This seems to refer to one who has no arm at all, yet he is obligated to place tefillin on the right. Why then did Tosafos write, according to the Others, that the case is specifically one who has an upper arm, whereas one who has no upper arm is exempt?
One may answer that they hold, etc. Tosafos hold that Rabbi Yose HaChoram too speaks specifically of one who still has an upper arm. Even so, he holds that the tefillin are placed on the right and not on the remaining upper arm, because for this law the upper arm is not called an arm.
Nevertheless, it requires study, for Rabbi Elazar answers, etc. The Gemara asks Rabbi Yose HaChoram, who holds that the right is also called an arm, how he knows that tefillin are placed on the left. It answers in the same way Rabbi Nasan derives it, from the juxtaposition of "you shall bind them" and "you shall write them." Just as writing is normally done with the right, so binding is done with the right. Since the binding is done with the right, the tefillin must be placed on the left. Rabbi Elazar answers that although the word arm can mean the right, here the verse spells the word in a way that is expounded as yad kehah, the weak arm, namely the left. The difficulty is how one can say that Rabbi Yose derives placement on the left from yad kehah, since in the Sifrei Rabbi Yose explicitly uses that same word to include one whose arm is amputated.
Nevertheless, it requires study, for one may say that Rabbi Elazar stated his own reasoning. This therefore proves nothing against Tosafos and does not justify rejecting the Sifrei. Rabbi Elazar did not intend to explain Rabbi Yose HaChoram. According to Rabbi Yose, one must say that he derives the law from "you shall bind them" and "you shall write them," as above. Rather, Rabbi Elazar stated his own view. He too holds that the right is called an arm, but in his own view placement on the left is derived from yad kehah, the weak arm. On this point Rabbi Elazar does not agree with Rabbi Yose's derivation that the verse includes an amputee.
And see what he wrote in seif 6, that we do not rule like any of them, etc. This is the correct reading. Rama writes that the custom depends entirely on the hand used for writing, which is called the right hand even if all other tasks are performed with the opposite hand. The basis must therefore be Rabbi Nasan's derivation from "you shall bind them" and "you shall write them," not the derivation of the weak arm. If one performs all tasks with the left, the right is his weaker hand, yet Rama writes that the hand used for writing is called the right. He must therefore derive the law from the juxtaposition of binding and writing.
And in the Mordechai, etc., both are derived, etc. This resolves the earlier difficulty concerning Rabbi Elazar. Rabbi Elazar explains Rabbi Yose's derivation as yad kehah, the weak arm, yet Rabbi Yose also includes an amputee from the same word. The Mordechai resolves this by holding that both laws are derived from it, the weak arm and the amputee.
For the one who expounds this, etc. The objection was raised against the opinion that one who writes with the left but performs all other tasks with the right should place tefillin on the right. If so, why is the word yadcha needed to teach the weak arm? The law should follow from "you shall bind them" and "you shall write them." It must therefore be needed for this very case. Since he writes with the left, one might have thought that he binds with the left and therefore places tefillin on the right. The expression yad kehah teaches that he places them on the left, because all his other actions are done with the right and the left is therefore the weaker hand. Magen Avraham answers that this is not a difficulty. The authority who derives the law from binding and writing does not derive yad kehah, and the authority who derives yad kehah does not use the juxtaposition of binding and writing. According to the Or Zarua, that authority exempts one from placing tefillin on the right even if he still has an upper arm.
But according to Tosafos, etc., without a blessing. This does not mean that according to Tosafos no blessing is recited. According to Tosafos, one who still has an upper arm is certainly obligated according to everyone and would recite a blessing. Rather, for us, although we act stringently in accordance with Tosafos, because there is a doubt concerning the blessing, since according to the Or Zarua he is exempt, he should not recite a blessing.
Know that what the Shulchan Aruch writes in seif 2, the custom, etc., and in seif 3, the custom, etc. The question arises regarding a left-handed person who has only tefillin made for a right-handed person, or the reverse, a right-handed person who has only tefillin made for a left-handed person. A left-handed person places tefillin on the right, and the knot shaped like a yud must face the heart. The yud is therefore positioned on the left side of the tefillin, while for a right-handed person the arrangement is reversed. What should he do?
Responsa Nachalas Shivah, siman 41, writes that he must remove the strap from the passage, reverse it, and insert it from the other side so that the yud will be near the heart. Although the yud will still be inverted, this is of no concern. He brings a proof. See there. Responsa Shevus Yaakov disagrees. He writes that one need not trouble himself so greatly. He may leave the strap in its passage, turn the tefillin around, place the box toward the armpit, and the passage toward the elbow. Although this is not the preferred custom described in the Shulchan Aruch, there is not such great concern in this case. See there.
See Eliyah Rabbah, who disagrees.
