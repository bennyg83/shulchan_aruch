1) [Se'if 1] An open area behind the houses, etc. It was customary to leave an open area behind the houses and another in front of the houses. The area behind the houses is called a rear open area, while the area in front of the houses is called a courtyard. The area in front of the houses, called a courtyard, was certainly enclosed for residential use, because it is customary to perform all household activities there. Therefore, even if it measures beis kor or two kor, it is permitted to carry throughout it, as is stated afterward in the name of Mishbetzos Zahav. It is even permitted to carry from the house into it if the house and courtyard belong to one person, or if they belong to two people who established an eruv. The open area behind the houses, however, is not ordinarily used for household needs to the same extent, and it is presumably not enclosed for residential use. Therefore, if it is larger than beis sasayim, its law is that of a karpeif, as stated in the Shulchan Aruch.
1) Know that four terms apply to this subject: karmelis, karpeif, rear open area, and courtyard. A karmelis under Torah law is an open plain through which the public does not pass. A karmelis by Rabbinic law is a karpeif larger than beis sasayim that was not enclosed for residential use. Under Torah law it is a complete private domain, but by Rabbinic law it is a karmelis. An area larger than beis sasayim that was enclosed for residential use is called a karpeif, and an area measuring beis sasayim or less is likewise called a karpeif even if it was not enclosed for residential use. A rear open area behind the houses that is larger than beis sasayim is also a karmelis by Rabbinic law when it was not enclosed for residential use. A courtyard in front of the houses was enclosed for residential use, and even if it measures several kor it is a private domain even by Rabbinic law. Nevertheless, one may carry less than four amos from a karmelis consisting of an open plain to a karpeif larger than beis sasayim that was not enclosed for residential use, or in the reverse direction, as stated in siman 346, se'if 3. It is forbidden, however, to carry from that karpeif to a courtyard, as stated in siman 372, se'if 1. A karpeif measuring beis sasayim that was not enclosed for residential use is called a karpeif, and one may carry throughout it. It is forbidden to carry from it to a karmelis, and it is forbidden even to carry from it to a karpeif larger than beis sasayim. One may carry from it to a courtyard, but not from it to a house, even if both belong to one person, as stated in siman 372, Magen Avraham, subsection 1. It appears to me that this applies specifically when it measures beis sasayim, in which case it is called a karpeif. If it is smaller than beis sasayim, it does not bear the designation karpeif, and one may carry from it to a house even though it was not enclosed for residential use. If it was enclosed for residential use, however, one may carry from such a karpeif to a house even if it measures several kor, for anything enclosed for residential use is deemed a courtyard and constitutes one domain with a house when both belong to one person. Know also that a karpeif is located at a distance from the city, whereas a rear open area is behind the houses. According to the authority who maintains that a karpeif behind the houses is likewise forbidden, it may be said that a karpeif differs in shape from a rear open area, such as by having an inferior fence or the like, or that it was made for wood and other items used only at infrequent intervals, unlike a rear open area. Mishbetzos Zahav.
