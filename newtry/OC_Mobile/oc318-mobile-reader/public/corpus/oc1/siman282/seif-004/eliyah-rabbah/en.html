[Levush] And on Yom Tov, etc. Because Yom Tov has a melachah prohibition, Yom Kippur carries karet, and Shabbos carries sekilah, as the Gemara explains. Mateh Moshe gives a mnemonic from the number of letters in the opening words of the blessing, totaling twenty-five, corresponding to "thus shall you bless."
[Levush] And on Shabbos, etc. Additionally, if an unavoidable circumstance prevented a person from attending synagogue all seven weekdays and he did not hear Barchu, on Shabbos he can listen to the seven aliyot recite Barchu and thereby make up the seven, as stated in old glosses to the Levush and in Beit Yosef. I found another reason: Shabbos is compared to the World to Come, the day of rest for the righteous, and therefore seven are called. Yom Kippur atones for deeds of this world, which is compared to the six weekdays, and therefore six are called.
[Levush] But one may add, etc. Tashbetz writes that nowadays, when every oleh recites blessings, one should not add aliyot because of unnecessary blessings. Knesset HaGedolah, Olat Tamid, and Magen Avraham say this is the proper practice except at a wedding or brit. Those who are lenient lose nothing, because all the other poskim imply permission, and Magen Avraham himself inclines toward leniency. His words are somewhat contradictory, and his criticism of Olat Shabbat is not compelling; perhaps there is a scribal error. My great-grandfather, the gaon, instituted here in Prague that no more than ten be called, apart from maftir, because of burden on the congregation. I added that even on the Yamim Noraim no more than ten should be called, for otherwise those days would appear holier than Shabbos.
[Levush] Permission, etc. This requires study. Rama writes that some prohibit and that this is the custom except on Simchat Torah. The Levush himself in siman 669 appears to agree, and likewise in siman 136 seif 4. Magen Avraham writes that even at a wedding or where many people have obligations, the practice is not to be lenient. Know that even those who permit agree that the added person does not count toward the required seven.
[Levush] And so is the practice. Malbushei Yom Tov notes that this is not the custom everywhere. Here in Prague the custom is to add aliyot on Yom Tov and Yom Kippur. I see support from the Mishnah at the beginning of chapter HaKorei Omed: regarding Monday and Thursday, and regarding Rosh Chodesh and Chol HaMoed, it explicitly says one may not add, but it does not say so regarding Yom Tov and Yom Kippur. Further proof is Megillah 23, where Rabbi Akiva says Yom Kippur has seven and Shabbos six, and the baraita says one may not subtract but may add. This certainly appears to apply to Shabbos as well, which is listed at the end. Consequently, according to Rabbi Yehudah, who holds Shabbos has seven, the same principle should allow additions on Yom Tov and Yom Kippur. The prohibiting view requires explanation. Tosafot there and Agudah also imply permission, and Responsa Rivash siman 84 rules so.
And a minor. Nowadays the practice is not to call a minor at all except for maftir. Perishah explains that only in earlier times, when the first oleh alone blessed before and the last alone blessed afterward, could a minor count, unlike nowadays when each oleh blesses before and after. He finds support in the Rosh, chapter 1 of Kiddushin. Magen Avraham writes that he found in Rivash siman 321 in Rashi's name that the Gemara's statement "all count, etc." refers specifically to maftir, and that Hagahot Maimoniyot and Beit Yosef siman 135 in the name of Rokeach imply the same. This is surprising: how can "count toward seven" mean maftir? I examined the responsum and found the opposite: Rivash cites Rabbenu Tam that a minor counts among the seven but not for maftir when maftir constitutes the day's obligation. I later saw in siman 326 that Rivash says in Rabbenu Tam's name that the Mishnah's rule that a minor reads may refer to maftir. This makes Magen Avraham's citation even more surprising, because Rivash explicitly concludes that the Gemara about counting among seven concerns one of the seven themselves even according to Rashi. The Mishnah alone could be explained as maftir, where an adult first reads the same passage and the minor repeats it for the honor of Torah. Beit Yosef's explanation of Rokeach in siman 135 is likewise incorrect. "A minor joins" means only one minor may be included among the seven, as Rivash siman 35 notes from the singular wording "even a minor, even a woman," not "women." This also appears to be the Gemara's meaning. Bartenura on Megillah chapter 4 Mishnah 6 and Rambam there say a minor counts only from the third aliyah onward. This also rejects Beit Yosef's view in siman 135. Magen Avraham writes that women are obligated to hear Torah reading like men and it is a mitzvah to translate it for them so they understand, citing Masechet Soferim chapter 18, yet then says the women customarily go outside. His words seem contradictory, though they may perhaps be reconciled with the context.
[Levush] Because it is not respectful, etc. Beit Yosef cites Ran: since they are not fully obligated, they cannot fully discharge others. That means women and minors cannot entirely discharge the obligation of adult men. The Levush appears to understand "discharge" as referring to bringing out the sefer Torah, which is imprecise.
