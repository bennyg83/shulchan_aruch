One who ate a forbidden item. So wrote Rambam chapter 1 of Berachot; and Raavad challenged him and said thus: He erred in this—for they only said there is no zimun upon them to say they have no significance of fixed eating since they eat a forbidden item like eating fruits that have no fixed eating for zimun; but an opening and closing blessing—why should they not bless, since he benefits, end of his words—and this is from the Mishnah at the beginning of chapter 3 "they ate": one ate tevel—even d'rabbanan one does not invite zimun upon him; and Raavad holds specifically for zimun no, but blessers we bless; and Rambam must hold they taught zimun as a leniency, for it is lighter than birkat hamazon—for even on a vegetable leaf one of them ate we invite zimun—it teaches that forbidden food is worse; all the more so he does not bless; and Rosh agreed with Raavad—from that which is in chapter HaGozel: R' Meir said one who stole a se'ah of wheat, ground, kneaded, baked, and separated challah—how does he bless? This one does not bless but blasphemes; and on this it says one who breaks a covenant blesses, blasphemes Heaven—behold he is obligated to bless only his blessing is blasphemy, end of his words; and Beit Yosef strengthened Rambam's view from Yerushalmi stolen matzah forbidden to bless on it, etc.; and further from Tosefta: on all of these one does not bless and one does not invite zimun, etc.; and that which "this one does not bless but blasphemes" means therefore he should not bless; and in my humble opinion certainly Rosh did not bring proof from blasphemy to learn that he should bless and blaspheme Heaven—for silence is certainly better for him; rather it seems Raavad too does not disagree on one who eats forbidden food b'mezid but on b'shogeg—as Rambam wrote there: anyone who eats forbidden food whether intentionally or unwittingly does not bless beginning or end, etc.; and it appears to me all hold the Mishnah that said one ate tevel one does not invite zimun means he ate b'shogeg—for b'mezid he would be completely wicked and obviously one does not invite zimun with him; rather certainly b'shogeg we say thus; and Rambam holds his eating is not regarded as eating at all and similar to what is written in Yoreh Deah siman 119 one who sells to his fellow something forbidden d'oraisa and he ate—they return the money, for it is as if he did not eat; so too for blessing, and stricter—for even d'rabbanan prohibition regarding blessing it is as if he did not eat at all; and from this reason it seems one who eats forbidden food at a time of danger does not bless for Rambam—for one reason is who understands his error, and it is retroactively as if he ate permitted; nevertheless he does not bless; and so Beit Yosef afterward in the name of Mahari Weil. And although Beit Yosef wrote it seems Rambam agrees that in all such cases he blesses, etc.—very puzzling why b'shogeg should be worse than time of danger like b'mezid—for there too there is coercion in b'shogeg, for his heart coerced him, for he is exempt from punishment of oath if he swore falsely as explained in Shevuot in the students of Rav for the reason "the person in the oath" except one compelled; and similarly a woman who committed adultery b'shogeg is like one compelled; rather it appears plain to my humble opinion that the essence is as Mahari Weil wrote—even at time of danger he does not bless for Rambam; and so from responsum Rashba siman 494 regarding a sick person who eats on Yom Kippur he ruled he must bless and wrote: although Rambam ruled on one who ate tevel he does not bless—but from where does he have it, and Raavad challenged him, end of his words—behold he equated this law of eating tevel to a sick person on Yom Kippur and certainly b'shogeg is dealt with and he compares to one compelled like a sick person—and in both Raavad disagrees that b'shogeg he blesses since he benefits, only one does not invite zimun upon him for his eating is not regarded as fixed eating; but for blessing beginning and end he blesses since he benefited; and although b'shogeg opening blessing is not applicable—for he did not know there is a prohibition here—nevertheless opening blessing applies where he is compelled, for that too is called b'shogeg even though he acts b'mezid; if so Rosh agrees well with Raavad's words from that which we say this one does not bless but blasphemes—it implies blessing applies only he blasphemes, and that is b'mezid; but if he did so b'shogeg there is no blasphemy; if so the obligation of blessing remains upon him properly; and what Beit Yosef brought proof from forbidden to bless from stolen matzah—and so Tosafot—that deals with b'mezid and there is no proof for b'shogeg; also Rabbeinu Tur's words can be explained on b'shogeg as to my humble opinion correctly; and even if you say Raavad and Rosh are not explained thus, nevertheless for the law it appears proper to rule that one who eats forbidden food b'shogeg blesses afterward, since he already ruled in danger one must bless—and certainly they are one matter; see responsum Rashba siman 497.
