He ate prohibited food. Rambam rules this in chapter 1 of Hilchos Berachos. Raavad objects that the Mishnah said only that they do not include such people in zimun, because their eating lacks the significance of a fixed meal, like eating fruit, but why should they not recite initial and concluding blessings after deriving benefit? Raavad understands the Mishnah at the beginning of the third chapter, which says that one who ate tevel, even D'Rabbanan tevel, is not included in zimun, as applying only to zimun. Rambam must understand that the Mishnah teaches the greater novelty regarding zimun, although zimun is otherwise easier than Bircas HaMazon, since even one who ate a vegetable leaf may join. Prohibited food is worse, and certainly no blessing is recited. Rosh agrees with Raavad from the statement in Bava Kamma that one who stole wheat, ground it, kneaded it, baked it, and separated challah from it does not bless but blasphemes. This implies that he is obligated to bless, though his blessing is blasphemy. Beit Yosef supports Rambam from the Yerushalmi concerning stolen matzah and from a Tosefta stating that no blessing or zimun is recited over prohibited foods, and explains that “he does not bless but blasphemes” means that he therefore should not bless. In my opinion, Rosh certainly did not intend to prove that one should bless and thereby blaspheme Hashem, for silence is preferable. Rather, Raavad disputes Rambam only where the prohibited food was eaten inadvertently. Rambam explicitly writes that whether one ate deliberately or inadvertently, he recites no blessing. All agree that the Mishnah’s case is inadvertent, for one who ate deliberately is plainly wicked and would not be included. Rambam holds that such consumption is not regarded as eating at all, comparable to Yoreh Deah 119, where one who sold another a Torah-prohibited food that was eaten must refund the money because it is as though the purchaser did not eat. For blessings even a Rabbinic prohibition is treated as though no eating occurred. For the same reason, according to Rambam one who eats prohibited food because of danger should not bless, since inadvertence and compulsion are alike. Beit Yosef, however, writes that Rambam would agree that in danger one blesses. This is very difficult, for why should inadvertence be worse than danger? Inadvertence is also a form of compulsion. Responsa Rashba 794 concerning a sick person who eats on Yom Kippur equates the two cases and writes that although Rambam ruled that one who ate tevel does not bless, Raavad disputed him. This shows that according to Rambam even in danger no blessing is recited, while Raavad holds that after inadvertent consumption one blesses because he benefited. Initial blessing can also arise in a case of compulsion, which is considered inadvertent even though he knowingly eats. Rosh can therefore agree with Raavad: the Gemara’s description of a blessing as blasphemy applies to deliberate sin, but not to inadvertence, so the obligation remains. Beit Yosef’s proofs from stolen matzah and the Tosefta concern deliberate sin and do not disprove this. Even if Raavad and Rosh are not interpreted this way, practical halachah should distinguish: one who ate prohibited food inadvertently should recite the concluding blessing, since we already rule that one who eats because of danger must bless, and the two are equivalent.
