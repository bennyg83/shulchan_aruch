But. See Taz, who cites Rambam and Raavad, chapter 1 of Hilchos Berachos, halachah 19. According to Raavad, who holds that one recites the blessing beforehand, Berachos 52b is difficult, for one does not recite a blessing over fragrances of idolatry. It must be that regarding a Torah prohibition even Raavad agrees that no blessing is recited. His principal proof is from the third chapter, 45a: over D'Rabbanan tevel one does not recite zimun, implying that one does recite a blessing. Likewise, according to Raavad, one would recite a blessing over fragrances associated with forbidden relations, whose smell is prohibited only D'Rabbanan. Mishneh LaMelech writes accordingly that Rambam’s ruling in chapter 9, halachah 8, that one does not recite a blessing over such fragrance follows his own view. According to Raavad, no blessing is recited over idolatrous fragrance, which is prohibited by Torah law, but one does recite a blessing over fragrance prohibited because of forbidden relations, which is only Rabbinic. A marginal note refers to siman 217, seif 5. According to Taz, however, Raavad disputes Rambam only where the prohibition was eaten inadvertently or because of danger. This is readily understood: Rambam holds that even in danger one does not recite a blessing, whereas Raavad holds that in danger one recites both the initial and concluding blessings. The case of idolatrous fragrance, where there is no danger, therefore remains one in which no blessing is recited. Taz writes that according to Rambam the Mishnah specifically teaches that there is no zimun because unlawful food is worse than fruit. This is difficult, for Rambam himself rules in chapter 8, halachah 8, that three people eating fruit do not combine, as the Mechaber also rules in siman 197. See Bach and Kesef Mishneh, who explain differently. Returning to Taz’s intent, he writes that the Mishnah’s case of eating prohibited food concerns inadvertent consumption, since one who ate deliberately is wicked. One may respond that so long as he has not been placed under a ban, why should they not recite zimun with him, as in siman 199, seif 1, and siman 55? The general explanation is that according to Rambam inadvertence and danger are alike. Responsa Rashba proves that according to Rambam a sick person eating on Yom Kippur does not recite a blessing. Rambam’s proof is from the Mishnah’s case of one who ate D'Rabbanan tevel, with whom no zimun is recited even when he ate inadvertently, because such eating is not considered eating at all; the same reasoning removes the blessing. Raavad holds only that there is no zimun, because the meal lacks permanence and resembles fruit. Rosh agrees with Raavad regarding inadvertent consumption, and the same applies in danger. Thus, Raavad’s statement that one recites the initial blessing refers to danger; Rosh agrees that after inadvertent consumption one recites the concluding blessing, and in danger even the initial blessing, though there is no zimun because this meal is inferior to a fruit meal. The proof is that the Gemara calls the blessing blasphemy only for deliberate sin; inadvertence and danger do not constitute blasphemy. The Mechaber here rules like Rambam that no blessing is recited, yet he rules that in danger one does recite a blessing. He apparently distinguishes between inadvertence, where one regrets the act and it is not regarded as significant eating, and danger. See Chemed Moshe. I nevertheless do not know why the Mechaber omitted the law of inadvertence. Responsa Rashba may be understood as stating the stronger point; alternatively, Rambam may agree that on Yom Kippur the food is permitted to the sick person at that prohibited time. With Hashem’s help this will be explained further in Magen Avraham; this is not the place to elaborate.
If. If one ate food whose Torah prohibition is uncertain, he does not recite a blessing. Even if it were certainly prohibited only D'Rabbanan, he would not recite a blessing; the rule that a doubtful Torah prohibition is treated stringently does not change this.
And if. If three people ate prohibited food because of danger, or one of the three did so, they do not include him in zimun. This is also implied by the Shulchan Aruch, which states that they recite blessings but does not state that they recite zimun. I wrote similarly in my novellae at the beginning of the third chapter. The Gemara did not answer that the tevel was eaten because of danger, for the ruling is true even where the prohibition is Rabbinic. Demai is different, for one does include its eater in zimun. With other foods, such as challah outside Eretz Yisrael, which some permit nullifying in a majority, he nevertheless ate prohibited food in its present state.
