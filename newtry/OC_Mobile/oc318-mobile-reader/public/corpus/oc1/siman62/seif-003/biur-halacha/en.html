If he did not make it audible to his ear, he has fulfilled his obligation. This applies even to Krias Shema, and certainly to other mitzvos, as stated explicitly in Berachos 15b and ruled by all the poskim. It is very surprising that Shaarei Teshuvah and Birkei Yosef, below in siman 185, cite Sefer Chareidim as saying that most poskim hold that if he did not make it audible to his ear he did not fulfill his obligation, for this contradicts the explicit Gemara cited above. Perhaps his intent is that he did not fulfill the Torah-level mitzvah in its most choice manner, since Halachos Gedolos, the Rif, and the Sheiltos conclude regarding this, "Shema," make it audible to your ear, etc. This requires study.
He has fulfilled his obligation. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote that he must therefore recite it again when the constraint passes. This is the consensus of Peri Chadash, Eliyah Rabbah, Mateh Yehudah, Binyan Olam, Magen Gibborim, and other later authorities, contrary to those who are lenient. It cannot be said that the Mechaber rules that, on a Torah level, mental recitation is equivalent to speech. His words in Beit Yosef in this siman, siman 85, and siman 185 do not imply this, and the explanation of the Gra proves that the Mechaber himself holds that mental recitation is not equivalent to speech. In truth, there is no proper basis for uncertainty regarding whether mental recitation is equivalent to speech, since the view of most Rishonim, indeed nearly all of them, inclines to the conclusion that it is not equivalent to speech. These include Rabbeinu Chananel, HaEshkol, Rabbeinu Yonah, the Rosh, Or Zarua, Raavad, Mordechai, Tur, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and Shulchan Aruch, all of whom rule that mental recitation is not equivalent to speech. This also appears to be Rashi's view in Berachos 15b, s.v. Belibo, where he explains that he did not make it audible to his ear, implying that mere thought is insufficient, as the Rosh, Rabbeinu Yonah, and the other Rishonim explain that baraisa. Baal HaMaor in the chapter Mi Shemeiso likewise explains the baraisa this way. I saw, however, a novel point in his words, which somewhat implies that he rules like Rabbi Chisda only because the issue is a Torah-level uncertainty. According to him, with other blessings, if one recited them mentally, he would fulfill his obligation after the fact. This is not implied by the other poskim, who rule simply that the halachah follows Rabbi Chisda based on the discussion in Shabbos 150a. He himself ultimately writes that Rabbi Chisda's view is preferable because of that discussion, implying that his own opinion on the matter was not settled. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch below in siman 206 also imply that the same applies to other Rabbinic blessings. Eliyah Rabbah writes that all the poskim he saw hold that mental recitation is not equivalent to speech, except for the Rambam and Semag, who maintain that, regarding blessings, even Bircas HaMazon, which is D'Oraisa, mental recitation is equivalent to speech after the fact. Riaz goes even further and maintains that even for Krias Shema mental recitation is equivalent to speech. Since all the Rishonim disagree with them, the Tur and Shulchan Aruch ruled without qualification against their view, as Bach and Eliyah Rabbah also write. This is straightforward. I saw that Chayei Adam writes that one who mentally recited a blessing requires study. In my humble opinion, regarding Bircas HaMazon he must certainly recite it again, and even regarding other blessings, one who relies on all the Rishonim cited above has certainly lost nothing.
