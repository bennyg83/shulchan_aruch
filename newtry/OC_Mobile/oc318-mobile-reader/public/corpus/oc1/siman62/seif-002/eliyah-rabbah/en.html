[2] In any language, because of "Shema," etc. In the chapter Hayah Korei the Sages taught: Krias Shema must be recited as written, as it says, "and these words shall be," meaning in their original form. This is Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi's view. The Sages say it may be recited in any language, as it says, "Shema," in any language that you understand. It is well known that the halachah follows the Sages. I am nevertheless surprised how Ravina, in the chapter Mi Shemeiso, inferred from the Mishnah stating that a baal keri mentally recites Krias Shema that mental recitation is equivalent to speech. If one fulfills his obligation through mental recitation, it is obvious that Krias Shema may be recited in any language, for language is inapplicable to thought, as the Rashba writes in his novellae to Berachos 14. It appears to me that even Ravina said this only regarding a baal keri, who is considered constrained, but without a constraint everyone agrees that he does not fulfill his obligation. Accordingly, since we rule like Rabbi Chisda, who disagrees with Ravina, as Beit Yosef writes, even under constraint he does not fulfill his obligation. I found that Sefer Tamim Deim, siman 240, rules this explicitly. The rulings of Shulchan Aruch and Levush in seifim 4 and 5, that one fulfills his obligation under constraint, are therefore surprising. As practical halachah, it appears to me that if he cannot articulate the words with his lips, he should contemplate them. If the constraint passes while the time for Krias Shema remains, he must recite it again. See below, siman 101, and what I write at the end of this siman. Knesses HaGedolah writes that one who heard Krias Shema without reciting it fulfilled his obligation, citing Re'em, part 1, siman 41. In siman 42, however, he cites Maharam Alashkar as ruling that he did not fulfill his obligation. I examined the responsum itself and saw that Maharam Alashkar challenges the Re'em because the mitzvah of Krias Shema is recitation, not hearing, as it says, "and you shall speak of them." He cites many poskim who rule this way, and his words are correct. Sefer Tamim Deim there also appears to hold this way. This applies specifically when he is proficient, or even when the listeners are not proficient if they are individuals. In the presence of ten, however, Maharam Alashkar himself cites Abudraham as saying that the shaliach tzibbur can discharge the obligation of one who is not proficient, as Magen Avraham also writes in siman 59. This too surprises me, for Abudraham, page 31, writes: One may ask why the shaliach tzibbur recites the blessings of Krias Shema aloud, to discharge the obligation of one who does not know them, while he recites Krias Shema quietly. The answer is that through the blessings of Krias Shema the shaliach tzibbur can discharge the obligation of one who does not know them, because the matter depends upon hearing just as upon recitation. He cannot discharge him from Krias Shema itself, because it says, "and you shall speak of them." Thus, even for one who is not proficient, the shaliach tzibbur cannot discharge his obligation. According to Maharam Alashkar, Abudraham's words would contradict one another. This requires study. Nevertheless, as practical halachah, Maharam's view appears correct, that the shaliach tzibbur discharges one who is not proficient. Since there are ten and he answers amen, it is as though he spoke himself, because he is not proficient, and "you shall speak of them" may be applied to him. A proof is found in Rabbeinu Yonah, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 69; see there. Further study is needed regarding Abudraham's statement there that there is no ignoramus in the world who does not know how to say the verse "Shema Yisrael," through which he fulfills his obligation.
