Regarding what the Mechaber writes in seif 1, that he has fulfilled his obligation, see Berachos 15a-b. One must examine what "he was not precise" means: whether he failed to distinguish a dagesh, a silent or mobile sheva, or adjoining words, or as Eliyah Rabbah, letter 1, cites from Sefer HaChinuch in parashas Vaeschanan. But one who literally reads merely to proofread does not fulfill his obligation, as Tosafos write there on 13a, because letters that are potentially present in the act of reading are lacking. It may be answered that he does fulfill his obligation, for according to the halachah mitzvos require intent, and the Mishnah is understood literally: if he intended, etc. Alternatively, an anonymous Mishnah followed by a dispute does not establish the halachah like the anonymous Mishnah, and even one who reads in order to proofread mezuzos and the like fulfills his obligation. See what we wrote in our novellae on this matter, Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah, Hilchos Krias Shema 2:1, and Kesef Mishneh there. The Tur and Beit Yosef cite Mahari Abuhav concerning the cooling of Gehinnom as measure for measure. Sometimes the response is literally measure for measure, and sometimes it is the opposite: because he heats himself, it is cooled for him, according to the occasion and need. All is included in the principle of measure for measure.
And even initially, see Taz. There is a solution, namely to clean one's hands with a pebble or earth, as Eliyah Rabbah, letter 4, writes; see there. Likewise, regarding Krias Shema, if his hands are soiled and there is no water, he should clean them with a pebble or earth and recite it aloud. There is some difficulty here. The Mechaber and Rama write here that if he is constrained he fulfills his obligation by mental recitation, and they rule similarly regarding prayer in siman 94, seif 6. It appears that the Mechaber holds that without constraint one does not fulfill his obligation even after the fact through mental recitation, based on the Mishnah on 15a, which says that if he did not make it audible he fulfills his obligation, implying that mere thought does not. Under constraint, however, he fulfills his obligation, based on the case of a baal keri on 20b, following Ravina there, and on the Yerushalmi cited by Beit Yosef here. See Magen Avraham 185:1, who writes that under constraint one fulfills Bircas HaMazon through mental recitation; see there. Accordingly, when the constraint passes he need not repeat Krias Shema or prayer. On a Torah level mental recitation is equivalent to speech; the Sages were stringent that without constraint it does not suffice, but under constraint they retained the Torah law. Since the Mechaber rules that Shema may be recited in any language, mental recitation should be no worse, as Eliyah Rabbah writes regarding Berachos 15. Also, making it audible to one's ear is necessarily only a Rabbinic requirement even initially. Regarding Shema, however, one must intend and understand at least the first verse, as stated in siman 63, seif 4, and in Berachos 13b and 16a, "Be attentive and hear." The Rambam has a different approach. In Hilchos Krias Shema 2:1, he writes that if one did not make it audible to his ear he fulfills his obligation, implying that mere thought does not suffice. Yet in Hilchos Berachos 1:7 he rules that all blessings may be fulfilled through mental recitation even without constraint; see Kesef Mishneh there. He thus distinguishes between Krias Shema, concerning which "Shema" permits any language but not mere thought, and other mitzvos. Even for Bircas HaMazon, which is D'Oraisa, one fulfills the obligation in his heart. Accordingly, there is no proof that Krias Shema requires comprehension of the first verse, only intent to fulfill the mitzvah, which is a Torah-level requirement. Rambam's Hilchos Krias Shema chapter one does not establish whether he holds that only the first verse is D'Oraisa. See Peri Chadash in siman 67, and, with Hashem's help, it will be explained there. Peri Chadash here and in siman 94 writes that when the constraint passes one must repeat Krias Shema and prayer; see there. Yet the wording of Shulchan Aruch implies as I have written, that one has fulfilled the obligation and need not repeat it whenever he was constrained, as in the Mishnah's case of a baal keri and following Ravina. Understand this. We discussed the matter at length in our novellae, and this is not the place to elaborate. Levush here, seif 5, concludes that mental recitation is equivalent to speech, and that it is forbidden to contemplate words of Torah in a filthy place because "your camp shall be holy." According to the view that mental recitation is equivalent to speech, however, the verse "your camp shall be holy" is unnecessary for this prohibition. See Beit Yosef here.
