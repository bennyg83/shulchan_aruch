Seif 2 And likewise foods and drinks that are hung in the sukkah in order to beautify it, etc. Note: The Mechaber wrote plainly that sukkah decorations are forbidden, and did not distinguish whether they are within four handbreadths of the roofing or not. In my novellae on the sugya of spreading a sheet over it, and in my novellae to siman 629, I wrote there a proof from the Talmud that this applies specifically when it is within four handbreadths of the roofing, in a case where the sukkah is valid even though the roofing is a broad object; then the decoration becomes forbidden. But if it is separated four handbreadths from the roofing, in a manner that the sukkah would be invalid if the decoration were a broad object, then automatically the decorations are not nullified to the sukkah and are not considered roofing, and they are not forbidden in benefit. Although wall decorations are also forbidden, that is because they are adjacent to the wall and are wall decorations. But if they are not wall decorations and are only hanging from the roofing, and are four handbreadths away from the roofing, then they are permitted in benefit. See there, where I wrote at length about this. It requires examination why the decisors wrote this plainly without distinction.
Seif 2 In the gloss: It appears to me that this is because the mitzvah of sukkah is a man and his household, a man and his wife, etc. Note: See the Taz, who objected that the Rema's words contradict one another from what he wrote in siman 640; see there. In my humble opinion, they do not contradict one another at all, as I will explain with God's help. First we must introduce that this matter is compelled from the Talmud: although one cannot sleep in the sukkah, nevertheless one is obligated in the sukkah for eating and drinking. This is from what we learn in Sukkah 43: "And the sukkah itself, from where do we know?" etc., until the end of the sugya: the verse says "you shall dwell," "you shall dwell," as a verbal analogy. Tosafot there asked why it did not bring the verse "you shall dwell" in the manner that you live. They also asked afterward: with sukkah one is obligated even to sleep there, and that did not apply to the miluim, for one was not permitted to sleep there; see there. In my humble opinion, one question is answered by the other. Certainly, where one can sleep there, he is obligated day and night. But the Talmud was uncertain in a case where one cannot sleep there at night: is he obligated by day and by night, or is he obligated only by day, for then it is not the time of sleep and he is certainly obligated, whereas at night, which is the time of sleep, if he cannot sleep there perhaps he is exempt even from sukkah? In this it discusses there whether we derive from the miluim that even though one cannot sleep there he is obligated even at night, or from lulav. The Talmud derives from "you shall dwell" that even though one cannot sleep there at night, he is obligated even at night. If so, on the contrary, from there we learn that even where one cannot sleep he is obligated even at night; and where he can sleep, we learn from "you shall dwell" in the manner that you live. However, the Mordechai holds that since it is proven in several places that something in one's power, even by way of prohibition, is called in one's power, since in any event he has the ability to do it, so too here: in the miluim, it was at least in his power to sleep there, except that because of the Torah's commandment he could not sleep. This is not considered compulsion, for if he wanted to, he could do it. Therefore the same applies to sukkah: where he can sleep there, but does not want to because he wants to fulfill the mitzvah of conjugal duty, and thus because of the mitzvah of conjugal duty he refrains from sleeping there, while if he wished he could sleep there, in this case he is obligated in sukkah for eating even at night. But where he is distressed and the like, so that he cannot sleep there and this is compulsion, there is no proof from the "dwelling" of the miluim, for there it was in his power to do, whereas here it is not in his power even to sleep there. Therefore this is again learned from "you shall dwell" in the manner that you live, and if he cannot sleep there he has not fulfilled even the eating. Therefore the Rema's words do not contradict one another. On the contrary, this is the proof for why the Rema wrote this reason and rejected the first reason that had been written because of cold: the Taz's question was difficult to him, that if so he would not fulfill even eating. Therefore he gives the reason that here he is able to sleep there, and it is comparable to the miluim, and it works well. Examine carefully. This appears correct in my humble opinion to resolve the matter with God's help. See Magen Avraham, who raised a contradiction between the words of the Talmud; and see what I wrote in my novellae to siman 631, in my novellae to the sugya there, properly resolving this difficulty. What I wrote there depends on whether, when it is impossible to sleep there, one fulfills the obligation of eating or not. Examine carefully.
