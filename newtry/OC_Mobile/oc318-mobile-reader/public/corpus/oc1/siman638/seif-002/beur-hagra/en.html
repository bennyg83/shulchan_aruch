Seif 2: Even if they fell. This is why the Gemara there gives the reason that they were set aside for the mitzvah. The reason stated in the second chapter of Shabbos, that using them is degrading to the mitzvah, is insufficient. Degradation applies only while the decorations remain suspended. Were it not for that reason, they would be permitted on Chol HaMoed even while suspended. Now that they are forbidden while suspended because of degradation, the additional reason of being set aside for the mitzvah makes them forbidden even if they fall during Chol HaMoed. This is the view of Tosafos, Shabbos 22a, in the passage beginning “Sukkah,” and of Rosh in a responsum, unlike Hagahos Ashri and Rabbi Shimon of Sens, who write that if they fell during Chol HaMoed they are permitted.
Rosh writes there that although regarding the wood of a sukkah the fourth chapter of Beitzah says that it is set aside for all seven days, and that discussion concerns a sukkah that fell, as Ri explains there, proving that muktzeh applies even during Chol HaMoed, this applies specifically to the wood of the sukkah. It was set aside because it may be needed to rebuild the sukkah. Decorations are different.
And on Yom Tov, etc. Anything forbidden for eating is also forbidden to move, even where the prohibition is only Rabbinic. Thus the eighteenth chapter of Shabbos states, “Untithed produce is obvious,” etc. Shabbos 45a likewise states that according to Rabbi Shimon the only muktzeh items are oil, etc., because they were set aside for their mitzvah. This shows that they are forbidden even to move. The Mishnah there states that Rabbi Shimon permits moving all lamps except, etc., and the Gemara compares this to sukkah decorations. The Jerusalem Talmud, chapter 3, similarly states regarding a lulav on the first day of Yom Tov that once one places it upon the ground it is forbidden to move. Rabbi Avin said that this teaches that it is forbidden for benefit, and for that reason it is muktzeh.
For all eight days. This is Ramban’s view, unlike Rashi there. Even if one did not separate himself from them on the first day, the restriction nevertheless takes effect during twilight of each later day, just as when one designates seven esrogim for seven days, as stated in the fourth chapter of Sukkah.
And this applies specifically when he stipulates, etc. This follows from the statement there, “but regarding the wood of the sukkah, upon which sanctity took effect,” etc. See Rashi there. Were a stipulation effective in every case, it would work even for the wood of the sukkah.
And if he said, etc. The Tosefta, chapter 1 of Sukkah, states: If one hung nuts, etc., in the sukkah, and stipulated that he may eat from them during the festival, they are permitted. The Tosefta, chapter 3 of Yom Tov, likewise states: One may not take wood from the sukkah, even on the final Yom Tov of the festival. If he said, “Whenever I wish, I will take,” it is permitted.
And he must make, etc. Rashi explains that once twilight begins, the item becomes set aside, in accordance with the principle that because it was set aside during twilight, it remains set aside, etc.
The first. A stipulation made afterward is ineffective, because the item was already set aside during the first twilight period. This resembles the law in the fourth chapter of Sukkah that an esrog that became invalid is forbidden for eating throughout all seven days, although it became invalid on the first day and was not set aside during the second twilight period.
Some authorities, etc. See Taz.
Because some say that there is no, etc. This is Rosh’s view. According to this position, the Gemara’s suggestion, “perhaps he took it from the side,” was merely a rejection and was not retained in the conclusion. See Magen Avraham.
If he used it as s’chach, etc.; and some say, etc. This follows the discussion in Sukkah 37b. The first opinion holds that the case here is unlike the case there. There, one cannot fulfill the mitzvah except with that very item. Here, since ordinary wood could have been used, he set it aside only from ordinary use of wood and from burning it. This is the meaning of the comparison to a myrtle used for a mitzvah, etc.
