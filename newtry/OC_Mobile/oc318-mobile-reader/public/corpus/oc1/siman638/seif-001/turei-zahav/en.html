Whether the wood of the walls. We learn this in the Gemara from the verse, "the festival of Sukkot": just as the Name of Heaven takes effect on the festival offering, so it takes effect on the sukkah; just as the festival offering is for Hashem, so the sukkah is for Hashem. The Rosh wrote that this is specifically regarding the roofing, as we say in siman 630 that it is permitted to make the walls even from things that are not from the threshing-floor and winepress, because the verse "when you gather" refers specifically to the roofing. So too here with the prohibition of benefit, for that very verse, in which "when you gather" is written, is itself expounded from "festival." How can one word be divided into two matters? It is understandable at the beginning of siman 630, where we expound an extra word for walls, which is another word. He wondered about the Rambam, who prohibited even the wood of the walls. One must say that the Rambam's view is that he prohibited the walls because of muktzeh. The Rambam was troubled by Tosafot's question in the first chapter, page 9: here sukkah wood is forbidden by Torah law, while in chapter Kirah it implies the reason is muktzeh, which is rabbinic. He resolved that chapter Kirah speaks of the walls, while here speaks of the roofing. The Rosh's wonder about the Rambam is because the Rambam combined the roofing and the walls together, implying that both are Torah law, which is certainly not so. The Rosh answers Tosafot's above question in another way, as the Beit Yosef wrote. If so, it implies that even rabbinically the walls are permitted where there is no dismantling of a tent, but it fell on its own. So too the Beit Yosef's words imply in the Rosh's view, that regarding the wood of walls, where there is no element of Torah prohibition, the rabbis did not decree. But this cannot be said, for in the first chapter, page 10, we say: let us say this supports R. Chisda, for we say that if he spread sheets under the roofing as decoration it is permitted, as taught in a baraita: if he roofed it properly and adorned it with painted sheets, it is forbidden to make use of them, etc. This implies that it is permitted to spread a sheet, and the Gemara rejects: perhaps it means from the side, at the walls; but above it is forbidden to spread a sheet because it receives impurity. We thus see that even sukkah decoration next to the walls is forbidden because of muktzeh, unless he stipulated about them. How then should there be no prohibition of muktzeh in the walls themselves? Rather, it is simple that even according to the Rosh there is a prohibition of muktzeh with the walls, but here he is speaking of a Torah prohibition because of "the festival of Sukkot," etc.; in that he distinguished between roofing and walls. This is unlike the Rambam as the Rosh understood him, wanting to equate them to the roofing and make them Torah-forbidden too. About that the Rosh wondered, for there are certainly many leniencies and distinctions between a Torah prohibition and a rabbinic prohibition. In truth, however, the Rambam too prohibited the walls only because of muktzeh. There is only a distinction regarding a condition, for a condition does not help with walls even if they fell, as the Rema writes nearby. The reason is that the statement that he does not separate himself from them throughout twilight so that sanctity should not take effect on them does not apply to walls, for if he wished to dismantle them he would not be permitted to do so, unlike decorations, which can be taken on Yom Tov; therefore a condition helps there. So it appears to me. There is no view here that permits the wall wood even rabbinically; there is only a dispute between the Rosh and the Rambam regarding a Torah prohibition with the walls, according to the Rosh's explanation of the Rambam. But we rule that even the Rambam prohibited the walls only rabbinically, as appears to me.
And see siman 518, etc. In the Beit Yosef there he wrote: However, rabbinically it became forbidden because of muktzeh, just as the sages prohibited what is more than the valid measure of the sukkah, as written in siman 638. End quote. Yet here he ruled that what is beyond the measure is permitted. So too the Rashal wrote in chapter HaMevi, siman 3, that with the festival sukkah it is no less than sukkah decorations. For designation is not legally effective. This is difficult for me: we compare sukkah to the festival offering, as above, that the Name of Heaven takes effect upon it. If so, let us say here too with sukkah that designation is legally effective, as with a festival offering, for we rule regarding an offering that if he said "this is an offering," it has bodily sanctity. So too let us say with sukkah. From where do we distinguish between them? We rule that an analogy is not partial, all the more so here where the Merciful One explicitly compared them. It appears that his view is that certainly one cannot say that all laws that apply to a festival offering apply to a sukkah, for if so we would say that the sukkah too has bodily sanctity. Rather, perforce, this is what the verse says: "the festival of Sukkot you shall make for yourself seven days"; only in this matter are they equal, regarding the seven days, that the sanctity of the sukkah should be for this time like the festival offering, and one should not take from it for his benefit any item that would nullify its sanctity. In this alone they are equal, and not in another matter. Although above we say that according to the sages we expound "make a sukkah" even on the seventh day, that is regarding "you shall make for yourself"; but regarding the comparison to the festival offering, the comparison applies to all seven days. Therefore it appears simple that what it says, "one may not benefit from them for another matter," means by way of taking from it, because through this one would nullify its sanctity. But while it stands, there is no prohibition of benefit from it, such as to lean on it or place something on it, since its sanctity is still upon it. So too in siman 518 in the Shulchan Aruch. Here, however, he wrote in the name of the Ran two views about whether it is permitted to smell myrtle. Is it that he set it aside from fragrance, as with myrtle of the lulav, or perhaps myrtle of the lulav is different, for it is needed for its mitzvah and can only be set aside from the fragrance for which it stands; but myrtle in the sukkah, since ordinary wood would suffice, functions only as wood, and he set it aside only from burning, so that he not diminish it, but did not set it aside from its smell. End quote. The Rema brings this at the end of the siman. From what I wrote, the permissive view in this matter is proven, as appears to me.
