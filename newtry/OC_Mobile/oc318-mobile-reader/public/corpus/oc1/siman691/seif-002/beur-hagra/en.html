Or if he wrote. Gittin 45b.
And its law is like a sefer Torah, etc. Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot cite Rabbeinu Tam. As the Ran said in the name of Ramban, from the gezerah shavah of "writing" we learn that in the essential writing its law is like a sefer Torah. "Written" and "truth" teach even the correction of the writing, such as ruling lines, that its law is like a sefer Torah; and "written" and "book" teach that even something not of its body, such as the stitching, has the law of a sefer Torah. They were lenient in it because it is not literally its body, allowing it when sewn with three threads, etc. Therefore they said that the reader spreads it out like a letter, which is something external to its body; for this reason they said that one who reads the Megillah may stand, etc., unlike with the Torah. In the first chapter of Megillah in the Yerushalmi: What is the difference between books and Megillat Esther? Books may be written in any language, etc. This implies that for every other matter they are equal. And there in chapter 2, end of halachah 2: one verse says "book" and one verse says "letter"; how so? They were lenient only with its sewing, etc., implying that specifically with its sewing they were lenient and not with anything else.

It also requires. Megillah 18b: "Let us say this supports him," etc.; they challenged from Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar, etc. In Yerushalmi Megillah chapter 4 halachah 1: Rabbi Zeira in the name of Rav Chananel said that even one fluent in Torah like Ezra should not recite from memory and read, as written in Baruch, etc. But was it not taught: there was an incident with Rabbi Meir in Asia where there was no Megillah written in Hebrew; he wrote it from memory and read it? We do not derive from an exigent circumstance. Some say two were written: he wrote the first from memory, wrote the second from the first, stored away the first, and read from the second.
And to utter every word, etc. Menachot 30a; see Tosafot there, s.v. u-Moshe. So too wrote the Mordechai in Hilchot Ketanot siman 1057: from here there is proof that for sifrei Torah, tefillin, and mezuzot, the scribe must utter with his mouth, etc. So wrote all the poskim, and so it is ruled in Tur and Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah siman 274 seif 2. It implies that this applies even when he writes from a written text, for one cannot write a sefer Torah not from a text. What is written in siman 32 seif 31, "if he is not," etc., is different regarding tefillin. See Magen Avraham there. This is the view of Ri, who inferred from the wording of Semag, who wrote that it is forbidden for another person to dictate to the scribe unless he repeats, etc. This is not precise, for he stated it as a greater novelty: even though when another dictates to him there is no concern of error, as with Birkat Kohanim, nevertheless it is required, as we find with Moshe, that even though the Holy One, blessed be He, would say it, Moshe would repeat and say it. This is the view of all the poskim, who did not distinguish and wrote sifrei Torah, tefillin, and mezuzot, even though a sefer Torah must be written from a text.
And they make. For it is called a letter.
And it requires, etc. Bava Batra 13b; see Tosafot there, s.v. ve-oseh. Ri's text appears to be: he leaves at its beginning enough margin to surround it, and at its end enough to roll around a pole. This is also the version of the Geonim there, 14a: all books are rolled to their beginning; and there, 14b: the Temple-court scroll was rolled to its beginning. In Yerushalmi Megillah chapter 1 halachah 11: they make a pole for a scroll at its end, and for Torah on both sides; therefore he rolls a scroll to its beginning and the Torah to its middle. So too in Masechet Soferim chapter 2 and in Yadayim chapter 3, Rabbi Yehudah says that the part at the end does not contract impurity until he makes a pole for it. See Rash there and the Rosh. Even according to Rashba, who maintains the version of the books and distinguishes between attaching, etc., he agrees that for books alone one makes a pole at the end, etc.
And some say it requires tagin, and some say it does not, etc. See Ran chapter 2. The first view holds that this is something of its body, and its law is like a sefer Torah, as above. The last view holds that tagin are not of the body of the writing, as stated in Menachot, where they found the Holy One, blessed be He, tying crowns, etc.; see there. Anything not of the body does not invalidate. We do not say that it is called a book, since it was necessary to learn writing with gum, etc., from the gezerah shavah of writing-writing and not from "book." See Ran and Rashba. But in chapter 12 of Shabbat, 40, without ziyyunin they are not letters at all; see Ran and Mordechai Padua, and see what I wrote in siman 35 seif 3.
