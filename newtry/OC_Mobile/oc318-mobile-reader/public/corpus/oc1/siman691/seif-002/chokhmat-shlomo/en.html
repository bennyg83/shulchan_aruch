Seif 2. In the gloss: after the fact, one should not invalidate a Megillah because of missing and extra letters, for it is no worse than a case where the scribe omitted letters from it, where it is valid. Note: although the Rema ruled this simply, it is not agreed upon. The main proof that even extra letters are valid comes from the rule that if the scribe omitted letters, it is valid, for this view treats extra as comparable to missing. But one who examines Tosafos in the first chapter of Megillah, page 9, s.v. “granted, Torah,” where they cite the Kuntres, will see that they hold a change is worse than an omission: even where an omission is valid, a change invalidates. If so, here too, although omitted letters are valid, if he changed the text by writing extra letters, that should invalidate, for it is a change. Although Tosafos gave another answer, the matter has not left doubt. Therefore it appears that with missing letters it is valid, as the Rema wrote, but with extra letters it is invalid out of doubt; if there is no other Megillah, at any rate one should not bless over it. See my novellae there on the words of Tosafos, and examine this carefully. Regarding whether one fulfills Megillah reading from writing when alone, and the explanation of the sugya in Megillah 18 concerning “he was writing it,” see my responsum to the holy community of Krakow in my work on Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De'ah, fourth edition, siman 48. While dealing with this, I was troubled: what is the Gemara’s challenge there to Rabbi Chelbo from Rabbi Meir, who had no Megillah and wrote it? Perhaps Rabbi Meir holds like the opinion in the first chapter of Megillah that Esther was said with ruach hakodesh to be read but not to be written, and that its permission to be written is only Rabbinic. If so, it is not included in the rule that written matters may not be said by heart, and therefore it is permitted to write it not from a written text, all the more so by heart. Other Writings, however, which were given to be written by Torah law, may not be written not from a written text, just as they may not be read by heart. Although the Talmud proves in the first chapter of Megillah that Rabbi Meir agrees that Esther renders the hands tamei and holds that it was said to be written, perhaps there are two Tannaim according to Rabbi Meir, and Rabbi Yehoshua there, who holds it was not given to be written, is disagreeing within Rabbi Meir’s own view. Perhaps this is forced; see the omissions in Sefer HaChayim at the beginning, siman 49, where I asked about this.
There, seif 2 in the gloss. And to utter every word with his mouth before writing it, as with a sefer Torah. Note: see the Magen Avraham, who wondered about this. In my humble opinion, it is well understood according to Tosafos in Megillah, page 7, who wrote that its being given to be written is only Rabbinic, and therefore one who read it by heart has not fulfilled the obligation Rabbinically. From their words it is proven that even according to the view that it renders the hands tamei and derives this from “in a book,” it is still only Rabbinic. So too the Maharsha appears to have understood in Chiddushei Halachos; otherwise, what did Tosafos ask from Yoma? One could say that there it speaks according to the view that it was given to be written, namely the view that it renders the hands tamei, while the view that it was not given to be written means only that it does not render the hands tamei, though it agrees that Rabbinically it was given to be written. Therefore the Gemara asks why Chanukah is different, since it too could be written Rabbinically. Necessarily, Tosafos hold that even the opinion that it was given to be written means only Rabbinically. Although this opinion derives it from “in a book,” one can explain that at first they thought “in a book” cannot refer to the Megillah, only to Neviim. Since it is written “Have I not written for you thirds,” it implies one may not make a fourth division; where there is a prohibition, the Sages cannot permit it actively. But afterward they found that “in a book” can be interpreted about the Megillah. Even though it can also be interpreted about Neviim, since at any rate there is no Torah prohibition, the Sages again had power to enact writing it. All agree, however, that this is only Rabbinic, though it renders the hands tamei Rabbinically. It is preferable to minimize dispute. The Talmud says only that the one who says it does not render the hands tamei must hold it is only Rabbinic; but the reverse is not necessary, for the one who says it does render the hands tamei can still agree that its writing is only Rabbinic. If so, the matter is clear. For tefillin, a sefer Torah, and the like, where writing is from the Torah, writing from a written text is as though he says it with his mouth. But Megillah itself was said as words of Kabbalah and is like Torah, while its writing is only Rabbinic. Since the Sages were stringent to make it render the hands tamei like Torah, they were also stringent that it not be written except from a written text. Therefore we follow stringency in both directions: writing not from a written text is forbidden because Rabbinically it was given to be written and is considered written matter; but a written text alone is not enough, because from Torah law it was not given to be written, and where it is not from a Torah-level written text he must say it by heart, as with tefillin. They made it like Torah for stringency, not for leniency. The matter can be understood further: the dispute whether it was given to be written concerns whether it has the status of an independent book, like other books, which have a mitzvah of being written even apart from public reading. One view says it was given to be written independently, and therefore has the status of a complete book to render the hands tamei. The other view says that in its own right it was not given to be written as a book; it was given to be written only for reading from it, so that one not read by heart. Without the need for reading, it has no status of a book and does not render the hands tamei. According to us, although it renders the hands tamei, it was given independent book status only Rabbinically; from words of Kabbalah it was given to be written only for reading. “Write this as a remembrance in a book” means for the sake of reading. Thus a Megillah written for reading has words-of-Kabbalah book status, but the existing Megillah from which one writes, when not at the moment of reading, has book status only Rabbinically. Therefore relying on it alone is like writing not from a written text, and one must also recite by heart as in other cases of writing not from a proper written text. This is a correct approach with Hashem’s help. What the Talmud answers on page 7, that it was said to be read and not said to be written, means it was said to be written only for reading, not as an independent book unrelated to reading, and therefore it does not have book status to render the hands tamei. Examine this carefully.
