(Seif katan 1) It is forbidden, etc. “But temporarily, etc.” If the tefillin themselves are hanging, it is forbidden even temporarily. Taz, seif katan 1, writes that when putting on the head-tefillin, one holds them by the straps while the bayis hangs in the air until it is placed upon the head. This appears permitted because it is necessary for putting them on. Magen Avraham may also agree with Taz. Maharit writes that it is permitted to write verses upon paper and hang them at the entrance to a synagogue. His responsum, part 2, siman 3, discusses the custom on Yom Kippur of writing verses upon paper to awaken the people to repentance, such as “For on this day He shall atone for you,” or “Hear, Israel,” and hanging them above the bimah or at the entrances of the synagogue. He rules that there is no concern of degrading sacred verses through suspension. This is unlike hanging tefillin, which is degrading because tefillin were made to be worn upon the head and arm rather than hung. These verses, however, were written from the outset for suspension so that those who see them will look upward and direct their hearts to Heaven. He nevertheless raises another prohibition: one may not write isolated Scriptural passages in scroll form for a child to study, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 283. Even according to Rif, who permits this because “It is a time to act for Hashem; they have nullified Your Torah,” since it is impossible for everyone to write an entire Torah, that leniency does not necessarily apply to these displayed verses. Taz in Yoreh Deah, siman 283, seif katan 2, likewise protests against those who write verses upon walls as a favorable sign. A practical difference remains. If one writes only three words in a single line, which is permitted under the law against writing isolated passages, it may also be suspended. Likewise, one may write upon a synagogue wall texts that appear in siddurim, such as the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy or the verses beginning “When the Ark traveled.” Since they may be written in siddurim under the principle “It is a time to act,” relying upon Rif, they may likewise be written upon a wall. A wall and a siddur are alike in this respect, for both are partial written collections. This is the accepted custom.
