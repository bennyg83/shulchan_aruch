(S.k. 2) More, etc. And if so, the tekiah too must be like both of them together, etc. I am compelled to present the sugya of the Talmud briefly, together with the three views needed for the Shulchan Aruch; the other views that are not needed here I will omit. We learned in the Mishnah, folio 33b: the measure of a tekiah is like three teruos; the measure of a teruah is like three yevavos. In a baraisa it was taught: the measure of a tekiah is like a teruah; the measure of a teruah is like three shevarim. The Gemara first challenges the first clause of the Mishnah, which teaches that the measure of a tekiah is like three teruos, while the baraisa teaches that it is like one teruah. Abaye answers: the tanna of the Mishnah counts the tekios of all the units. Rashi explains that this is what it means: the measure of the three tekios, meaning those before the teruos or after the teruos, is like the three teruos; if so, the measure of one tekiah is like the measure of one teruah. But the tanna of the baraisa counts the tekiah of one unit.
Then the Gemara asks from the end of the Mishnah, which teaches that the measure of a teruah is like three yevavos, which are short sounds and are the teruah, whereas the baraisa teaches that it is like three shevarim, which are longer. Abaye said: in this they certainly disagree. The tanna of the Mishnah holds that the yevava which Onkelos translates for teruah means wailing, short sounds; and the tanna of the baraisa holds that yevava means groaning, long sounds. Rashi explains on the Mishnah, 'the measure of a teruah is like three yevavos,' meaning three mere sounds of any length.
And since they had a tradition that the measure of a tekiah is like a teruah, that means the measure of three minimal forces. According to this, Rashi wrote that although shevarim are longer than teruos, meaning yevavos, nevertheless one should not extend one shever like three minimal forces, for if so it leaves the category of shever and becomes a tekiah, since this is the measure of a tekiah. But Riva and Rivam, Tosafos and the Rosh wrote, hold that the measure of one yevava is like three minimal forces.
If so, three yevavos are the measure of nine minimal forces. Therefore the measure of one shever is the measure of three minimal forces.
For the measure of three shevarim must be extended at least the measure of a teruah, which is three yevavos. Since each yevava is three forces, three yevavos are nine tromitin, and so too three shevarim must be.
If so, each shever must be the measure of three forces. According to Rashi, one must extend the tekiah of tashrat the measure of shevarim and teruah, because we do it out of uncertainty, perhaps the teruah of the verse is both of them together, and they had a tradition that the measure of a tekiah is like the measure of a teruah. If so, one must extend the tekiah the measure of both of them. However, in any event, one need not extend the tekiah more than nine forces, because according to Rashi a teruah is three forces, and one shever is necessarily not as long as three forces, as above. If you will say that the length of one shever is like two forces, then three shevarim are six forces, and three yevavos of the teruah are three forces, which together are nine forces; one need only extend the tekiah of tashrat the measure of nine forces. But according to Riva and Rivam, where the measure of a teruah is nine forces and three shevarim are likewise nine forces, one must extend the tekiah of tashrat the measure of eighteen forces. The Raavad is a third view. He holds like Rashi, that the measure of three yevavos is three minimal forces, but he holds that after Abaye concludes at the end that in this they certainly disagree, namely that the tanna of the Mishnah holds teruah means wailing and the baraisa holds groaning, he retracted from the first answer and holds that the tanna of the Mishnah also counts the tekiah of one unit. As for the Mishnah teaching that the measure of a tekiah is like three teruos, while the baraisa teaches that it is like one teruah, each follows its own view. The Raavad holds that there was no tradition that the measure of a tekiah is like a teruah, but rather that the measure of a tekiah is nine forces. If so, the tanna of the Mishnah, who holds that the measure of a teruah is like three yevavos, meaning three forces, teaches that since a tekiah must be nine forces, it is like three teruos, which are nine forces. But the tanna of the baraisa holds that a teruah is three shevarim, each shever being three forces, unlike Rashi, and three shevarim are nine forces; therefore he said that the measure of a tekiah is like one teruah, meaning nine forces. If so, regarding the measure of a tekiah the Mishnah does not disagree with the baraisa, and according to both of them the measure of a tekiah is nine forces. According to this, in tashrat as well one need not extend the tekiah more than nine forces. Correspondingly, in tarat too one must extend the tekiah the measure of nine forces. The first opinion brought in the Shulchan Aruch is Rashi's view. The view of the 'some say' is Riva and Rivam, and the view of the Raavad is not brought in the Shulchan Aruch.
If so, even if he extended somewhat in the shevarim, it is nothing so long as he did not extend to the measure of eighteen forces, for it does not become a tekiah, since in tashrat the measure of a tekiah is eighteen forces. This is the view of Hagahos Ashri that the Rema brought. But the view of the Beis Yosef, which is the view of Tosafos and the Rosh, is that even in tashrat one should not extend more than the measure of the tekiah of tarat. Although regarding its own set it is not like a tekiah, nevertheless it leaves the category of shever; so wrote the Beis Yosef. The Bach wrote the reason: Hagahos Ashri and the Rema hold like the Rambam and Semag, that what we do - tashrat, tashat, and tarat - is because of a doubt, for this is what Rabbi Avahu instituted, as we say there. We say that Rabbi Avahu's reason was that he was uncertain, meaning a genuine uncertainty. Therefore, in the tashrat set, one does not fulfill at all if he made tarat, and the name tekiah is not upon it unless he extended it like eighteen forces. Therefore it is permitted to extend in the shevarim up to eighteen forces, as Magen Avraham wrote. But the view of Tosafos and the Rosh is like Rav Hai Gaon, that the Talmud's statement that he was uncertain is not precise. Rather, in truth, whether wailing, groaning, or both groaning and wailing are done, one fulfills the obligation of teruah, for all are called teruah. But in the days of Rabbi Avahu there were differing customs among Yisrael: some made the teruah as wailing, some as groaning, and some as both groaning and wailing. To ordinary people this looked like two Torahs; see Ramban in Milchamos. Therefore Rabbi Avahu gathered all the customs together, and through this there would be one Torah for all Yisrael, with no difference among them.
According to this, even in the tashrat set, one fulfills with the tekiah of tarat, and the measure of three forces suffices for a tekiah. Therefore, even in the tashrat set, one should not extend a shever as long as three forces, for it has left the category of shever and become a tekiah, since even when they blow tashrat, he holds that if one blew tarat, with a short tekiah, he fulfilled.
The Bach wrote: nevertheless, according to the Raavad, etc., even according to Hagahos Ashri above, one should not extend a shever as long as nine tromitin.
Shevarim, etc. Up to nine forces, but not including nine, according to the Raavad.
He fulfilled after the fact with the teruah of tarat, for we rely on Rashi. And against the Rav, the Tur, and the Beis Yosef. So too he ruled here in the Shulchan Aruch. The custom is not to add more than three shevarim; Maharil. I saw in Perishah that he brought the words of Maharil as follows: Maharil Segal wondered why people are not also accustomed to make more than three shevarim, just as they do with teruah; examine this. The Perishah wrote: In my humble opinion, to give some reason for the custom of Ashkenaz, they hold like the poskim who require making shevarim and teruah in one breath, as written in seif 4. Although the Rema wrote that this is not the custom, nevertheless the Taz wrote that one should make them close and adjacent to one another. Therefore they shorten the shevarim and do not make more than the measure, because of concern that they will not be able to make the teruah after it in that same breath. This is unlike teruah, which comes last, where one can extend it as much as he wants. Since there is a reason to be lenient by shortening the shevarim in the order tashrat, they instituted to shorten all shevarim. This is easy to understand, end quote of the Perishah. See Bach, who wrote another reason.
Three shevarim are required, etc. Bach, Minhagim, etc.; 'it also seems to me, what is written in Minhagim,' etc., another explanation. That is to say, the Bach wrote that one should not combine the long sound according to Minhagim, for the Bach had the text before him in Minhagim: 'and he should make three shevarim apart from the interruption.' According to this, the Bach explained that it comes to teach that he should make three shevarim besides the long sound, which is called an interruption, with which the sound is interrupted at the end. On this Magen Avraham first wrote, 'and this is not the custom,' and therefore one must say that we do not hold like Minhagim in this. Magen Avraham then added afterward, 'it also seems to me, what is written,' etc. In truth, we do not disagree with Minhagim, for Minhagim itself did not speak about this long sound at all, and perhaps Minhagim itself holds that a long sound joins with the three shevarim, and only two shevarim are needed besides the long sound. He explained the words of Minhagim as the Bach initially understood them, and emended Minhagim so that it should read: 'and he should make three shevarim apart from the interruption.' This is the language of the Bach: He taught us two things. One, that ab initio he should make three shevarim alone and no more, as Maharil wrote. The second, that he should pause between the three shevarim and the teruah after it; even according to the Rosh, who requires shevarim-teruah in one breath, nevertheless one must pause between them. So ruled Maharai, etc., for if not, it is not called one breath; rather, he should pause slightly, only not take a breath in between, end quote of the Bach. According to this, Minhagim did not speak at all about the law of the long sound.
