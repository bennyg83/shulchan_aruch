S.k. 5, and some say, etc. 'And if he blew,' etc., everyone agrees that it counted, etc., for they are one mitzvah. That is specifically where he blew between tashrat and tashat and extended it like two blasts; there is an opinion that even for one it does not count. Since he intended to divide the tekiah, half for the end of tashrat and half for the beginning of tashat, and one of the two orders is not true, either tashrat or tashat, for we make both only because of uncertainty, it follows that half of the tekiah, according to his intention, counts toward an order that is not true, either the beginning or the end. That part which joins the order that is not true is like blowing merely as an occupation, not for the sake of a mitzvah. If so, the second part of the tekiah that joins the true order lacks either an end or a beginning, since the beginning or the end is like one merely occupied. Therefore, according to these 'some say,' it does not count even for one. This is unlike between one tashrat and another: although his thought to divide the tekiah into two was not fulfilled, for then one would have no beginning and one would have no end, nevertheless it counts as one tekiah, because in any event, both the beginning and the end, even according to his intention, are for a mitzvah and not like one merely occupied, for both the first tashrat and the second are true.
And in the Ran it implies, etc. For the sake of a mitzvah. He disagrees in this with what the Levush wrote at the beginning.
For according to the one who says that a tekiah as one merely occupied is an interruption and he must go back, etc. This implies that according to the one who says that one merely occupied is not an interruption, but only where he blows for the sake of a mitzvah, as Magen Avraham wrote in s.k. 10, here too it is not an interruption. On this he wrote that here, according to everyone, it is an interruption, because the tekiah here is not called merely occupied, but rather blowing for the sake of a mitzvah, which is an interruption for us even according to the view of Magen Avraham in s.k. 10. His proof is from the Ran, who challenged the opinion that if one extended it like two blasts, it does not count even for one: why should that be, since even according to the view that mitzvos require intention, here his intention is for a mitzvah, and it is better than one who blows for song, etc., end quote. Thus he called it intention for a mitzvah. There is also some proof from what the Ran wrote to reconcile the Yerushalmi according to the view that in any event it counts for one tekiah; see there, though that proof can be rejected.
He lost nothing. And what he wrote, etc.; this is how the text should read. That is to say, now he comes to disagree with the second law that the Levush wrote: that if one blew one tekiah between one tashrat and the second, intending that it count for two, everyone agrees that it at least counts for one. On this Magen Avraham wrote that he did not look into the Ran, etc., where it is proven that this is not so.
That it refers to the Mishnah, meaning that the dispute about whether he extended, etc. Whether it counts at least for one or not, their dispute is in the explanation of the Mishnah, which teaches: if he extended the final tekiah like two, he has only one. Some say that according to the explanation of the Mishnah, in any event that tekiah counts for him as one. Others say that it does not count even for one, and what the Mishnah said, 'he has only one,' means the first tekiah before the teruah. Thus those 'some say' hold to explain the Mishnah this way, that even for one it does not count.
After all, in the time of the Mishnah they had not yet become uncertain about the meaning of teruah as Rabbi Avahu later became uncertain, as above. It was obvious to the Mishnah that teruah meant wailing, for it teaches that the measure of a teruah is like three yevavos. They would blow only tarat three times, and in any event, what the Mishnah teaches, 'he extended the second like two,' means between one tarat and another tarat. Even so, the Mishnah said that he has only one. These 'some say' hold that this means the tekiah before the teruah, but the last one, which he extended like two, does not count even for one. If so, the same applies between one tashrat and another tashrat, for what difference is there? This is unlike the Levush.
