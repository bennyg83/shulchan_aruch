(There, seif 6) And some say that it does not count for him even for one. Note: This is difficult for me, for the words of the Shulchan Aruch contradict one another. Above, in siman 587, seif 3, he brought the first opinion, which holds that he has not fulfilled even if there is the measure of a required tekiah, and he brought the second opinion, that he fulfilled, in the name of 'some say.' This implies that he holds the primary view is like the first opinion, according to the rule the later authorities gave us. Here he brings the reverse: the first opinion, anonymously, that he initially fulfilled for one, and the second opinion, that it does not count even for one, in the name of 'some say,' implying that he holds the primary view is like the first opinion. The matters contradict one another, for it seems that their law is equal. According to the reason of the 'some say' there, who hold that the end of a tekiah without the beginning of a tekiah does not help, the same should apply here, that he should not fulfill even one, since it is not the beginning of a tekiah but only the end of a tekiah. The same reasoning applies: its beginning is not its end, and its end is not its beginning. It is true that in Beis Yosef, in the name of the Yerushalmi, the source of the law implies a distinction: where one is merely occupied, the beginning of the tekiah was not for the sake of a mitzvah, whereas here the beginning of the tekiah was also for the sake of a mitzvah. But this does not satisfy me. Granted, the Yerushalmi follows the law of the Gemara, when they would blow only one order and there was no uncertainty, as Magen Avraham wrote in his objection to the Levush. Therefore, since either way it is a definite mitzvah and not a doubtful one, it does not have the law of one merely occupied, and he properly distinguishes between this and one merely occupied. But for us, where there is uncertainty whether the correct order is tashrat or tarat, if one blows one tekiah for tashrat or tashat, part of it is not for the sake of a mitzvah, either this part or that part, and therefore it is only like one merely occupied. If so, this is exactly the first law of siman 587, that its beginning is not its end and its end is not its beginning. If so, from where did the Shulchan Aruch derive a distinction in this? I later saw that in the book Yeshuas Yaakov on this siman he felt this somewhat, but did not explain all that was necessary, as I wrote, and with what I wrote his words there are rejected; see there. What seems to me as an answer is as follows. One can prove from here a new law: even those who hold above in siman 587 that it does not count for him even for one say this only if he heard the beginning of the tekiah not in obligation or not according to the law; then, even though the end is according to the law, it does not help. But in the reverse case, if he heard the beginning of the tekiah in obligation and its end not in obligation, such as near nightfall, or where he was initially outside the pit and afterward entered the pit, since the beginning was proper and had the measure of a tekiah, we do not care that its end was not in obligation, and in this case he fulfills according to everyone without doubt. There is a great distinction between a beginning that was improper and a beginning that was proper. According to this, it works well. Above, in siman 587, the Mechaber saw that since there are two opinions and the person has a presumption of obligation, being obligated to blow, and there is doubt whether he fulfilled with this, we leave him in his presumption of obligation; therefore he ruled that he did not fulfill. But here it is the reverse. Either way, if he did this within tashrat itself, the reason of the Beis Yosef applies, because all of it is a mitzvah. And if he did this between tashrat and tashat, there is a double uncertainty in the other direction: perhaps the halacha follows those who hold that the end of a tekiah without the beginning of a tekiah is effective; and if you will say that the end of a tekiah without the beginning is not effective, perhaps tashrat is the primary order, in which case he fulfilled with the beginning of the tekiah without its end, and this works according to everyone. If so, it is a double uncertainty: perhaps the first order is primary and he fulfilled with the beginning without the end; and if you will say the second order is primary, perhaps he fulfills with the end of a tekiah without the beginning. Therefore in this case the Shulchan Aruch decided like the second opinion, that he fulfilled with this, and it works well and correctly with Hashem's help. A double uncertainty is stronger than a presumption, as is known; analyze it well. It also seemed to me to distinguish between one who hears and one who blows. Even according to those who hold that the end of a tekiah without the beginning does not help, one can say this is regarding a listener, since he performs no act; therefore it does not help unless he hears from beginning to end for the sake of the mitzvah. But one who blows for himself, since he performs an act, one may say they agree that the end helps without the beginning. In any event, one may say the Mechaber decided this way as halacha. Therefore in siman 587, where he discusses a listener, he decided that he did not fulfill; but here, where he speaks explicitly of one who blows, he decided that he fulfilled with the end without the beginning; analyze this well. Regarding what the Pri Chadash asked in his book Yom Teruah: the Gemara asks, when did he misuse sacred property? After he blew? When he blew, he blew in prohibition. Let us say that there was the measure of a tekiah afterward, according to those who hold that one fulfills with the end without the beginning. See what I wrote about this in my novellae in several places. Now it seems as follows: the matters are not comparable. One can say that this resembles what we hold regarding an esrog: a deficient esrog is valid on the other days, but nevertheless, if it was deficient from being eaten by mice or was speckled, even if he cut that part off, it is invalid because it came from something repulsive; see siman 649. So one may say here. The reason a mitzvah that comes through a transgression is invalid is because it is repulsive before Heaven. When the Talmud asks, 'when he blew, he blew in prohibition,' its intent is that it is then a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, as written in Turei Even. If so, it works well. Certainly in general, such as where he heard not at the time of obligation or where he was merely occupied, one can say that he fulfilled with the end without the beginning, for remove the beginning of the tekiah from here and treat it as nonexistent. But in this case, where it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression and invalid because of repulsiveness, it does not help to say: remove the beginning from here and treat it as nonexistent, just as there, although a deficient esrog is valid, nevertheless where the deficiency comes from something repulsive, it does not help. The same applies here. Especially here one can say even more than the Magen Avraham's point, for in truth this was an olah. If so, if there was no beginning of a tekiah here, it would not leave sacred status for mundane status except through the end of the tekiah, and then he would not fulfill with it, since he blew in prohibition. It is impossible for us to say that he fulfills with it except because there was a beginning of the tekiah here and it already left sacred status through it. If so, necessarily one must include the beginning here as well, and one cannot say it is as nonexistent. In this case, the end certainly does not help without the beginning, especially since it comes from something repulsive. But where he was merely occupied or not in obligation, where there is no need to join the beginning of the tekiah to the end, we say that the beginning is as nonexistent, as though it never was, and he fulfills with the end without the beginning. This is clear in reasoning, with Hashem's help; analyze it well. While engaged in this, I saw in the Mishnah in chapter 3 of Rosh Hashanah: 'And likewise, one who was passing behind a synagogue and heard the sound of a shofar or the sound of the megillah,' etc. I wondered about the language 'and likewise': what connection does this have to the previous case, where it first taught about one who blows into a pit? I then looked at Tosafos there, who felt this and were pressed. In my opinion it seemed at first glance that from here there is proof for those who hold that if one heard the end of a tekiah without the beginning, he fulfilled. One can say that this is what the 'and likewise' refers to: just as with one who blows into a pit, if he heard the sound of the shofar, even though the beginning of the tekiah was not so, so too one who was passing behind a synagogue and heard the sound of a shofar is also such a case; if he directed his heart, even though the shaliach tzibbur had already been blowing before this person began to direct his attention, he has fulfilled. The Mishnah equated them because in both, the end of the tekiah without the beginning is enough. This is unlike where one blows in such a case, where he would not fulfill, as above. Although we hold that mitzvos were not given for benefit, the practical difference is in rabbinic shofar blasts according to the Baal HaMaor, who holds they were given for benefit; in such a case he would not fulfill with an olah, and the end of the tekiah without the beginning would not help. Therefore it teaches us here that one can say: remove the beginning of the tekiah from here, and the end of the tekiah without the beginning helps. This can be said. But according to those who hold that the end of a tekiah without the beginning does not help, one may explain the intent as follows. If we say that we hold like the opinion that mitzvos do not require intention, and according to what I wrote in my work Sefer HaChaim, that one who hears from another requires intention according to everyone, one can say this: since the poskim wrote that this distinction of the Mishnah, whether he heard the sound of a shofar or an echo, applies only to one who hears, but the blower certainly fulfills, then it properly says 'and likewise.' For also with one who passes behind a synagogue there is this distinction between one who blows and one who hears: the blower fulfills even if he did not intend, but regarding the listener there is a distinction whether he directed his heart or not. But if we do not say so, especially according to the opinion that mitzvos require intention, and according to the Gemara's challenge to this Mishnah according to the opinion that intention is not required, where it did not accept the distinction between one who performs an act and one who does not, for otherwise it would not have challenged anything, then one may say that what it says 'and likewise' follows what the Yerushalmi, brought in Magen Avraham, wrote: these words apply when he was passing, but if he was standing, the presumption is that he intended. One can say the same applies to one who blows. Therefore it says 'and likewise,' for in this too there is a distinction between listener and blower: with a listener we require it to be known whether he directed his heart, and if he does not know clearly that he intended, he has not fulfilled; but the actual blower, even if he does not know whether he directed his heart, has nevertheless fulfilled. This works well; analyze it carefully.
