(1) One may not launder on the moed. Even for the need of the moed; and the reason is so that one will launder before Yom Tov for the need of Yom Tov, and not enter the moed while unkempt.
(2) And these may launder: one who comes from an overseas land, etc. See above in siman 531, se'ifim 3 and 4; and all the matters explained there in the text and in the Mishnah Berurah regarding haircutting also apply in our subject regarding laundry.
(3) And one under niddui whom they released, etc. For one under niddui is forbidden in laundry.
(4) And hand towels. The reason is that their nature is to become dirty immediately; and if so, even if one had laundered them before Yom Tov, they would become dirty again. And therefore even if he has many towels, it is permitted to launder them, for he needs a different towel for every day. However, according to what is the practice now, that they change them only from Shabbos to Shabbos, therefore even if he has only two, one should not launder them on Chol HaMoed, for the laundering from before Yom Tov suffices for them. Chayei Adam wrote that the same law applies to a handkerchief for the nose: its law is like hand towels.
(5) The books. That is, the cloths that they place upon them when they cut hair; and some explain [it as] books literally, meaning sacred books; and the reason in all of this is like hand towels mentioned above.
(6) The sponge. That is, what they dry themselves with when they leave the bathhouse. And Beis Yosef wrote in the name of Kol Bo that nowadays they adopted a stringency in this, and so it is proper to do; and see Eliyah Rabbah.
(7) And garments of small children. For it is the way of small children to become dirty with mud and feces constantly.
(8) And a woman who has a stain that was found on the moed. And the same applies when she became a niddah on Yom Tov and needs to wear white garments on the moed.
(9) But one shirt. For one shirt becomes dirty even if he laundered it before Yom Tov. And there are among the poskim who hold that all this is specifically in the time of the Talmud, when it was recognizable when he removed his shirt to launder it that he has only one shirt (for initially he would gird his belt over the shirt, and when he removes it he girds his belt over his outer garment); but according to our clothing custom, where we have no belt over our shirt, one should not be lenient in this. And nevertheless it appears that if the shirt was of linen, one should not be stringent in this.
(10) And in public, by the river. See Magen Avraham, whose view is to be stringent not to launder them in public, for not everyone knows that he was in an overseas land or in captivity, and the like; except for a hand towel and the like, other things that are permitted for every person: one may permit [those] in public. But several later authorities agree with the view of Rama below, that since it is laundered well by the river, and it is for the need of the moed, the Sages were not stringent regarding all those listed here.
(11) The smallest children. That is to say, to exclude ordinary small children: one should permit laundry of only one shirt, as above.
