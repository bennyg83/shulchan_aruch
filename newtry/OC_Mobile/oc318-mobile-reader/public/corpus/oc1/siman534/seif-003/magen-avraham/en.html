One may not bring. In Pesachim, Rashi explained the reason as because of the toil; and Beis Yosef explained the reason as that they will think he gave it to be repaired on the moed, and they will say that the craftsman repaired it on the moed; end quote. And if so, [this applies] even from house to house in that same courtyard, and even from a non-Jewish craftsman, for they will think that the homeowner commanded him to repair it on the moed (Bach, siman 535).
He gives him his wages. The same applies that even if he has something to eat, it is permitted to give him his wages, for what prohibition is there in this? Rather, this is what he means: even though he has nothing to eat, so that one must give him his wages, and one might have thought that it is permitted to take them from there so that he will not demand of him a second time; it teaches us that one gives him his wages and leaves it with him; and if he does not trust him, he leaves them, etc. But if he has something to eat, one should not give him his wages, and he is not permitted to clear them to a house adjacent to him. And at first glance this requires further study: if he has nothing to eat, why should he not bring them in public? For it is permitted to give him [work] to do melacha, as is written at the end of siman 542. One can say that nevertheless it is forbidden, for the observer will not know that he has nothing to eat; and according to this, all the more so it is forbidden for him to do the melacha in public.
