(15) One brings, etc. But one may not take [items] to the house of the craftsman, even for the need of the moed [Bach and Eliyah Rabbah].
(16) One may not bring. Whether the craftsman finished it on the moed or beforehand; and the reason is because of toil. And some say because they will think he gave it to be repaired on the moed, and they will say that the craftsman repaired it on the moed; and according to this, even from house to house in that same courtyard, where there is no toil, and even from a non-Jewish craftsman, it is forbidden to bring, for they will think that the homeowner commanded him to repair it on the moed. Peri Megadim wrote that for a matter of a mitzvah, even though it is not a need of the moed, one may say that from a non-Jewish craftsman it is permitted to bring.
(17) He gives him his wages. The same applies that even if he has something to eat, it is also permitted to give him his wages. Rather, this is what he means: even though he has nothing to eat, so that one must give him his wages, and one might have thought that it is permitted to take them to himself so that he will not demand of him a second time; it teaches us that one gives him his wages and leaves them with him. Or alternatively, because he wishes to finish [the matter]; and if he does not trust him, he leaves it in a house adjacent to him. And if he has something to eat, one should not give him his wages, and he is not permitted to clear them to a house adjacent to him.
(18) But in private. At first glance this requires further study: if he has nothing to eat, why should he not bring them in public? For it is permitted to give him [work] to do melacha, as is written at the end of siman 542. One can say that nevertheless it is forbidden, for the observer will not know that he has nothing to eat; and according to this, all the more so it is forbidden for him to do the melacha in public.
