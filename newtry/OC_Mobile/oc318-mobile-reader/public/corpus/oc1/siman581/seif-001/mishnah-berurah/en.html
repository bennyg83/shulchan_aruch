The custom is to rise before dawn. At the end of the night the Holy One, blessed is He, moves through this world, and it is an auspicious time. The custom begins from Rosh Chodesh Elul because Moshe then ascended Mount Sinai to receive the second Tablets, and a shofar was sounded throughout the camp announcing that Moshe had ascended the mountain, so that the people would not again be led astray after idolatry. It was therefore an auspicious time. There is also an allusion in the verse, “I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine.” The initial letters spell Elul, and the numerical value of the final letters is forty, corresponding to the forty days from Rosh Chodesh Elul until Yom Kippur. During these forty days teshuvah is accepted, so that one's heart draws near to his Beloved through teshuvah, and his Beloved draws near to him to accept his teshuvah with love. Another allusion is found in the verse, “And Hashem your God will circumcise your heart and the heart of your offspring,” whose initial letters spell Elul.
(1) From Elul onward. On Rosh Chodesh itself, however, Selichos and supplications are not recited. Likewise, those who recite Tehillim throughout Elul together with supplications and a Yehi Ratzon should not begin on Rosh Chodesh, as stated by Peri Megadim.
(2) Until Yom Kippur. In our lands the custom is to recite the chapter of Tehillim “L'David Hashem Ori,” morning and evening, every day from Rosh Chodesh Elul until Yom Kippur, after the conclusion of the prayer, followed by Kaddish. Our custom is to continue through Shemini Atzeres, inclusive. On a day with Musaf, it is recited after Shacharis before Ein Kamocha, and in the evening after Minchah. In places where it is recited after the conclusion of the prayer on Rosh Chodesh, the chapter of Tehillim Barchi Nafshi should be said first. Likewise, in places where it is recited after Shacharis and the Shir Shel Yom is also recited after Shacharis, the Shir Shel Yom should come first, as stated by the acharonim.
(3) From Rosh Chodesh onward, etc. Some begin on the first day of Rosh Chodesh, and some begin on the second day, as stated in Derech HaChaim. A shofar should be sounded every morning after the prayer. The custom is also to recite ten chapters of Tehillim publicly on every weekday from Rosh Chodesh Elul onward. In the Yehi Ratzon recited before them, the words “which contain seventy years” should be omitted. Since a complete book of Tehillim is not being recited, the prayer afterward should say, “In the merit of the chapters of Tehillim that we have recited before You,” etc. Kaddish is recited after the Yehi Ratzon. If ten people were not present when the Tehillim were recited and they wait for another person to complete the minyan, they should leave one chapter unrecited, recite it after the minyan is complete, and then recite Kaddish. During the days of teshuvah between Rosh Hashana and Yom Kippur, more than ten chapters are recited each day so that Tehillim will be completed for the third time before Yom Kippur, as stated by Mateh Ephraim.
(4) To recite Selichos. An individual may recite those passages that do not contain the Thirteen Attributes. In a Selichah that mentions the Thirteen Attributes, he should omit those words. Likewise, requests written in Aramaic, such as “Machei u'Masei,” etc., and “Maran di biShmaya,” etc., should not be recited unless ten people are present, as stated by Eliyah Rabbah. When beginning Selichos, one recites Elokeinu v'Elokei Avoseinu, except before a Selichah that begins with one of the names of the Holy One, blessed is He. Abudraham writes that in the phrase “Vayikra v'shem Hashem,” one should pause slightly between “v'shem” and “Hashem.” If no minyan is present when Ashrei is recited, they should begin Selichos, and when a minyan arrives they should recite Kaddish in the middle of Selichos. Eliyah Rabbah writes that they should first recite three verses once a minyan is present. If a minyan was present at the beginning of Selichos and some people later left, it appears that Kaddish may nevertheless be recited after Selichos, similar to the law explained in siman 55, as stated by Peri Megadim.
(5) On the first day before Rosh Hashana. Meaning, on the Sunday that precedes Rosh Hashana.
(6) From Sunday of the preceding week. Many people have the custom to fast ten days together with Yom Kippur. Four days between Rosh Hashana and Yom Kippur are always unavailable for fasting, namely the two days of Rosh Hashana, Shabbos Shuvah, and erev Yom Kippur. They must therefore make up four days before Rosh Hashana. In order to establish a fixed starting day, the custom was always set for Sunday. Another reason for establishing four days is that offerings require examination for blemishes during the four days before they are brought. Regarding all the offerings in Parashas Pinchas, the Torah says, “You shall offer an olah,” while regarding Rosh Hashana it says, “You shall make an olah.” This teaches that on Rosh Hashana a person should regard himself as though he were offering himself. Four days were therefore established so that he may inspect all the blemishes of his sins and repent for them. The shaliach tzibbur wraps himself in a tallis when the Thirteen Attributes are recited, even before daylight. He does not recite a blessing over it, although it is a garment designated for daytime use, because he wears it at night and one opinion exempts such a garment from tzitzis at that time, as explained above in siman 18, according to the Levush. The Taz objects that there is no reason for him to enter a situation of doubt. He should therefore not use his own tallis or a communal tallis, which is likewise treated as his own, but should borrow another person's tallis. All opinions agree that he is exempt from tzitzis in that case, provided that he intends to wear it only for honor and not to acquire it.
(7) And a mourner is forbidden, etc. During the twelve months after the death of a father or mother, or during the thirty days after the death of another relative, a mourner may not lead the prayers on Rosh Hashana or Yom Kippur, since those days are treated like festivals in all respects. During the days of Selichos, however, even on erev Rosh Hashana and during the Ten Days of Teshuvah, he may lead. When no other shaliach tzibbur is available, he may lead even on Rosh Hashana and Yom Kippur. Likewise, on days when Tachanun is not recited, he may lead Shacharis if another person will recite Hallel, but he should not lead Musaf. Chayei Adam writes that it appears the Gra did not permit a mourner to lead even Shacharis on Rosh Chodesh. When no one else is available, however, all agree that he may lead even Musaf.
(8) On erev Rosh Hashana. The same applies on erev Yom Kippur in a place where many Selichos are recited.
(9) And they should be careful. The custom was for community trustees and leaders who understand the suffering of the generation to lead the prayers. This applies only when they are trustworthy, protect the generation, and are acceptable to the people. Through our many sins, in this generation, etc. To repair this breach, it is proper for the regularly appointed shaliach tzibbur to lead.
(10) The most suitable person. Anyone who helps or supports an unsuitable shaliach tzibbur is considered as though he steals goodness from the congregation, and he will ultimately be held accountable, as stated in Sefer Chassidim. One who knows that he is not thoroughly proficient and cannot guard himself even from an inadvertent error should not place himself in the service of Hashem. If he is not fit, suitable, and proficient, punishment is not delayed as it may be for other sins, but is exacted immediately, as stated by Eliyah Rabbah.
(11) In Torah and in deeds. It is proper that the shaliach tzibbur and the shofar blower be complete penitents. It is also proper that they study the intentions associated with the prayers and the shofar blasts, as stated in the Zohar. The custom is that one who began to lead the prayers or sound the shofar, even once, should not have the mitzvah given to another person. If he becomes ill, the congregation may appoint someone else, but when he recovers the mitzvah returns to the first person. One who committed sexual immorality is not fit to serve as shaliach tzibbur on Rosh Hashana and Yom Kippur unless he has repented. If he sees that the prayer assignment is causing a dispute, he should not lead, even though an unsuitable person will lead instead.
(12) That he be thirty years old. At that age a Levi became fit for the Temple service, and prayer corresponds to the service. At that age his heart is also more broken and contrite.
(13) That he be married. This resembles the Kohen Gadol, for whom another wife was prepared, and having a wife helps guard him from sin. It is obvious that if two candidates are available, one a Torah scholar who fears sin but lacks these particular qualifications, and the other an ordinary person who is married and over thirty years old, the Torah scholar takes precedence.
(14) The entire day. This means Shacharis and Minchah, because one who begins a mitzvah is told to complete it. Some say that it includes the preceding Maariv as well, but the Maariv following that day does not belong to him. Magen Avraham writes that for this reason he takes precedence over a mourner, a mohel, and one observing a yahrzeit, since appointment to lead the prayer depends on the congregation's wishes. Eliyah Rabbah questions this and holds that one observing a yahrzeit should lead if he is fasting, and that from Lamnatzeach onward even a mourner should be permitted to lead. His words are cited by Peri Megadim. See references 17, 32, 40, 60, 100, 130.
