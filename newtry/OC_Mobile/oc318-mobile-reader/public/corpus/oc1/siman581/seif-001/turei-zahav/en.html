In the Tur he brought the Midrash: "Is it the first?" One can ask: it could properly mean the first of the festival, as in "on the first day, a rest day." If the same difficulty applies there too, it is not resolved by the answer given here. The Beit Yosef brought in the name of Mahari De Leon that it should have said "on the fifteenth of the month," since it is a time-bound mitzvah. To my humble mind there is no difficulty, for wherever the Torah says "first" it means there is a second afterward or another count, as Rashi wrote in Parashat Bereishit, that according to the order of the section it should have said "first." Likewise, "on the first day, a rest day" is proper, since afterward it writes "and on the eighth day, a rest day"; and in Parashat Bo, "on the first day a holy convocation and on the seventh," etc.; and "on the first, on the fourteenth," until "the twenty-first day," etc. But here, in the verse "you shall take for yourselves on the first day," there is no connection to any subsequent day in this matter, for biblically the obligation applies only that day. Therefore it had to mean first in the accounting of sins. The mitzvah of lulav is connected to the accounting of sins, as stated in Midrash Rabbah, Parashat Emor, that if one stole a lulav and blesses over it, his advocate becomes his accuser. Therefore it properly says "first in the accounting of sins" next to the word "for yourselves," which we expound as excluding stolen property. Thus "first" is well settled, for it has a second after it in this matter. Further there: on Motzaei Yom Kippur they are occupied with the mitzvot of sukkah and lulav and do not commit sins; therefore it calls the first day of Yom Tov the first in the accounting of sins. This is very difficult to me. First, why mention everything about the pardons before Yom Kippur? It could have answered briefly that since on Yom Kippur all sins were forgiven, the fifteenth is first in the accounting of sins. Second, since they do not commit sins, what would the Holy One, blessed be He, have to count? Even on the first day no accounting applies if they do not commit sins. In the Midrash itself the phrase "and do not commit sins" is not actually present; one may therefore say that it means even if they commit sins, they are not included in the accounting because they are occupied with sukkah, etc. But according to this it is difficult why preparation for the mitzvah of sukkah should be superior to the day itself when the mitzvah is fulfilled, and the difficulty on the Tur is stronger. It appears to me to answer after another precision: in what it says that during the ten days the average people fast and He waives two thirds for them, why say two thirds? He waives only one third, since the first third was already waived. The later statement that He waives everything is even more difficult. It appears to me that the explanation is this: on erev Rosh Hashanah He waives one third, meaning it is not completely forgiven but removed from the accounting, like the verse "the Lord has also put away your sin; you shall not die," which means it was not forgiven but only put away. The Gemara in Yoma also says that the sin itself is not erased. This is conditional: if they do not later merit the second waiver, meaning they commit sins and do not seek complete repentance, the first third that had already been put away and stood pending will also be counted. During the Ten Days of Repentance the first third is counted together with the second, both still being in a state of removal and not completely forgiven, until on Yom Kippur the first two thirds are counted with the final third. Lest you think that at that time too it is only removal and not full forgiveness, as with the earlier ones, Scripture teaches "on the first day," meaning first in the accounting of sins: the earlier sins were fully forgiven, not merely removed and held in suspension. We know this because it calls it first in the accounting of sins. It does not come to exclude the days between Yom Kippur and Sukkot, for they need no exclusion, since presumably they are occupied with mitzvot all day and do not sin. But on the first day, when they are not occupied all day with preparing mitzvot but rather eat, drink, and rejoice, sins are possible; yet the kindness of the blessed God overpowers us, so what had existed earlier is not aroused as it was in the first two portions with what preceded them, but on Yom Kippur everything is fully forgiven. Thus the Midrash properly says "what has gone has gone," meaning there is no mere removal of the earlier sins, but full forgiveness. With this the Beit Yosef's question is answered, that if so, erev Rosh Hashanah is as important as Yom Kippur, since on it one third is atoned, and he strained to answer that the first third is easier to atone than the second. According to what I wrote, all is settled: Yom Kippur certainly has a superior quality because of forgiveness, as I wrote, and this is explicit in the verse "for on this day He will atone for you, to purify you; from all your sins before the Lord you shall be purified." This teaches the superiority of Yom Kippur over the earlier atonements, which were only by way of removal and suspension, whereas "on this day, before the Lord, you shall be purified" fully from the earlier sins. From this it is proven that there are atonements before Yom Kippur, but Yom Kippur is greater, since it says specifically "on this day." With this the Levush's difficulty is answered, from where do we know there are earlier atonements? This explanation appears perfect and beautiful according to my humble opinion.
(1) From Rosh Chodesh Elul. According to Rashi and Tosafot, Moshe ascended on the first day of Rosh Chodesh, which is the thirtieth of Av. Maharshal wrote that therefore we blow shofar on the first day of Rosh Chodesh. See siman 565 regarding whether an individual says selichot.
And if Rosh Hashanah falls on Monday or Tuesday, etc. Levush gave the reason: the custom is to fast ten days together with Yom Kippur, and four days are always missing between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur on which they cannot fast, namely the two days of Rosh Hashanah, Shabbat Shuvah, and erev Yom Kippur. Therefore four days must be completed before Rosh Hashanah, and in order to have a fixed day for beginning they instituted Sunday each time. Levush wrote that the chazzan who prays selichot wraps in a tallit and does not bless, as stated in siman 18 regarding a night garment. This seems astonishing to me: according to the Rosh one is obligated to bless on a garment designated for day even if he wears it at night, as written in siman 18; why should he enter himself into a doubtful blessing by wearing such a garment and not blessing? Therefore it appears to me that without any wrapping at all is certainly impossible because of the honor of the Shekhinah, especially during selichot when he says "Vayaavor," and the Gemara states that the Holy One, blessed be He, wrapped Himself like a chazzan, etc. Rather, he should take another person's tallit, and with this he does not transgress anything, for we have already proven in the laws of tzitzit that one does not bless over a borrowed tallit. Even according to the one who rules there that one blesses, that is when he borrowed it in order to fulfill the mitzvah with it; here he intends only for modesty, and all agree he need not bless. This appears correct. Levush further wrote that since throughout the year we customarily recite birkat haTorah before saying any verse, one should do so also on these days when many verses are said before selichot, and skip them afterward, etc. See what I wrote on this in siman 6, that one need not skip afterward. One must say so, for otherwise no day would be found on which the chazzan says them after selichot, since every person is obligated to bless before selichot.
Acceptable to the congregation. Sefer Chasidim, siman 758, wrote that any mitzvah a person is accustomed to perform and then does not perform, he is punished for this. Therefore the custom is to seek to lead the prayers one is accustomed to lead, only they should not pray by force. In the laws mentioned at the end of Responsa Mahariv it is written that if one is a mourner, may the Merciful One protect us, and there is no one preferable to him, he may lead the prayer. It is written in Levush that one who fasts three consecutive days from Motzaei Shabbat and cannot make havdalah until Wednesday should hear havdalah from someone who makes havdalah on Motzaei Shabbat, even though he will not taste. It appears to me that even one who fasts two consecutive days should do this, for it is better to fulfill the mitzvah at its proper time, and tasting is not indispensable.
