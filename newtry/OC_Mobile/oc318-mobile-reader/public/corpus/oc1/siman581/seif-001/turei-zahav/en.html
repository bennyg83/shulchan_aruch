The Tur cites the Midrash, 'Is it then the first?' One may ask why this is difficult, for the verse can readily mean the first day of the festival, just as it says, 'On the first day shall be a rest day.' If the same difficulty applies there, the answer given here does not resolve it. The Beit Yosef cites Mahari de Leon, who explains that since this is a time bound mitzvah, the verse should have said, 'on the fifteenth day of the month.' In my humble opinion, there is no difficulty. Wherever Scripture uses the term 'first,' it implies that a second day or some further count follows it, as Rashi writes regarding the opening of Bereishis, that according to the sequence of the passage it should have said 'first.' Thus, in the verse 'On the first day shall be a rest day,' the wording is appropriate because it later says, 'and on the eighth day shall be a rest day.' Likewise, in Parashas Bo it says, 'On the first day shall be a holy convocation, and on the seventh day,' and also, 'On the first, on the fourteenth day,' continuing until 'the twenty first day.' Here, however, in the verse 'You shall take for yourselves on the first day,' none of the following days has any connection to this matter, since according to the Torah the obligation applies only on that day. The Midrash was therefore compelled to explain 'first in the accounting of sins.' The mitzvah of lulav is indeed connected to the accounting of sins, as stated in Midrash Rabbah, Parashas Emor, that if one steals a lulav and recites a blessing over it, his defender becomes his accuser. The Midrash therefore properly interprets 'first in the accounting of sins' adjacent to the word 'for yourselves,' which the Sages interpret to exclude a stolen lulav. In this manner the word 'first' is appropriately followed by a second matter within the same subject. The Midrash further states that after Yom Kippur the people occupy themselves with the mitzvos of sukkah and lulav and do not commit sins, and therefore the first day of Yom Tov is called the first day in the accounting of sins. I find this very difficult. First, why was it necessary to mention all the forgiveness that occurs before Yom Kippur? It could have answered briefly that since all sins are forgiven on Yom Kippur, the fifteenth day is first in the accounting of sins. Second, if they commit no sins, what is there for the Holy One, blessed be He, to reckon? Even on the first day there should be no accounting if they commit no sins. In the Midrash itself, the phrase 'and they do not commit sins' does not actually appear. It can therefore be explained that although they do commit sins, those sins are not included in the accounting because they are occupied with preparing the sukkah, etc. Yet according to this, why should preparation for the mitzvah of sukkah be superior to the day on which the mitzvah itself is fulfilled? The Tur's wording presents an even greater difficulty. It appears to me that this can be resolved after examining another point. The Midrash says that during the Ten Days of Repentance the intermediate people fast and He waives two thirds for them. Why does it say two thirds? He waives only one third, since the first third was already waived. The later statement that He waives everything is even more difficult. It appears to me that the explanation is as follows. On erev Rosh Hashanah He waives one third, meaning that it is not completely forgiven, but is merely removed from the accounting, as the verse says, 'Hashem has also removed your sin, you shall not die.' This means that it was not forgiven, but only removed. The Gemara in Yoma similarly states that the sin itself is not erased. This is conditional. If they do not later merit the waiver of the second third, because they commit further sins and do not seek complete repentance, the first third that had already been removed and remained suspended will also be counted. During the Ten Days of Repentance, the first third is counted together with the second, while both remain merely removed and are not completely forgiven. On Yom Kippur, the first two thirds are counted together with the final third. Lest one think that even then the sins are merely removed and not fully forgiven, as in the earlier stages, Scripture teaches, 'on the first day,' meaning first in the accounting of sins. The earlier sins were entirely forgiven, not merely removed and suspended. This is known from the fact that the day is called first in the accounting of sins. The verse does not come to exclude the days between Yom Kippur and Sukkos, because no exclusion is needed for those days. Presumably, since the people are occupied with mitzvos throughout the day, they do not commit sins. On the first day, however, they are not occupied throughout the day with preparing mitzvos, but instead eat, drink, and rejoice, so sins can occur. Nevertheless, the kindness of the blessed God prevails for us, and what existed previously is not reawakened as it was when each of the first two portions was joined to what preceded it. Rather, on Yom Kippur everything is fully forgiven. The Midrash therefore properly says, 'What has gone has gone,' meaning that the earlier sins are not merely removed, but are entirely forgiven. This resolves the Beit Yosef's question that, according to this, erev Rosh Hashanah is as significant as Yom Kippur, since one third is atoned on each. He was forced to answer that the first third is easier to atone than the second. According to my explanation, everything is settled. Yom Kippur certainly has a unique superiority because it grants complete forgiveness, as I have written. This is explicit in the verse, 'For on this day He shall atone for you, to purify you; from all your sins, before Hashem, you shall be purified.' This teaches the superiority of Yom Kippur over the earlier atonements, which were only by way of removal and suspension. On this day, however, 'before Hashem, you shall be purified' completely from the earlier sins. This also proves that there are atonements before Yom Kippur, but that Yom Kippur is greater, since the verse says specifically, 'on this day.' This resolves the Levush's difficulty as to the source for earlier atonements. In my humble opinion, this explanation is perfect and beautiful.
(1) From Rosh Chodesh Elul. According to Rashi and Tosafos, Moshe ascended on the first day of Rosh Chodesh, which was the thirtieth of Av. The Maharshal writes that for this reason the shofar is sounded on the first day of Rosh Chodesh. See siman 565 regarding whether an individual recites Selichos.
And if Rosh Hashanah falls on Monday or Tuesday, etc. The Levush explains that the custom is to fast ten days together with Yom Kippur. Four days between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur are always unavailable for fasting, namely the two days of Rosh Hashanah, Shabbos Shuvah, and erev Yom Kippur. Four days must therefore be made up before Rosh Hashanah. To establish a fixed day for beginning, the custom was instituted to begin on Sunday each time. The Levush writes that the chazzan who leads Selichos wraps himself in a tallis and does not recite a blessing, as stated in siman 18 regarding a garment worn at night. This appears astonishing to me. According to the Rosh, one is obligated to recite a blessing over a garment designated for daytime even when wearing it at night, as stated in siman 18. Why should he deliberately place himself in a situation of doubtful obligation to recite a blessing by wearing such a garment without reciting one? It therefore appears to me that it is certainly impossible to lead without any wrapping at all, because of the honor of the Shechinah, especially during Selichos when he recites the Thirteen Attributes. The Gemara states that the Holy One, blessed be He, wrapped Himself like a chazzan, etc. Rather, he should use another person's tallis. He thereby violates nothing, because we have already demonstrated in the laws of tzitzis that no blessing is recited over a borrowed tallis. Even according to the authority who rules there that one does recite a blessing, that applies when one borrowed it in order to fulfill the mitzvah. Here, his intent is only ordinary modesty, and everyone agrees that no blessing is required. This appears correct. The Levush further writes that since throughout the year our custom is to recite birkas haTorah before saying any verse, the same should be done on these days when many verses are recited before Selichos, and those verses should then be omitted afterward, etc. See what I wrote concerning this in siman 6, that they need not be omitted afterward. This must be so, for otherwise there would never be a day on which the chazzan could recite them after Selichos, since every individual is obligated to recite birkas haTorah before Selichos.
Acceptable to the congregation. Sefer Chasidim, siman 758, writes that a person who is accustomed to perform a particular mitzvah and then fails to perform it is punished for this. For this reason, people make an effort to lead the prayers that they are accustomed to lead, but they should not seize the prayer by force. The laws cited at the end of Responsa Mahariv state that if a person is a mourner, may the Merciful One protect us, and no one more qualified is present, he may lead the prayer. The Levush writes that one who fasts for three consecutive days beginning on Motzei Shabbos and cannot make Havdalah until Wednesday should hear Havdalah from someone who recites it on Motzei Shabbos, even though he himself will not taste the wine. It appears to me that even one who fasts for two consecutive days should do so, because it is preferable to fulfill the mitzvah at its proper time, and tasting is not indispensable.
