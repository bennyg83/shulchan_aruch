(9) It is insufficient. Meaning, if one builds a wall on his own property opposite his fellow’s window, the law ordinarily requires him to distance it four amos so as not to darken the window, and that suffices. A synagogue, however, requires abundant light. Therefore, if the congregation has an established right to existing windows, the builder must distance his wall farther, though the exact additional distance is not stated. Eight amos certainly suffices. See Shaarei Teshuvah in the name of Noda BiYehudah. Peri Megadim is uncertain whether four amos is likewise insufficient for the men’s and women’s galleries. In my opinion, one should not extend the rule, especially since the Mechaber’s basic law itself is not clear. See Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger. Rama writes in a responsum, end of siman 32, that where a synagogue courtyard is not used by the public for private matters, the congregation may not object to an individual opening windows toward it, since this resembles opening a window toward a public domain. If, however, the congregation opens synagogue windows toward his courtyard, he may object. This law concerns an individual opening windows toward a place used for public passage where the congregation can never build. Otherwise, they may object, lest they later wish to build there and he claim a prescriptive right to his windows and prevent construction. Although Rama rules in Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 149, that possession is ineffective against communal property, the congregation may say that they do not wish to engage in litigation with him. Sefer Chassidim, siman 816, writes that one should not pour urine from a window onto a public path used by people going to synagogue, because some go early in the dark and may be unable to avoid being soiled. It is written, “Guard your foot when you go to the house of God.” In any event, one must avoid pouring wastewater where people walk, lest they slip, for this is like creating a pit in the public domain. She’eris Yosef writes that neighbors whose privy emits a foul odor into the synagogue must remove it. Even if the privy preceded the synagogue, they must remove it, because harm to the public differs from harm to an individual. The congregation must, however, compensate the owner, analogous to the law in Choshen Mishpat, siman 155, seif 22. See what Magen Avraham writes at the end of siman 154.
