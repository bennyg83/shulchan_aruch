1) [Seif 1] The residents of a city compel one another, etc. Even a minority may compel the majority. Choshen Mishpat, beginning of siman 163, in the gloss. This applies specifically when there are ten men, as written below in paragraph 4 in the name of Rambam, and Levush likewise writes. Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 2. It appears that in small communities there is certainly authority to compel one another to go to the synagogue and pray with a minyan, unless one has an unavoidable circumstance. Bach; Ateres Zekeinim; Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, paragraph 1. See above at the end of siman 55 and our comments there, with the help of Heaven.
2) There: To build a synagogue. The funds for constructing a synagogue are collected according to wealth, not according to the number of people. For renting a synagogue, however, they are collected according to the number of people. So write Knesses HaGedolah in Hagahos HaTur and Olas Tamid, paragraph 5, in the name of Maharam of Padua, siman 42. Elya Zuta, paragraph 1, writes that the body of that responsum appears to indicate that rental costs are collected half according to wealth and half according to the number of people; see there. Olas Tamid, paragraph 1, and Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1, likewise write this. Nevertheless, see Ikarei HaDat, siman 8, paragraph 21, who cites Mekor Chaim, siman 3, as proving directly that the obligation rests upon each individual. He adds that it is proper for the wealthy to concede somewhat for the poor, and the rabbis there agreed with him; see there. The present custom is likewise to collect rental costs according to wealth, in order to ease the burden upon the poor, since some poor people cannot contribute anything. The wealthy are also pleased to concede and contribute more on behalf of the poor, so as to bring them merit.
3) The ruling that synagogue construction is assessed according to wealth applies specifically in a place where the custom is not to purchase seats in the synagogue, and the first person to arrive takes a seat. In places where seats are purchased, however, the seat purchasers are obligated to provide nearly the principal part of the expense. Shaarei Ephraim, siman 145; see there. Mishna Berurah, paragraph 2, cites this. See also Pischei Teshuvah, Choshen Mishpat, siman 163, paragraph 22, who discusses the matter at length. Regarding Torah scholars: are they obligated to contribute toward synagogue construction, since they are exempt from taxes, as stated in Yoreh Deah, siman 243, seif 2, and Choshen Mishpat, siman 163, seif 4? It appears that they are exempt only from a tax imposed for protection, since a Torah scholar does not require protection, as explained there and in Levush. Synagogue construction, however, is not for protection but is a need of body and soul, and they are therefore obligated to contribute. This also appears from Maharam’s statement there in Choshen Mishpat that orphans need not contribute toward synagogue construction, implying that Torah scholars must. It appears, as written in the preceding paragraph, that synagogue construction is assessed according to wealth; see there.
4) There: To build a synagogue. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, second edition, Orach Chaim, siman 18, who writes that it is permitted to construct a synagogue with eight sides and eight corners; see there. Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 6, cites him. Nevertheless, Nefesh Chaim, maareches beis, paragraph 15, cites the author of Vavei HaAmudim, of blessed memory, who writes that the Beis HaMikdash was square in order to indicate unity. Nefesh Chaim writes that this suggests that a synagogue likewise should be square to indicate unity. Pischei HaDevir cites him at the end of paragraph 2; see there. Yafeh LaLev, paragraph 2.
5) There: To build a synagogue. Rambam writes in chapter 11 of Hilchos Tefillah: ‘Every place that contains ten Jews must prepare a building in which they enter to pray at every prayer time, and this place is called a synagogue.’ This concludes his words. Accordingly, in a place where they cannot build a synagogue because they are poor or for another reason, they should at least rent a building in which to pray. If they cannot do even that, at the very least one of them should volunteer to prepare a place in his home during the prayer times, so that they can come pray there. He will thereby bring merit to the public, and the merit of the public will depend upon him.
6) There: To build a synagogue. They may likewise compel one another to repair and strengthen the synagogue. This is included in the positive mitzvah: just as it is a positive mitzvah to build the Chosen House and strengthen its breaches, as the verse states, ‘They shall make Me a Sanctuary,’ so in the present era there is a mitzvah to build and strengthen the breaches of synagogues and batei midrash. So writes Orchos Yosher, chapter 13, page 171a; see there. Yafeh LaLev, paragraph 1.
