[7] If non-Jews and a Jew were partners in chametz and they divided it after Pesach, the non-Jew's portion is permitted and the Jew's portion is forbidden, for in rabbinic law bereirah applies; Chok Yaakov 2. See Eliyah Rabbah 1, who brought from Yoreh De'ah 146:4 regarding a convert and a non-Jew, money and chametz, etc. Eliyah Rabbah inclined to be lenient regarding a non-Jew who stole chametz from a Jew and Pesach passed over it. He also inclined to be lenient in the incident where a non-Jew ground a Jew's grain, baked bread on Pesach, and brought it after Pesach. If a Jew left wheat in a mill and a non-Jew came and told the Jew that he had soaked and ground it, see Chok Yaakov, note 4, in the name of Chinuch Beis Yehudah, siman 11, that there are many doubts for leniency. If he speaks casually, perhaps his intent is that the Jew should be grateful to him. And perhaps even without this, a non-Jew is not believed to forbid; see Yoreh De'ah 48 in Taz, note 19. However, if the usual practice is to soak the wheat before grinding, it is proper to forbid it. If he is the owner of the mill, this requires examination. If he is just an ordinary non-Jew, one can say that it is like theft by a non-Jew, and he acquired it through a change, as Chok Yaakov wrote. One can say that the non-Jew should exchange it and give him other wheat. If a non-Jew brought a gift to a Jew and there was also chametz inside it, and the Jew accepted it on Pesach without knowing, see Chok Yaakov 5; one can say that he does not want to acquire a prohibition, and an acquisition made in error is not an acquisition, and he left it requiring examination. With Hashem's help, this will be explained further in Magen Avraham. If a non-Jew cooked chametz on Pesach for a sick Jew and it was absorbed into the vessel, then after Pesach, even according to the one who says that a vessel that absorbed non-Jewish cooking requires kashering, in such a case one can say there is a side for permission, for there is a recognizable marker; see Yoreh De'ah 113 in Taz, note 15, and there are two sides for permission. But if he cooked a dish and there was chametz in it that was nullified in sixty, it is permitted after Pesach. However, there is a prohibition of non-Jewish cooking; see Taz there, and see siman 318 and what I wrote there.
[7] There was an incident involving a Jew who sold chametz to a non-Jew and said, "Everything in this room is yours." Afterward, another Jew came and gave this Jew chametz as a gift and placed it there. This requires examination. If the first Jew later put the chametz there, when it was in his possession, one can say it is permitted, because he said to the non-Jew, "Whatever you find there is all yours." But regarding other chametz, a person cannot transfer something that has not yet come into existence, and this requires examination. See siman 446, Magen Avraham 1, and siman 450 in Taz 5 regarding something not yet in existence with respect to chametz. As for a non-Jew's courtyard, according to the one who says that a non-Jew can become an agent for another non-Jew, one can say that the same applies to his courtyard; see siman 448 and Chok Yaakov there, and understand this.
