To feed it during Pesach. Beit Yosef cites this anonymously, but it is found in Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 191. The Agudah itself states: “A person should likewise not tell a non-Jew, ‘Take my animal into your home and feed it chametz,’ but he may give it to him without specification to maintain during the days of Pesach.” This implies that only an express instruction to feed chametz is forbidden, whereas an unspecified arrangement is permitted according to the Agudah even when one knows certainly that the non-Jew will feed it chametz. This parallels the explicit wording in the law of one who tells his servant, for all these cases are treated alike there. Initially, however, one should not rely upon this leniency.
That it is chametz. Olas Shabbos writes that after the fact it is permitted, like a cow fattened with idolatrous vetch, as explained in Yoreh Deah 142. This comparison may be inexact. Here the Jew’s animal consumed during Pesach chametz belonging to a non-Jew, from which benefit is forbidden during Pesach, so the Jew benefited from chametz. He should therefore cast away the value of the benefit, meaning the amount he would otherwise have spent feeding the animal. A cow fattened with idolatrous vetch, by contrast, concerns an animal belonging to a non-Jew. Similarly, an animal of a non-Jew fattened on chametz during Pesach is certainly permitted. Here, however, at least the value of the Jew’s benefit should be cast into the sea, or the entire animal should be sold to a non-Jew except for the value of the prohibited benefit, following the law of one who benefits from an item forbidden in benefit. Nevertheless, one who studies the sugya at the end of the chapter Kol HaTzelamim will see that after the fact it is permitted in every respect because two causes produced the result. This requires study.
