[7] That is outside the house, etc. The Bach explained that the chametz should be outside the house, so that the Jew not be like a depositary for the chametz from the non-Jew. Nachalas Tzvi wrote that if it is in a designated room or behind a partition ten handbreadths high, that is sufficient, and Taz likewise wrote that designating a corner is effective. Nevertheless, ab initio it appears that one should not be lenient with actual chametz, and one should not sell to a non-Jew if the chametz is in a Jew's house, even if he sells everything to the non-Jew according to the law, except as the Bach wrote. This is his language: in our countries, where most business is in liquor and it is impossible for them to sell it to non-Jews outside the house, especially for holders of an arenda, one may permit selling the non-Jew the room and the chametz there, etc. On this basis I answered the beer sellers here in Prague, who asked why all our earlier rabbis forbade selling the beer with their cellar to non-Jews, while the liquor sellers had the custom to permit the sale. I answered that specifically regarding liquor sellers, where it is theirs and they usually distill it themselves, and it is impossible for them to sell it outside the house, as in the countries about which the Bach testified, this was allowed. But regarding beer that they buy from non-Jews, they did not permit this at all, because it is easy to return it to the non-Jews and also not to buy so much that some remains over for Pesach. They also certainly saw that there was concern for laxity in this, for it is accessible to everyone to enter the cellar on Pesach for some Yom Tov need through the small opening they have, and other such matters. Therefore they saw fit to be completely stringent about this. I wrote at length in Eliyah Zuta regarding the law of sale, and so did all the later authorities and Responsa Nachalas Shiv'ah, simanim 31 and 32. The main rule is that he should sell the room to the non-Jew for a perutah and say that together with the room he should acquire all the chametz that is in it, and he should hand him the key so that he can enter it whenever he wishes. Alternatively, he should receive the perutah and the non-Jew should pull the chametz into his own domain or into a side alley, for then pulling acquires it for him together with the money. After the fact, if he sold it with money alone, or with pulling alone, or in a place where merchants customarily acquire with a mark or a key even though he did not hand over the key, or by giving his palm to his fellow, or by a perutah, as written in Choshen Mishpat, siman 201, one should not forbid the chametz. But what people sell as the key is an error, for he is selling only the key, and his chametz remains forbidden for benefit. He must also be careful not to leave any seal on the room or on the chametz, for then even after the fact one should forbid it, because the non-Jew does not rely on the transaction. If he made a seal after the sale, even though they acted improperly, nevertheless one should not forbid the chametz. Magen Avraham wrote: if the non-Jew does not want to return it to him, it is forbidden to sue him in their courts. He wrote further at the end of this siman that even if the chametz is not with him, he can sell it to a non-Jew through one of the modes of acquisition mentioned above.
