[6] And he should not give it to him on condition. It appears that the meaning is: on condition that he return it to him after Pesach, for then it is neither a sale nor a gift. But if he said to him: on condition that you keep it for yourself and do with it as you wish, only that you not sell it to any other person, this is a complete sale even during the entire year. It appears that every person should say this explicitly to the non-Jew. This helps in that no person can buy that chametz from the non-Jew, because he did not sell it to him on that condition; it therefore turns out that the sale reverts to the non-Jew, and the Jewish owner of the chametz can go back and buy it. According to this, however, the owner of the chametz is forbidden to sue the Jew who bought it from the non-Jew and say, "Give me the chametz for that purchase price," for through this he reveals his view that the sale was void, and automatically the chametz returns to its first owners, making it Jewish-owned chametz that remained over Pesach. Rather, he should compel the one who bought it unlawfully, for he sold it to the non-Jew only on condition that it remain with the non-Jew and automatically be sold back to him. He should say to him: I have this right in this chametz, that no one can buy it from the non-Jew if he wishes, only I. Even if he did not explicitly stipulate with the non-Jew that he cannot sell it to another, nevertheless we are witnesses that on this understanding the chametz left his domain, and there is no prohibition in this. This is similar to one who sells chametz to his fellow before Pesach and stipulates with him that after Pesach this buyer must have some chametz at that time to sell specifically to this Jew, which involves no prohibition at all. Here too it is so, as appears to me. Therefore, if another Jew bought this chametz from the non-Jew, he must go back and sell it to its owner. One should not say that there is another reason he must give it to the owner of the chametz, namely that he sold it to the non-Jew only to remove the prohibition of chametz, not to remove it from his ownership. This is not so, for if so it would be Jewish-owned chametz. Furthermore, if so, what does the Gemara ask in the first chapter, page 6, "Does this mean that rental acquires, etc."? Why does it not answer that here it is different, for he designated a house for him only to remove the prohibition? Rather, this is certainly not a valid argument. I already wrote at the end of siman 246 that even regarding Shabbos, when one declares his animal ownerless, no permission is learned from there. In Sefer Mas'as Binyamin he wrote here that the reason the one who bought it from the non-Jew must return it to the owner of the chametz is because he has priority to buy it under the rule of ma'arufya.
