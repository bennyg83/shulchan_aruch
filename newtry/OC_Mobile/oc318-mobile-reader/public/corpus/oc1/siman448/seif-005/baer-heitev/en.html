[5] That he nullified it. Even though he did not transgress biblically, nevertheless we are concerned that he will act evasively and say that he nullified it even though he did not nullify it, since he acted contrary to the sages' enactment by not destroying the chametz or selling it to a non-Jew. Even with complete ownerless designation, the law is also so. And even if there are witnesses that he made it ownerless and nullified it, this does not help if he intentionally left chametz in his house and domain and knew about it throughout all the days of Pesach; certainly in such a case nullification or ownerless designation does not help. But whenever he did not know about it throughout all the days of Pesach, and there are witnesses that he nullified it or made it ownerless before Pesach, or there was a complete unavoidable circumstance in the matter of the sale and throughout all the days of Pesach he thought that he had sold it, and after Pesach it became known to him that he had not sold it, then since he was under such a known unavoidable circumstance, and he says that he nullified it, even though there are no witnesses we are not concerned for evasion, and one may permit it at least for benefit after Pesach. Chok Yaakov; see there. R. Shmuel HaLevi wrote in Orach Chayim, siman 12, that if chametz was delayed for a Jew on the road, while it was on a ship, it would appear permissible to declare all his chametz ownerless and then reacquire it after Pesach and sell it, but not eat it. Yet because of the stringency of chametz, my heart did not permit me to act in practice; see Kenesses HaGedolah. Magen Avraham wrote: it is obvious that even if the chametz is not with him, he can sell the chametz to a non-Jew through one of the modes of acquisition mentioned above, and then the non-Jew acquires the chametz wherever it is; see there. See Responsa Olas HaGer, se'if katan 5, and see Chok Yaakov.
