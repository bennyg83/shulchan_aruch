It is forbidden even though he nullified it. We are concerned for subterfuge, that he will claim to have nullified it although in fact he did not, since he acted contrary to the enactment of the Sages by neither eliminating the chametz nor selling it to a non-Jew. Although everyone now performs bittul, we nevertheless remain concerned for subterfuge. The same applies even to complete hefker, and even witnesses to the hefker and bittul are ineffective if he knowingly left the chametz in his home and possession throughout Pesach. In such a case, bittul or hefker certainly does not help. If, however, he was unaware of it throughout Pesach and witnesses attest that he nullified or declared it ownerless before Pesach, or there was a complete and known coercive circumstance in the sale, so that throughout Pesach he believed it had been sold and only afterward learned that the sale was ineffective, then if he states that he nullified it we do not suspect subterfuge even without witnesses. At least benefit after Pesach may be permitted. I discussed and established all of this at length in Toras HaShelamim, question 6, contrary to several later authorities who adopted excessive stringencies. See also what I wrote in Soles LaMinchah concerning the glosses to Shaar Ephraim. If a Jew’s chametz was delayed on the road, see Responsa Rabbi Shlomo HaLevi, Orach Chaim, siman 12, who ruled that it should be declared ownerless before a beis din, but remained uncertain in practice whether benefit after Pesach could be permitted. Where the chametz is due to arrive in a city containing Jews and customs charges, carriage fees, or similar expenses must be paid during Pesach, what should be done? Declaring it ownerless is possible, but a sale to a non-Jew for money alone is, as I wrote above, initially ineffective according to most authorities, while meshichah is impossible because the chametz is not present. It appears that he should sell it to the non-Jew for money, stipulating that if it arrives during Pesach the non-Jew will pay the customs and carriage charges and take possession. After the sale he should declare before two people that if the non-Jew does not acquire it through money, it is at least completely ownerless. In this manner, according to all views, it leaves his ownership even initially. See Magen Avraham, who writes that there are additional methods of acquisition even where the chametz is not present.
Even though he nullified it. At first glance, based upon Moed Katan 13a, where a penalty imposed for a Torah prohibition extends to one’s son after him but a penalty for a D’Rabbanan prohibition does not, one might say that if a person nullified the chametz and then died, it should at least be permitted in benefit to his son, since no Torah prohibition was violated. This is incorrect, however, according to Ran’s explanation of why one may not feed chametz after Pesach even to another person’s animal: the Sages did not penalize only the transgressor; they prohibited the chametz to everyone. This requires study.
