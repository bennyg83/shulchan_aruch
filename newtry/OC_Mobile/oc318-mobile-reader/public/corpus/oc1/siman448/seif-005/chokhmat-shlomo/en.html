Shlomo Zalman, Av Beis Din of the holy community of Warsaw, may his light shine, sent to me as a gift. I saw in his book, Orach Chayim, siman 1, that he wondered at the later authorities who were lenient several times regarding chametz that remained over Pesach when it was sold with money alone or pulling alone, saying that since there is a dispute whether money acquires for a non-Jew or pulling, it is a rabbinic doubt and lenient. He wondered about this, because in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, in the passage of "if the kohen seized it," it is explicitly proven that if the kohen seized it we remove it from him, for it is considered entirely his and entirely his property. If so, here too, since if the non-Jew seized it we would remove it from him, it is entirely the Jew's property and he transgressed bal yera'eh. He raised this difficulty also about the Talmud in Bava Basra regarding two trees, where he brings and does not recite; see there at length. In my humble opinion this is not difficult at all. Certainly if he nullified it, it bears no comparison to that case. There, if the kohen did not acquire it, it belongs to the Jew, the original owner, and it is proper to follow the prior owner's presumption and the presumption of the one in whose hand it was until seizure; therefore seizure does not help. But with chametz, he nullified it and it became ownerless. If so, at least it is certainly not the seller's. The sages only said that although he nullified it, it is forbidden for benefit as a decree because of one who did not nullify it, and it has no permission unless another acquired it before Pesach. In this, if the non-Jew seized it, he certainly would not need to return it, because at least it certainly does not belong to the owners. The only practical question is whether it is ownerless or whether the non-Jew acquired it. Against ownerless property, certainly if he seized it we do not remove it from him, and the non-Jew would not need to return it, for here there is no prior owner's presumption, and according to everyone, if the kohen seized it, we do not remove it from him. But even if he did not nullify it, it still appears settled. The Talmud states that general rule, that where if he seized we remove it from him it is considered certainly the Jew's, only where the opposing party has no acquisition based on the transferor's intent, for the transferor did not transfer it to him; rather, the Torah granted it to him. In such a case his acquisition is not considered an acquisition. This is similar to what Tosafos wrote at the beginning of Yoma and in Kesubos, that where the majority is Jewish one must return a found object to him, even though we do not follow majority in monetary law, because the finder does not have a strong presumption in it. Therefore, regarding damages, we follow the majority because it did not come into his hand with the owners' knowledge. It is proven that whenever it did not come into his hand with the owners' knowledge, it is not considered a strong presumption. Therefore, since even if the truth were with him he has no strong presumption, and the owners did not transfer it to him, only the Torah transferred it to him, here we say that since the Torah said that in such a case it belongs to the prior owner and that even if he seized it we remove it from him, it is considered certainly the prior owner's money. Either way, without the Torah's view, the other has no acquisition in it at all, only what the Torah granted him; and here the Torah did not grant it to him, but said that wherever it stands, let it stand, and even if he seized it we remove it from him. Therefore it is certainly considered the property of the first owners. But where the owners transferred it to him with their consent, and we are merely uncertain whether this is an acquisition or not, then certainly we do not consider the fact that the law leaves it in the hands of the first owners. Rather, since if it were revealed that this is an acquisition it would belong to the buyer, the buyer has a side of acquisition in it. Through this, all the difficulties of the above gaon, of blessed memory, are removed. Examine this. It also appears that Tosafos asked at the beginning of Bava Metzia: how does the kohen's seizure help? The owner can say, "I want to give it to another kohen." They answered that it refers to a kohen to whom he regularly gives. In my humble opinion, their words require examination. How does the Talmud bring proof generally from this case that if the kohen seized it we remove it from him? One can say that it is different there: with a kohen, were it not for regular priestly gifts, seizure would certainly not apply, for he can give it to another kohen. It is only because of regular priestly gifts. The reason regular priestly gifts treat what he did not yet acquire as though he acquired is that since the owner's way is to give to this kohen, we are witnesses that he resolved and transferred it to him. This is understandable where he knows that he certainly must give it to a kohen; then we say that even before giving it, he certainly transferred it to him. But here, since there is not certainly a kohen, he is not obligated, and the only point would be to say that if the kohen seized it we do not remove it from him. In this, it certainly does not apply to say that he resolved and transferred it to him, for who knows whether he will seize it or not? Especially here, there is certainly an opposite presumption, that he did not resolve and transfer it to him so that his seizure would not help. How then does regular priestly giving apply? Their words require examination. Therefore the main answer appears to be the second answer, that he must give him its value as priestly benefit, etc. According to this, since Tosafos at the beginning of Bava Metzia asked, when one says "half is mine" and one says "all is mine," we should say that doubt does not remove certainty, as with a doubtful heir and a yavam, and they answered that there the yavam is certainly an heir, but here, because this one certainly has it in this courtyard, why should he certainly have it in another courtyard? See there. According to this, it appears that if we had said that if the kohen seized it we do not remove it from him, it would also be considered the kohen's money, even though the owners at least have the benefit of deciding which kohen receives it and have some claim to the value of that benefit. Nevertheless, we would not say that doubt does not remove certainty, according to Tosafos' reasoning that because it is certainly in this one's courtyard, why should it be certainly in another courtyard? But this applies only if seizure is effective. Since, however, if the kohen seizes it we remove it from him, the kohen has no acquisition in it before us. Then that seizure is indeed effective, since at least the owners have some certain acquisition in it, namely the benefit of deciding whom to give it to. Therefore we say that since he has some acquisition in it, doubt does not remove certainty, and it is considered entirely his. This rule therefore applies only to priestly gifts. But elsewhere, such as chametz or one who buys two trees, where he has no acquisition at all in the chametz or the land if it belongs to the opposing party, then even if seizure would be removed from him, the buyer is still considered to have a side of acquisition, because if it were revealed retroactively that it was his, it would be his. This is clear and correct in my view as a matter of reasoning. Examine carefully.
