[4] To a non-Jew outside the house. The Beis Yosef wrote that it sounds from the words of Terumas HaDeshen that giving chametz to a non-Jew is ineffective unless he is a non-Jew outside the house. The intent is not that the non-Jew must be a foreign man who does not live in the Jew's house, while there is no concern if the chametz is in the Jew's house. That certainly has neither reason nor sense, for it is worse when the chametz is in his house. Furthermore, Terumas HaDeshen did not write "outside the house" regarding the non-Jew, but "outside the house" meaning that he said the chametz should be outside the house. As for the non-Jew, we do not care even if he is his household servant, for immediately afterward he permits it even where he recognizes him and is used to him, and knows, etc. All this I wrote according to the Beis Yosef's understanding in the name of Terumas HaDeshen. But the main point does not appear to me, for it is stated explicitly in the first chapter of Pesachim, which I brought in siman 475, that if he designated a corner for a non-Jew's chametz, he is not required to destroy it, because when the non-Jew brings it in, he brings it in for himself, as it says, "it shall not be found in your houses," excluding this, which is not in your control. Rashi explained: for that corner is given over to the non-Jew. We learn from here that even in the house it helps if he designates a corner for him. Rather, Terumas HaDeshen also certainly did not come to exclude designating a corner, for this too is called outside the Jew's house. This appears simple to me. And it is obvious that the chametz must be placed in a location where the non-Jew can enter on his own, as is implied by the language of the Gemara, that the non-Jew enters for himself. All the more so one should not lock it with a lock while the Jew keeps the key in his hand or places his seal on it. He must also explicitly sell him that room together with the chametz inside it, for otherwise it is still "in your houses." Anyone who does not act in the manner we mentioned transgresses bal yera'eh and bal yimatzei, and his chametz after Pesach is forbidden for benefit. It is also obvious that one must not sell the chametz to an apostate, for even though he sinned, he is a Jew. There was an incident involving one who held an arenda in a village and sold his liquor to his official, who was an apostate. The amount was large, and it was difficult to lose the liquor, and it was likely that the Jew would not obey in this matter. I said that the official should exchange it with another non-Jew and take other liquor from him, and then it is permitted to this Jew, as stated in the first chapter of Chullin: the chametz of transgressors is permitted after Pesach because they exchange it. This means that chametz does not render its proceeds forbidden. The same applies if the official sells it to a non-Jew: the money is permitted to the Jew for this reason, as appears to me.
