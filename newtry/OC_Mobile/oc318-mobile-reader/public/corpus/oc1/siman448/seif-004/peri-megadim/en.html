Ab initio, the authorities wrote that it is proper also to sell the room; see Magen Avraham 4, where, with Hashem's help, this will be explained. Even so, his handiwork and whatever he has belongs to his master, and there is a practical difference only if he immersed, for then he goes free; see Shach, note 30. If he immersed, it is obvious that his master transgresses bal yera'eh regarding the positive commandment, and perhaps even the positive commandment of tashbisu until nightfall; elsewhere we wrote about this. If someone said to a slave or maidservant, "I hereby give it to you as a gift on condition that you return it after Pesach," according to what Eliyah Rabbah wrote in note 18, and so it appears in Levush, that a gift on condition to return is forbidden ab initio because of the stringency of chametz but after the fact is permitted for benefit after Pesach, as we will say below, with Hashem's help, in note 6 and Magen Avraham 5, one can say that the master did not acquire it. For he also did not transfer it to the maidservant absolutely, but only on condition that she return it to him. This resembles Yoreh De'ah 266:22 and Shach, note 39. This requires examination.

[4] And there was an incident involving one who sold large barrels of liquor and placed a seal after the sale; one may permit it. Even before the sale, regarding our sale that is done properly by document, with each quart for such-and-such amount, and the non-Jew may open them if he wishes and do what he wants, while the seal is only until measurement, it is permitted, and perhaps even ab initio. Chok Yaakov wrote the reason as evasion; see Bach. That is with a small sale, where it is a void sale and only a disclosure of intent is enough, as Ran wrote at the beginning of Pesachim. Whenever he placed the seal beforehand, it appears that he does not want the sale. But in a case like this, as stated, this is not so. Also, what the Bach wrote that one should not place a seal on the room implies that on the barrels he may, for the non-Jew can sell the barrels to his fellow by pulling, and they can be measured after Pesach. See Eliyah Rabbah, that the non-Jew does not rely on the transaction. But in our sale, which is proper and is not a disclosure of intent, it is certainly permitted after the fact, and even ab initio in such a case, where the seal is only until measurement and he records the debt as a loan, while the liquor is his from now. Know that if one sells each quart for a very large sum, one can say that this is a void sale with a non-Jew, and in this case the principle of mere disclosure does not apply. It is proper to sell in the ordinary way so that it be a proper sale. All the more so if the Jew sold to a non-Jew in a village the liquor that he had in the city, and he left a seal, and the non-Jew did not know of this, it is better, for the issue that the non-Jew does not rely on it does not apply. Even if he knew, in such a case it is certainly permitted after the fact. [4] And what he wrote, that one may not sell the chametz to an apostate, and the chametz of an apostate is forbidden to every Jew: see Ran, and so it appears in chapter 1 of Hilchos Chametz U'Matzah, halachah 4, and Chullin 4, "their chametz, etc." See Chok Yaakov, note 14, that one should not buy chametz from an apostate immediately after Pesach. Perhaps a doubt that it may have been made after Pesach is permitted; see Chok Yaakov 449:1, Chullin 4b, s.v. "permitted," and Maharsha there. One cannot say that he is telling the truth, for even in rabbinic law one who transgresses for appetite does not leave permitted food and eat forbidden food, for if so, even one who transgresses spitefully should be permitted. We wrote about this there in Sefer Rosh Yosef. However, one can say that we do not create a double doubt ab initio, or even a rabbinic doubt; see Yoreh De'ah 110. It is somewhat comparable to buying cooked items from non-Jews where the default is that the utensil was not used that day and there is a double doubt, but there it is only under pressure.

One can say that it is permitted to add and nullify it; see there. Without great loss, one should not permit this, for there is a double doubt toward prohibition: perhaps the halachah follows Rema that one may not add to nullify even a rabbinic prohibition, and perhaps everything was made during Pesach. All the more so if he mixed everything together, it becomes forbidden like a neveilah, liquid without cooking; see Yoreh De'ah 99. But in a case of great loss, one can say that even a majority of prohibition with a minority of permitted matter is like the case of wood that fell into an oven, dry with dry, which shows that a majority of prohibition can have permitted matter added to nullify it in a rabbinic case; I explained it there. Barrels among large barrels are an important item; see Yoreh De'ah 132:2, that even rabbinically it is not nullified, and see Taz 447, note 18, regarding its remedy. There was an incident involving one who sold chametz and liquor to a non-Jew properly, and recorded the money as a loan. During Pesach, the non-Jew ruined much of the liquor, and a non-Jew who was friendly with the Jew took the key from the non-Jew; there is no concern. Perhaps the Jew may complain before the official so that he take the key from the non-Jew, lest he destroy everything, because he is a poor non-Jew. But if the Jew took the key from the non-Jew, one can say that responsibility rests on the Jew and it is forbidden after Pesach; this requires examination.
