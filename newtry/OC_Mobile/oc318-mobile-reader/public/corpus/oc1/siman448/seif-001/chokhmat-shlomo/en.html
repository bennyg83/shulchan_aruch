[1] Se'if 1. "Chametz of a non-Jew over which Pesach passed, etc." Note: I will copy here several novel points that are very necessary as a matter of law, with Hashem's help. First: an incident came before me involving someone who had chametz from which he had to pay the king's tax. Because he did not pay the tax from it according to the law, the matter became known to the king's officers, and they took the chametz from him to the king's customs house. The case came to trial and was uncertain: either all the chametz would be confiscated for the king, or the Jew would prove his righteousness and the judgment would be clarified that the chametz should be returned to him. The questioner then asked what, according to law, he should do on Pesach with this chametz standing in the king's house, and whether a sale to a non-Jew is effective for it. This requires analysis: perhaps a sale to a non-Jew is not effective at all, for we hold that something which is his but not in his possession cannot be sold by him. If so, here too, since it was taken by the king's officers, it is not in his possession and he cannot sell it; nevertheless it is his, and he transgresses bal yera'eh. One should not say that if so, this is like where a non-Jew stole the chametz from him, and where a non-Jew stole chametz from him the Jew does not transgress bal yera'eh, as the later books wrote. That is not so. With stolen chametz, at least responsibility rests on the non-Jew, and if it is lost and the Jew is able to force the non-Jew in court, the non-Jew must pay him. Therefore it is under the non-Jew's responsibility, and therefore the Jew does not transgress bal yera'eh. But here, responsibility does not rest on the king's officers. If it is ruined, even if it is later clarified that the Jew wins the case, they will not be obligated to pay him. If so, it is not under the non-Jew's responsibility, and perhaps in such a case he transgresses bal yera'eh. Nevertheless, perhaps one can be lenient here too through sale, for two reasons. First, what we hold, that something which is his but not in his possession cannot be sold by him, can be said to apply specifically where, for example, the item was stolen from him, because at present, in the robber's view, he wants to rob it from him entirely and does not intend to return it. Therefore it is presently not in his possession. But here, the king's officers do not intend to rob it outright, but to return it to him if he wins the case. If so, either way: if the king wins the chametz, then retroactively it belongs to the king and is not his at all, for the law of the kingdom is law, and he did not transgress bal yera'eh. If the ruling is issued that it belongs to the Jew, then from now their intention is to return it to him, and this is not included in something that is not in his possession. It is better than a pledge, for a pledge has a clear lien on it through the debt, and therefore he cannot sell it; but here there is no lien or clarified debt on it. Therefore it is proper to say this either-way argument. One can say even more. What we hold, that if one stole and the owners did not despair, neither can consecrate it, and the same applies to selling, is learned from the verse, "when a man consecrates his house as holy" - just as his house is in his possession, etc. And this matter, that a Jew's chametz in a non-Jew's domain is not transgressed by bal yera'eh, is also learned from the verse "in your houses" - just as your house is in your possession, etc., excluding a Jew's chametz in a non-Jew's domain, which is his but not in his possession, as is written explicitly in the Mechilta and brought in the early and later authorities. If so, both laws are learned from one word, from the language of "house." Therefore, either way: if this matter in our case is considered in his possession regarding bal yera'eh, it should likewise be considered in his possession regarding sale; and if it is not in his possession regarding sale, it is not in his possession to transgress bal yera'eh. Therefore, either way, it is permitted to sell it to a non-Jew in such a way that the non-Jew will stand in his place to acquire it if he wins it. For extra measure, I gave him a remedy: after the sale he should say before three people that he makes it ownerless and despairs of it completely, accepting the loss of money. Therefore, although from the angle of ownerless designation it would not help if it is not in his possession, for if it is not in his possession to sell it, it is not in his possession to make it ownerless, since their laws are equal, nevertheless it will help through the language of despair. Despair is certainly effective for something not in one's possession, for the essential law of despair is so regarding robbery or theft. Therefore, either way: if it is in his possession, he has already sold it and his sale works; and if it is not in his possession, despair is effective for it and it leaves his domain completely through despair. Therefore, from the angle of despair, two people would suffice, and three are not required; but to satisfy all concerns I instructed him also to make it actually ownerless, according to the law of ownerless designation, which requires three. By this he satisfies all views, with Hashem's help. Even with a pledge in such a case, although the pledger cannot generally sell it, here it is an either-way argument, for biblically he does not transgress bal yera'eh, only rabbinically, because it appears like his. If so, either way he can sell it in this case and it is effective. Examine this, for I have been brief. May Hashem save me from errors and teach us wonders in His Torah. See the later authorities regarding what they concluded in the laws of selling chametz, that ab initio pulling is required, etc.; see there. See Shach, Yoreh De'ah, siman 320, that the money must be given immediately at the time of sale, not afterward. In a responsum on the laws of firstborn written in 5593, I wrote that this means at the time of pulling. But this is specifically if the acquisition is not in the buyer's courtyard. If it was in his courtyard at the time the money was given, it is enough even if he gives the money afterward, at least while it is in his courtyard; see there and examine. Second: regarding the law of selling chametz to a non-Jew, it had been simple in my eyes that there is no need for it to be written in the law books of the king, may his glory be exalted, since it is only to remove a prohibition. But now, when I studied Eruvin 62, where it says that R. Chisda said we require a strong rental and R. Sheshes said a weak rental, etc., and the Talmud concludes: what is a strong rental? With seals and royal officers. Tosafos and the Rosh there explained that this means with the seal of kings and the agents of the officers; see there. If so, it is proven that although the rental is only to remove a prohibition, nevertheless we require a strong rental according to the law of the kingdom. If so, although R. Sheshes holds that a weak rental is enough, perhaps that is specifically regarding eruvin, where many leniencies were made, as the sages said in many places that many leniencies were made in eruvin more than in other rabbinic prohibitions. Also, according to many poskim, eruvin has no root in Torah law; not only according to those who hold that beyond twelve mil is also rabbinic, but even according to those who hold that beyond twelve mil is biblical, nevertheless it is not considered to have a Torah root, as I wrote elsewhere. See Hagahos Ashri at the end of the first chapter of Eruvin; see there. Therefore they were lenient there. But who says to be lenient regarding chametz, whose root is biblical, and where they imposed many stringencies because of the stringency of chametz? Perhaps everyone agrees that a strong rental and sale is required. It is preferable to minimize dispute. Who says to be lenient about this? Therefore it appears that the main view is that it should be written in the courts, and it requires examination why the custom is not so. Examine this. Third: whether an acquisition by admission is effective regarding prohibition, see Ketzos HaChoshen, siman 194, and what I wrote in my large work on the laws of Pesach, siman 448. Now it appears to me that there is a clear proof that admission is effective, from Gittin, chapter 3, page 30b, in the passage there: the sages taught, if a Jew said to a Levi, "I have ma'aser of yours in my possession, etc." Abaye said: this is what it means; see there. That passage is difficult: how can the Levi separate terumas ma'aser when he has not yet acquired it? Secondly, why should the homeowner's courtyard not acquire it for him? The early authorities already struggled with this, as brought in the later commentators and in Tiv Gittin on Gittin, recently printed. In my view, it is settled well. Since he said to him, "I have a kor of ma'aser of yours in my possession," this is an admission that he has ma'aser of his in his possession. Through this admission, it is acquired by him and becomes his, and he can separate it for another place. It is proven from here that admission is effective regarding prohibition and creates an acquisition. This also resolves the second question. Certainly according to Taz in Choshen Mishpat, siman 189, that the courtyard of a depositary does not acquire for him from the depositor, this is well understood: since the Jew admitted that he has ma'aser belonging to the Levi in his possession, it is as though the ma'aser is in the Levi's hand, and therefore the courtyard does not acquire for him. Even according to those who disagree with him, see Ketzos HaChoshen, siman 194, nevertheless here it is settled. One can say that although admission creates acquisition, nevertheless it is ineffective for priestly gifts, since the Talmud says at the beginning of Bava Metzia that exchange is a form of sale and does not work for priestly gifts, because the verse writes "giving" regarding them. But agav karka is merely giving; see there. It is also proven there that giving to his courtyard is also considered giving for priestly gifts. If so, here too, since every Jew is presumed fit, and we are witnesses that he wants to fulfill the mitzvah of giving, although he says to him, "I have a kor of ma'aser of yours in my possession," thereby making an acquisition for the Levi, nevertheless that is a form of sale and does not fulfill the mitzvah of giving. Therefore, we are witnesses that he admits to him in an effective manner, and his intent is that he transfers to him the courtyard where the kor of ma'aser rests. If so, it is as though he gave it to the Levi's courtyard, and through this he fulfills the mitzvah of giving, like the incident of Rabban Gamliel and the elders at the beginning of Bava Metzia. According to this, although generally a depositary's courtyard acquires for him, that is where he did not transfer the place to the depositor, so it remains the depositary's courtyard. But here, where we are witnesses that through this admission he also transferred the place to him, and conversely the Levi sold him only the ma'aser but did not sell him its place, since there is no mitzvah then there is no presumption to say that he went back and transferred the place. He sold him only what he specified, the body of the ma'aser and not the place. If so, the place of the ma'aser remains the Levi's, and therefore it does not acquire the produce for the depositary. This is a great proof, with Hashem's help, to the above law and to the resolution of the passage. Through this one can also resolve the language of the Yerushalmi brought in Sefer Tiv Gittin on Gittin in the above passage. This is the Yerushalmi's language in Terumos, chapter 1: R. Yochanan in the name of R. Yannai: if a Jew said to a Levi, "A kor of ma'aser, etc.," and the Levi went and made it terumas ma'aser for another place, and the Jew went back and gave it to another Levi, he has only a grievance against him. This applies where this one gave it before that one made it terumas ma'aser. But if that one made it terumas ma'aser before this one gave it, is this not like the Mishnah: if before he separated he canceled it, it is canceled; but if after he separated he canceled it, his terumah is terumah? "Is it all from him?" They explain this as an expression of "who gave him the power?" It resolves it according to R. Yosi, for R. Yosi says that if the homeowner separated the ma'aser, what he did is done. This is the end of the language; see there in Tiv Gittin. The commentators' main explanation there, that the Yerushalmi at first means it through the Jew's agency, is not, in my view, its intent. See what he asked, that anything one cannot do oneself one cannot appoint an agent for; and he himself explained that this is indeed the Talmud's challenge there, "is it in his power?" But in my view, the Yerushalmi at first means as follows. It is true that admission creates acquisition, but that is specifically when he admits before witnesses in a manner where there is no claim of jesting or avoiding appearing wealthy. But if he admitted not before witnesses, he can retract, because speech comes and cancels speech. This is the Yerushalmi's intent: if this one gave it before that one made it terumas ma'aser, then since it is only speech, that he admitted to him that he has a kor of ma'aser of his in his possession, he can later retract and say, "I was jesting with you," for speech comes and cancels speech, similar to what is taught regarding an agent: if he canceled before he separated, because speech comes and cancels speech. So too here. But if that one first made it ma'aser and only afterward he gave it away, then an act has already been done, and speech does not come and cancel an act. Therefore it asks: who gave the Levi power to make an act? It is not his, so how does his act help such that the homeowner cannot retract from his speech? It answers according to R. Yosi, that the homeowner can separate ma'aser. If so, here too, as long as the homeowner did not retract, the Levi is doing his agency, and it is as though the homeowner made him an agent, and speech no longer comes and cancels the act. This is well resolved. I also have another proof from what the Talmud says in Gittin, that a slave who married in the presence of his master, or whom he allowed to put on tefillin, goes free; see the Talmud there, 39. This is difficult: one who makes his slave ownerless still requires a bill of emancipation. Here, although it is like an admission, that would be only regarding money; why should it help regarding prohibition? Perforce, admission creates the acquisition even for prohibition and is like writing him a bill of emancipation. Examine this carefully.
