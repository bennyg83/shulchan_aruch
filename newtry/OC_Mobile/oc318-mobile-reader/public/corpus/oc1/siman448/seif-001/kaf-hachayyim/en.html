1) [Seif 1] Chametz belonging to a non-Jew, etc. The Yerushalmi in Perek Kol Sha'ah states: a convert who died leaving chametz, and Pesach passed over it, whoever first takes possession of it after Pesach may use it according to all opinions. This does not contradict our Talmud, Bava Kamma 110b, regarding one who stole the chametz of a convert and retained it through Pesach, where benefit is prohibited. There the convert did not die until the prohibition of chametz had already taken effect; alternatively, even if the convert died before Pesach, the chametz was in the robber's possession when the prohibition took effect. Here, however, after the convert died the chametz remained ownerless, so no one violated bal yeraeh, for the Torah says that you may not see what is yours, but you may see ownerless chametz. Chok Yaakov, letter 1; Mekor Chaim, letter 1; Chayei Adam, rule 124, letter 24. The same applies to any ownerless chametz, which is permitted after Pesach. 2) There, "chametz belonging to a non-Jew," etc. The entire world customarily permits chametz belonging to a non-Jew that remained through Pesach, and no one is careful to avoid it. Chametz belonging to a Jew, however, is prohibited for benefit even if the Jew nullified it. Responsa Radbaz, volume 1, siman 486; Birkei Yosef, letter 1. This excludes Baal HaIttur, as stated below, letter 7. See also below, seif 5. 3) There, "chametz belonging to a non-Jew," etc. If a Jew and a non-Jew jointly own chametz and it remained through Pesach, although the Jew's half is prohibited for benefit, when they divide it after Pesach the non-Jew's share is permitted for benefit. Since chametz that remained through Pesach is prohibited only rabbinically as a penalty, we apply bereirah. Whether it is permitted for eating requires study. Chukat HaPesach, letter 1, beginning "It appears," however, writes that it is permitted even for eating, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes likewise. The Jew's share is certainly prohibited for benefit. Chok Yaakov, letter 2; Eliyah Rabbah, letter 1; Yad Aharon in Hagahot HaTur; Mekor Chaim, letter 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, letter 27; Chayei Adam there, letter 25. Mekor Chaim considers whether the non-Jew's share is permitted specifically when it was kept in the non-Jew's house, and concludes that it is permitted even if kept in the Jew's house. Responsa Shaagat Aryeh, siman 90, permits even the Jew's share, and Machazik Berachah, letter 1, and Shaarei Teshuvah, letter 1, cite him. Although Mekor Chaim challenges him, Responsa Maharam of Rothenburg, Later Responsa, siman 9, reconciles Shaagat Aryeh's position. Responsa Zera Emet, volume 3, siman 46, permits benefit even from the Jew's share where there is a very great loss. 4) A Jew and a non-Jew who jointly own chametz and other items may not say to the non-Jew, "You take the chametz and I will take the other things." Mekor Chaim, letter 3; Chayei Adam there. Eliyah Rabbah appears lenient because chametz does not transfer its prohibition to its monetary proceeds. It is possible that one may rely upon this where there is a substantial loss. 5) Two Jews were partners in chametz. One sold to a non-Jew before Pesach the share that would fall to him, while the other did not sell. After Pesach they divided the chametz. The portion that fell to the partner who sold his share to the non-Jew before Pesach is permitted; the portion of the one who did not sell before Pesach is prohibited. Shaagat Aryeh, siman 91; Machazik Berachah, letter 2; Mekor Chaim, letter 1; Shaarei Teshuvah there. 6) There, "permitted even for eating." The Mishnah, Pesachim 28a, states that chametz belonging to a non-Jew that remained through Pesach is permitted for benefit. This does not mean that eating it is prohibited. Rather, because the Mishnah wished to state that chametz belonging to a Jew is prohibited for benefit, it used the parallel expression regarding a non-Jew that it is permitted for benefit. Rif and Rosh. In the name of the Yerushalmi, Rosh writes that the Mishnah speaks in a place where people do not customarily eat bread of a non-Jew, but in a place where they do, it is permitted even for eating. Beit Yosef cites this. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, letter 2. See Yoreh De'ah, siman 112, seif 2, regarding which bread of a non-Jew is customarily treated leniently. 7) There, "permitted even for eating." This is evident from the Talmud, as stated in the preceding letter. Tur, however, cites Baal HaIttur as prohibiting eating it. We have already written above, letter 2, that the universal custom is to permit it.
