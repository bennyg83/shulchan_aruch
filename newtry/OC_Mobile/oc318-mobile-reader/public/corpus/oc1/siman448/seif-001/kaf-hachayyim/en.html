1) [Se'if 1] Chametz belonging to a non-Jew, etc. The Yerushalmi in the chapter Kol Sha'ah teaches: If a convert died and left chametz, and Pesach passed over it, whoever first takes possession of it after Pesach may use it according to all opinions. This does not contradict the Babylonian Talmud in Bava Kama 110b, which states that one who stole a convert's chametz and retained it over Pesach may not derive benefit from it. There, the convert did not die until the prohibition of chametz had already taken effect. Alternatively, even if the convert died before Pesach, the chametz was in the robber's possession when the prohibition took effect. Here, however, when the convert died the chametz remained ownerless. Consequently, no one violated the prohibition against chametz being seen, for the verse excludes ownerless chametz just as it excludes chametz belonging to others. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1; Mekor Chaim, subsection 1; Chayei Adam, rule 124, subsection 24. The same therefore applies to any ownerless chametz, which is permitted after Pesach.
2) There. Chametz belonging to a non-Jew, etc. Everyone customarily permits chametz belonging to a non-Jew that remained over Pesach, and no one avoids it. Chametz belonging to a Jew, however, is prohibited for benefit even if the Jew nullified it. Responsa Radbaz, volume 1, siman 486; Birkei Yosef, subsection 1. This excludes the opinion of Baal HaIttur, as stated below in subsection 7. See there. See also below, se'if 5.
3) There. Chametz belonging to a non-Jew, etc. If a Jew shares ownership of chametz with a non-Jew and it remained over Pesach, the Jew's half is prohibited for benefit. Nevertheless, when they divide it after Pesach, the non-Jew's share is permitted for benefit. Since chametz that remained over Pesach is prohibited only rabbinically as a penalty, we apply retroactive designation. Whether it is permitted for consumption requires further consideration. Chukas HaPesach, subsection 1, beginning with the words 'It appears,' however, writes that it is permitted even for consumption. See there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes likewise. The Jew's share is certainly prohibited for benefit. Chok Yaakov, subsection 2; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1; Yad Aharon in Hagahos HaTur; Mekor Chaim, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 27; Chayei Adam, there, subsection 25. Mekor Chaim there considers whether the non-Jew's share is permitted only when it was kept in the non-Jew's house, and concludes that it is permitted even when kept in the Jew's house. See there. Responsa Sha'agas Aryeh, siman 90, however, permits even the Jew's share. Machazik Berachah, subsection 1, and Sha'arei Teshuvah, subsection 1, cite this ruling. Although Mekor Chaim in the explanations there challenges it, Responsa Maharam of Rothenburg, Later Responsa, siman 9, reconciles the position of Sha'agas Aryeh. See there. Responsa Zera Emes, volume 3, siman 46, also permits benefit even from the Jew's share where a very substantial loss is involved. See there.
4) A Jew and a non-Jew who jointly own chametz and other items may not say to the non-Jew, 'You take the chametz and I will take the other items.' Mekor Chaim, subsection 3; Chayei Adam, there. Eliyah Rabbah there, however, appears to rule leniently because chametz does not transfer its prohibition to the money received for it. See there. It is possible that one may rely upon this where a substantial loss is involved.
5) Two Jews were partners in chametz. One sold the share that would be allocated to that Jew to a non-Jew before Pesach, while the other did not sell. After Pesach they divided the chametz. The portion allocated to the one who sold a share to the non-Jew before Pesach is permitted. The portion of the one who did not sell before Pesach is prohibited. Sha'agas Aryeh, siman 91; Machazik Berachah, subsection 2; Mekor Chaim, subsection 1; Sha'arei Teshuvah, there.
6) There. It is permitted even for consumption. The Mishnah states in Pesachim 28a that chametz belonging to a non-Jew that remained over Pesach is permitted for benefit. This does not mean that consumption is prohibited. Rather, because the Mishnah wished to state that chametz belonging to a Jew is prohibited for benefit, it used the corresponding formulation that chametz belonging to a non-Jew is permitted for benefit. Rif and Rosh. In the name of the Yerushalmi, Rosh writes that the Mishnah's formulation applies in a place where people do not customarily eat bread baked by a non-Jew. In a place where they do customarily eat it, the chametz is permitted even for consumption. Beis Yosef cites his words. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2. See Yoreh De'ah, siman 112, se'if 2, regarding which bread baked by a non-Jew is customarily treated leniently.
7) There. It is permitted even for consumption. This is evident from the Talmud, as stated in the preceding subsection. Tur, however, cites Baal HaIttur as prohibiting consumption. We already stated above in subsection 2 that everyone customarily permits it. See there.
