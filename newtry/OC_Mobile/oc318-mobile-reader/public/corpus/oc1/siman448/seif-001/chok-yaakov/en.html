Belonging to a non-Jew. The Yerushalmi in the chapter Kol Shaah states: If a convert died and left chametz, and Pesach passed over it, whoever first takes possession of it after Pesach may use it according to all opinions. Do not mistakenly think this contradicts our Talmud in Bava Kamma 110b concerning one who stole a convert’s chametz and Pesach passed over it, where benefit is forbidden. There the convert had not died before the prohibition took effect, or even if he died before Pesach, the chametz was in the thief’s possession when the prohibition began. Here, by contrast, when the convert died, the chametz remained ownerless. No one therefore violated bal yera’eh, for the Torah forbids seeing what is yours, but permits seeing ownerless chametz.
A non-Jew. It appears to me that where a Jew and a non-Jew jointly own chametz that remained over Pesach, the Jew’s half is forbidden in benefit, but after they divide it the non-Jew’s share is permitted in benefit like any chametz belonging to a non-Jew. We do not say that there is no bereirah. Since chametz that remained over Pesach is forbidden only D’Rabbanan as a penalty and has no underlying Torah prohibition at that stage, we apply bereirah. Whether it is permitted for eating requires study; see the beginning of siman 467. This follows Beitzah 38a. See Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 5, siman 32, concerning bereirah. The Jew’s share is certainly forbidden, because even chametz owned jointly is forbidden in benefit, as stated above at the end of siman 445.
Even for eating. This is agreed upon by the Gemara and all the authorities. Tur, however, cites Baal HaIttur as stringent. His stringency is merely a protective fence, as Tur himself concludes. Sefer Tanya and Shibbolei HaLeket, klal 7, siman 62, likewise cite Baal HaDibros explicitly in the language of Baal HaIttur and explain it as a mere fence. It appears that Baal HaIttur is the author called Baal HaDibros, as stated in Shabbos Kallah, page 49, and in She’eilos Yaavetz, that Riva authored both. Although all authorities agree in practice that no stringency is required, and Shibbolei HaLeket itself says so there, I have written this because Bach found Baal HaIttur’s words difficult in light of several sugyos, and Taz strained to reconcile them. In truth, there was never a difficulty.
