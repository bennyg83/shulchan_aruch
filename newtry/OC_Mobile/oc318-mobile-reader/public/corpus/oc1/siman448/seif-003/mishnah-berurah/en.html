[3] (7) Forbidden for benefit. For the sages penalized him since he transgressed bal yera'eh and bal yimatzei [Gemara].
[3] (8) He left it inadvertently. Meaning that he did not know about that chametz.
[3] (9) Or under unavoidable circumstances. This means that he knew about it but was under unavoidable circumstances and could not destroy it. Even though in this case he did not transgress bal yera'eh, nevertheless we penalize permitted cases because of prohibited ones, for if we permit it to him, he may come to leave it intentionally ab initio and transgress regarding it. See Biur Halacha. Beis Hillel in Yoreh De'ah, siman 132, wrote: I was asked about a Jew who before Pesach had grain at a non-Jew's mill to grind, and the grain was delayed at the mill until Chol HaMoed of Pesach. When the seventh day of Pesach came, the non-Jew went and ground the Jew's grain, made bread from the flour, baked the bread, and brought the bread to the Jew immediately after Pesach. I answered that it is permitted to take payment for that bread from the non-Jew, and that non-Jew should eat it or sell it to a non-Jew; see there. Chok Yaakov agreed that it is permitted even for eating, because perhaps the non-Jew exchanged the grain, so it is only doubtful chametz that remained over Pesach (see below, siman 449). Beis Meir inclines to permit eating it for another reason: although the Mechaber ruled that whether inadvertent or unavoidable it is forbidden, not all unavoidable circumstances are equal. In such an unavoidable case, where he could not at all have considered that the non-Jew would bake his flour into chametz, the sages certainly did not penalize him; see there. See in Biur Halacha what I wrote in the name of Radbaz. If a Jew left wheat in a mill and a non-Jew came and told the Jew that he had soaked and ground it, see Chok Yaakov in the name of Chinuch Beis Yehudah, that there are many doubts here for leniency. Pri Megadim questions this where it is the practice of the local people to soak wheat before grinding, and he inclines that the non-Jew should exchange it for other wheat.
[3] (10) And if he sold it, etc. Which modes of acquisition a non-Jew uses to acquire will be written below at the end of the se'if.
[3] (11) To a non-Jew. It is forbidden to sell to an apostate Jew, for his law is like that of a Jew in every respect, and one also transgresses lifnei iver; the chametz is forbidden for benefit after Pesach. After the fact, if he sold to an apostate Jew for a small amount because he recognizes him and knows that he will return it to him after Pesach, since he erred and thought that an apostate's law is like that of a non-Jew, in a case of great loss one may permit him to tell the apostate to exchange the chametz with a non-Jew for other chametz, or to sell it, and those exchanged items or the money will be permitted, as explained at the end of siman 443.
[3] (12) Outside the house. The later authorities wrote that there is no concern about the non-Jew himself, whether he lives in his house or not. Rather, the main concern is that the non-Jew remove the chametz from the Jew's house. As for the non-Jew himself, even if he is the Jew's servant and lives in his house, we have no concern (unless he is permanently acquired by him, for then his hand is like his master's hand, and selling to him is ineffective, since the item has not left his domain through this). As to why they required here that the chametz be removed from his domain, although he has already sold it and it is merely with him as a deposit without responsibility, and in general in such a case we require only a partition, as above in siman 440, Chok Yaakov wrote that here too the law is so. They required here that he remove it from his domain only so that the non-Jew acquire the chametz by pulling; and indeed, if the non-Jew already removed the chametz and acquired it by pulling to his house or to a side alley according to law, he may afterward bring the chametz into the Jew's house, and a partition of ten handbreadths alone is enough. But several later authorities wrote that here it is worse, for since the non-Jew knows that the main chametz belongs to the Jew and will not touch it, even though he acquired it, if it remains in the Jew's house it appears like the Jew's chametz. There is also concern that he may come to eat it, because since it was originally his, he does not keep away from it. According to this view, even if he designated a separate corner in his house or made a partition of ten handbreadths, it also does not help. All this, however, is only ab initio. After the fact, it is obvious that one should not forbid it at all, and even if he did not designate a place for it, as long as the sale was according to law. The later authorities wrote that if he has much chametz and it is impossible to remove it from his house, he should also sell him the room, and it is like selling it to him outside his house. Acquisition of the room is by money together with a document. If he fears giving him a document lest he decide to take possession of the chametz, and there is great loss, he should stipulate with him that he acquire it with money alone without a document, and such a stipulation is effective according to several poskim; one may rely on this leniently in such a case. He must hand him the key to the room in which the chametz is located so that he can enter whenever he wants and take his chametz that was sold or given to him. Or at least the Jew should say to him: whenever you want, you can take the key to your room and enter it to take your chametz. It is an absolute prohibition to leave any seal or lock on the room or on the chametz so that the non-Jew cannot take the chametz, for this proves that the whole sale was merely evasion. If he did so from the outset, at the time he sold it, the chametz is forbidden even after the fact, because the non-Jew did not rely on the sale. Nevertheless, if at first he sold it according to law and afterward placed a lock on the room or a seal on the chametz, although it is forbidden to do so, the chametz is not forbidden because of what he did improperly after the sale. See Biur Halacha, where we wrote in the name of Pri Megadim and Chayei Adam distinctions in this law.
[3] (13) And he will go back and give it to him. If the non-Jew does not wish to return it to him, it is forbidden to sue him in their courts or compel him by other forms of compulsion. But if he gave him only part of the money and the rest was recorded as a loan, and he does not wish to pay the remainder, it is certainly permitted to sue him in their courts so that he pay what is owed, or give him the chametz as payment for his debt. The later authorities wrote that another Jew is forbidden to buy the chametz from the non-Jew after Pesach. If he bought it, he must return it to the original owners. If he paid the non-Jew more than what is proper to pay in such a case, the original owner need return to him only the amount that is proper to pay.
[3] (14) Provided that he give it, etc. Even through his agent or his wife he can transfer ownership of the chametz to the non-Jew, but not through a non-Jew, for there is no agency for a non-Jew, and his sale is nothing. Likewise, he cannot transfer it to the non-Jew's agent, because the Torah included agency only for a Jew. This is so even if the agent is also a non-Jew, for there is no agency for a non-Jew, whether from a non-Jew to a Jew or from a non-Jew to a non-Jew. See in Biur Halacha what I wrote in this regard in the name of several poskim.
[3] (15) A complete gift. Acquisition by gift is through lifting, or through pulling in the case of an item that cannot be lifted.
[3] (16) Without any condition. For perhaps the non-Jew will violate the condition, and the gift will be void retroactively, and he will have transgressed bal yera'eh, as below in se'if 4.
[3] (17) Or that he sell it to him. Regarding which acquisitions a non-Jew uses to acquire, there are different views among the poskim. Some say that a non-Jew's acquisition is specifically by money. Others say that a non-Jew acquires only through pulling into his domain, or by lifting an item that can be lifted. Others wrote further that other acquisitions also apply to a non-Jew, such as acquisition of movable goods together with land, acquisition through a courtyard, and exchange acquisition, meaning kinyan sudar. One must be careful with this that the sudar belong to the non-Jew, and it is not enough for the witnesses to the acquisition to give their sudar as with a Jew, because that works only by the principle of acquiring on behalf of the transferor, and regarding a non-Jew there is no law of acquiring on another's behalf. Some question these acquisitions and say that we find them only regarding a Jew. As a matter of law, ab initio he certainly must transfer it through a complete acquisition that is agreed upon by all, namely with money and pulling together, since this touches on the biblical prohibition of bal yera'eh. At the very least, pulling alone is sufficient, for through this he also fulfills the view of most poskim. But after the fact, even if he sold it only with money, or sold it with one of the other acquisitions we mentioned, and Pesach passed over it, it is permitted. Since chametz after Pesach is forbidden only because of a rabbinic penalty, and furthermore, since chametz after the time of its prohibition is in any case no longer in his domain, except that Scripture made it as though it were in his domain so that he transgresses regarding it, any acquisition is enough, for at least he reveals his intent that he does not want it and has removed it from his domain. In a place where it is impossible to transfer it by pulling, such as where there is much chametz or it is on the road, this will be explained below.
[3] (18) A complete sale. If a Jew leaves his chametz in the room of his fellow, who is selling his chametz, he must inform the Jewish seller and make him an agent to sell it. If he left it without his knowledge, it is forbidden after Pesach, since the Jew did not know to transfer ownership and the non-Jew did not know to acquire it [later authorities]. They wrote further that a person may say to a non-Jew: here is this chametz, and after Pesach you will give me other chametz.
[3] (19) For a small amount. There is no concern for voiding the sale because of underpricing, for he himself knows that it is worth more and agrees to transfer it to him so that it not remain in his domain and become forbidden for benefit. It is better for him to sell it to his acquaintances, because in that case presumably the non-Jew will return it to him after Pesach. See Biur Halacha regarding our present custom. The later authorities wrote that although ab initio one should sell with money and pulling, nevertheless, if there is much chametz and it is impossible to pull all the chametz, and likewise if the chametz is not here so that the non-Jew can pull it, but is in another city or on the road, he should transfer it to the non-Jew through other acquisitions, such as through a mark, where in some places the custom is that the buyer marks the acquired item so that he has a known sign on it, and through this he acquires it, or through a handshake, called "tzu shlag," meaning that they strike their palms against one another, or through giving a perutah in places where the custom is that when the buyer gives a perutah to the seller the sale is completed, or in a place where the custom is that handing over the key completes the sale. In any case, however, he must say, "I hereby sell you such-and-such chametz for such-and-such sum," unlike what the masses say, "I hereby sell you the key," for that is not considered anything. So too, anything like this, each place according to the merchants' custom, is considered an acquisition according to Torah law, and one may properly transfer the chametz through such an acquisition as well. Or he may sell the chametz through agav, meaning that he should sell the non-Jew land or a room, or rent it to him, and together with it say to him, "Acquire the chametz that I have in the place of the land, or wherever it is," for through agav he acquires even if the items are not piled there. The land is acquired by the non-Jew with money and a document, and for rental, money alone is enough. Even if the non-Jew does not give him all the money, but only a few coins, called "auf gab," that is enough, and the Jew should stipulate with him that with this auf gab he acquires the room and the chametz, while the rest of the money is recorded as a loan. They wrote further that if the house where the chametz is located is not his, but is rented by him, then his sale is ineffective, for how can he sell something that is not his? Rather, he should rent that room to the non-Jew, and rent it to him without specification, so that he may keep his vessels and movable goods there, and he should not explicitly say that he is renting it to place chametz inside it, as below in siman 450. A Jew who bought chametz from a non-Jew and gave money but has not yet pulled it: although ab initio he certainly should sell it before Pesach, for according to several poskim money acquires between a Jew and a non-Jew, nevertheless, if he did not sell it and Pesach passed over it, it is permitted, for according to most poskim he acquires only by pulling. But if he pulled it, even if he did not give money, and Pesach passed over it, one should forbid it. However, if according to the merchants' custom acquisition is made with money, Pri Megadim inclines to be stringent even in the first case; see Sha'arei Teshuvah, se'if katan 9.
[3] (20) On condition to return. Meaning after Pesach.
[3] (21) It is ineffective. Even though throughout the Torah a gift on condition to return is considered a gift if the condition is fulfilled, here the sages required a complete gift because of the stringency of chametz. Regarding after the fact, if the condition was fulfilled and he returned it, whether it is permitted is disputed by the later authorities; see Biur Halacha. Where he said, "I hereby give it to you and you will return it to me," see Biur Halacha.
