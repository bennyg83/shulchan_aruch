It is forbidden in benefit. This is stated in the Mishnah in Pesachim 28a and agreed upon by all authorities. It is surprising that Piskei Tosafot at the beginning of Beitzah, siman 6, states that chametz after Pesach is permitted in benefit. Perhaps it refers to chametz belonging to a non-Jew, but that too is an explicit Mishnah in Pesachim.
Or he was compelled. See Maggid Mishneh, chapter 1 of Hilchos Chametz, who asks for Rambam’s source for this ruling. In Toras HaShelamim, question 6, I supplied another source; see there.
He gives it. This means to a non-Jew whom he knows. Otherwise, giving a gift is prohibited under “lo sechanem,” as explained in Yoreh Deah 151:11. Alternatively, it may be permitted to give it even to a stranger because it has no value to the Jew, since benefit is forbidden. With this reasoning my accomplished son, Rabbi Shimon, while still young, resolved Tosafot’s words in Eruvin 64b, s.v. “And we learned,” as to why Tosafot did not ask this question earlier. Study it carefully.
To a non-Jew. Taz writes that this applies even if the non-Jew is his servant, meaning one who is not his Canaanite slave. Shaar Ephraim, Orach Chaim, responsum 3, writes that a Canaanite slave is considered the master’s own domain. One cannot answer that the master desires acquisition only of permitted objects, not prohibited ones, because the slave’s hand is like the master’s hand. Giving it to the slave does not remove it from the master’s possession at all. The same principle applies to acquisition for an eruv in Eruvin 79b. This differs from Yoreh Deah 169:9 in the gloss, where according to the view that a non-Jew has no agency, one may give collateral to his slave or maidservant to pledge with a Jew for interest. There, since the lender does not receive the interest directly from the Jewish borrower, the defining form of interest is absent. Here acquisition is required, and no independent acquisition by the slave occurs.
Outside the house. Terumas HaDeshen, simanim 119 and 120, means that the chametz should be placed outside the Jew’s house. The later authorities explain that otherwise it resembles a deposit left with the Jew. In my view, the primary reason is simpler: how does the non-Jew acquire the gift without removing the chametz from the Jew’s domain? In a sale as well, according to most authorities a non-Jew acquires movable property through meshichah, not money alone. Therefore, initially the non-Jew should give money and also perform meshichah into an alley or his domain, or the Jew should transfer ownership of a room and, together with the land, the chametz within it. The room should not function merely as the non-Jew’s courtyard, because courtyard acquisition derives from agency and a non-Jew lacks halachic agency. Although acquisition of land ordinarily requires a document, the parties may stipulate that money or chazakah alone will acquire it. After the fact, meshichah alone is effective without money, as in a gift, and money alone may also suffice regarding chametz because any minimally valid acquisition is adequate once the Jew has revealed that he does not wish to retain rights in it. Commercial customs, such as marking the goods, clasping hands, giving a coin, or delivering the key, also acquire where merchants customarily rely upon them, as explained in Choshen Mishpat 201. Merely saying, “I sell you the key,” is ineffective if the chametz itself is not sold. The chametz should be kept where the non-Jew can enter, and the key should be in his possession. If, at the time of sale, the Jew locked it away so that the non-Jew could not reach it, the sale is a mere subterfuge and even after the fact the chametz should be forbidden. If, however, the sale was initially complete and the Jew only afterward added his own lock or seal, this is improper and should be protested, but after the fact the chametz is not forbidden. The non-Jew may remove the lock whenever he wishes because he lawfully purchased the chametz. If he refuses to return it after Pesach, the Jew may not sue him in their courts, though the Jew may repurchase it from him after Pesach. A Jew who sells chametz to another Jew who openly transgresses may, in a case of substantial loss, instruct the transgressor to exchange it with or sell it to a non-Jew, and the money is permitted. One should therefore avoid purchasing beer or spirits immediately after Pesach from a Jewish public transgressor, because it may be chametz that remained over Pesach. The presumption that transgressors exchange prohibited chametz for permitted chametz applies only to one who sins for appetite, not one who sins defiantly. See Magen Avraham regarding agency from one non-Jew to another, and Beis Shmuel at the end of siman 5 and Yoreh Deah, siman 164, concerning acquisition by one non-Jew for another. The matter requires study.
A complete gift. One may transfer the chametz even through his agent or wife. We do not say that a wife lacks power to convey an item that will later return to her, as concluded in Gittin 19. Chinuch Beis Yehudah, responsum 13, records a person who placed his chametz in another Jew’s room, knowing that the owner would sell the room and its chametz to a non-Jew. The owner indeed sold the room and delivered the key, but neither the seller nor buyer knew about the other Jew’s chametz. He ruled that this chametz was forbidden, and explained his reasons at length.
For a small amount. There is no concern that the sale is void because of gross underpayment. We are certain that the Jew fully intends to transfer ownership in order not to violate bal yera’eh and bal yimatzei. Tosafot write similarly in Avodah Zarah 41a, s.v. “Rav Ashi,” regarding a firstborn animal, and in Bava Metzia 30b, s.v. “He removed.”
A gift on condition that it be returned is ineffective. This is only a special stringency of chametz and should not be extended to other matters. Magen Avraham writes that all the more so where one says, “I give it to you and you shall return it to me,” it is a complete gift and is legally permitted, though one should be stringent because of chametz. His words are exceedingly difficult. Rama in Yoreh Deah 305:8 rules that when the giver says, “and return it to me,” it is not a gift at all, based upon Rashba, responsum 198. Only the formulation “on condition,” which operates immediately, creates a gift. See Rashba, responsum 186. This requires study.
