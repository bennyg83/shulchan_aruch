[3] Outside the house. This means that he should also take the chametz outside the house, for if it remains in his house, he appears to be accepting the non-Jew's chametz as a deposit. But by strict law it is permitted, since the Jew is not responsible for it. However, if, should the chametz be lost, the Jew would have to return the sale money to the non-Jew, it is forbidden. If he has much chametz and it is impossible to take it outside the house, he should also sell him the room. But since we hold that a non-Jew does not acquire land with money alone, but only with a document, he should stipulate with him at the beginning of the transaction that he transfers it to him with money alone, as written in Choshen Mishpat, siman 194 and siman 207. He must hand him the key so that he can enter there, for otherwise this is mere evasion (Bach). See siman 450, se'if 5, and see Shlah. Mas'as Binyamin, siman 59, wrote that one must transfer it to him through money and pulling, or through transferring the place, as written in Yoreh De'ah, siman 320. But after the fact, he acquired it with money alone, because most poskim hold like R. Shimon, and so it also appears in Choshen Mishpat, siman 194. Furthermore, since the chametz is not in his domain, except that the Torah placed it in his domain, a disclosure of intent is enough, as written in Beis Yosef, siman 435; this is his language. It sounds there that even if he did not hand him the key it is permitted after the fact. In siman 97 he wrote that if he transferred it to him by pulling alone without money, he acquired it, and if the non-Jew sent his agent and he pulled it, he also acquired it. Even though there is no agency for a non-Jew, that applies to a non-Jew for a Jew or the reverse, but a non-Jew can make another non-Jew an agent. This requires examination, for in the Yerushalmi at the beginning of chapter 6 of Demai it states that a non-Jew cannot make his fellow non-Jew an agent; see there. So too is the plain meaning of the Talmud at the beginning of the second chapter of Kiddushin. Therefore one should not be lenient about this. Any mode by which merchants are accustomed to acquire, such as a mark, a key, giving his palm to his fellow, or a perutah, also acquires here. All the more so regarding an acquisition between a non-Jew and a Jew, for in their law it is simple that once he has taken the merchandise home and also given part of the money, the transaction stands (Mas'as Binyamin in the cited siman). What Mas'as Binyamin wrote, that we follow their laws, etc., is somewhat difficult, for we say at the beginning of chapter 4 of Bechoros: if a non-Jew gave money to a Jew, according to their laws he acquired, etc. What does "according to their laws" mean? According to the laws that the Torah prescribed for them. This implies that non-Jewish civil laws are not effective. So too it appears in Tosafos, Avodah Zarah 71, and so too it appears in Yoreh De'ah, siman 320, that we follow Torah law and not their laws. There is no proof from what is written in siman 441 regarding asmachta, because there it is different: since in their law it is not an asmachta, he fully resolves and transfers it. But for the acquisition itself, we require Torah law. However, he must say, "I hereby sell you the chametz," unlike the common people who say, "I hereby sell you the key," for that is nothing. Even after the fact the chametz is forbidden, since he did not sell it at all. In siman 43 he wrote that if another Jew bought the chametz from the non-Jew after Pesach, he must return it to the owners, and the owner does not have to reimburse him if he paid the non-Jew more than its proper value. The reason is that we say: he made it ownerless with the non-Jew in mind, but he did not make it ownerless with everyone in mind. But if the non-Jew does not wish to return it to him, it is forbidden to sue him in their courts.
[3] It is ineffective. Even though elsewhere it is considered a gift, as stated in the first chapter of Kiddushin and in the third chapter of Sukkah, because of the stringency of chametz it is ineffective. This is an extra stringency, and we should not add to it by comparing another matter to it (Terumas HaDeshen and Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 6 of Shabbos). Radbaz, part 1, siman 140, wrote the reason: as long as the condition is pending, it is like a deposit; and even if the condition is fulfilled, so that it is found retroactively that the chametz belonged to the non-Jew, nevertheless before the condition was fulfilled the Jew had already transgressed regarding it, and fulfillment of the condition will not help to uproot the prohibition that he already transgressed. This is his language (Kenesses HaGedolah). This is not comparable to what is written in siman 441 regarding a non-Jew who lent money to a Jew against his chametz and said, "If I do not pay you by Rosh Chodesh Iyyar, it shall be yours from now," where it is permitted. For one can say that there it is as I wrote above: since it lacks money, we do not say "since," as I wrote above in the name of Ran. But here we say: since, if the condition were not fulfilled, he would transgress regarding it, now too he transgresses regarding it. However, it seems to me that this should not be compared to "since" at all. We say "since" specifically where the matter is in the Jew's power, such as since it is in his power to redeem it, so it is like his, and similarly regarding challah; see the Gemara. But here, if the non-Jew wishes, he fulfills the condition, and his gift is a gift. If so, the chametz is not in the Jew's domain on Pesach. And what he wrote, that as long as the condition is pending it is like a deposit, does not sound so regarding a pledge in siman 441; see Choshen Mishpat, siman 241. It seems to me that even according to Radbaz, this is specifically when he makes the gift depend on its return, by saying, "I hereby give it to you on condition that you return it to me," so that if he does not return it, his gift was not a gift. But if he says, "I hereby give it to you, and you will return it to me," so that the gift does not depend on its return, and even if he does not return it to him it is a gift, it is obvious that by strict law this is permitted, except for the stringency of chametz.
