[3] There, se'if 3. "Chametz of a Jew over which Pesach passed is forbidden for benefit, etc." Note: I will copy here several observations and novel rulings, with Hashem's help. [1] I was asked about someone who owed money to a woman and did not want to pay her. When the woman pressed him to pay what she was owed, because she needed to get married, he had mercy on her and gave her chametz as a pledge for her debt. But he placed the chametz with a third party on condition that if the match were completed as she said, the third party should give her the chametz, and if the match were not completed, the third party should return the chametz to him. Now the match was not completed and the borrower wants his chametz, but the woman refuses to give it to him, since he truly owes her the money, saying: what do I have to do with his condition? Meanwhile, the eve of Pesach arrived, and the woman wants to sell the chametz to a non-Jew according to our custom, either she or the third party selling it. The owner of the chametz protests against them selling it, saying that the chametz belongs to him. I was asked what, according to law, should be done. This is what I answered briefly. It appears clear that the woman is obligated to sell it, and that her sale is effective according to the law. Although it sounds from Taz in siman 443 that the obligation to destroy it falls on the borrower, that is only when he pledged it at the time of the loan. But when he pledged it not at the time of the loan, where the Talmud explicitly states that a creditor acquires the pledge and can betroth a woman with it and acquire through it all acquisitions, the same applies here: the creditor is obligated to sell it, and if she sells it, her sale is certainly effective. Therefore, if the pledge had not been conditional, her sale would certainly have been effective, and even if the borrower protested, it would not help. Now, although in our case the pledge was under the above condition, nevertheless, either way, the sale of the lender and the third party is effective. If his condition is ineffective because in truth it was hers, then she can seize it against the borrower's will, and it is obvious that her sale is effective. This is the main law, that a creditor acquires a pledge, as written in Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 72. If his condition is effective, then since they are obligated to return it, and when they do not want to return it their intent is to rob it, it is like one who sends his hand into a deposit, who also has the law of a robber. Since we hold that where a robber sold, the buyer acquires through despair and change of domain, and according to most poskim there is no difference whether despair came before the change of domain or afterward, here too, since he intends to rob it, and once the time of prohibition arrives the owner despairs, this is despair and change of domain, and therefore his sale to the non-Jew is effective. I later found in Sefer Mekor Chayim that he wrote similarly in our case regarding one who stole chametz and sold it to a non-Jew, that it is effective; see there. The same applies here, that her sale is effective either way, even though the borrower protests that it should not be sold. All this we wrote regarding ab initio. But regarding after the fact, if the borrower later objects that he did not sell it and challenges the woman's sale as unlawful, it appears certain that he is not believed to cause the woman to lose the chametz. Either way: if the woman's sale was lawful, then it is automatically permitted through her sale. If it is as the borrower says, that it was unlawful, then the borrower transgressed bal yera'eh and the obligation to sell it was upon him, as Taz wrote in siman 443; if so, presumably he acted lawfully and sold it. One should not say that his sale is ineffective because the borrower robbed it, and with stolen property where the owners have not despaired neither party can consecrate it. Here it is different. First, he does not intend to rob it outright, but as a pledge; and as a pledge the borrower can sell it, as Taz wrote in siman 443. See what I wrote in my novellae there to explain his words. Furthermore, here it is an either-way case: if we judge it as theft, then the robber's sale is effective because there is despair and change of domain; and if we do not judge it as theft, then the borrower can sell it, as implied by Taz's words. Since the borrower had to sell it, we are witnesses that presumably he sold it beforehand so as not to transgress bal yera'eh. Although he now says that he did not sell it, he is not believed to cause the lender loss, for a person does not render himself wicked, and he is not believed to cause loss to others, as we hold regarding "we were coerced," etc. Furthermore, since Beis Shmuel in Even HaEzer, siman 28, wrote that although with stolen property where the owners have not despaired neither party can consecrate it, nevertheless with the consent of both the consecration takes effect, the same applies to sale. If one does not agree to the other's sale, and his own sale alone would not help, he will transgress bal yera'eh. Therefore we are witnesses that each one consents in his heart to the other's sale so that he not transgress bal yera'eh, and it is as though each sold with the other's consent. Although at first he protests, we say that we are witnesses that afterward he agreed in his heart and afterward consented. One can also say that even at the time of his protest he consented, according to what Rashba wrote in his novellae to Kiddushin 23, brought in Avnei Milu'im, siman 1: where it is a complete benefit, even if he stands and protests explicitly, we do not pay attention to his protest; see there. If so, here too, being saved from transgressing the Torah prohibition of bal yera'eh is a complete benefit for him. Therefore, even though he stands and cries that he does not want to sell it, we do not pay attention to him, and we say that we are witnesses that he wants this. Therefore, in any event, this chametz is certainly permitted, and the borrower is not believed to render himself wicked and thereby cause the loss of his chametz that is in the lender's hands. Examine this; so it appears to me correct as a matter of law. [2] I was also asked regarding our times, where the law of the kingdom is to pay the king, may his glory be exalted, a certain tax on liquor. If one takes the liquor through the customs station outside the country, the king returns that payment. If on Pesach a Jew forgot to sell his liquor, so that the law requires him to destroy it and pour it out, may he keep it in order to take it outside the country so that he can receive back from the king the tax payment that he paid for the liquor? I answered that it appears forbidden. Although this is not considered deriving benefit from chametz, because he is not taking money for the chametz, but rather taking what is his, since the tax was due only if it was sold in this country, and if it is clarified that it was sold outside the country it is revealed retroactively that no payment was due from it and he is taking his own, nevertheless it appears forbidden for another reason: because he wants its continued existence. He wants it to remain in existence until he can bring it outside the country. There is an explicit proof to my words from Avodah Zarah 63: if one hired him to break a barrel of forbidden wine, what is the law? Do we say that since he wants its continued existence it is forbidden? Rashi explained: this Jew wants the barrels to remain in existence, so that they not break by themselves now, until he breaks them and receives his wages, and it is forbidden. Or perhaps, anything done to reduce impurity is proper. The Talmud concludes: anything done to reduce impurity is proper. So too we hold in Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De'ah, siman 133, that it is permitted because reducing impurity is proper. This implies that if it were not reducing impurity, it would be considered that he wants its continued existence until he breaks it. If so, there it is understandable, for if it is not broken he will not reduce impurity. But here, in the end he will pour it out, and there is no reducing of impurity in this. It is certainly forbidden because he wants its continued existence until he takes it outside the country. This is clear and correct in my humble opinion: he is obligated to pour it out immediately. Examine carefully. [3] I was also asked by the Rav of Kozienice about someone who did not sell his wheat before Pesach, and there were sprouted kernels among them that were recognizable, and it was impossible to sort them out. What is their law? I answered him that I had already shown him, when he was here, the view of Levush, brought in Turei Zahav, siman 632, that even where the prohibition is recognizable, it is nullified biblically, and only rabbinically is it not nullified; Taz wondered greatly about this. But he did not see that R. Ovadiah of Bartenura in tractate Kilayim also explained this way, and Tosafos Yom Tov likewise wondered about him as Taz wondered about Levush. In any event, we have two pillars of the world who hold that even a recognizable prohibition is nullified, and I have already discussed this at length elsewhere, with Hashem's help. We also have the dispute of Rambam and Rashba brought in Beis Yosef, Yoreh De'ah, siman 115, regarding butter of non-Jews. It is proven there that according to Rambam, where the prohibition is recognizable but impossible to sort out, it is not nullified; but according to Rashba, even where the prohibition is recognizable, whenever it is impossible to sort out it is nullified. See Taz, Yoreh De'ah, siman 104, who wrote at length about this, and this is also explained there; see there. According to this, according to Rashba here, where it is impossible to sort out, it is nullified. Therefore, regarding chametz that remained over Pesach, all these sides mentioned above are enough for us to be lenient, that it is nullified in the majority like the law of chametz that remained over Pesach. However, if possible, it is better to sell it to a non-Jew, so that it will be permitted only for benefit and not for eating. If that is impossible, it is permitted to sell it even to a Jew. But during Pesach, one must not rely on this permission; rather, he must sort out whatever possible, throwing the chametz kernels into the sea, or else throwing everything into the sea. So it appears to me correct as a matter of law, and there is no need to elaborate further. Examine this.
