[3] Chametz of a Jew. Regarding one who stole chametz and Pesach passed over it, whether the chametz is permitted to the one from whom it was stolen, see Noda BiYehudah, first edition, Orach Chayim, siman 20, who inclined to permit. The later authorities disagree with him, and their words are explicit in Itur, Hilchos Bi'ur Chametz, page 125, and in Ramban in chapter Kol Sha'ah, and in Ritva and Maharam Chalavah there, all of whom took from the passage of "one who steals" that it is forbidden according to everyone. Some later authorities have already noted the words of Ramban mentioned above. However, regarding a non-Jew who stole a Jew's chametz and Pesach passed over it, Eliyah Rabbah inclines to permit, and Pri Megadim copied this; so too wrote Maharit Algazi.
[3] Over which Pesach passed. The law of doubtful chametz over which Pesach passed will be explained below in siman 449.
[3] Even if he left it inadvertently or under unavoidable circumstances. See Mishnah Berurah. The Mechaber wrote without specifying whether he refers to a case where because of inadvertence or unavoidable circumstances he did not destroy it and also did not nullify it, and therefore he forbade it, but if he nullified it, even though he did not check because of unavoidable circumstances, it is permitted, since at least he fulfilled the main biblical law; or whether even if he nullified it and left it without destroying it because of unavoidable circumstances, it is also forbidden. The later authorities disagree about this. See below, se'if 5, regarding nullification. According to those who are stringent there when one checked and nullified and found chametz after Pesach, all the more so here, where he did not check at all, even though he was unavoidably prevented from doing so. But according to those who permit there when he checked and nullified, one can say that the same applies here: even though he did not check, nevertheless, since he was unavoidably prevented from doing so, it is no worse than one who checked and nullified and then found chametz after Pesach. So it is explicit in Responsa Toras HaShelamim and in Eliyah Rabbah. See Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 23. As a matter of law, we already wrote in se'if 5 to rely on the lenient view regarding benefit in a case of great loss, and the same applies here. See Sefer Sedei Chemed, who wrote at great length about the details of these laws. Know further that the later authorities copied a responsum of Radbaz regarding an incident where non-Jewish servants in a Jewish mill soaked wheat belonging to the owner of the mill so that it would be ready for grinding on the day after Pesach. He ruled that it is permitted to mix it with other wheat, grind everything, and sell it to a Jew. Pri Chadash wondered about this: even if they did not split open, according to the Mechaber's ruling in siman 467 that eating them as they are is at least forbidden, from now one should forbid grinding them in order to nullify them in a majority, because one may not nullify a prohibition ab initio. Sefer Mateh Yehudah answered him that since on the last Yom Tov its prohibition is only rabbinic, and soaking wheat is not forbidden by basic law but only because of custom, as below in siman 453, se'if 5, one should not forbid it here because of nullifying a prohibition ab initio. He concludes further that Radbaz permitted only selling it to another, but for the owner himself it is at least forbidden, since at any rate he nullified a prohibition ab initio, and as we hold, when one nullifies a prohibition, it is forbidden to him; see there. We found this responsum in Radbaz, siman 1015, and we will copy it here in full, because it appears that neither of them saw the body of the responsum. This is its language there, after he wrote the language of the question: I permitted it for several reasons. First, they brought me some of the wheat at the conclusion of Yom Tov, and I saw that it was dry and had not reached chametz or splitting. Furthermore, it was during a time when its prohibition is rabbinic, making it a rabbinic prohibition at a rabbinic time. Furthermore, the reason for the prohibition is a penalty, and here there is no reason to penalize him, for he was under unavoidable circumstances. If because he transgressed bal yera'eh and bal yimatzei, that is not so, for on Yom Tov it was impossible to destroy it. And if because he should have nullified it before it became chametz, in his understanding this was not chametz; therefore there is no reason to penalize him. Furthermore, since after its time its prohibition is only rabbinic, one may nullify it in a majority ab initio, like the lightest of prohibitions, namely terumah of the diaspora, which one may nullify in a majority ab initio. For these reasons I permitted him to mix it little by little with the rest of the wheat and sell it even to a Jew. This is the end of his language. It is obvious that Radbaz's intent is to permit even for himself. Because they copied his words as "and sell it to a Jew" and omitted the word "even," they led Mateh Yehudah to say that it is forbidden to himself, but this is not so.
[3] And if he sold it. See Mishnah Berurah, where we wrote that if he placed a seal on the chametz at the time of sale, it is forbidden even after the fact. See Pri Megadim, who inclines that this is specifically with a small sale, meaning that he sells him all the chametz for a small amount, which is a void sale, and the permission is only because in truth the chametz is not in his domain, since the Torah forbade it for benefit and the Merciful One placed it in his domain with respect to bal yera'eh. Therefore, whenever he reveals that he does not want it and agrees that it leave his domain, even for a small amount, that is enough. Thus, whenever he places a seal before the sale, it is visible to all that he does not want the sale. This is not so with our sales, where we make a proper document, each given measure for such-and-such amount, and the remaining money is recorded as a loan, and the chametz belongs to the non-Jew from now. If the non-Jew wants, he opens the seal and does what he wants, and the seal is only until measurement; after the fact, it is certainly permitted, and perhaps even ab initio. Similarly, according to Eliyah Rabbah, who wrote the reason as being that the non-Jew does not rely on the sale when he sees that the Jew leaves a seal, this applies only to a small sale, where the non-Jew sees that all the chametz is being sold to him for a small amount and then sees that they leave a seal. This is not so with our sale, which is proper, as above, and the seal is only until measurement, as above. All the more so if the Jew sold to the non-Jew in the village the liquor that he has in the city, and a seal rests on it there, and the non-Jew does not know about it; it is certainly permitted, for the concern that the non-Jew does not rely on the sale does not apply. Even if he knew, in such a case it is certainly permitted after the fact, as above. See there. This is the end of his language. At first glance, his whole argument applies only to placing a seal on the chametz, not to placing a lock on the room, and so it appears from what I copied from him below. He wrote further regarding an actual case: one person sold chametz and liquor to a non-Jew properly and recorded the money as a loan, and during Pesach the non-Jew ruined much of the liquor. If a non-Jew who is friendly with the Jew takes the key from the non-Jew, there is no concern. Perhaps the Jew may complain before the official so that he take the key from the non-Jew, so he not ruin everything, since he is poor. Chayei Adam, klal 124, law 2, likewise inclines to be lenient in this. But the Jew should not himself take the key from the non-Jew, for one can say that from now on the responsibility is on the Jew, and it is forbidden after Pesach; this requires examination. This is the end of his language. See Shulchan Aruch HaRav, who is also stringent in such a manner.
[3] Provided that he give it to him. See Mishnah Berurah, that there is no agency for a non-Jew, even from one non-Jew to another. This is from Magen Avraham and Sha'ar Ephraim, unlike Mas'as Binyamin and Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 243. See Mateh Yehudah, who wrote at length about this and wrote that at least where the chametz has already been brought to the house of the non-Jewish sender, the sale certainly stands, for the chametz was brought to the house of the non-Jewish buyer with the seller's intent, and the non-Jew accepted it in his house with his own intent; the agent's action does not diminish it, for he is like a mere monkey. See there. He concludes that in this case, even if the agent was a Jew, where according to everyone we say there is no agency for a non-Jew, it is nevertheless effective for the reason above. It is obvious that his words apply only if he brought the chametz to the buyer's actual house. But according to our custom, where we transfer the chametz to the non-Jew together with the house, this does not apply, for if there is no agency for a non-Jew, then the house is also not acquired by him. Know further that the author of Machaneh Ephraim, Hilchos Sheluchin, siman 15, introduced another argument: when do we say there is no agency for a non-Jew? Specifically where the acquisition is made through the agent. But with money this does not apply, meaning that where the acquisition was with money, we do not look at the agent at all; remove the agent from here, and the money itself acquires the item, and the owner of the object transfers it to the owner of the money without the agent's mediation; see there. According to this, there would be a great leniency for us, for after the fact we rely on the view of some poskim that money acquires. If so, perhaps one can transfer the chametz to a non-Jew even through his agent, if only the non-Jew sent the money for the chametz. However, Magen Avraham's words are not agreed upon, and Responsa Rivash, siman 401, does not sound this way. He wrote there regarding one who bought chametz for a non-Jew on Pesach, that he transgresses bal yera'eh and is liable for lashes, for since there is no agency for a non-Jew, it is as though he buys it for himself; see there. This refers to where he paid money for the chametz, and this law is brought below in siman 450 in the gloss. According to Machaneh Ephraim's view, why was it so obvious to Rivash? According to Rashi and those who follow him, a non-Jew acquires only with money; and according to Rambam, Ramban, Rashba, and other poskim, a non-Jew acquires both by pulling and by money, as written in Mishkenos Yaakov, siman 149. If so, according to these poskim there is no prohibition here. Although there is no agency for a non-Jew, nevertheless his money acquires for him, and the agent merely performs an act like a monkey. Even though one can say that Rivash holds like those poskim who say that a non-Jew acquires only by pulling, nevertheless he should not have written without qualification and been so stringent, since the matter depends on great authorities. It must be that he does not hold like the reasoning of Machaneh Ephraim. It is somewhat surprising that Machaneh Ephraim himself, in the following simanim, brings these words of Rivash and discusses them; this requires examination. I found that Sedei Chemed also wrote regarding these words of Machaneh Ephraim that some disagree with him; see there.
[3] A complete sale. The Rav of Bigdei Yesha, siman 2, note 5, wrote that since the sale of chametz to a non-Jew must be a complete sale without evasion, it appears that after Pesach, when the non-Jew returns the chametz to the Jew, he must acquire it through one of the modes of acquisition and not merely receive it back from him, as the poskim wrote regarding a gift on condition to return in siman 658. It appears that after Pesach it is enough that he acquire the chametz and the rooms from the non-Jew with money alone, and that the non-Jew return the bill of sale that he received; this is enough. Although a Jew buying land from a non-Jew does not acquire land with money alone, but only with a document, that is because the Jew does not rely on the transaction. Here, however, where the non-Jew returns the document to him, see there.
[3] For a small amount. See Mishnah Berurah. Nowadays the custom of all Israel is not to sell for a small amount, but for a price close to the value of the chametz. They receive from the non-Jew a few coins, called auf gab, as the beginning of payment, and the remainder is recorded as a loan, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 190, that this is effective. The later authorities wrote that it is good to be careful not to raise the price far above its value by a large amount, for one can say that this is a void sale. It sounds from Pri Megadim that for this reason, after the fact, it does not invalidate, because under their laws he cannot retract. This implies that if the custom of that place is to retract because of a void sale, it invalidates, and this requires examination. They wrote further that it is ineffective if he says to him, "I sell you this house," or "my chametz," unless he says, "I sell you this house for such-and-such, and this chametz, each measure for such-and-such amount." Without setting a price, there is no acquisition at all, unless he says, "as such-and-such person will appraise it," or "as three experts will appraise it." He must specify every type of chametz he has, and should not write simply, "I sell you everything that has a concern of chametz," because that is like a case where its type is not known, for he does not know whether it is worth much or little. Rather, he should specify each and every type. Only the measuring need not specifically be before Pesach; he may stipulate with the non-Jew that the measuring or appraisal will be after Pesach [Mekor Chayim and Sha'arei Ephraim]. After the fact, if he said simply, "everything that has a concern of chametz," and Pesach passed over it, see Sefer Sedei Chemed in the name of several later authorities who incline to be lenient after the fact. One must specify the buyer's name in the document. Know further that what we wrote in Mishnah Berurah regarding where the chametz is on the road, that he should acquire it by agav, even though it is not piled on the land, according to Mekor Chayim in this siman and above in siman 440 this applies only when he sold the chametz and took all the money. But if he took part of it and recorded the rest as a loan, it does not help, for if the chametz is lost the non-Jew will not want to pay him what remains due. According to what we hold in siman 440, that where the non-Jew is forceful and compels him to pay it is considered under the Jew's responsibility, the same applies here: since he must guard it, because if it is lost he will lose what the non-Jew owes him, if he recorded the rest as a loan it is permitted only if the chametz is in the place of the land. Then, when he transfers the land to him, the chametz is like being in the non-Jew's house, and at least according to those poskim who hold that a Jew's chametz in a non-Jew's house is not transgressed, they are covered. However, several later authorities disagree with Mekor Chayim and wrote that because the non-Jew will not want to pay what he owes him, the chametz is not called the Jew's responsibility because of this; see Nishmas Adam, siman 6, and Sedei Chemed, who wrote at length to bring the view of several later authorities who are lenient about this. Regarding the acquisition of agav itself: although above in Mishnah Berurah, se'if katan 16, we included it among the other acquisitions on which one should not rely ab initio, since each one has those who question it, nevertheless where it is a pressing circumstance, such as where there is much chametz, and especially where the chametz is on the road, we did not want to be stringent and prevent this acquisition even ab initio, because almost all the later authorities upheld this acquisition as the choicest of acquisitions, even ab initio and even without pressing circumstances. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, brought in Responsa Shivas Tziyon, siman 10, and Chasam Sofer, Orach Chayim, simanim 113 and 117. He wrote that he found Ramban in Milchamos, chapter 2 of Pesachim, who wrote this acquisition explicitly regarding chametz. So too Taz in Yoreh De'ah, siman 320, in explaining the language of the Shulchan Aruch; see there. So too the Gaon R. Shmuel Kaidanover in Responsa Nachalas Shiv'ah, siman 30, and all the later authorities copied it in this siman. Nevertheless, ab initio it appears proper to combine another acquisition, such as situmta or kinyan sudar, as the custom today is to double several acquisitions, since the Tumim in siman 123 questions this acquisition, and even the Ketzos, who rejected the words of the Tumim there, nevertheless in siman 194 also questioned acquisition by agav; see there at length. Likewise Machatzis HaShekel in this siman wrote to question this acquisition. Regarding an acquisition that works under their laws, whether we rely on it also regarding prohibition, as with selling chametz, there is a dispute. See Magen Avraham, se'if katan 4, who questioned Responsa Mas'as Binyamin, whose view is that it works, all the more so from the customs of merchants, which work. However, the view of the Gaon R. Shmuel Kaidanover in Responsa Nachalas Shiv'ah is like Mas'as Binyamin. In Nachalas Shiv'ah he challenged him there, and in his view even situmta is ineffective. So too is the simple view of Responsa Meshivas Nefesh, siman 9. So too the Rav Chasam Sofer wrote in several places in his responsa that it is stronger than situmta; see Yoreh De'ah, siman 311 and siman 314. In the gloss of Chasam Sofer he marked to see the responsa attributed to Rashba, siman 225. I examined there, and at first he also wrote as a simple matter that it works, all the more so from situmta, which is a mere custom, and certainly a fixed custom and law. The passage in Bechoros from which Magen Avraham noted that we do not consider their laws for matters of mitzvos he also brought, and wrote that there is no proof, because perhaps there it speaks where they had no known custom. However, at the end he is uncertain, because perhaps regarding mitzvos, such as to incur obligation of firstborn sanctity, which is biblical, one is not obligated until he acquires according to Torah law. There is a printing error there, and instead of "and it does not appear" it should be "and furthermore it appears," as is also proven there from the end of the responsum. See there. In any event, at least it is proven from him that acquisition by situmta is not stronger than acquisition under their laws. According to this, even acquisition by situmta is not clear ab initio to rely upon. Therefore, it is proper ab initio not to rely on the merchants' custom alone, but to see that another acquisition be combined with it, as above. Where one makes several acquisitions, it is proper that he explicitly say to him that he is transferring it to him even by one of the acquisitions, and certainly by all of them. It is good also to make it ownerless before a beis din, as Chok Yaakov wrote in a similar case in se'if katan 14. This is his language: if he knows that chametz will be brought to him on Pesach by wagon or ship and he will have to pay customs and wagon fee and the like, he should sell it to a non-Jew with money and the other acquisitions mentioned above, and when they bring it afterward on Pesach, the non-Jew should take everything to himself, into his domain, and he should also pay the customs and wagon fee. After the sale he should say that if the non-Jew does not acquire it through these acquisitions, at least it should be completely ownerless.
[3] A gift on condition to return. The same applies to a sale on condition to return [Chemed Moshe].
[3] It is ineffective. This law is from Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 6 of Hilchos Shabbos, and he derives this law there from the Tosefta; see there. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 120, brings it, and at first glance it sounds there that if not for the stringency of chametz, it would be permitted even ab initio to act this way. In truth, this requires examination, for ab initio it is certainly forbidden by law, since he made his gift depend on a condition, and if the condition is not fulfilled and it is not returned, the chametz is retroactively his. If so, he brings himself into a doubt of the biblical prohibition of bal yera'eh, especially since the condition does not depend on him but on others, and perhaps that person will not fulfill the condition. I found that Machaneh Ephraim, Hilchos Ma'achalos Asuros, wrote this, that ab initio it is certainly forbidden to do so, for the reason we wrote. But the language of the poskim, who made it depend on a mere stringency, is difficult. If we also uphold the view of the later authorities who are stringent about this even after the fact, like the simple language of the Mechaber, it is understandable: perhaps the core law of Hagahos Maimoniyos concerns after the fact, if the condition was indeed fulfilled. But according to those later authorities who wrote that after the fact it is permitted according to everyone, and this entire law is only ab initio, the matter requires examination. Afterward I found in Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger that he wonders similarly about Magen Avraham, and suggests that he can say, "I consider it as though I received it," which is like fulfilling the condition. Where he said, "I hereby give it to you and you will return it to me," Magen Avraham wrote that here it is obvious that it is permitted by law, and is better than "on condition to return," for if he wishes he need not return it to him, and the gift does not depend on the return, except for the stringency of chametz. This is the content of his words. It appears that according to him, in this case one certainly should not be stringent after the fact. However, Chok Yaakov challenged Magen Avraham from Yoreh De'ah 305, where it is proven that in this language it is not a gift at all. See Eliyah Rabbah and Machatzis HaShekel, who wrote that there it is different: since he says, "and you will return them to me," the gift does not take effect even for one hour. This is not so here, for at least until after Pesach he certainly resolves and transfers it to him so that he not transgress bal yera'eh, and all the more so when he says explicitly, "and you will return it to me after Pesach." So too wrote Dagul MeRevavah. However, according to the view of Taz there this requires examination, and especially according to the view of Gra there, that a gift for a limited time is not a gift; at first glance there is certainly no room for Magen Avraham's words, even if he says explicitly, "and you will return it to me after Pesach." However, Chemed Moshe and Bigdei Yesha incline to Magen Avraham's view and brought some implication for this from several passages, that this is not called a retraction and the gift stands even if he does not return it to him, as Magen Avraham wrote. Only the case in Yoreh De'ah, siman 305, mentioned above stood against them, for it is proven there that it is a retraction from his first words. However, Eliyah Rabbah wrote in one answer to distinguish that there, regarding a firstborn, it depends on the giver's intent and thought, as is explained there explicitly in se'if 8, and with this language, where he says, "and you will return it to me," it is proven that he did not intend in his mind to truly give it. This is not so here with chametz, where intent does not prevent the sale at all, since even if he knows that he will not touch it and both parties intend that he return it after Pesach, it is nevertheless permitted. One who examines Responsa Rashba, siman 198, the source of this law, will at first glance see that it implies like this answer of Eliyah Rabbah; see there. In the words of Responsa Chasam Sofer I found a contradiction about this: in siman 113 he wrote explicitly that if he said "and you will return it to me," even if he said after Pesach, he transgresses bal yera'eh; but in siman 116 he concludes that in this case it is permitted by law.
