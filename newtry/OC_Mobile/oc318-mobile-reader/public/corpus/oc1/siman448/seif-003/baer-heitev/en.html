[3] For benefit. For they penalized him since he transgressed bal yera'eh. See siman 447, se'if 12. (And even for others it is forbidden; Pri Chadash. See siman 449.)
[3] Outside. He stated the usual case; the same applies even to a non-Jew who lives in the courtyard, even if he is his servant (Taz). This is where he is not his Canaanite slave; Chok Yaakov. So too is written in Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim, siman 3, that one should not sell chametz to a maidservant acquired by a Jew. The Bach wrote that where there is difficulty, one may sell to a non-Jew all the chametz in the room and also the room itself (see Choshen Mishpat, siman 196, and R.Y.). He must say to him: even though I have not written you a document for this sale, I transfer it to you with money alone. He must hand him the key. After the fact, with this one should not forbid it, since in their law the buyer can take possession even without a key; Chok Yaakov. He must also be careful not to place any seal on the room or on the chametz so that the non-Jew cannot enter there; even after the fact one should forbid it, for this is not a sale but mere evasion. Chok Yaakov wrote: it seems to me that this is specifically if he made the seal before the sale or at the time of the sale, so that there is no sale here at all. But if he first sold it as a complete sale and later the Jew placed his lock or seal, then even though ab initio one should not do this because of concern for evasion, and it is fitting to rebuke those who do so, nevertheless after the fact one should not forbid the chametz; see there. See Responsa Mas'as Binyamin, siman 59, that after the fact he acquired it with money alone, and if the non-Jew sent his agent and he pulled it, he also acquired it; see there. Magen Avraham says not to be lenient about this; see there. Shlah wrote: in a place where it is clearly known that the custom of merchants is to acquire movable goods by handing over the key, or by giving one's palm to his fellow, or by a perutah, this also acquires here, and then he does not need to sell. However, he must say, "I hereby sell you the chametz," unlike the common people who say, "I hereby sell you the key," for that is nothing, and even after the fact the chametz is forbidden, since he did not sell it at all; see Magen Avraham.
[3] And give it to him. If the non-Jew does not want to return the chametz to him after Pesach, it is forbidden to sue him in their courts. But it is forbidden for another Jew to buy it from him after Pesach, and if he bought it, he must go back and sell it to the original owners. Mas'as Binyamin, siman 43; see Magen Avraham. See Yad Aharon. If Reuven and a non-Jew were partners and both together sold chametz to Shimon before Pesach and received the money, but Shimon had not yet pulled the chametz from the partners' domain and Pesach passed over it, Shimon is obligated to destroy it. Bnei Yaakov, siman 3. See Responsa Nachalas Shiv'ah, siman 33, Chok Yaakov, se'if katan 14, and Pri Chadash.
[3] Complete. Even through his agent or his wife he can transfer ownership of the chametz, and we do not say that a woman does not know how to transfer ownership in a matter that will later return to her. Chok Yaakov. There was a person who had chametz and wanted to sell it to a non-Jew according to the law. He contrived not to say anything to the non-Jew about his chametz, but merely placed it in his friend's room, knowing that his friend would sell that room, together with the chametz inside it, to a non-Jew, and would give him the key. This is what happened: his fellow Jew sold that room to the non-Jew, and the chametz he had placed inside was included among the chametz in that room, but neither the Jewish seller nor the non-Jewish buyer knew about it. In Responsa Chinuch Beis Yehudah, siman 13, he ruled that the chametz of the other Jew is forbidden; see there.
[3] A sale. It is permitted to say to him, "Here is this chametz; give me chametz after Pesach." So ruled the Gaon Maharash, of blessed memory, in Eliyah Zuta. It is forbidden to sell one's chametz to an apostate, for even though he sinned, he is a Jew. If one did sell it, Taz wrote that in a case of great loss one may tell the apostate to exchange it with another non-Jew or to sell it to a non-Jew, and the money is permitted to the Jew. There was an incident with one who held an arenda in a village and sold his liquor to the official, who was an apostate. The amount was large, and it was hard to lose the liquor, and it was likely that the Jew would not obey in this matter. I said that the official should exchange it with another non-Jew and take other liquor from him, and then it would be permitted to this Jew. Taz. See Responsa Chok Yaakov, siman 41. For this reason one should refrain from buying beer or liquor from an apostate immediately after Pesach, because it is chametz that remained over Pesach. Even though we hold that the chametz of transgressors is permitted immediately after Pesach because they exchange it, that is regarding one who transgresses for appetite, for he does not leave permitted food and eat forbidden food. This is not so regarding one who transgresses spitefully, and nowadays all of them are considered transgressors out of spite. Chok Yaakov.
