If a non-Jew brings. Beis Hillel, Yoreh Deah 132:1, relates a case in Hamburg. Before Pesach a Jew left grain at a non-Jew’s mill to be ground. It remained there until Chol HaMoed. On the seventh day of Pesach the non-Jew ground the Jew’s grain, baked bread from the flour, and brought the bread to the Jew immediately after Pesach, believing he had done him a favor. Beis Hillel permitted the Jew to collect the bread’s value from that non-Jew, who could eat it himself or sell it to other non-Jews, because the non-Jew had destroyed the Jew’s property. Although at first glance one might hesitate, since unlike wine, bread may be sold to Jews in places where non-Jewish bread is eaten, and bread baked by a non-Jew from Jewish dough has an independent prohibition under Yoreh Deah 112, the bread could be cut into small pieces as required there. In this case there are additional grounds to permit even eating. According to Rambam, who forbids receiving the value of wine when the non-Jew caused the prohibition unintentionally, that is because the damage is not externally recognizable and a Jew would be exempt. Here, however, the non-Jew took the grain without permission, ground it, and baked it. This is like an artisan who spoiled an entrusted item, who must pay; it is direct and recognizable damage. Before fermentation, the non-Jew acquired the grain through his unauthorized change and damage, even without intending to acquire it, so it ceased to belong to the Jew. Even if the damage and acquisition occurred only after fermentation, the Jew may at least collect damages from this non-Jew, and there is no concern that the Jew is selling chametz to another Jew. There is also support from Demai 3:4: produce deposited with a non-Jew is treated as certainly exchanged for the non-Jew’s own produce. Although Rav Ovadiah of Bartenura distinguishes a non-Jewish miller, where one suspects exchange only with another Jew’s grain, that applies when Jews commonly owned fields and brought grain to non-Jewish millers. In our time, especially on Yom Tov of Pesach itself, this is uncommon, and all the more so where it is known that the miller had no other Jewish grain. Raavad distinguishes between produce in a basket and in sacks, while Rambam treats the matter as doubtful. I wrote in siman 449 that doubtful chametz that remained over Pesach is permitted even for eating. The same therefore applies here if there is at least a doubt whether the non-Jew exchanged the grain. These grounds may also permit a case where a non-Jew stole a Jew’s chametz and Pesach passed over it, though this requires study. Chinuch Beis Yehudah, responsum 11, records a similar incident and permits even eating because chametz after Pesach is only a D’Rabbanan penalty and the case contains several doubts: perhaps the grain was not moistened, perhaps not enough to ferment, and perhaps it was ground after Pesach. Since the prohibition is D’Rabbanan, there is no basis for stringency. The non-Jew’s statement is not accepted merely as innocent conversation, because he may have intended the Jew to appreciate his service.
A gift of chametz. If a non-Jew gave or sold a Jew wheat or similar produce and the Jew accepted it, and after Pesach it became known that chametz was mixed into it, the chametz appears permitted after Pesach as chametz belonging to a non-Jew. Nedarim 35a states that where one says, “My loaf is forbidden to you by konam,” the recipient can say, “I wanted a permitted item, not a prohibited one.” Rashi, Ran, and Rosh explain that when another person gives him a prohibited object unknowingly, his acquisition is mistaken, because had he known it was prohibited he would not have accepted it. This differs from one who himself spends consecrated money unknowingly, since he actively took it to acquire it. Although the Gemara concludes that the recipient commits me’ilah when he later spends the loaf, that is because he then performs an additional prohibited act unknowingly. Here he did nothing further after receiving the produce. See Ran there. This requires study.
On the final day of Pesach. The ruling mentions the last day to teach that even then the Jew should not accept the chametz. Rashba, responsum 171, writes likewise. In truth, the same applies on the earlier days; the common case was stated, because gifts were ordinarily brought on the last day after Minchah, as Tur writes in the name of Baal HaIttur. Nachalas Shivah explains that the last day is mentioned as a novelty, whereas on the earlier days, when the prohibition is according to the Torah, it is obvious that the Jew does not wish to acquire the prohibited item. On the final Yom Tov one need not cover it with a vessel or erect a ten-handbreadth partition, because there is no concern that he will eat it during such a short interval. During Pesach, however, covering or a partition is required, as explained above. The same law applies where a non-Jew forgot his chametz in the Jew’s home.
The Jew should not accept it. If he did accept it, Tur in the name of Baal HaIttur implies that it is forbidden even in benefit after Pesach. Although the final Yom Tov is D’Rabbanan and no Torah prohibition of bal yera’eh or bal yimatzei then applies, whatever the Sages enacted they patterned after Torah law, and this also prevents belittling the second day of Yom Tov. This requires study.
His conduct should not indicate acceptance. This appears to mean that he did not say, “Place it upon the rock,” or “Give it here,” and similar expressions. Although Kiddushin and Even HaEzer, siman 30, explain that a woman using such expressions is not betrothed, there she must reveal a desire to receive the money for kiddushin, and these expressions indicate that she does not desire the kiddushin. Here the Jew must show that he has no desire for the chametz at all. See Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 82.
It is preferable that he state that he does not want it. Then, if he takes it after Pesach, his later conduct will not prove that during Pesach he intended to acquire it. Even if he did not say so, the law permits it, even where the chametz entered his guarded courtyard, because a person does not acquire a prohibited item against his will; he does not desire the prohibition to be acquired for him. This applies to a gift and similar cases where the Jew bears no responsibility. If the non-Jew brings chametz to the Jew’s home to sell it or deposit it with him, the Jew must expressly state that he does not wish to accept it, lest he become responsible for it. Indeed, even without legal responsibility, if the non-Jew is violent and will compel him to pay, it is forbidden, as explained above in siman 440.
