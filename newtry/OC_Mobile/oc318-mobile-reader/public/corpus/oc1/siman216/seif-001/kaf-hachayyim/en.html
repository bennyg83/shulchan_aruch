1) [Seif 1] It is forbidden to derive enjoyment from a pleasant fragrance until one recites a blessing, etc. As it is stated, 'Let every soul praise God.' What is something from which the soul derives enjoyment but the body does not? One must say this is fragrance. Berachos 43b; Beis Yosef; Levush.
2) There: Until he recites a blessing before smelling. If wine and fragrant oil were brought to him to smell, he first recites the blessing over the wine. Tur at the end of siman 212, cited by Magen Avraham at the beginning of this siman, who explains that a blessing over physical enjoyment takes precedence. This precedence applies when both are before him. If the fragrance was brought before him first, he need not wait until the item providing physical enjoyment is brought, as stated above in siman 211, paragraph 23; see there.
3) There: Until he recites a blessing, etc. If one is uncertain whether an herb has a fragrance, he may test it before reciting a blessing. Adnei Paz on this siman; Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 1; Ruach Chaim, paragraph 3. Likewise, if one is uncertain whether he himself can smell, he may test before reciting a blessing. Ben Ish Chai, parashas VaEschanan, paragraph 2.
4) It is forbidden to smell a rose, lily, or spices belonging to consecrated property. Even if one assumes that the prohibition of misuse does not apply, a prohibition nevertheless remains. Radbaz, volume 3, siman 621, in the newly printed Ashkenazic edition; Machazik Berachah, paragraph 1. See the following paragraph.
5) It is permitted to smell the blossoms of orlah trees even though they contain the beginning of the fruit. It is forbidden, however, to smell the fruit once it has grown somewhat, since it is orlah. Radbaz, new responsa, siman 44. Responsa Zera Yaakov, siman 7, however, concludes that it is permitted to smell consecrated fruit or fruit forbidden by a vow, but one should not recite a blessing over it; see there at length. Machazik Berachah, paragraph 8, cites this. It appears that since the matter is disputed and it is forbidden to derive enjoyment from the world without a blessing, refraining from action is preferable. One should not smell something from which enjoyment is forbidden, even without a blessing. See Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah, siman 108, seif 7.
6) Something forbidden for consumption but not for benefit may be smelled even initially. Responsa Emunas Shmuel, siman 45; Pri Chadash on Yoreh Deah, siman 108, paragraphs 21 and 25. Maamar Mordechai in this siman, paragraph 4, writes this and challenges Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, paragraph 8, who writes that anything forbidden to eat is forbidden to smell; see there. Zera Yaakov, siman 7, and Erekh HaShulchan, paragraph 4, agree. See below, paragraph 22.
7) A rose or myrtle detached on Yom Tov may be smelled without moving it. Responsa Zera Yaakov, siman 8; Machazik Berachah, paragraph 9. The Shulchan Aruch below, siman 336, seif 10, likewise rules that even an attached myrtle may be smelled; see there. Erekh HaShulchan, paragraph 8, writes the same.
8) One may recite a blessing over roses placed upon the finials of a Sefer Torah. Pri Chadash, Yoreh Deah, siman 116, paragraph 7; Zechor LeAvraham, paragraph 2.
9) There: Until he recites a blessing, etc. Bnei Chaim writes in the name of the elder R' Chaim Alfandari that it is reasonable to recite the blessing over half a fruit but not over a fruit peel. It records, however, in the name of R' Yosef, son of that Rav, that one does recite a blessing over a fruit peel; see there. Machazik Berachah, paragraph 4, challenges this and writes that one should not recite HaNosen Rei'ach Tov BaPeiros over peels, but should recite Borei Minei Besamim; see there. Beis Menuchah, paragraph 1, and Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 6, write the same.
10) There: Afterward, however, he need not recite a blessing. Rashi at the beginning of Niddah 52 explains that the enjoyment is slight, cited by Bach. Bach writes that this means the enjoyment ends immediately after one smells, resembling one who ate and whose food became digested in his intestines, who does not afterward recite a blessing. Taz, paragraph 1, writes the same. Magen Chaim, paragraph 1, writes the same in the name of Kol Bo. Olas Tamid, paragraph 1, gives another reason: the fragrant item is not consumed through being smelled; see there. It appears to me that another reason is that a concluding blessing is recited only over something to which satiation applies. Fragrance is different, for even if one smells day and night, he will not become satiated. All these reasons imply that if one did not recite a blessing before smelling or while smelling, he cannot recite it afterward. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3, and Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1, write the same.
11) There: Afterward, however, he need not recite a blessing. Regarding Shehecheyanu, Radbaz, volume 2, siman 4, and new responsa, siman 297, writes that over a pleasant fragrance renewed from year to year, such as Indian jasmine, jasmine, orange blossom, rose, and similar species, he is accustomed to recite Shehecheyanu when deriving enjoyment from the fruit; see there. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, siman 225, and Magen Avraham there, paragraph 12, cite this. Shevus Yaakov, volume 2, siman 38, writes the same, cited by Shaarei Teshuvah there, paragraph 11. Lechem Chamudos, chapter Keitzad Mevarchin, paragraph 120, however, writes that Shehecheyanu is not recited over fragrance; see there. Magen Avraham in this siman, paragraph 1, cites this. Knesses HaGedolah in this siman, Hagahos HaTur, paragraph 2, writes the same; see there. See Birkei Yosef, paragraph 1, who discusses this and concludes that as practical halachah one should not recite the blessing, since the matter is disputed. He writes that this is the common practice; see there. He writes the same in Machazik Berachah, paragraph 3, cited by Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 2. Likewise, one should not recite Shehecheyanu when eating roses preserved in honey, since they are unfit to eat by themselves. Maaseh Rokeach on chapter 9 of Hilchos Berachos, law 1, further explains that people do not plant them with the intention of preserving them in honey, but for fragrance. Therefore, the Sages did not institute Shehecheyanu even when they are eaten; see there.
12) Mordechai in the chapter Eilu Devarim writes that blessings are recited only over something from which the body derives enjoyment. Therefore, a blessing is recited over fire only after Shabbos, since that was the beginning of its creation. Darkei Moshe, paragraph 1, cites this and writes that for this reason no blessing is recited over a pleasant voice. Mateh Moshe writes that the reason is that it has no substance; see there. No blessing is recited over bathing or anointing because they do not enter the body. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1.
13) Regarding the fragrance of mint, called nana in Arabic, dindana in the seventh chapter of Sheviis, minta in the first chapter of Uktzin, and amita in Shabbos 128, Ginas Veradim, Orach Chaim section, rule 1, siman 41, writes that no blessing is recited over it. Perach Shoshan, rule 10, siman 13, disagrees and writes that one recites HaNosen Rei'ach Tov BaPeiros. Halachos Ketanos, volume 2, siman 115, writes the same, but notes that the universal practice is to recite Borei Isvei Besamim and that he does not know why; see there. Yad Aharon, Hagahos HaTur. Shibolei HaLeket at the end of siman 44, however, writes that over rue, mint, and any herb whose fragrance spreads, one recites Borei Isvei Besamim. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 2, cites this. The universal practice is indeed to recite Borei Isvei Besamim over mint, called nana in Arabic, in accordance with Shibolei HaLeket and Halachos Ketanos.
14) Over the herb called reihan in Arabic, all agree that one recites Borei Isvei Besamim. Ben Ish Chai, parashas VaEschanan, paragraph 8.
