[Levush] Every soul, etc. Meaning, Scripture could have said "the soul shall praise Hashem," but instead says "every soul."
[Levush] It appears to me because, etc. Lechem Chamudot, page 294, and Malbushei Yom Tov write that the Levush overlooked Rashi in Niddah 52, who explains that the reason is that the benefit is slight. It appears to me that the Levush is explaining Rashi: because the scent dissipates, the benefit is slight. Bach likewise explains Rashi this way. Moreover, the Levush, Lechem Chamudot, and Malbushei Yom Tov all overlooked Kol Bo, page 21, which explicitly gives the Levush's explanation. Olat Tamid gives another reason: since the fragrant substance is not consumed through smelling, an after-blessing is not applicable. Lechem Chamudot writes that the reason of slight benefit may also explain why Shehecheyanu is not recited over fragrance. The late Maharam Dayan said that since the blessing over fragrance is derived from "every soul shall praise Hashem," and the soul is eternal, it is inappropriate to say "Who has kept us alive and sustained us," because the soul itself endures eternally. Magen Avraham writes that the reason is because fragrances are available all year, but sensory experience contradicts this. See Shiyarei Knesset HaGedolah.
[Levush] It has no substance, etc. From this I was surprised by Mahari Pinto in chapter Keitzad Mevarchin, who writes that if one smelled a fragrance without blessing and then left the place of the fragrance, he may immediately recite the blessing. He reasons that after food has already been eaten one cannot recite the initial blessing retroactively, but with fragrance, although he has already smelled it, the soul still retains benefit and can praise Hashem for the fragrance already experienced. This is difficult, because if so an after-blessing should also be required. Indeed, it is an a fortiori argument: eating, which requires an after-blessing, does not allow the initial blessing after eating; fragrance, which has no after-blessing, all the more so should not allow the initial blessing afterward. Taz at the end of siman 217 also implies this. Mahari Pinto's explanation of Abaye is also difficult, because Abaye's statement concerns the three matters that should follow immediately one after another, including a blessing immediately after Torah scholars, as explained in Berachot 42. His question why the derashah "what is something from which the soul benefits" was not stated in Mar Zutra's teaching is likewise difficult, because this wording appears in the Gemara, Ein Yaakov, Baal Halachot Gedolot, Rif, Rosh, Tur, and all the poskim. He clearly encountered a corrupt text.
[Levush] Congealed blood, etc. Therefore it is prohibited to eat it, as the Tur writes. Magen Avraham and Shiyarei Knesset HaGedolah discuss this at length. Maadanei Melech, page 53, and Taz permit it, and so appears from Rabbenu Yerucham. Shiltei Giborim in chapter HaSocher Et HaPoalim rules permissively as well, and Knesset HaGedolah writes that this is the common practice.
[Levush] Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe, Who gives, etc. Orchot Chaim writes that one should not say "Who gave" or "that He gave," because in His goodness He renews continually each day. His words are difficult. Shulchan Aruch once writes "that He gave" and once "Who gives," implying there is no concern, and I found the same in Piskei Recanati, simanim 78 and 79. Rama at the end of this siman writes "that He gave," as do Abudarham, Shibbolei HaLeket, and Tanya. Lechem Chamudot, page 61, writes to say "Who gave" in the past tense, and on page 54 he implies that "that He gave" and "Who gave" are equivalent. More surprisingly, I saw that the Gemara, Baal Halachot Gedolot, Rif, Rambam, Rosh, Tur, Raavan, Semag, Semak, Rokeach, Tashbetz, Rabbenu Yerucham, Mordechai, Agudah, and Kol Bo all write "Who gave." Who would lightly disregard them? Orchot Chaim also writes that the Levush was precise to write "Who gives" everywhere, and Lechem Chamudot says likewise, but this is incorrect, for below in siman 653 the Levush writes "Who gave."
To eat it and to smell it, etc. Olat Tamid writes that one first blesses over eating and afterward over fragrance, based on the Tur at the end of siman 212, who says that if the blessing over fragrance and the blessing over drinking are before him he first blesses over the drink. The same should apply to eating, and one blessing is not considered an interruption for the other, as on Pesach one says Hamotzi and then Al Achilat Matzah before eating. In my humble opinion this is incorrect. There both blessings are obligations caused by the eating, whereas here fragrance does not depend on eating and one can bless over the fragrance first. It also resembles siman 165 regarding Netilat Yadayim and Asher Yatzar. One should not bless first over the food and eat, because he will immediately smell it, and when he later blesses over the fragrance the blessing will no longer precede the benefit; indeed, as soon as he places it in his mouth before eating he smells it. It therefore appears better to bless first over the fragrance and smell, and afterward bless and eat. This differs from the Tur in siman 212, where there are two separate items, one for smelling and one for drinking. See what I wrote at the end of siman 212.
[Levush] And it exempts the subordinate item, etc. I searched the early and later poskim and found no source for this reason. I am also surprised: regarding incense in siman 217, spices for the dead, a privy, and oil made to remove filth, what can be said? The fragrance is inevitable and one necessarily smells it. Rather, anything not made for fragrance requires no blessing even if one smells it, since his intent is not to smell it. This is clear. We therefore do not need the Levush's very strained distinction between this and siman 212 concerning exemption of a subordinate item. This is explicit in Abudarham and Tashbetz, page 29.
And over all of them, etc. This applies even when smelling a fruit. Olat Tamid writes that even if one recited Shehakol over any type of fragrance he has fulfilled his obligation after the fact. This requires study.
And cinnamon, etc. The Levush omits Shulchan Aruch's terms for cinnamon and cloves and substitutes kaneh and kinnamon. It appears that he holds the first term refers to cinnamon bark, which is kaneh and kinnamon, as above at the end of siman 202. Lechem Chamudot, page 45, explains Shulchan Aruch's separate reference to kinnamon. It seems to me that this is not cinnamon bark but another fragrant bark, so the Levush does not disagree with Shulchan Aruch, unlike Malbushei Yom Tov. As for cloves, the Levush omitted them because he holds they are not considered a fruit, since no eating blessing is recited over them. Nachalat Tzvi similarly explains Shulchan Aruch's words. The clove mentioned by Shulchan Aruch here is another species and not ordinary cloves, unlike Olat Tamid, who rules that over cloves one says "Who gave a good fragrance in fruits" because they are the principal fruit, whereas roses are not. This is incorrect, for the Rosh and Tur explain that roses differ because their primary purpose is not eating. Nevertheless, Taz writes that cloves are principally intended for eating and one recites "Who gave a good fragrance in fruits." One must say he means when eaten in mixtures, as Magen Avraham implies. The matter requires study. In any event, many poskim also hold that on cinnamon bark one says Borei Atzei Besamim. Because of the doubt one should say Borei Minei Besamim over cloves, as Agur writes regarding cinnamon bark. Regarding ginger, see below in siman 298 concerning havdalah. Olat Tamid writes that over cinnamon bark one should say "Who gave a good fragrance in fruits of the ground," because one should specify whether the fruit grows from the ground or a tree, and claims Beit Yosef cites this from the Rosh. But the Rosh mentions this only for produce of the ground, whereas for tree fruit he writes the generic "Who gave a good fragrance in fruits," as do all the poskim. Moreover, even regarding ground produce the Tur writes the generic formula. Perhaps the Rosh was not being literal, but only meant that it is a fruit of the ground like an etrog is a fruit of a tree, excluding those who recite Borei Atzei Besamim. Thus the Tur does not contradict his father. I found in the glosses to Semak and Shiltei Giborim that they cite the Rosh as using the generic formula, which confirms this explanation.
