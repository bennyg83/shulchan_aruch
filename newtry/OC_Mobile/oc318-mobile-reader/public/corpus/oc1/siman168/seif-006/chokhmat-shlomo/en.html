Seif 6. For pas haba b'kisnin one recites Borei Minei Mezonos, etc. Note: I saw that Peri Megadim is uncertain whether one who ate pas haba b'kisnin to satiation is obligated in Bircas HaMazon by Torah law or only D'Rabbanan. At first glance, this doubt may be resolved from the Gemara in the first chapter of Arachin 4a. It states: “All are obligated in zimun, kohanim, leviim, and Yisraelim.” The Gemara asks that this is obvious, and answers that the novelty concerns kohanim who ate sacrificial food. One might have thought that because the Torah said, “They shall eat those things through which atonement was effected,” and the eating itself effects atonement, they are exempt. The Gemara therefore teaches that the Torah said, “You shall eat and be satisfied,” and that condition exists here. Yet bread is found among offerings only in meal-offerings, for meat has no relevance to Bircas HaMazon. The novelty can therefore apply only to meal-offerings. But meal-offerings contain oil, and one does not fulfill the mitzvah of matzah with them. Consequently, they would not ordinarily require Bircas HaMazon at all. How, then, can the Gemara say that the kohanim are obligated in zimun? Necessarily, it refers to a case where they ate an amount sufficient for satiation in the manner of a fixed meal, and concerning this the Gemara says, “The Torah said, ‘You shall eat and be satisfied,’ and that condition exists here.” This implies that Bircas HaMazon applies to such food by Torah law. One cannot say that the Gemara refers to the loaves of a thanksgiving-offering, with which one may fulfill the mitzvah of matzah because they are not matzah ashirah and therefore require Bircas HaMazon. That cannot be, because concerning a thanksgiving-offering one cannot say, “They shall eat those things through which atonement was effected,” since it is not brought for atonement. According to this, however, our proof also falls away, because the novelty could apply among meal-offerings only to the sinner's meal-offering, which effects atonement, and that offering contains no oil and is not pas haba b'kisnin. Yet from this itself one can prove the opposite, that Bircas HaMazon on pas haba b'kisnin is only D'Rabbanan. Otherwise, why was the Gemara compelled to explain that one might have thought the kohanim exempt because the eating effects atonement? According to that explanation, the novelty applies only to a sinner's meal-offering, and we must also introduce a new principle that a food which effects atonement might not require a blessing, which is contrary to reason. Admittedly, one might derive from here support for those who hold that one who eats something under compulsion does not recite a blessing. The kohanim are obligated to eat the offering, and the Torah compels them even if they do not desire to eat. One might therefore have thought that no blessing applies. The Gemara teaches that since the Torah says, “You shall eat and be satisfied,” and that condition exists, they are obligated. According to the conclusion, this would prove the opposite, that even one who eats under compulsion recites a blessing. This is not the place to elaborate. In any event, why did the Gemara need that explanation? It could have answered that the novelty concerns other meal-offerings, even where the kohanim did not eat the amount upon which others establish a meal. One might have thought they were exempt because the food is pas haba b'kisnin. The Gemara would then teach that since eating the offering is a mitzvah, the mitzvah itself establishes the meal as fixed, and the kohanim are obligated in zimun even with less than the ordinary amount for a fixed meal. We find similarly in the fifth chapter of Beitzah that Shabbos eating establishes produce for tithing, and in many other places. It is evident there that eating matzah is not regarded as casual eating but as fixed eating, necessarily because the mitzvah makes it fixed. The same novelty could have been taught here regarding kohanim. Necessarily, this proves that even when one actually establishes his meal upon pas haba b'kisnin, the obligation is only D'Rabbanan. Here, where the obligation itself is a Rabbinic enactment, we cannot say that the Sages further enacted that kohanim should recite Bircas HaMazon even for a smaller amount because the mitzvah makes it fixed, for we do not make an enactment upon an enactment. We say that a mitzvah makes eating fixed only where the underlying obligation is Torah law, or at least where the legal force of fixed eating is Torah law. Regarding Bircas HaMazon, however, the amount of a fixed meal for pas haba b'kisnin is itself only D'Rabbanan. By Torah law the obligation depends only upon satiation, not upon establishing a meal. The Sages' rule that pas haba b'kisnin requires Bircas HaMazon when one eats the amount upon which others establish a meal is itself only Rabbinic. We therefore cannot further say that the mitzvah makes it fixed, for that would be an enactment upon an enactment. This supports the conclusion that the obligation is only D'Rabbanan. I had more to write on this, but time did not permit. Perhaps Hashem will grant me another opportunity to explain it further. Examine this carefully.
