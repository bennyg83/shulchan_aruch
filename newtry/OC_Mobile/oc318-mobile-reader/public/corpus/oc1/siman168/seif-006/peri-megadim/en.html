The reason is that according to Torah law Bircas HaMazon is required only when one is actually satiated, and the Sages were therefore lenient in a case of uncertainty. According to Levush, however, who writes in siman 184:6 that one is obligated by Torah law after eating a kebeitzah, see Magen Avraham there, seif katan 11, it requires study why they were lenient here in a case of uncertainty and required the amount upon which others establish a meal, namely three or four eggs, as Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 17, writes in the name of Or Chadash. Nevertheless, the blessing of HaMotzi is Rabbinic, as stated in siman 167, Magen Avraham seif katan 7 and Taz seif katan 10. Moreover, even if it were Torah-level, what difference would there be between HaMotzi and Borei Minei Mezonos?
The humble writer says: I have several thoughts here, and with Hashem's help I shall set them forth. Know that the three blessings of Bircas HaMazon after full-fledged bread are Torah-level. There is room to question whether, after one eats enough pas haba'ah b'kisnin to be satiated, his obligation is Torah-level or Rabbinic. A practical difference arises if he is uncertain whether he recited Bircas HaMazon. Regarding pretzel-like bread, see Yoreh Deah 329, Shach seif katan 9, where some hold that dough kneaded with fruit juice is not considered bread for challah. The same would apply to Bircas HaMazon: it would not be considered bread, and even when one is satiated the obligation would be only Rabbinic, as a decree because of true bread. If one is not satiated, the Sages did not decree. According to the Mechaber there, however, the obligation would be Torah-level. Regarding teruknin, the Tur holds that Mar bar Rav Ashi disagrees, as explained in Berachos 37a and Beit Yosef here. Ring-shaped crackers are full-fledged bread, but since they are ordinarily nibbled for pleasure and not eaten to satiation, the Sages did not impose Bircas HaMazon unless one establishes a meal upon them. If one eats enough to be satiated, however, the obligation is Torah-level. Likewise, bread filled with sugar and almonds is full-fledged bread in the manner of those crackers, and bread formed as filled pockets is Torah-level when one eats enough to be satiated. In a case of doubt he must recite Bircas HaMazon, as in siman 185. According to the Rosh, bread kneaded with fruit juice remains doubtful, since if it is not obligated in challah it may not be considered bread. If one is uncertain whether he recited Bircas HaMazon, there is a double doubt: perhaps he did recite it, and perhaps the halachah follows the Rosh. A double doubt suffices regarding a Torah obligation, and a single doubt suffices regarding a Rabbinic obligation, as explained in Yoreh Deah 110 in similar cases. Kova d'ara is likewise doubtful. We discussed this in our novellae to Berachos 35a and 42a. In Magen Avraham seif katan 16, however, we wrote that the obligation is Torah-level and that challah is different; see there.
I was uncertain about the following case: two people ate full-fledged bread, and a third ate only a kezayis of pas haba'ah b'kisnin, for which he is not required to recite Bircas HaMazon. May he discharge their obligation? What led me to this question is what is written in siman 197, Magen Avraham and Taz, seif katan 11: one who ate a kezayis may discharge those who ate any amount, because by Torah law all Jews are responsible for one another, except that he cannot say, “of which we have eaten.” Here, however, he can truthfully say, “of which we have eaten.” The Sages merely did not obligate him to recite Bircas HaMazon after a kezayis, since there was no need to decree because of a smaller amount, as people do not ordinarily establish a meal upon this food. Nevertheless, he can say, “of which we have eaten.” Or perhaps, whenever one is not Rabbinically obligated, the Sages did not institute that he should discharge others. Two people who ate do not need to say, “of which we have eaten,” yet one cannot discharge the other unless he ate a kezayis, as Magen Avraham 197:11 writes, following siman 193:1. The reason appears to be that the Sages were stringent because the principle of mutual responsibility is weaker here, since had he not eaten he would not be obligated. They therefore required him to be Rabbinically obligated. The same may apply here. With Hashem's help, this will be explained further in siman 197, seif katan 11. See also Magen Avraham 167:40, what I wrote there, and the general introduction on this subject.
