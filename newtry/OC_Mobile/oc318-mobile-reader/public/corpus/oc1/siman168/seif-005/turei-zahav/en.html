If he is not careful regarding gentile bread, etc. The source of this law is plainly the Yerushalmi cited by the Beit Yosef regarding impure and pure bread. The authorities derived from there the law of gentile bread and Jewish bread, where the one reciting the blessing likewise has a choice. Tosafos conclude: “But Rabbeinu Shimshon, the officer of Coucy, instructed that the gentile bread be removed,” and so forth. Certainly the officer of Coucy had no authority to dispute the Yerushalmi. Rather, he did not compare gentile bread to the impure bread mentioned by the Yerushalmi. He held that Jewish bread is preferable and that there is no choice. Nevertheless, the matter was not entirely clear to him, so he instructed that the gentile bread be removed in order to avoid the doubt. Mordechai in the chapter Keitzad Mevarchin likewise records the words of Tosafos, but explains that according to the first opinion one has a choice regarding gentile bread only if he is not careful to avoid it. The Beit Yosef writes that this implies that if he is careful, he must recite the blessing over Jewish bread even if it is coarse. It follows that the officer's instruction applied even to one who was not careful, since Mordechai writes, “But the officer,” and so forth. According to the first opinion, the choice granted by the Yerushalmi concerning impure and pure bread likewise applies only to one who is not careful to avoid the impure bread; one who is careful must bless specifically over the pure bread. Accordingly, the words of the Shulchan Aruch are difficult. It writes that one who is not careful has a choice, while one who is careful should remove the gentile bread. This follows neither opinion. According to the first opinion, one who is careful should bless specifically over Jewish bread; according to the officer, the gentile bread should always be removed. There is a further difficulty. The Beit Yosef cites Terumas HaDeshen as understanding Raavyah in Mordechai to mean that even one who is careful, eating alone, and has before him clean gentile bread and coarse Jewish bread, has a choice. This is cited immediately afterward in the ruling, “If the homeowner is careful,” and so forth. It is clear that Raavyah does not agree with the officer. How, then, could the Shulchan Aruch first rule like the officer and immediately afterward rule leniently where one who is careful has with him someone who is not careful? In truth, the words of Terumas HaDeshen are independently difficult. He rules that it is a mitzvah for one who is careful to break bread over clean gentile bread, whereas the Yerushalmi grants a choice in such a case according to his explanation. Moreover, one would have to say that Raavyah disagrees with Mordechai's earlier statement that one who is careful specifically breaks bread over Jewish bread, which is strained. I have already written in Yoreh Deah, siman 112, that Raavyah permits gentile bread only where a guest who is not careful is eating with him. The Yerushalmi also speaks of one who is not careful. Raavyah brings proof from the Yerushalmi concerning the guest who is not careful, and the guest consequently has a choice according to the Yerushalmi. Here, where one who is not careful is eating with one who is careful, the latter must break bread over that which is preferred by the guest. When eating alone, he can say that he prefers coarse Jewish bread; when the matter depends on another person's preference, he cannot. According to this, the second difficulty is resolved. The earlier ruling to remove the gentile bread like the officer concerns one eating alone, where it is not permitted to him. The later ruling permits it only where he has a guest who is not careful, not as Terumas HaDeshen interpreted that Raavyah's mention of a guest was not specific. Nevertheless, a difficulty remains. Since one who is careful is prohibited from eating gentile bread, he has no intention of eating it; why should he remove it? Rama already rules in the name of Terumas HaDeshen that if one has no intention of eating the second type, one need not consider it at all. Terumas HaDeshen himself is not troubled by this because he follows his own view that even one who is careful and eating alone may eat it. According to my explanation, however, why did the Shulchan Aruch rule that one who is careful must remove it? This is even less relevant than a permitted food that he simply does not intend to eat. It therefore appears to me that words are missing from the Shulchan Aruch, and it should read: “If he is careful regarding gentile bread, he should bless specifically over Jewish bread even if it is coarse; and some say that he should remove,” and so forth. Everything is then clear, for it presents Mordechai's opinions accurately. Nor is there a difficulty from the later ruling concerning one who is careful and has with him someone who is not careful, for there the permission exists specifically because of the guest, as I explained above, not according to Terumas HaDeshen's interpretation. A proof is that the Shulchan Aruch writes, “If the homeowner is careful regarding gentile bread and a Jew who is not careful is eating with him.” Had it followed Terumas HaDeshen, there would have been no reason to mention that a Jew who is not careful was eating with him, since that detail would be nonessential. The Shulchan Aruch should have stated the basic novelty directly. This proves my explanation. My father-in-law likewise rules that one who is careful need not remove the gentile bread at all. Levush raises the following difficulty: Why did the Shulchan Aruch rule that where the homeowner is careful and no other person who is not careful is present, he should remove the gentile bread? Why should he not also have a choice for the same reason that permission was granted where another person who is not careful is present, as Terumas HaDeshen writes? Levush suggests that when another person is present he does not remove it because the clean bread is certainly preferred by that person and must be included in the blessing. When no other person is present and he does not wish to eat it, it is better to remove it. This is not satisfactory. If he has a choice when alone, then it is a mitzvah to give precedence to the clean bread, as Terumas HaDeshen writes and as the Shulchan Aruch immediately cites. If it is prohibited to him, then removal is unnecessary.
