Fine bread and coarse bread of a Jew, etc. In the Shelah, folio 89, he wrote at length to infer that if both are equal, of one species and of one degree of cleanliness, one must give precedence to the bread of a Jew; see there. This is obvious, and I also saw that Tosafot, Berachos, folio 39, and the Mordechai there wrote so explicitly.
He blesses on whichever, etc. The Bach here and in Yoreh Deah, siman 112, challenged the Shulchan Aruch and Levush in this, for it is explained in the Mordechai, chapter Keitzad Mevarchin, that regarding one who is not careful, the minister of Coucy instructed that the white bread be removed; but regarding one who is careful, it need not be removed. Nachalas Tzvi added to the difficulty, for he saw this explicitly in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 32. In my humble opinion, the words of the Mordechai should be explained that the minister of Coucy is indeed always discussing one who is careful. This also seems so, for it implies that the minister of Coucy instructed this for himself, and presumably he was careful about bread of non-Jews. The reason the Mordechai brought this regarding one who is not careful is that he brings proof from one who is careful to one who is not careful: since regarding one who is careful he instructed to remove it, the same applies to one who is not careful, that he blesses on whichever he wishes; if he wants to bless on the kosher bread, he removes the white bread from the table. But in truth he has permission to bless on the white bread, since he is not careful. So it also seems to me to explain the words of Tosafot there, folio 39, s.v. aval, etc. A clear proof to this is that Tosafot and all the poskim bring proof for this law from the Tosefta, that he blesses on whichever he wishes. If so, how could the minister disagree with a Tosefta? And if he disagreed with the proof, Tosafot or one of the poskim should have explained his reason. Rather, perforce, they do not disagree, and according to everyone, one who is not careful blesses on whichever he wishes. So too in the Agudah it implies that the minister refers to one who is careful; see there, and see what I wrote at the end of s.k. 13. As for the halacha: one who is careful should always remove the white bread and bless on kosher bread, and one who is not careful blesses on whichever he wishes; if he wants to bless on the kosher bread, he should remove the white bread. The Magen Avraham in siman 284 wrote in the name of the Maharshal that if another person is with him, he should give him the kosher bread to bless on, and he should bless on the white bread, for in such a case everyone agrees that it is a mitzvah to divide them, end quote. So too the Perishah wrote in his name, that he should give kosher bread to one of the members of his household. In my humble opinion, this does not seem so from the Mordechai and Terumas HaDeshen, siman 32, and it also implies otherwise from the Shulchan Aruch and Levush in the law of a householder who is not careful while the members of his household are careful, and in the law of a householder who is careful while a Jew reclines with him; examine it carefully. It is strained to distinguish between them, and so it also implies explicitly from Issur VeHeter HaAruch, klal 44, and the Bach in Yoreh Deah there. It seems that for this reason the later authorities did not write this, as they wrote the words of the Maharshal above regarding a piece of wheat bread. The Shelah, folio 89, wrote that it is reasonable that the rule that he blesses on whichever he wishes applies when both are whole or both are not whole; but if one is whole, the whole one takes precedence, because it has an additional advantage over the other, end quote. So Nachalas Tzvi also inferred, and I also found in a small Mordechai on parchment as follows: fine impure bread and coarse pure bread, he blesses on whichever he wishes, end quote. Tosafot and the poskim compare bread of non-Jews to impure bread. Nachalas Tzvi further wrote that if the Jewish bread is a fine piece and the non-Jewish bread is a whole coarse loaf, since they said that it is a mitzvah to bless on a whole loaf even if it is coarse, as above in seif 1, it seems that he should place the piece beneath the whole loaf and break from both. But if both are fine, or both are coarse, and the Jewish bread is a piece while the non-Jewish bread is whole, he blesses on whichever he wishes, for they are equal in their advantages, as mentioned above. I am astonished, for in the first case, where the Jewish piece has two advantages, he nevertheless ruled that one should not set aside the whole loaf; all the more so in the latter case, where the piece has only one advantage, one should not set aside the whole loaf because of the piece. If so, at any rate he must break also from the whole loaf; why then did he rule that he blesses on whichever he wishes, which implies that if he wishes he blesses on the piece and leaves the whole loaf entirely? Furthermore, it is difficult to me: since we hold that with coarse Jewish bread and fine non-Jewish bread, he blesses on whichever he wishes, perforce the advantage of Jewish bread is not greater than the advantage of fine bread, but they are completely equal. If so, since we hold in seif 1 that the advantage of whole bread is greater than the advantage of a fine piece, for we bless on the whole loaf even when it is coarse bread, the same should be true that the advantage of whole bread is greater than the advantage of Jewish bread. If so, it would be proper to bless on the whole loaf of a non-Jew even when both are fine; examine this carefully. However, from the small Mordechai cited above it also implies that if both are fine, one does not bless on the whole loaf of a non-Jew, for otherwise it should have taught a greater novelty, even when both are fine. It is possible to force a distinction and reject this, and examine it carefully; so it also seems more from the Shelah that I cited. As a matter of law, it seems to me that even when both are fine and the non-Jewish bread is whole, he should place the piece beneath the whole loaf. One can further say that since the Tosefta and all the poskim mentioned only fine bread of non-Jews and coarse bread of a Jew, it implies that if the non-Jewish bread is wheat and the Jewish bread is barley, he should bless on that of the non-Jew. One whose heart troubles him in this matter should break from both and place the Jewish bread beneath that of the non-Jew.
He removes the bread, etc. The Bach challenged this, saying that he need not remove it. In my humble opinion, the essential law is like the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch and Levush, for had the Bach seen the words of the Agudah and Maharil, he would not have written so. Also, what he wrote above, that the minister of Coucy discusses one who is careful, has its words rejected. The Taz too wrote at length here for no need. Rather, it seems to me that since he eats it only because of perishus, this is not comparable to a case where he does not want to eat from it in seif 1.
Since it is, etc. The Olas Tamid wrote that the Mechaber wrote this reason unnecessarily, for even when the householder is not the one breaking bread, he must break on fine bread, as he wrote regarding a Jew who is not careful reclining with him, etc., end quote. He was not precise in the words of the Levush, for above he wrote that it is permitted to break, which implies that it is optional, and if he wishes he may break on bread of non-Jews, as the Levush wrote, that the Sages permitted him to break on whichever he wishes, and so he wrote at the end of this seif. But here he wrote that he must bless on fine bread, since his intention is to eat only from that bread. The Taz also erred in this, for he learned from the wording that it is a mitzvah that the same applies in every place where he has a choice, that it is a mitzvah to bless on fine bread, and he was astonished at the Levush; see there. He was not precise, for here it is different, since his intention is to eat only from that bread, and this is comparable to seif 1. Even without this, one can distinguish: here, if one of the members of the household were to break bread, he would certainly bless on Jewish bread, since he is not eating fine bread, for the householder does not give him from it. This is unlike above, where one who is not careful reclines with him, in which even the one who is careful wants to eat from it, but refrains because of perishus; whenever it is possible to find a basis that he should eat it permissibly, the Sages permitted him. All this is clear. It is also difficult on Olas Tamid: what did he challenge against the Mechaber, since he copied his words from Terumas HaDeshen, siman 32?
To break on the nice one, etc. The Bach disagrees with this, saying that since he is careful he must bless on that of a Jew. The Nachalas Tzvi explains the matter that the householder should break on his bread, and the guest with him should break on the bread of non-Jews. See the Shach and Taz to Yoreh Deah, siman 112, who upheld and agreed to the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch and Levush; and so is the essential law. For I wrote in Eliyah Rabbah there that the view of the Agudah, Maharai, and Maharil in the laws of cooking of non-Jews is that even one who is careful, when he eats alone, it is good to break and eat from the nice bread of non-Jews. Although we do not rule like them, nevertheless they are worthy to rely upon when another who is not careful reclines with him.
[Levush:] It requires study why, etc. Lechem Chamudos, folio 49, wrote: there is no basis here for difficulty against the Beis Yosef, for the author of Terumas HaDeshen himself wrote that this was the custom, end quote. In my humble opinion, it seems that the Levush holds that what Terumas HaDeshen wrote, that this was the custom, means when he wants to bless on Jewish bread. In truth, when he wants, he may bless on bread of non-Jews, as he wrote explicitly at the end of his words. I wrote similarly in s.k. 9.
[Levush:] And so the custom is, etc. So wrote the Rama. The Nachalas Tzvi wrote that the world is lenient like the first opinion, while in Emek Berachah and the Shelah they wrote that a God-fearing person fulfills all views and should not initially eat bread baked from dough into which honey, oil, milk, or spices were mixed, if the taste of the mixture of fruit juice or spices is recognizable in the dough, except within a meal, meaning that he first blesses HaMotzi on proper bread, or eats the amount that others ordinarily establish as a meal, in which case he blesses HaMotzi and Birkas HaMazon over it. The same law applies to shmalz or putter-kuchen. So wrote the Bach, that one does not take that shmalz and putter-kuchen for lechem mishneh, even though there is a mixture of water there. But bread kneaded with milk and containing no water at all, only actual milk, and the taste of the milk is clearly recognizable, according to everyone it has the law of pas haba'ah b'kisnin, and one does not bless HaMotzi and Birkas HaMazon over it, but only Borei Minei Mezonos when eating it outside a meal, unless he eats the amount that a person normally establishes as a meal. All the more so the kreplach that we make on Purim, end quote. So it is proper to practice. However, what he wrote, that it contains no water at all, is difficult, for he wrote in the gloss there that the Rama and Shulchan Aruch disagree only when the majority is water, but when the majority is fruit juice, even if the minority is water, according to everyone it has the law of kisnin. I then saw in the Bach that he ruled that even when it has no water at all, such as ordinary lekach sold in the market, a God-fearing person should be careful not to eat it except within a meal, because there are poskim who require that the flour not be the primary component. Nevertheless, regarding Purim kreplach and hoizen-blazen, according to everyone it has the law of kisnin, end quote. However, the difficulty on the Shelah still remains. Regarding the words of the Bach, the Taz wrote that they are a lone view. Regarding what the Bach wrote, that hoizen-blazen according to everyone has the law of kisnin, the Magen Avraham wrote that this is specifically when it was kneaded with fat; see seif 13, end quote. This implies that kreplach, even when kneaded with water, according to everyone has the law of kisnin; see below, s.k. 16. It is strained to say that he spoke specifically of hoizen-blazen and that the same applies to kreplach, and so it implies in the Taz. This requires study.
[Levush:] For appetite, etc. Not in order to satisfy, but only for mere dessert. The Bach wrote: such as our kichel. The Magen Avraham wrote that they are called reslech. The Taz wrote that kreplach, even when their filling was removed, have the same law as when eaten with the filling. See below, s.k. 19.
[Levush:] We rule, etc. So wrote the Beis Yosef, as follows: with respect to the halacha, since it is a doubt in a D'Rabbanan matter, we rule like all of them leniently, that one does not bless HaMotzi or the three blessings over them unless he established his meal upon it or ate the amount that people normally establish as a meal, end quote. The Bach wrote: this is astonishing. Why did he call it a leniency? If he does not recite the blessing that is proper for it, either way it is a stringency, for he transgresses You shall not take. Therefore he disagrees with this ruling, that even our kichlin should not be eaten except within a meal, as above. But I found in the Derishah that he wrote as follows: perhaps this is because the blessing Borei Minei Mezonos is a general blessing like Shehakol, and therefore one should bless Borei Minei Mezonos on all of them. For the final blessing we are lenient and shorten, blessing Me'ein Shalosh, even though Me'ein Shalosh does not stand in place of the three blessings, end quote. Similar to this I now saw printed in the Taz, as follows: certainly Borei Minei Mezonos exempts everything, even dough that was mixed. However, because of the importance of bread, HaMotzi and Birkas HaMazon were fixed upon it; therefore, whenever it is not clear to us that it has that importance and is not included in kisnin, we rule leniently, end quote. See below, siman 268, s.k. 18, and see below, s.k. 26; this requires study. I am also astonished at the Beis Yosef, for he contradicts himself, since he already wrote that the matter does not depend on his own establishment of a meal, but on most people, and so he ruled in the Shulchan Aruch. How then did he write here, unless he established a meal or ate the amount, etc.? Perhaps because with all these cases there is a dispute among the poskim, he holds that one may rely on the Raavad cited in the Beis Yosef, that the matter also depends on his own establishment. Although Purim kreplach are also included in this, and I have already written in the name of the Bach and Shelah that everyone agrees in this case, perhaps the Beis Yosef holds that the one who explains kakin as dry cakes holds that regarding kreplach their law is like bread, while the Bach does not hold so. Alternatively, the Bach and Shelah discuss our kreplach, which are kneaded with honey, and in this case everyone agrees that it is permitted. But the Beis Yosef and Shulchan Aruch refer to kreplach that are kneaded with water, only filled with honey and fat; examine this carefully, and it requires study. Also, according to this, why did they not write it in the Shulchan Aruch? Perhaps he retracted.
