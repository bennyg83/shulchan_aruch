If. See Taz and Beit Yosef, who cite Rambam, chapter 3 of Hilchos Berachos, halachah 1, and Bach's explanation. The word “as a stringency” does not appear in Bach; it seems to be Taz's own addition, consistent with his position in seif katan 6 that wherever Borei Minei Mezonos is not recited, HaMotzi exempts everything. It would then follow that Bircas HaMazon is preferable to the abridged blessing, since it contains three blessings. According to Bach there, however, this is difficult. Although Taz rejects Bach's interpretation, if one ate and did not pay attention whether there was a kezayis in one piece, or whether the pieces retained the appearance of bread, there is room to say that even if he did not eat to satiation, so that the obligation is Rabbinic, there is a double doubt: perhaps one crumb contained a kezayis, and perhaps the food retained the appearance of bread. In such a case he should recite Bircas HaMazon; see what I wrote in the conduct of prohibitions and permissions. If one point is certainly defective and only the other remains doubtful, the matter requires analysis. If he ate enough to be satiated, the doubt concerns a Torah obligation and he must recite the blessing; if he did not eat enough to be satiated, it is a Rabbinic doubt and we are lenient. Yet one may argue that according to Torah law he is not obligated because cooking nullifies baking, while the obligation remains Rabbinic. It would then be a Rabbinic doubt, and he should be lenient and recite only the abridged blessing. This depends upon the dispute between Kesef Mishneh and Lechem Mishneh, chapter 8, halachah 5 of Hilchos Korban Pesach, concerning one who roasted the korban and then cooked it. According to Kesef Mishneh, Rambam rules unlike Rav Huna in Pesachim 41a, and the prohibition is Torah-level; according to Lechem Mishneh it is Rabbinic. Yet in Hilchos Chametz U'Matzah, chapter 6, halachah 6, Rambam concludes that the food no longer has the taste of matzah, and so rules Shulchan Aruch in siman 461. This implies that cooking does not nullify baking in itself. Accordingly, the obligation is Torah-level whenever there is a kezayis or the appearance of bread, as stated.
And Taz follows Beit Yosef's general approach. Beit Yosef begins with cooking and broth, but concludes with baked bread. Beit Yosef understands that Rambam rules that regarding broth one favorable factor suffices, following Rav Yosef and Rav Sheshes, whereas regarding cooked bread two conditions are required: a kezayis and the appearance of bread. Taz understands that according to Rambam both conditions are required, whether the bread is cooked or soaked in broth. In cooked bread, even if there was not initially a kezayis in a single crumb, once cooking in a primary vessel causes the pieces to adhere firmly and form a single piece containing a kezayis, it is properly considered a kezayis. If it retains the appearance of bread, one recites HaMotzi and Bircas HaMazon. Broth, however, does not cause the pieces to adhere well, and therefore there must have been a kezayis in one crumb. The appearance of bread is certainly retained in broth so long as it has not soaked for a considerable time; sensory observation shows that its bread-like form has not disappeared. The meaning of Rambam's language is therefore: “if there is a kezayis,” which is the condition for broth, “or the appearance of bread,” which is the condition for cooked bread, while in truth both conditions are required in each case. The opening and conclusion refer to both categories, unlike Beit Yosef's interpretation.
