Obliyash. I do not know what this is, but it appears that it is baked without being rolled out. Rather, dough is mixed with water into a thick batter. According to Rashi, the Gemara's statement that one recites Borei Minei Mezonos over lachmaniyos and Hamotzi if one establishes a meal upon them refers to this. Tosafos reject this because it is full bread, and explain that the passage refers instead to nilush, whose batter is thin and whose product is very thin. This is the item mentioned by the Shulchan Aruch at the end here, called nivlash. Emek Berachah writes that this is what is called nalashniki: flour and water are mixed in a pot like porridge, the batter is poured onto broad leaves, and it is baked in the oven together with the leaves. My father-in-law rules likewise. Emek Berachah asks that Mordechai's statements in the beginning of Keitzad Mevarchin contradict one another. He writes that Rabbeinu Yitzchak was accustomed, even on Purim when one establishes a meal upon nilush, to eat it only during a meal, because perhaps establishing a meal is ineffective. Later in that chapter he writes that Rabbeinu Yitzchak explains lachmaniyos as nilush, while the Gemara states that establishing a meal is effective for lachmaniyos. He answers that there are two kinds of nilush. This does not appear correct to me. Nilush is precisely the kind mentioned by Rabbeinu Yitzchak, baked between two irons, as I also heard from a speaker of the foreign language. Nalashniki, however, are not included in this category but in the category of terisa mentioned in seif 15, for this item is not thin at all. Even if one establishes a meal upon it, he recites Borei Minei Mezonos, as stated below regarding terisa. The Tur writes that the batter is thin and the products are extremely thin. This is the very item Rabbeinu Yitzchak also describes as extremely thin. Although Rabbeinu Yitzchak explained the Gemara's lachmaniyos as nilush, in practical halachah he did not wish to rely on his own explanation. Tosafos likewise write under another interpretation that Rabbeinu Shimshon did not wish to recite Hamotzi over an established meal of nilush. Yet the Beit Yosef cites Rabbeinu Yonah that Rabbeinu Yitzchak the Elder said that regarding the extremely thin wafers made between two irons and called obliyash, establishing a meal upon them is ineffective, and one recites Borei Minei Mezonos. It appears that this cannot be the obliyash mentioned earlier, which we hold to be full bread. The correct reading must be nivlash, meaning the nilush discussed above. Emek Berachah writes that this is not the earlier nilush but an even thinner type, called in the region of Prague waflatkes, baked between two irons, and that Rabbeinu Yitzchak agrees that establishing a meal is ineffective for this type. My father-in-law writes similarly. This is astonishing, for if so we have no clear measure of what is called thin and what is called exceptionally thin. Rather, Rabbeinu Yitzchak the Elder did not state his view as practical halachah, as I explained above. Rosh and Tur, however, had no doubt and hold as practical halachah that thin products made from thin batter require Borei Minei Mezonos, and Hamotzi if one establishes a meal upon them. Nevertheless, as practical halachah, since Rabbeinu Shimshon and Rabbeinu Yitzchak were uncertain, and Tosafos likewise write in Keitzad Mevarchin concerning kuba d'ara that Rabbi Meir was accustomed even on Purim to eat it only during a meal because of the doubt whether an established meal by itself requires Hamotzi, one should not establish a meal upon any very thin product made from thin batter and then recite Hamotzi. The kind called nalashniki, however, may acquire the status of an established meal requiring Hamotzi, since it is not thin at all.
And if he eats them during the meal, etc. This law also applies to pas haba'ah bekisnin mentioned immediately before, as Beit Yosef explicitly writes in the name of Shibolei HaLeket. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch state it here regarding lachmaniyos as an even greater novelty. It is more reasonable that lachmaniyos should be regarded as part of the meal than pas haba'ah bekisnin, since they are called bread, yet even so the blessing over the bread does not exempt their initial blessing. All the more so does this apply to pas haba'ah bekisnin. Mordechai in Keitzad Mevarchin presents the opposite reasoning in the name of Maharam. Regarding lachmaniyos he derives by a kal vachomer from kisnin that they require a blessing during the meal: if kisnin, over which one recites Hamotzi when establishing a meal, is nevertheless not exempted by the bread, and so forth. This appears difficult, for the Gemara also states regarding lachmaniyos, understood as nilush, that if one establishes a meal upon them he recites Hamotzi. It appears that regarding nilush there was doubt whether it is truly the meaning of lachmaniyos. Maharam therefore derives the rule by a kal vachomer from kisnin, regarding which all agree that an established meal is effective. The reason kisnin was clearer to him than lachmaniyos is that Rif explicitly states the blessing of Borei Minei Mezonos regarding kisnin and derives lachmaniyos from them. I will quote Rif: “The conclusion is that when one eats them in the manner of kisnin, he initially recites Borei Minei Mezonos and afterward nothing; when he eats them as an established meal, he recites Hamotzi and the three blessings of Bircas HaMazon, for Rav Yehudah said in the name of Shmuel that one recites Hamotzi over lachmaniyos, and we establish this as referring to one who established a meal upon them.” His meaning is that if one eats a small quantity, not as an established meal, it is by definition eaten in the manner of kisnin, so one recites Borei Minei Mezonos rather than Hamotzi. If one establishes an independent meal upon them, he recites Hamotzi, as the Gemara states regarding lachmaniyos. It follows that if one eats a small quantity even during a meal, he recites Borei Minei Mezonos, because it is unrelated to the meal and has the same law as fruit in siman 177. This law does not depend upon the eater's subjective intention, but upon the ordinary manner of eating among most people, as with kisnin. I write this because I saw in a gloss to Emek Berachah that it distinguishes between lachmaniyos and kisnin on one side and fruit on the other. Regarding fruit, even if one eats it during a meal as an established food rather than dessert, it is considered unrelated to the meal and requires an initial blessing. Regarding kisnin and lachmaniyos, however, it makes the law depend on the eater's intention: if eaten as dessert they require a blessing, while if eaten for satiation they are part of the meal and require no blessing. Its primary basis is that the Tur here says “if he eats them,” while in siman 177 concerning fruit it says “brought before him.” Such a structure need not be refuted, for it is merely homiletic. Beit Yosef, in the name of Shibolei HaLeket, actually uses the expression “brought” regarding these items as well. We have already demonstrated that there is no such distinction during the meal. Rif's essential distinction is between establishing an independent meal upon lachmaniyos without bread and not doing so. A distinction within a bread meal based on the eater's intention never existed. In truth, regarding fruit too, if it is eaten as part of the meal and constitutes his primary food, it requires no blessing. Likewise, if these products are his primary meal, they certainly require no blessing. The true distinction in both cases is whether he eats a small amount in circumstances where ordinary people would or would not establish a meal upon it. One should not listen to a view that causes an unnecessary omission of a blessing, and all the more so one should not make an unnecessary blessing over fruit merely because it is eaten as established food rather than dessert. Furthermore, I saw in that work that specifically kisnin and waflatkes require Borei Minei Mezonos during a meal, but hoizen blasen, which are extremely thin, fried in oil, and always require only Borei Minei Mezonos because they lack the appearance of bread, do not establish a blessing for themselves when served during a meal. Since their original batter was thick and they are not filled with anything, the bread exempts them. This does not appear correct to me. These hoizen blasen, called fritlach in other lands, certainly have the law of pas haba'ah bekisnin because they are fried in fat or another liquid. For that reason one recites Borei Minei Mezonos, not because they lack the appearance of bread, for they certainly do have the appearance of bread. It is therefore clear that one must recite Borei Minei Mezonos over them during a meal, like other kisnin. See what I will write, with Hashem's help, in seif 13 of this siman.
They are secondary to the confection. This refers, for example, to the nougat fried on dough on Purim. One does not recite a blessing over the dough itself even after eating the nougat and leaving the dough alone, because at the time of baking it was not baked as bread but only as secondary to the nougat, similar to what I wrote immediately above regarding pas haba'ah bekisnin. There is, however, a distinction. As long as he eats it together with the nougat, he does not recite any blessing over the dough, following the rule that the blessing over the primary item exempts the secondary item. If he eats the dough by itself, he recites Borei Minei Mezonos over it.
