If it was cooked, etc. The Beit Yosef cites Rambam: “Bread that one broke into pieces and cooked in a pot, or kneaded with broth, if the pieces contain a kezayis or are recognizable as bread and their form has not changed, one recites Hamotzi. If they do not contain a kezayis or the appearance of bread was lost through cooking, one recites Borei Minei Mezonos.” His wording appears self-contradictory. At first, one favorable factor suffices, either a kezayis or the appearance of bread; at the end, two favorable factors appear necessary. The Beit Yosef cites explanations from great authorities and offers his own. My father-in-law also explains that the concluding clause concerns uncertainty: if there is no kezayis and it is uncertain whether the appearance of bread remains, or conversely if the appearance is certainly gone and there is uncertainty whether there is a kezayis, we rule stringently. I do not understand what uncertainty has to do with this, for no special expertise is needed to determine whether the appearance of bread remains, and all the more so whether there is a kezayis. It appears to me that Rambam holds that cooking creates a stronger bond than broth. The case is that initially no individual crumb contained a kezayis, but through cooking the crumbs adhered and became one. Adhesion through cooking is stronger than adhesion through broth, since pieces joined by broth can be separated again. Therefore, in cooking the only distinction is whether the appearance of bread remains. Regarding broth the reverse is true: there is no relevant change in the appearance of bread merely because broth joins the crumbs. Although the next ruling states that when crumbs are placed in water and the water whitens, this changes the bread, here it does not concern soaking in broth but merely crumbs joined by broth. There the law depends upon whether a kezayis existed before the adhesion. Rambam derived this from the Gemara. Initially the Gemara makes the law depend on whether the pieces remain intact, meaning whether they contain a kezayis, as Tosafos explains from the Yerushalmi, and it does not mention the appearance of bread. Later, in the statements of Rav Sheshes and Rava, the matter depends upon appearance and not upon a kezayis. One must therefore say that the discussion of intact pieces concerns broth, where a change of appearance is irrelevant, while Rav Sheshes and Rava concern cooking, where size is irrelevant because cooking creates a strong bond and the law depends upon appearance. Rambam thus properly states at first that there are two kinds of adhesion, one through cooking and one through broth, each with its own criterion. With broth there must be a kezayis, after which a change of appearance is irrelevant. With cooking the appearance must remain recognizable, while a kezayis is unnecessary. He then concludes that if the broth-bound pieces do not contain a kezayis, or if cooking destroyed their appearance, meaning that each case contains the defect relevant to it, one does not recite Hamotzi. This is why he writes at the end “or its appearance was lost through cooking,” which otherwise appears superfluous, in order to identify the context in which loss of appearance is relevant. This is the correct explanation of Rambam. As practical halachah, however, we have only the words of Rabbeinu Yonah copied here in the Shulchan Aruch.
