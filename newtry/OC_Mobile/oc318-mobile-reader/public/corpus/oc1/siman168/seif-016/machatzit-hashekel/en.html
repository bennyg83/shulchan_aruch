(Seif katan 16) Into which he mixed, etc. It is difficult from the Rambam, etc. His intent is that the Beit Yosef changed the Rambam's language according to the Beit Yosef's own interpretation of the Rambam, and the Magen Avraham wanted to prove from the Rambam's own words that the Beit Yosef's interpretation is necessarily incorrect.
And the Gemara implies somewhat. This refers to page 37, which the Magen Avraham cites afterward: Rabbi Yosef said that if chavitzah contains crumbs of an olive-bulk, one recites HaMotzi, and if not, Borei Minei Mezonos. The Gemara asks from a case where one collected from all of them, meaning from all five grain species according to Tosafot, an olive-bulk and ate it: if it is chametz he is liable to kares, and if it is matzah he fulfills his obligation on Pesach. This proves that even if no single crumb contains an olive-bulk, it is nevertheless called bread, for it says he collected from all of them, implying no one piece alone is an olive-bulk. The Gemara held that since it is called bread regarding matzah, so that one fulfills the mitzvah with it, it is likewise called bread for HaMotzi, and this challenges Rabbi Yosef. Thus the Gemara compared HaMotzi to matzah on the night of Pesach. Although one could reject this and say that it compares them only regarding quantity and measure, namely whether an olive-bulk measure is called a measure for matzah and therefore for HaMotzi, but it does not prove that what is called bread for matzah is also called bread for HaMotzi, nevertheless there is some implication.
So too it appears from the Rambam, chapter 6, law 7, where he writes: a person does not fulfill his obligation by eating matzah forbidden to him, such as, etc.; the general rule is that anything over which one recites Birkas HaMazon, he fulfills his obligation with it, and anything over which one does not, etc. Birkas HaMazon and HaMotzi have one law. Still, one may reject this somewhat and say that only regarding eating a forbidden item their laws are equal, not regarding what is called bread. Nevertheless, there is some implication, since he stated it as a general rule.
And it is further difficult: if so, he should eat, etc. This does not appear from siman 188, where we rule that if one forgot Ya'aleh VeYavo in Birkas HaMazon on Yom Tov, he must return and recite Birkas HaMazon again. This is unlike Rosh Chodesh, for on Rosh Chodesh, if he wishes he need not eat bread, whereas on Shabbos and Yom Tov it is impossible not to eat bread.
The Ran, cited by the Magen Avraham below in siman 188, asked that we say only the first night of Pesach has an obligation to eat matzah, not the other days. The Ran answered that on the first night, in order to fulfill the obligation of matzah, it must be poor man's matzah, whereas on the other nights and days of Pesach one may eat rich matzah. But rich matzah means dough into which fruit juice was mixed. If the Beit Yosef's view here were correct, one would not recite HaMotzi or Birkas HaMazon on rich matzah. If so, how can we say that on Yom Tov it is impossible not to eat bread, and therefore one who forgot Ya'aleh VeYavo must go back and recite Birkas HaMazon? He could eat rich matzah, which is not bread.
And one should not say that the Rambam specifically, etc. That he does not fulfill his obligation with it on Pesach, and that what the Rambam said in chapter 7, that if he kneaded with fruit juice he fulfills the obligation, refers to other fruits apart from the four items, as the Maggid Mishneh wrote there.
Wine is like honey, as written in the Rambam in the laws of chametz.
And alternatively, here, etc. Where he wrote 'kneaded,' etc., it should apparently read: where it was all kneaded with honey.
But there it is speaking, meaning in chapter 7 of Hilchos Chametz, where the Rambam wrote that if he kneaded with fruit juice he fulfills the obligation. This refers to where there is a little water in it, and in any case it must be speaking where there is a little water, as the Maggid Mishneh wrote there, for otherwise it cannot become chametz, and we rule that one does not fulfill the mitzvah with such matzah, as it is written: do not eat chametz with it, seven days you shall eat matzah, from which we derive that only something capable of becoming chametz qualifies for matzah. Since it contains a little water, it truly is bread and one blesses HaMotzi. This resolves the first difficulty from the Rambam and the Gemara in Berachos. But the difficulty from siman 188 remains, whether according to the first answer or the second, since the Ran wrote that on the other days one may eat rich matzah. According to the first answer, this means specifically where one mixed in one of the four items, honey, milk, etc., and one would not recite HaMotzi or Birkas HaMazon on it; according to the second, where it lacks any admixture of water, one likewise does not recite HaMotzi or Birkas HaMazon. How then can we say it is impossible not to eat bread? In truth, the Magen Avraham did not intend this difficulty from siman 188 as a challenge to the Rambam, for perhaps the Rambam does not accept the Ran's answer, but follows the poskim who say that the statement that on Yom Tov one cannot avoid eating bread refers specifically to the first night of Pesach and the first night of Sukkos. Rather, the Magen Avraham's challenge is to us, according to the Beit Yosef, who here copied the Rambam according to his interpretation, that if one mixed in honey, etc., he does not recite HaMotzi; yet below in siman 188 he seems to accept the Ran, as the Magen Avraham writes there. The Magen Avraham's answer is therefore sound: regarding the Rambam there is no difficulty, and regarding us, we may not accept the Rambam's rule that anything over which one does not recite HaMotzi and Birkas HaMazon cannot be used for matzah. Once the Magen Avraham concludes against the Beit Yosef, holding that one must recite HaMotzi even if fruit juice was mixed in, unless there is no water at all, and below in siman 471 he concludes that even a little oil makes it rich matzah so one does not fulfill the first-night obligation, then throughout Pesach one can eat rich matzah containing a little oil and nevertheless recite HaMotzi and Birkas HaMazon according to the Magen Avraham. This is unlike the Beit Yosef's view, that even if only a little oil was mixed in, if its taste is recognizable one does not recite HaMotzi; for then if the oil is recognizable, he would not recite HaMotzi, and if it is so little that its taste is not recognizable, it is not called rich matzah either, as the Magen Avraham agrees in siman 471.
It requires study that the Beit Yosef changed the Rambam's language. The Rambam wrote 'kneaded,' which supports the Magen Avraham's explanation, while the Beit Yosef wrote 'mixed,' following the Beit Yosef's interpretation in his work. That interpretation is necessarily incorrect, as the Magen Avraham wrote.
The Rama's words are primary, for he wrote that the honey and spices are nearly the primary element. Although the Rama implies that there is some water mixed in, with the honey primary, while the Magen Avraham proved above that it is speaking where there is no water at all, nevertheless for practical law he agreed with the Rama.
