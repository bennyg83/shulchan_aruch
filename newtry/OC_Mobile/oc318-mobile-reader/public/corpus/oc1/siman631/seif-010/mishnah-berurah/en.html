(27) Like a hut. Meaning, it is made like a beehive, narrowing as it rises, so that its roof and walls are one continuous surface.
(28) It is invalid. This applies even if he made the sloping walls from valid s'chach, because there is neither a wall nor a roof here.
(29) If it had a roof even one tefach wide. Whether he made it like a hut but did not slope it all the way to a sharp point, leaving an opening one tefach wide, or he leaned the top of the wall against another wall but left a space of one tefach between them, in all these cases we treat the open space as closed. Any gap of less than three tefachim is treated as lavud, and it is therefore considered to have a roof. See Magen Avraham, who writes that even if there is not a full tefach at the very top, it is permitted provided that within less than three tefachim of the top there is a width of one tefach.
(30) Or if he raised, etc. Meaning, he raised it vertically. This applies even if the raised area is open space, such as when he set its corners upon stones. Any gap of less than three tefachim is treated as lavud and is considered closed. That tefach is regarded as the wall of the sukkah, and its slope is regarded as the roof. Nevertheless, the sukkah must be ten tefachim high, as explained below.
(31) Some say that the tefach may not consist of open space, etc. Although the rule of lavud applies, an area of open space is not called a roof. Likewise, an open space below does not count toward the height of the partition.
(32) From the wall or from the s'chach. Meaning, when the tefach is made near the ground, it must be a constructed part of the wall and not open space. Likewise, when the tefach is made above as a roof, it must be s'chach and not open space.
(33) Ten tefachim high. This excludes a case where the sukkah is seven by seven tefachim below the height of ten tefachim, but when the slope reaches a height of ten tefachim it is no longer seven by seven tefachim wide. Such a sukkah is invalid. The ten tefachim of height are measured vertically, not along the diagonal.
(34) For they are the s'chach. It appears that this ruling applies to both cases mentioned above, whether he made a tefach below as part of the wall or distanced the upper end of the wall from the adjacent wall and made a roof one tefach wide. In both cases, from the height of ten tefachim and upward, the sloping section is regarded as s'chach. Peri Megadim writes that whenever the early authorities dispute the validity of a sukkah, if the issue concerns a Torah law one must rule stringently. If he has no other sukkah, he should eat there without a blessing. If the issue is rabbinic, then when he has another sukkah it is proper to be stringent and use it, but when he has no other sukkah he fulfills his obligation in this sukkah and may also recite the blessing. This still requires some further study. End of his words.
