Se'if 1. A sukkah whose sunlight and shade are equal above, etc. Note: The source of this law is Sukkah 22b, where it asks: "And whose shade is greater than its sunlight is valid" - implying that if they are equal it is invalid. But did we not learn: "whose sunlight is greater than its shade is invalid" - implying that if they are equal it is valid? This is difficult to me according to those who hold that we do not say a double doubt in the place of a presumption, and according to what I proved in my book Pri Etz Hadar, that a presumption of obligation in a mitzvah is considered a presumption. Tosafos in the first chapter of Eruvin asked why breached space equal to standing material is permitted, since it is impossible to be exact. They answered in the name of Rabbeinu Shemayah that it is a double doubt: perhaps they are equal, and perhaps the standing material is greater. See there. If so, why does the Talmud ask here? It is known that sukkah is obligatory on the first night, and from then onward optional. If so, let us say that the Mishnah which teaches that we require its shade to be greater, implying that if they are equal it is invalid, refers to the first night, when there is an obligation; therefore a double doubt does not help, and since it is impossible to be exact, we require that we know that its shade is greater. But the Mishnah which teaches that if its sunlight is greater than its shade it is invalid, implying that if they are equal it is valid, refers to the other days, for if he wishes he need not eat; if so, there is no presumption of obligation, and a double doubt helps, and therefore it is valid when they are equal. This requires analysis; examine carefully. Now in the Yerushalmi, first chapter of Sukkah, halachah 1, we have the text: "Whose sunlight is greater than its shade is invalid" - implying that half and half is valid. "A disordered sukkah whose shade is greater than its sunlight is valid." Here you say valid, and here you say invalid. The Rabbis of Caesarea, Rabbi Yitzchak bar Nachman in the name of Rabbi Hoshaya: it is different with a tree, whose way is to stride. These are its words. Now the Korban HaEidah explained that "it is different with a tree, whose way is to stride" means things whose way is to become spoiled, etc. This is astonishing, for if so, why speak specifically when shade and sunlight are equal? Even if its shade is greater, we should be concerned for that. Rather, it appears that the intent is another way, and it is itself the intent of the Talmud's answer, which answers: here above, here below. This will be a proof to Rashi's explanation, for he explains "like an istira coin from below," that below it is more. This is what it says: here, that it answers like the Talmud's answer, here above and here below, meaning that if above they are equal, below the sunlight is greater. This is what it says: although half and half is like a majority, it is different like a tree whose way is to stride and spread, for things whose way is to stride and spread more, in this case half and half is not like a majority, since below the sunlight is greater than the shade. This is what it means by "a tree whose way is to stride and spread," and it works well. Now, while on this matter, I saw that from here one can resolve what the Pri Megadim was uncertain about in the introduction to Hilchos Berachos, in the general introduction, part 3, law 12: whether a double doubt also helps to recite a blessing, or whether regarding a blessing a double doubt does not help. It appears that this sugya resolves it. It is difficult to me: why does the Talmud ask from the Mishnayos against one another according to the poskim who hold that we do not say a double doubt in the place of a presumption? The Rashba answered the Rambam's words, for the Rambam holds that a Torah doubt is lenient by Torah law. They asked against him from the Talmud in the first chapter of Chullin, which asks that we follow the majority from the head of the burnt offering; yet one could say that a Torah doubt is lenient by Torah law. The Rashba answered that there is a presumption of life, etc., and the Rambam agrees that it is stringent. Now Tosafos in the first chapter of Eruvin asked regarding breached space equal to standing material: it is impossible to be exact. Rabbeinu Yeshayah answered that it is a double doubt: perhaps the standing material is greater, and perhaps they are equal. According to this, it is difficult: why does the Talmud ask? Let us say that the Mishnah which invalidates when they are equal is speaking of the first night, when there is a presumption of obligation; therefore a double doubt does not help, and because of Tosafos' question that it is impossible to be exact, equality does not help. And the Mishnah which validates equality refers to the other days, when one is not obligated to eat in the sukkah and there is no presumption of obligation; therefore we again rely on the double doubt, and equality is permitted. What is the question? The Talmud answers similarly in the third chapter regarding one who was traveling and had no lulav, that it refers to the second day of Yom Tov. It could have answered here likewise. Rather, perforce it is proven that one does not recite a blessing based on a double doubt; and if one does not recite a blessing, the language "valid" does not apply to this, for it should have clarified that one does not recite a blessing. This is proven; examine carefully. Now regarding what is stated in Shulchan Aruch, that a sukkah whose sunlight and shade are equal above is invalid: in the Mishnah in the first chapter of Sukkah we have the text, "whose sunlight is greater than its shade is invalid." See Rashi there, and see what the Ran wrote about this. I do not know why he did not prove otherwise, that wherever the matter is recognizable we rule in every place that nullification is not relevant at all. True, Rashi's language does not bear the Ran's language, for he wrote, "and because of the s'chach it is called sukkah," implying that he holds that the s'chach is nullified; for if not, he should have said, "because of the shade it is called sukkah," as it is written, "and a sukkah shall be for shade by day." This requires analysis; examine carefully. So I wrote in the margin of the Ran in my youth. In truth, the Ran himself wrote this reasoning later regarding where one trained a vine over it, that nullification does not apply when the prohibited matter is recognizable, and his words seemingly contradict one another. But now I saw and set my heart to resolve his words as follows. The rule is this: the fact that a recognizable prohibited item is not nullified applies only when we wish to use the body of the minority itself, such as a prohibited item that fell into permitted items, where we want to eat the prohibited item too. Since we want to use the minority just as the majority, we require that the prohibited item not be recognizable; if it is recognizable, it is not drawn after the majority. But if we do not want to use the minority itself, only to investigate some other matter, whether it has this status or that status, in that case we follow the majority even when the prohibited item is recognizable. A proof is from Sanhedrin, where we follow the majority although the minority is recognizable on its own. True, one could say that there the speech is nullified in the majority, and the speech is not recognizable, as I wrote something similar regarding a fixed item, that speech is considered mobile. However, from the fact that they asked from the law of a fixed item and did not ask even without that, that it is like a recognizable prohibited item, perforce this is otherwise acceptable. That is because we do not use the body of the minority, but only investigate another matter: whether this one should pay or be exempt, and the like. In such a case we follow the majority even though the prohibited item is recognizable. Nevertheless, this person is not called liable or innocent except according to the majority, not according to the minority. Therefore here too, regarding sukkah, there are two matters. One is that it must actually be roofed with s'chach, and the second is that the name sukkah must be upon it, for Scripture called it a sukkah. Therefore, there, where one trained a vine over it, where he wants to validate the invalid material so that he may sit beneath it and so that it should be actual s'chach, then although regarding the name it may be called a sukkah because the majority is valid, nevertheless how can he come to use the minority itself? In truth the prohibited material is recognizable, and whenever it is recognizable it is not nullified for using it itself as valid. But where its sunlight is greater than its shade, the reverse is true. If we investigate whether it is permitted to sit beneath the s'chach, one could certainly say that it is permitted to sit even if its sunlight is greater, for whenever the prohibited matter is recognizable, it is not nullified to be used like the majority. However, regarding the name sukkah, since in any event its sunlight is greater, the name sukkah is not upon it. In this respect the minority is indeed considered as though it does not exist, even though it is recognizable, because we are not investigating the actual use of the minority, but the name sukkah, whether it is called a sukkah or not. In that respect it is certainly not called a sukkah, since the majority is sunlight, and in this the minority is nullified in the majority even though the prohibited matter is recognizable. How sweet is Rashi's language according to this, for he wrote, "and because of the s'chach it is called sukkah." What did Rashi need with this? Did we not know without this that a sukkah requires s'chach above, and that open air is not s'chach? Rashi should have explained this earlier, immediately in the Mishnah of a sukkah that is higher than twenty amos, that it means the s'chach, for a sukkah requires s'chach. But according to what I wrote it works well: Rashi did not need to say that a sukkah requires s'chach above, for that is obvious. Rather, his intent is that it is nullified in the majority, and even though the prohibited matter is recognizable, nevertheless since it is called sukkah because of the s'chach, here, since the majority is sunlight and there is no s'chach, the name sukkah is not upon it. If so, one does not fulfill the obligation with it, for we require the name sukkah to be upon it, and in this respect, in any event, its name is not sukkah. This works well. However, the Ran did not intend this in Rashi's words, for he explained Rashi's intent differently, and perforce he was not precise about what Rashi wrote, "and because of the s'chach." Nevertheless, the core reasoning is correct in the intent of the Ran's words. According to what I wrote above, that Rashi's language does not indicate like the Ran, what Rashi wrote, "and because of the s'chach it is called sukkah," works very well. This is fine; examine carefully. See what I wrote about this in my novellae, siman 629, and below, siman 651:1, with Hashem's help. Examine carefully.
