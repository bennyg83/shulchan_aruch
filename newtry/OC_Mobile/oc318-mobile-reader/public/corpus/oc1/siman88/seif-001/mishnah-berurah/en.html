(1) In order, etc. This is also because an emission results from lightheadedness, whereas words of Torah must be approached with awe and fear, trembling and perspiration, as at the time the Torah was given.
(2) And afterward they abolished it. Nevertheless, one who wishes to observe the practice and immerse is deserving of blessing, provided that this will not cause him to miss the proper time for Krias Shema or prayer. It is possible that even if it will cause him to miss communal prayer, it is not proper to be stringent in this matter.
(3) That enactment. It was a decree that the majority of the community could not uphold, and they abolished it because it caused neglect of Torah study and neglect of the mitzvah of procreation.
(4) The custom. Nevertheless, there are exemplary men who observe this enactment and immerse after an emission. If immersion is difficult for them, they wash themselves with nine kav. The later authorities write that forty se'ah of drawn water are valid for this immersion. The nine kav may likewise consist of drawn water, even when poured from three vessels, provided that one does not pause after pouring from one vessel before beginning the next. More than three vessels do not combine to form the measure of nine kav. These nine kav must be poured over him; he may not immerse in them, whether they are in a vessel or collected in the ground. Whenever purification is performed by immersion, forty se'ah collected in the ground are required, as with other immersions. Nevertheless, if he entered a mikveh with half his body but did not bend his head to immerse the half remaining outside, and nine kav were poured over him from above, this is effective for purification. Mateh Efrayim writes that when the nine kav are poured over a person, he should stand upright with both hands placed opposite his heart. He should not press them tightly against his body, but leave a slight space so that the water reaches there as well. He should caution those pouring to direct the water upon his actual body. Regarding the measure of nine kav, Baer Heitev states that it equals fifteen Polish quarts. Mateh Efrayim writes that according to our quart measure, twenty-four quarts are needed for pouring nine kav. In pressing circumstances, when the vessels do not hold so much, one may be lenient with eighteen quarts. Those who customarily purify themselves on Erev Rosh Hashanah and Erev Yom Kippur by means of nine kav should likewise observe all these details. Magen Avraham writes that even one who observes immersion after an emission is obligated only if he is healthy, or if he is ill but had marital relations. An ill person who experienced an involuntary emission is entirely exempt and need only wash that place so that it is not soiled with semen. Likewise, a healthy person who saw separated drops is entirely exempt, because Ezra did not decree concerning this with respect to words of Torah and prayer. Only with respect to terumah is he impure because of separated drops.
(5) A niddah. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that some mistakenly say that women after childbirth may not enter the synagogue until forty days have passed after the birth of a boy and eighty days after the birth of a girl. This is an erroneous custom and it must be protested.
(6) Or to pray, etc. Binyamin Ze'ev, siman 153, writes that the custom was only that she not enter the synagogue or look at a Sefer Torah. Even when she prays, she does not stand before her companions. They do this as a matter of custom and respect, not because of any prohibition. The later authorities likewise agree that she must pray at home and recite all the blessings, especially Bircas HaMazon and Kiddush, which are D'Oraisa. See Magen Avraham.
(7) But the custom, etc. In our countries the accepted practice is always to permit them, and they recite blessings and pray. Nevertheless, they should not look at the Sefer Torah when it is raised to be shown to the congregation. Chayei Adam. He further writes that they should not enter a cemetery until they have immersed.
(8) Awe-inspiring. From the first day of Selichos onward, the period is called the Days of Awe for all matters of this kind. The same applies when she is marrying off her son or daughter, or when she herself has given birth and women go to the synagogue after four weeks. This is permitted even though she is a niddah. Since they are permitted to go to the synagogue, all opinions also permit them to pray in all these cases.
