Anything, etc. Meaning, even if they brought him several kinds that have an aroma or acidity, he must give him from each one. This is implied by the Gemara; see there.
He must give him from it, etc. It is reasonable that if one has a servant whom he hired for the year and told him at the outset, “Whenever you bring me something that has acidity or aroma, I permit you to taste it first,” this is sufficient.
To the attendant. This applies even if he does not serve during the meal but merely cooks the food, and even if his food is not the employer's responsibility. Magen Avraham below, siman 170, seif katan 8. The Gemara implies even more: even an ordinary person who is not serving him, if he happens to be standing there when something aromatic or acidic is brought, must be given a small amount to taste so that he not come to danger. The Gemara, in Rav Huna's statement there, and likewise the Shulchan Aruch, state the law regarding an attendant perhaps because they describe the usual case. An attendant is certainly present when the food is brought, whereas another person does not ordinarily sit there while they eat. It is possible that this is the basis of the universal Jewish custom that when a person enters another's home while they are eating, they invite him to eat.
Immediately. See Mishnah Berurah. It appears that this entire law applies in places where the servant does not eat from the same kinds of food served to the employer, and his appetite is therefore aroused. In places where the custom is that the servant also eats from the foods brought to the employer, even if he has not yet eaten from them, there is no obligation to give him first. This resembles Magen Avraham's ruling regarding one who reclines at the table with them, where there is no concern. It is possible that this is why people are not accustomed to be careful about this law.
