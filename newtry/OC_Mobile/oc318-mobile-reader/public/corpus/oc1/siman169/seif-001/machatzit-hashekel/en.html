Seif katan 1: To the attendant immediately. Meaning, a small amount. This is also implied by seif 3, which states that if an important person is present beside the attendant, he need not recite a blessing over every slice because he knows that they will give him all the bread he needs. Taz writes that for this reason the Shulchan Aruch was precise in writing “from bread.” Specifically regarding bread, which they are in any event obligated to give him, the important person will certainly warn them. This does not refer only to bread, but to anything they are obligated to give him, as stated at the beginning of this siman. Regarding something that is only an act of piety, however, he must recite a blessing over everything they give him, because perhaps they will not act piously toward him, and the scholar will not warn them about something that is not obligatory. Yet the question remains: why mention bread specifically? The same is true of another food that has an aroma, which they are legally obligated to give him, and the scholar would warn them about that as well. It should therefore have said that in such a case too he need not bless each time they give him. It must be that for something with an aroma, it suffices to give him a small amount. Even if the scholar warns them and they listen and give it to him, the small amount given immediately is sufficient. If they later give him more, it is considered a new decision. We certainly cannot say that they must give him a large amount and that even that large portion must be given immediately, since a small amount suffices to calm his desire. Even if they must ultimately give him more, they certainly need not give it immediately. It is no more stringent than bread, regarding which seif 3 implies that they do not give him all the bread he needs at once.
Siman 170, seif 3, also provides some proof. It states that each diner leaves a portion in the bowl. Although as an act of piety one must give the attendant immediately from every kind, one could say that the passage there states only the strict law and does not discuss acts of piety. Yet its unqualified wording implies that for all foods it suffices to leave him a portion in the bowl. But for something with an aroma, it must be given immediately. It must therefore be that a small amount suffices immediately, while that passage discusses what must be left for his full needs.
And if he stipulated, etc. He need not give, etc. Meaning, even one who ordinarily conducts himself piously.
For this reason we are not accustomed, etc. Since the worker knows the prevailing custom, it is as though it was stipulated.
We also allow servants to carry, etc. This is ordinarily forbidden because the verse states, “You shall not make him perform the labor of a slave,” which Toras Kohanim expounds as forbidding work uniquely associated with slaves, as Rambam writes in chapter 1 of Hilchos Avadim.
But Sifrei expounds, etc. Meaning, regarding the permissibility of having him carry our clothing after us to the bathhouse, Shelah's answer that it is as though stipulated is unnecessary, because it is permitted even without that.
“You may not rule ruthlessly” means that one may not tell him, “Dig beneath the vine for me,” when he has no need for it and intends only to distress him. Rambam likewise writes this in chapter 1 of Hilchos Avadim. See there regarding the dispute between Rambam and Raavad on this matter.
“But you may rule over a free person.” Magen Avraham understands that the same exposition applies to the verse, “You shall not make him perform the labor of a slave”: You may not make him do so, but you may make a free person do so. In truth, however, I examined Yalkut in Parashas Behar and did not find the exposition “but you may rule over a free person” on the verse “You may not rule over him ruthlessly.” Rather, it expounds this only on “You shall not make him perform,” etc.: “but you may work a free person.” See there.
Our servants are considered like free persons, since they may withdraw even in the middle of the day, as is the law of a hired worker. Rambam likewise writes there: “When does this apply? To a Hebrew slave, because his spirit is lowly on account of his sale. But an Israelite who was not sold may be used as a slave, since he performs the work only willingly and of his own accord.” Magen Avraham holds that the same applies to a hired worker. Rambam's words are cited in Sema, siman 369, seif katan 18.
It appears to me that with regard to food, etc. Meaning, Shelah's answer is insufficient for one who conducts himself piously. Even with other kinds that have no aroma there is nevertheless some distress, and perhaps this is why, as an act of piety, one gives from every kind. A food with an aroma, however, causes greater distress, and there is concern that he may be harmed if he does not eat, as Rashi writes; therefore, one must give it to him as a matter of law. The phrase “and specifically,” etc., begins a separate matter and refers back to the words of the Shulchan Aruch.
