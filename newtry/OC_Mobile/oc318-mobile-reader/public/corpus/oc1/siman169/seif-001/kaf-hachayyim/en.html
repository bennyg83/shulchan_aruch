1) [Seif 1] Anything that is brought, etc., one must give some of it to the attendant, etc. For we say in Kesubos 61a: all foods may be kept waiting before the attendant, except fatty meat throughout the year and aged wine during the season of Tammuz. The Gemara concludes that the general rule is that anything with an aroma or pungency must be given to him. Rashi, of blessed memory, explains: all foods may be kept waiting before the attendant serving at the meal. The diners may eat before him and delay feeding him until the guests arise, except for fatty meat, etc., which he craves and whose sight causes him distress. During the season of Tammuz, aged wine has a strong aroma, and the heat of the day burns within him like venom. The general rule is that anything with an aroma or pungency causes aching of the teeth and harms one before whom others eat while he does not eat. Avuh bar Ihi and Minyamin bar Ihi acted differently. One fed his attendant from every type of food, while the other fed him from only one type. Eliyahu would speak with one but would not speak with the other. There were likewise two pious men. One fed the attendant early, while the other delayed feeding him. Eliyahu would speak with the one who fed early but would not speak with the one who delayed. The Tur and Beit Yosef cite this.
2) There: One must give some of it to the attendant, etc. Meaning, a small amount. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1. This implies that even when the food has an aroma, a small amount suffices. We likewise find in Kesubos 61a that he took some on his finger and placed it into his mouth. Eliyah Rabbah there. Levushei Serad writes the same.
3) There: One must give some of it to the attendant, etc. The Shelah, folio 82a, writes that if one stipulates with the attendant at the time of hiring that he will not be given food from the dishes his employer eats, but will receive the same food as the other household members, then even as an act of piety one need not give him from every type, except a type with an aroma that a person craves. We find similarly regarding a Hebrew servant: the Torah says that one may not impose slave labor upon him, such as having him put on his master's shoes or carry his utensils behind him to the bathhouse. We nevertheless require all these tasks from our attendants because we hire an attendant from the outset on the condition that he perform them. Regarding the Shelah's statement that attendants are considered to have accepted these terms, Sifrei expounds: 'You may not rule over him with crushing labor, but you may rule over a free person with crushing labor.' Magen Avraham cites this and writes that our servants are considered free people, since they may withdraw in the middle of the day and do not have the status of servants. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 333, seif 3. Regarding the Shelah's ruling that one may stipulate with the attendant and need not give him from the food, Magen Avraham writes there that a stipulation is ineffective concerning food, because the reason for the obligation is his distress. His intent is that even regarding food without an aroma the stipulation is ineffective, for in the preceding paragraph the Shelah also agrees about food with an aroma, as explained above. Machatzis HaShekel writes the same; see there. Regarding R' Nesiv Chaim's question upon Magen Avraham, see Petach HaDevir, paragraph 1, who resolves it. He also writes that the dispute between Magen Avraham and the Shelah as to whether a stipulation is effective was already disputed by the great earlier authorities, the Rambam and Rashi, of blessed memory; see there.
