We find similarly regarding a Hebrew servant: the Torah says that one may not impose slave labor upon him, such as having him put on his master's shoes or carry his utensils behind him to the bathhouse. We nevertheless require all these tasks from our attendants because we hire an attendant from the outset on the condition that he perform them. Regarding the Shelah's statement that attendants are considered to have accepted these terms, Sifrei expounds: 'You may not rule over him with crushing labor, but you may rule over a free person with crushing labor.' Magen Avraham cites this and writes that our servants are considered free people, since they may withdraw in the middle of the day and do not have the status of servants. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 333, seif 3. Regarding the Shelah's ruling that one may stipulate with the attendant and need not give him from the food, Magen Avraham writes there that a stipulation is ineffective concerning food, because the reason for the obligation is his distress. His intent is that even regarding food without an aroma the stipulation is ineffective, for in the preceding paragraph the Shelah also agrees about food with an aroma, as explained above. Machatzis HaShekel writes the same; see there. Regarding R' Nesiv Chaim's question upon Magen Avraham, see Petach HaDevir, paragraph 1, who resolves it. He also writes that the dispute between Magen Avraham and the Shelah as to whether a stipulation is effective was already disputed by the great earlier authorities, the Rambam and Rashi, of blessed memory; see there.
