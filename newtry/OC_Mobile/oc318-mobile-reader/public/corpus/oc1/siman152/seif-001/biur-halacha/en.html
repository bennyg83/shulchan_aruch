One may not demolish, etc. The same applies if they wish to sell it and purchase another with the proceeds: it is forbidden before they have purchased the other one. So write the later authorities.
Lest they fail to build the other one. See Mishnah Berurah, where we cited the Taz and Magen Avraham. Many later authorities rule in accordance with the Taz, while many others are stringent like Magen Avraham. In my humble opinion, since all agree that demolishing in order to rebuild does not involve a Torah prohibition, and the Sages prohibited it only out of concern that they might later be negligent and fail to rebuild, and since we do not find any Rishon who explicitly adopts the stringent view, as Taz discusses at length, while many Rishonim explicitly rule leniently, one should not be stringent. These include Ramban, cited by Ran in Megillah and quoted by Rama below in siman 153, seif 7, in the gloss, as Taz writes. This language also appears in Sefer HaEshkol. Although Magen Avraham reconciles it in seif katan 16, the plain wording supports Taz. Eliyah Rabbah likewise understands Rama in accordance with Taz, and this is also clear from Biur HaGra there and here. Rashba, Nimukei Yosef, and Tosafos explicitly permit it where another established synagogue exists in the city. This is also the view of Agudah, as Eliyah Rabbah writes, and it appears likewise from Meiri, who writes, “lest they be negligent and find themselves without a synagogue,” implying that where there is an established synagogue, it is permitted. One should therefore not be stringent. All this applies only if the established synagogue has ample room for everyone to pray. Otherwise, it appears that all agree it is forbidden.
They first build the other one. See Mishnah Berurah, where we explained that they must complete the entire building. Otherwise, there remains concern for negligence, namely that they may sell it for redeeming captives. This is evident from the discussion in Bava Basra: “If so, even after they built it,” and the Gemara answers that people do not sell their dwelling. This implies that only once it is completed and has the status of a dwelling is there no concern. Before completion, negligence remains a concern. This is likewise evident from Rambam, chapter 8 of Hilchos Matanos Aniyim, cited by the Mechaber below in siman 153, seif 13, that only when the building is complete may it not be sold to redeem captives. His source is this very passage. It follows that until completion it is forbidden to demolish the old synagogue, because negligence remains a concern. Although Magen Avraham below, seif katan 32, gives a different reading in Rambam, the correct reading is that quoted by the Mechaber. This is also the reading in Semag and Kol Bo, which reproduce Rambam's wording, and in the Amsterdam edition of Rambam. Taz in Yoreh Deah, siman 252, likewise holds that completion is required. Tosafos there, s.v. “If so,” also implies this, as does Shitah Mekubetzes in the name of Aliyos of Rabbeinu Yonah. The plain sense of the Gemara in Megillah 26b, in the episode of Rami bar Abba, proves the same. Meiri states explicitly: “Even if they built the new synagogue but did not complete it, and roofing beams and bricks are still lacking, and they wish to remove beams from the old one to place upon the new one, they may not.” I have elaborated to reject the implication of Bach, who seems to permit it once construction of the new building has begun and another place to pray exists. This is incorrect, for negligence remains a concern, as explained.
The same law applies. See Mishnah Berurah. Darchei Moshe quotes this ruling from Rambam and Rabbeinu Yerucham. It is somewhat surprising that Rabbeinu Yerucham does not mention the view of his teacher, the Rosh, which implies the opposite, namely that where they wish to enlarge the synagogue and cannot do so without first demolishing the structure, it is permitted. This is unlike his usual method throughout his work. Were I not hesitant, I would suggest that the lines in Rabbeinu Yerucham were transposed, and the phrase “and likewise to enlarge it” belongs after the words “immediately”; see there. According to that reading, his words would accord precisely with the Rosh, that this is comparable to structural deterioration and is permitted, as Rabbeinu Yerucham regularly rules anonymously like his teacher the Rosh. It is possible that Rambam and Rosh do not disagree at all. Rambam may refer only to a case where enlargement is possible without demolition. But where the synagogue is too small for all the worshippers and cannot be repaired unless the entire structure is first demolished, it is permitted, for this resembles structural deterioration, which the Sages permitted, in accordance with the Rosh. I found that Peri Megadim also raised this point. Nevertheless, Tashbetz, part 2, siman 170, likewise rules without qualification that demolition for enlargement is forbidden, like Rambam, and his wording somewhat implies that it is forbidden in every case, following Rambam's plain language.
But if its foundations were ruined, etc. See Ritva, Megillah 26, who writes that even where structural deterioration is evident, they may demolish it only with the consent of the communal leaders.
They demolish, etc. This applies even where there is no place to pray before the new building is constructed. This is clear from the view of Ramah cited in his name, that Herod's case is considered one where there was no place to pray, yet the Gemara permits it because structural deterioration was evident.
And it is forbidden to demolish, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, where we cited Mahar"am Padua. Peri Megadim understood Mahar"am to refer only to something actually attached, and therefore challenged him from the case of cooking with consecrated wood, where Makkos 22 states that one is liable under “You shall not do so.” He was forced into a difficult resolution. In truth, Mahar"am never intended this. Otherwise, according to him, one who destructively breaks sacred service vessels in the Mikdash would not violate this prohibition, which is certainly incorrect. It is also clear from the plain verse, “You shall utterly destroy all the places ... their gods upon the mountains,” which refers to detached objects, as stated in Avodah Zarah 45, and “you shall cut down the carved images of their gods.” See Yereim, mitzvah 71. The verse concludes, “You shall not do so to Hashem your God,” showing that every sacred object, whether movable or attached, may not be destroyed. Rather, Mahar"am was responding to a question about removing benches and furnishings from a synagogue, whether this is considered demolishing part of the synagogue. He answered that demolition applies only to something attached. Meaning, since the furnishings are not attached to the synagogue but are independent objects, their removal does not damage the synagogue walls, nor are the furnishings themselves damaged; the term demolition therefore does not apply.
Anything from the synagogue. The same applies to all synagogue furnishings, for they share the synagogue's law, as explained in the Yerushalmi, chapter Bnei HaIr, and Rambam, chapter 11 of Hilchos Tefillah, halachah 15. It is reasonable that this rule applies to them as well, as I wrote above.
Unless he does so in order to build. Although it was explained above that they may demolish the old synagogue after the new one has been built, that case is different. From the outset, they intended to demolish it for the benefit of the new building, but the Sages initially prohibited it because of concern for negligence. The new building was constructed on the understanding that the old one would later be demolished for its benefit. Therefore, even now, it is no less than demolition for the sake of construction, and is permitted.
