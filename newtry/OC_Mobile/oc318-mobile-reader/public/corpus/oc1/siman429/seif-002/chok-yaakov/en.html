To announce publicly. This means that the fast is not publicly announced. Nevertheless, according to the Mechaber, an individual accustomed to fast on Mondays and Thursdays may fast. Our custom follows Rama, that one does not fast at all.
Tziduk HaDin is not recited. Sefer HaMinhagim writes that likewise the Yehi Ratzon following Torah reading, VeHu Rachum, Rachum VeChanun, memorial prayers, and Tzidkascha are not recited. The glosses to Minhagim further state: “When Tziduk HaDin is not recited, Kaddish is not recited either, except on Erev Shabbos after midday. Then Tziduk HaDin is recited while accompanying the deceased, but Kaddish is not recited.” This is cited from Piskei Tosafos to the chapter Elu Megalchin. Olas Shabbos comments that this must refer to a case where the deceased was accompanied before midday and the return occurred after midday. If the procession itself began after midday, Tziduk HaDin would not be recited. His words are entirely mistaken. He did not examine the source. Piskei Tosafos, Hagahos Ashri, and Rosh there state that even on Chanukah, Rosh Chodesh, and Chol HaMoed, when eulogies are prohibited, Tziduk HaDin may nevertheless be recited during the procession. This is also explained in Beit Yosef, Orach Chaim, siman 420, and Yoreh Deah, siman 401. Our practice, however, does not follow this, as stated there in Shulchan Aruch. On these days neither Tziduk HaDin nor Kaddish is recited at all. The distinction in Minhagim between Tziduk HaDin and Kaddish in the name of Piskei Tosafos is not found in their words. Likewise, on Erev Shabbos or Erev Yom Tov after midday they are not recited. The later authorities write that on Erev Chanukah and Erev Rosh Chodesh they are recited. Olas Shabbos writes that after the time of Minchah Gedolah, even on Erev Rosh Chodesh and Erev Chanukah they are omitted. Observe actual communal practice. Here in Prague, the custom is not to recite them after midday on these days, except on Erev Rosh Chodesh. Since Selichos known as Yom Kippur Katan are also recited after midday, Tziduk HaDin is likewise recited. Shulchan Aruch and Levush, Yoreh Deah, siman 395, write that on days when Tziduk HaDin is not recited, the deceased need not be set down three times, because spirits of impurity are not common on those days. My brother-in-law, the author of Eliyah Zuta, questions this from the ruling there in siman 315 that the custom is to sit three times and recite Vihi Noam because of destructive spirits, and when the deceased is buried on Yom Tov they may also sit as on a weekday. See there for his answer. One may add that the distinction applies specifically to the deceased himself. Since the attribute of judgment is not extended against him on those days, we are not concerned that spirits of impurity will attach themselves to him. Those accompanying him, however, should still sit to remove such spirits from themselves. See simanim 420 and 670 regarding whether Tziduk HaDin is recited for a Torah scholar in his presence.
No fast at all. General rules admit exceptions even where the phrase “except” is used. Bridegrooms do fast, even on Rosh Chodesh Nisan itself, as Rama states below in siman 573. Regarding a yahrzeit for one’s father or mother on Erev Rosh Chodesh Iyar, some authorities require fasting after Pesach has passed, while other later authorities disagree. The latter view appears primary. See siman 573.
A dream fast may nevertheless be observed. One need not observe an additional fast for having fasted on that day. If one fasted a dream fast on Shabbos, he may observe the compensatory fast during Nisan. The same applies between Yom Kippur and Sukkos or during the days preceding Shavuos. Magen Avraham.
Mizmor LeSodah. The later authorities write in the name of Shelah that on each day of Nisan one should read the passage concerning the prince of that day. On the thirteenth day, one reads Parashas Behaaloscha through “Thus he made the Menorah.” Magen Avraham writes that he found it recorded that one may pronounce a communal ban in the synagogue during Nisan for a matter of great necessity.
