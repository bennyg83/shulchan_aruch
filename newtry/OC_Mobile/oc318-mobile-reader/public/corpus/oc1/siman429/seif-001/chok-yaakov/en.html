One asks concerning the laws of Pesach. Beit Yosef asks from the statement in the chapter Bnei HaIr that Moshe instituted for Israel that they should ask and expound the laws of Pesach on Pesach, etc., which implies that beforehand there is no obligation to expound them. He answers that the statement that one asks thirty days before Pesach does not mean that one is obligated to ask then. Rather, a question asked during that period is considered relevant to the subject. The practical difference is where two students ask questions, one asks something unrelated and the other asks concerning the laws of Pesach. The one asking about Pesach is answered first. Thus far his words. For this reason Tur and the Mechaber did not use language of obligation, but merely quoted the Talmudic phrase. Although the Gemara says “they ask and expound,” Tur and the Mechaber omitted “expound,” because the principal public exposition in Temple times concerned the laws of the sacrifice, as stated in Beit Yosef and at the beginning of the chapter Lifnei Eideihen. See Tosafos there. Taz writes that another practical difference concerns the law of borrowing while the owner is employed by the borrower, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 346, seif 13. Before the festivals, when a rabbi regularly teaches his students the laws of the festival, he is considered in their service. If they borrow an article from him at that time, it is a case of borrowing while the owner is employed by the borrower. See Beit Yosef and the other later authorities for the several answers they give to this difficulty. All of them overlooked the explicit language of the Jerusalem Talmud, first chapter of Pesachim: “They ask concerning the laws of Pesach on Pesach, the laws of Shavuos on Shavuos, and the laws of Sukkos on Sukkos. In the study hall, they ask thirty days beforehand.” Concerning the laws of Pesach. The same applies to the other festivals, as the later authorities agree. The Gemara and poskim mention Pesach specifically because regarding the other festivals this is not an absolute law, but only an accepted custom. Bach also appears to imply this, as is explicit in Tur, Choshen Mishpat, siman 346. This is also Rashi’s intent in Sukkah 9. Maharsha’s challenge there against Beit Yosef is therefore unwarranted, unlike Nachalas Tzvi. Before Pesach. On the festival itself one must also ask and expound upon the subject of the day, as stated in the first chapter of Megillah. Magen Avraham writes likewise. Bach wonders about our custom not to expound on Pesach and the other festivals themselves. He therefore writes that the baraisa’s phrase “the laws of Pesach on Pesach and the laws of Sukkos on Sukkos” does not refer to the festival itself, but to the period immediately preceding it. This resembles the statement that a person must purify himself for the festival, which means before the festival. He writes that this is explicit in Rokeach, siman 30. This interpretation is very surprising. If so, what is the Gemara’s challenge at the beginning of Megillah against the statement that when Purim falls on Shabbos they ask and expound the subject of the day? The Gemara asks why Purim is different, since even on Yom Tov they do so, as Moshe instituted that Israel should ask and expound, etc. According to Bach, there is no difficulty at all. On Yom Tov the exposition takes place only before the festival, whereas on Purim that falls on Shabbos it occurs specifically that day, to publicize the miracle because the Megillah is not read due to the decree lest one carry it four amos in a public domain. Rather, it is obvious that the baraisa means the festival itself. Bach’s assertion that this is explicit in Rokeach, siman 244, was written in haste and is a serious error. One who examines Rokeach there will see the opposite. Rokeach writes: “Although they expound the laws of the festival on the festival, one should expound thirty days before the festival.” This clearly implies both on the festival itself and thirty days beforehand. When Rokeach uses the term “near,” he means that the relevant verses are expounded by proximity, as his words there make clear, not as Bach understood. To justify our custom not to expound on the festival itself, it appears that we rely upon the piyyutim and Torah reading. Semag, positive commandment 19, also implies this. Bach further wonders about our custom not to expound thirty days before Pesach, but only on the Shabbos immediately before Pesach, called Shabbos HaGadol. He writes at length. In truth, there is no difficulty. Raavan in the first chapter of Megillah, Rokeach, siman 241, Kol Bo, siman 46, and Tanya explain that Parashas Parah is read because the Sages said that one asks and expounds the laws of Pesach thirty days beforehand. We therefore read Parashas Parah, which contains the laws of impurity and purification, to remind people to prepare the Pesach offering in purity. Parashas HaChodesh likewise concerns Pesach. They explicitly state that these readings fulfill the obligation. All the more so do the piyyutim, which contain many laws and halachic rulings. Jewish custom has the force of Torah. Magen Avraham writes that nowadays the custom is to expound on Shabbos HaGadol even when it does not fall on Erev Pesach, and also on Shabbos Shuvah. The principal purpose is to teach the people of Hashem the ways of Hashem and the practical conduct they must follow. Thirty days. The period begins on Purim itself, as implied in the first chapter of Rosh Hashanah. Tosafos in Bechoros 57b, under the heading “At the approach of Pesach,” also writes this and discusses it at length. Twelve months. Magen Avraham writes that wheat is not distributed to a poor person unless he has lived in the city for twelve months. Semak, siman 247, writes that nowadays thirty days suffice, and Rama rules likewise in Yoreh Deah, siman 256. If the person intends to settle permanently, he immediately acquires the status of a city resident, as explained there in Yoreh Deah. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 162. Rama was brief here because he relied upon the primary ruling in Yoreh Deah. Beit Hillel’s effort there was therefore unnecessary. Magen Avraham further writes that although wheat is not given to poor people from elsewhere, they must nevertheless be given matzah to eat. This is obvious from the law of the communal food fund for transient poor people. Our custom here is to distribute flour rather than wheat.
