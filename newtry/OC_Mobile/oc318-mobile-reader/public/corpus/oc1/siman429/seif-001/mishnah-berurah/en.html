(1) One asks about the laws of Pesach, etc. Moshe stood at the time of the first Pesach and warned them concerning all the laws of Pesach Sheni. The same applies to the other Yamim Tovim, whose laws are expounded thirty days beforehand. See Biur HaGra, whose view is that for Shavuos it is sufficient to begin from the first of Sivan. Some say that the thirty day obligation applies only to Pesach because it has many laws, such as grinding wheat, baking matzos, kashering utensils, and destroying chametz. If these are not performed according to their laws before Pesach, they cannot be corrected on Pesach itself. This is unlike the other Yamim Tovim, for which a few days of preparation are sufficient. In any event, on Yom Tov itself all agree that one must ask and expound the laws of that Yom Tov, as stated at the end of Megillah, folio 32: Moshe instituted for Israel that they ask and expound concerning the matter of the day, the laws of Pesach on Pesach, the laws of Shavuos on Shavuos, and the laws of Sukkos on Sukkos.
(2) Thirty days. The period begins on Purim itself. Nowadays the custom is to deliver the derashah on Shabbos HaGadol, unless erev Pesach falls on that Shabbos, in which case it must be delivered on the preceding Shabbos, and on Shabbos Shuvah. The essential purpose is to instruct the people in the ways of Hashem and teach them the practical conduct they must follow, namely the laws of hag'alah, destroying chametz, baking matzah, and the other laws of Pesach. Likewise, on Shabbos Shuvah one should expound the laws of Yom Kippur and Sukkos, in addition to speaking about teshuvah. If the derashah consists only of pilpul or a general homiletic discourse, the obligation has not been fulfilled. Nevertheless, it is a mitzvah for every person to study the laws of Pesach during the thirty days beforehand and on Pesach itself. Later authorities.
(3) And the custom, etc. This is an ancient custom from the time of the Gemara. It is brought in the Yerushalmi, first chapter of Bava Batra, and the residents of a city may compel one another regarding it.
(4) Wheat to distribute. In our countries the custom is to distribute flour to them, because this brings the benefit closer to actual use. The amount given should meet their needs for all the days of Pesach. It is obvious that if a person is extremely poor and has no means to bake the matzah, they must also give him the cost of baking, for this is included in "the lack that he lacks."
(5) Twelve months. If we see that he entered the city intending to settle there, he becomes obligated immediately. See Shaarei Teshuvah. This law of twelve months also applies to a poor person, so that after that period the residents must provide for him like the other poor people of the city. If he intends to settle there immediately, they must provide for him immediately. The later authorities write in the name of Semag that nowadays, for all charitable obligations, both regarding householders and regarding the poor, the accepted period is thirty days. Even a poor person who has not lived there for thirty days, although they are not obligated to give him flour for all the days of Pesach at once, must nevertheless be given matzah on Pesach just as he must be given bread throughout the year: food for two meals on each weekday that he remains in the city, and on Shabbos food for three meals, as written in Yoreh De'ah, siman 256; see there.
(6) To contribute toward this. Even a talmid chacham who is exempt from taxes is obligated. Later authorities. See Peri Megadim. The amount of each contribution should be assessed according to the person's wealth. Those who evade contributing flour for the poor bear a sin. It is well known that the eyes of the poor are turned toward this assistance. If they remain in distress and hunger while he turns his eyes away, the statement of the Gemara in Sanhedrin 35 concerning every fast is well known; see Rashi there. This case is similar.
