He should grasp the affected place in his hand, and afterward all the remaining flour must be sifted. This is from Maharshal, and such is the custom. Flour in a sack that mice have bitten has the same law. If this is discovered before Pesach, the flour must be sifted. If pieces resembling crumbs of dried dough remain upon the sieve, they must be discarded, and the flour may then be retained until after Pesach. If it is discovered during Pesach, the flour is forbidden for benefit, and sifting is ineffective. If the sack remained undisturbed in its place and the flour was not mixed, he should grasp the bitten area, tie the sack below the bite together with a small quantity of flour, and sift the remainder. The remaining flour may then be retained, although it may not be eaten during Pesach. All this applies only where it appears that the mice ate some of the flour. If they merely bit the sack and there is doubt whether they ate any flour, then even if this is discovered during Pesach, the flour may be retained, although it may not be eaten. This applies even if it was discovered before Pesach, because there may be chametz present that dried and crumbled. This is from my teacher, Bach, siman 453. However, the great authority Maharam of Lublin writes in responsum 119 that other guarded flour should be taken for the matzos of the first two nights. The flour from the sack bitten by mice may be used for other matzos, provided all those matzos are baked before Pesach after the flour has been sifted. There is no need to be stringent regarding the sieve used for this flour, and it may afterward be used for any purpose.
