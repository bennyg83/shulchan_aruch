Sifting does not help. It implies that the first remedy, namely that he takes in his hand the moistened place, does help even when dry, since he removes the entire place in which there is doubt: what further concern have we? And in the Mordechai, chapter Kol Sha'ah, he wrote: an incident came before Rabbeinu Yehudah of Vienna regarding a sack of flour that fell into mud on Pesach; he ordered to empty the sack and said that one should sift the flour so that the moisture remain above, and that be thrown away, and the rest be permitted thereby, etc. And this is specifically so long as it is moist; but if the sack stood until the moisture dried, sifting does not help, for it crumbles and enters with the rest and nothing remains above, and one must take other flour for Pesach needs, and this he should sift: if he finds something like dough, he should throw it away, and the rest he should keep until after Pesach. And this is specifically if it dried before Pesach; then he may keep it until after Pesach, etc.; but on Pesach he should throw everything away; end quote. Now he did not mention here at all the remedy of holding the moist place, and that is because it is impossible, since the entire sack or a large part of it fell into mud, and there is no remedy except by sifting; regarding this he said that when it dried, sifting does not help, lest it crumble and go down with the flour through the holes of the sieve. But as for the remedy that he take the moist place and take the rest: why should that not help even when dried? For afterward it is permitted even without sifting. And although my teacher, my father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote in the name of Maharshal also regarding this remedy of taking the moist place that nevertheless one must sift all the remaining flour, and so is the custom; end quote: it appears that this is only a mere stringency. And a proof is from what the Tur wrote: if a little water or snow fell on the grain, he should scrape the upper [part] and take the lower. Now regarding grain, sifting is not applicable; rather, certainly the sifting that Maharshal mentioned is only a stringency, since when it is possible to do thus we do so, but not to forbid without later sifting; and all the more so when it dried and he sifts after he removed all that was in it, we do not say it is forbidden lest something of the dried parts fell below through the sieve. And see what I wrote in siman 453 se'if 3 regarding nullification of flour in flour. And if mice ate from the flour, he should likewise take that place and carefully around it, and he should sift the rest and eat it on Pesach, for it is no worse than water that fell on flour in one place; and in this it does not appear proper to be stringent even if it happened on Pesach, for the law of water that fell on flour deals even with Pesach, as appears to my humble opinion from the essence of the laws. And although my teacher, my father-in-law, of blessed memory, went beyond measure in siman 453, not as written [there]. Further the Tur wrote in the name of Rabbeinu Eliezer of Worms: a sack with flour in which chickens sat and it became moist from their dung does not cause chimutz; however, he should sift the flour and throw the dough outside, and the rest is permitted; end quote. And in the Rokeach there is added to this: we say in Menachos folio 69 regarding barley found in animal dung, etc.: if for menachos, "present it to your governor," and it does not forbid because of chametz; end quote. And it appears to me simply that for this reason he said here that he should sift and throw outside, etc.: since matzah is for a mitzvah, and even the other matzos are for Yom Tov needs, it is not fitting to treat them with disgrace. And a practical difference in this is that if it happened that they did not sift the flour, there is no prohibition here at all.
