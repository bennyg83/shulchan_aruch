And urine. See Mishnah Berurah. The Mishnah in Machshirin, chapter 6, states, “Urine, whether of adults or of minors.” Rash and Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura explain “of adults” as liquids that emerge from the rectum. At first glance, this contradicts the accepted ruling that chicken droppings and animal dung do not cause fermentation. Tur therefore rules that if chickens sat upon a sack of flour and the flour became moist from their droppings, the damp flour there does not become chametz. See Bach and Taz. I found that Eliyah Rabbah resolves this by saying that the case there concerns thick excrement. It does not appear that he means literally thick material, because then how did the flour in the sack become moist and turn into dough merely because chickens deposited excrement upon it from above? Rather, he means that it was not completely liquid. Had it been completely liquid, it would be regarded as liquid emerging from the rectum. See Pri Megadim. I have written what appears correct to me, and perhaps this is also his intent where it contains abundant moisture. I saw that Chayei Adam records two views regarding soft excrement. Some say that it has the law of fruit juice, while others say that it does not cause fermentation at all. The second view is clearly that of the poskim who state without qualification that excrement does not cause fermentation and make no distinction between thick and soft. I do not know the source of the first view. The distinction between thick and soft appears only in Eliyah Rabbah, and his inclination is that soft excrement is like urine and causes fermentation. Regarding the basic matter, however, even if it were as liquid as water, I have serious doubts. Tosafos Yom Tov asks there against the explanation of Rash. The end of the Mishnah states that one who drinks the waters of Tiberias, although they emerge clear, no longer gives them the status of a liquid because they emerged through the rectum. See there, where he struggles greatly with his answer, and the proof he brings for his reasoning is not compelling at all. Were it not for the explanation of Rash and Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura, it would appear obvious to me that “whether adults or minors” means whether the urine is from an adult person or from a child, provided the child is viable, excluding an eight month fetus, as taught in the following Mishnah. I later found that Rambam, in chapter 10 of Hilchos Tumas Ochlin, cites the Mishnah, and the straightforward meaning of his words indicates that he explains it as we have. Responsa Chut HaShani, siman 30, likewise interprets Rambam in this manner. Eliyah Rabbah on the order of Taharos attributed to the Vilna Gaon explicitly explains it this way. It is clear that they departed from the explanation of Rash because of Tosafos Yom Tov’s question. According to this, even excrement as liquid as water retains the law of excrement for this matter. Responsa Chut HaShani further suggests that even according to Rash and Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura, the Mishnah does not mean that liquid excrement has the law of urine regarding rendering produce susceptible to impurity, as the case of one who drinks the waters of Tiberias proves. Rather, it refers to actual urine that, because of an illness, exits through the place ordinarily used for excrement, namely through the rectum. According to his explanation, we presently have no opinion that excrement as liquid as water is considered urine regarding susceptibility to impurity, and consequently the same applies regarding fermentation. All this follows the view of the poskim who equate the laws of fermentation with those of rendering produce susceptible to impurity. Whatever is considered a derivative of water for susceptibility is also treated as water regarding fermentation, and whatever is not considered a derivative of water there is likewise not called water regarding fermentation. Some Acharonim, however, maintain that this comparison is not conclusive. They therefore hold that the urine of animals, dogs, and mice causes fermentation, although the Tosefta states that animal urine does not render produce susceptible to impurity. See Eliyah Rabbah. In any event, where another doubt is also present, one may certainly be lenient. This appears correct to me.
And human perspiration. See Mishnah Berurah regarding chicken droppings. Maamar Mordechai, seif katan 4, writes that he ruled concerning one who purchased a sack of wheat in which dry cat droppings were found that the wheat was permitted for Pesach. One cannot derive this permission from Rokeach’s ruling regarding chicken droppings, because birds are different. There is no concern for urine, since any creature that does not nurse has no separate urinary opening, as stated in Shaarei Shamayim, discourse 4. Regarding cats, however, there is reason to suspect that they urinated there just as they deposited excrement, and the wheat may have fermented. Nevertheless, we do not presume a prohibition. Perhaps they did not urinate at all; perhaps they urinated away from the wheat; perhaps the wheat did not ferment; and even if a small amount fermented, it would be only an extremely small minority. Moreover, the basic law of urine is disputed, as explained in Beit Yosef. In addition, Pri Chadash and Chok Yaakov hold that only human urine causes fermentation. Therefore, where no defect is visible in the wheat, there is no basis for stringency. Otherwise, there would be no grain store free of this concern. This is the primary ruling.
