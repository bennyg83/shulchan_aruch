1) [Se'if 1] A person may not chew wheat, etc. This implies that one may chew and swallow it, because it does not become chametz immediately. Magen Avraham, subsection 1; Chok Yaakov, subsection 2; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1; Mekor Chaim, subsection 2; Chemed Moshe, subsection 1. As long as one is actively chewing and working the grain, it does not become chametz. Eliyah Rabbah, there, in the name of Lechem Mishneh, chapter Kol Sha'ah; Eshel Avraham, subsection 1. Nevertheless, one must afterward clean one's teeth so that no small amount remains and later becomes chametz.
2) There. A person may not chew, etc. This applies even to less than an olive-sized quantity. Although one does not violate the prohibition against chametz being seen with less than an olive-sized quantity, one may nevertheless be obligated under Torah law to eliminate it, just as half the prohibited measure is forbidden for consumption under Torah law; the same may apply to elimination. Peri Chadash. See, however, Erech HaShulchan, subsection 1, who discusses this at length and writes that half the measure in such a case is prohibited only rabbinically. He writes that one may leave this poultice when made from nullified chametz if it contains less than an olive-sized quantity. See the full discussion there. See above, siman 442, subsection 77.
3) There. A person may not chew wheat, etc. This is the meaning of a poultice. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1. See above, siman 442, se'if 2, and our words there, with the help of Heaven.
4) There. One may not chew, etc. Only actual chewing causes the grain to become chametz, not merely biting it. A mouse's bite likewise does not cause leavening. Responsa Rashba, siman 488; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1; Eshel Avraham, subsection 1. See above, siman 453, se'if 3, and our words there, with the help of Heaven.
5) There. And place it upon a wound, etc. The grains become chametz through the saliva in one's mouth when they remain on the wound for the time needed to walk a mil, and one then violates the prohibitions against chametz being seen and found. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 3. Even if the grain was already chewed and placed on the wound before Pesach, it must be eliminated and removed. Rokeach, siman 271; Chok Yaakov, subsection 1; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2; Mekor Chaim, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, there. It must be removed when the sixth hour begins on the eve of Pesach, unless it has already spoiled to the point that it is unfit for a dog's consumption, as stated in siman 442. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, there. See se'if 2 there and our words there, with the help of Heaven.
6) There. And place it upon a wound, etc. This applies specifically when it belongs to a Jew, because the Jew violates the prohibition against chametz being seen. If the chametz belongs to a non-Jew who lent the poultice to a Jew or placed it upon the Jew's wound, and the Jew bears no liability for it, it is permitted. If the non-Jew has no poultice, the Jew may transfer ownership of flour to the non-Jew, who prepares a poultice from it and places it upon the Jew's wound. Magen Avraham, subsection 2, in the name of Kesef Mishneh, chapter 5 of Hilchos Yesodei HaTorah, halachah 8, who writes this in the name of Orchos Chaim. See there. Mateh Yehudah in siman 448, se'if 5, agrees. See there. Eshel Avraham, subsection 2, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 4, write likewise. We also wrote this above in siman 465, subsection 6, in the name of Eliyah Rabbah. See there. Mekor Chaim, subsection 1, however, questions this permission. Once the poultice is placed upon the wound it will be completely ruined, so it is certainly not a loan whose object will be returned intact; it is effectively a complete gift. He therefore prohibits it unless the dressing already became spoiled in the non-Jew's possession before it was placed upon the wound, as stated in siman 442. See there. Peri Chadash likewise declines to permit this case. See there. Although Mateh Yehudah there resolves the objection and agrees that it is permitted, it nevertheless appears that initially one should be stringent when possible. The poultice should be arranged so that it becomes spoiled in the non-Jew's possession before being placed upon the wound, or it should be kneaded with fruit juice, or another type of dressing should be used. If this is impossible and the person is in great pain, one may rely upon the permitting authorities. The Jew should intend not to acquire it at all; it remains in the non-Jew's ownership while upon the wound, until it is thrown into the refuse.
