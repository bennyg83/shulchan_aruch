"A walled city about which there is doubt whether it was surrounded in the days of Yehoshua or not, they read on the fourteenth and fifteenth, etc." Note: Mordechai wrote in the first chapter of Megillah: why do we not practice reading the megillah on the fourteenth and fifteenth because of doubt nowadays, etc., until: alternatively, there is here a violation of adding, for it is its time, or it may occur for those walled by a wall and he could discharge their obligation, etc. I was asked by one scholar: it is explicit in the Yerushalmi, brought in the poskim, that a resident of a walled city cannot discharge a resident of an unwalled town, and a resident of an unwalled town cannot discharge a resident of a walled city, because he is not obligated in the matter. How then did Mordechai write that he can discharge a resident of a walled city? I answered that this is not difficult at all. Specifically where the resident of an unwalled town is completely exempt, and likewise the resident of a walled city, there he cannot discharge another who is obligated in the matter. But where the resident of the unwalled town is also involved and obligated in the matter at least because of doubt, he is considered obligated in the matter and can discharge even the one who is certainly obligated. This is similar to what Kesef Mishneh wrote, brought in Even HaEzer, siman 44, regarding one who betroths a yevamah to the marketplace after the yavam will perform chalitzah for her: since kiddushin take effect in her now because of doubt, it is not considered something that has not yet come into the world; see there. So too here: since he is obligated because of doubt, he can discharge his fellow who is certainly obligated. Therefore Mordechai properly wrote that if we enacted concern for this doubt, he would automatically be obligated in the matter because of doubt, and then could discharge his fellow who is certainly obligated, and consequently he would violate "it shall not pass" or the prohibition of adding. This seems correct and simple in my humble opinion; analyze carefully. The Ran wrote in the name of the Geonim that they read only on the fourteenth, and he wrote two reasons. One is that we follow the majority of the world. The other is that in truth it would be fitting to say that a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently, except that if so they would be exempt entirely, and this is impossible; therefore they should read on the fourteenth, because it comes first, and a mitzvah that comes to your hand, do not let it become delayed, etc. What he wrote, that we follow the majority of the world, is difficult, for anything fixed is considered half and half, and here there are several known fixed places that are walled, and the fixed place is recognizable. Therefore the main reason is the second reason. Mishneh LaMelech in chapter 1 of Hilchos Megillah asked that the Ran's words contradict each other: here he wrote that the fourteenth alone is enough, while in the chapter Arvei Pesachim regarding reclining for the four cups he wrote that reclining is required for both. He answered that with the four cups, if we say that both do not require reclining, the Sages' enactment is entirely uprooted; therefore both need reclining. But with megillah, since the Sages' enactment is not entirely uprooted, the fourteenth is enough; see there. With this I saw how to settle Pesachim 54b, where it says that what was taught, "this one's doubt is forbidden and this one's doubt is permitted," means fixing the month. Rashi explained that this means making two days, and Tosafos asked that we say at the end of Rosh Hashanah that Yom Kippur is made only one day. What Tosafos answered, that the intent is one who travels in the desert, does not appear correct, for the language "fixing the month" does not imply this. Therefore it appears that the intent is as follows. If Rashi's statement, to make two days, is certainly astonishing from the Gemara mentioned above, nevertheless the Gemara's intent appears this way, with Hashem's help: one can say that specifically regarding megillah or reclining, since it is possible to be stringent and perform on two days, therefore, since the two sides contradict one another and it would have been possible to be stringent and perform on two days, although we do not make two days, they must perform at least one day. But where it is impossible to make two days in any manner, we again say that a rabbinic doubt is lenient, and it is entirely permitted. This is what the Gemara means, that the intent is fixing the month, or Yom Kippur: since it is a Torah doubt, one is obligated to observe at least one day, and this is called its doubt being forbidden at least for one day. With Tishah BeAv, its doubt is permitted on both days, since it is impossible to make two days, as with Yom Kippur, because of danger to life, as written at the end of the first chapter of Rosh Hashanah. Therefore it is a rabbinic doubt treated leniently, and it is entirely permitted. This is the meaning of "its doubt is permitted"; analyze carefully, and it works well with Hashem's help. See Tosafos there under "is it not that its twilight is permitted, etc.," who asked that Yom Kippur also has addition, for the addition of Yom Kippur is Torah-level, and their answer is forced; see there. In my humble opinion this will be a proof for the Rambam that a Torah doubt is lenient by Torah law. The later authorities wrote that where a prohibition was established, as with one piece from two pieces, the Rambam agrees that it is Torah-level. The law of twilight, which is a doubt whether it belongs to this day or that, is like one piece from two pieces. From this it will be proven that a rabbinic doubt where prohibition was established is permitted, for otherwise why do we say that on Tishah BeAv, its twilight is permitted because of rabbinic doubt, since prohibition was established? Perforce, even in this case a rabbinic doubt is lenient. In truth, according to this it is difficult for me: why does the Gemara challenge Shmuel and Rabbi Yochanan from that baraisa? Regarding whether a rabbinic doubt where a prohibition was established is stringent or lenient, Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yose disagree in Eruvin, and see the later authorities in Yoreh Deah, siman 110, section 1. Perhaps that baraisa, which says its doubt is permitted, follows Rabbi Meir, who holds that in rabbinic matters, even where prohibition was established, it is lenient, while Shmuel and Rabbi Yochanan hold like Rabbi Yose, that a doubt is stringent where prohibition was established. This requires analysis. In any case, twilight is a doubt where prohibition was established. Therefore it works well: if Yom Kippur's doubt were permitted because even where prohibition was established the doubt is lenient, then although the addition of Yom Kippur is forbidden, nevertheless it would be permitted to make the twilight into the addition, since its doubt is lenient; if so, the twilight could be the addition. Therefore it teaches us that Yom Kippur's doubt is forbidden by law because a Torah doubt is stringent, since prohibition was established, and therefore one cannot make this period the addition, and the addition too is forbidden then. This works well with Hashem's help; analyze carefully.
