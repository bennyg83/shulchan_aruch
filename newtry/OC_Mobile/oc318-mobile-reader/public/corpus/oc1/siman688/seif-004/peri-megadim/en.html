In Shulchan Aruch he did not explain the uncertainty, whether it is a legal doubt, like Teveria, or whether one witness says it was surrounded and one witness says it was not, like Hotzal; see Beis Yosef. In Be'er HaGolah, os 20, he marked Chizkiyah, and there it is a legal doubt: there is an enclosure, but Hotzal, etc., Megillah 5b. However, Beis Yosef inclined that every city with a wall is fitting for doubt even without a tradition. Nevertheless, he concluded in the name of an Ashkenazic book that it appears that wherever there is no doubtful tradition, they read on the fourteenth; see there. So it appears as the law, though in Pri Chadash it does not sound so. I did not understand what the Taz wrote, that they fulfill on the fourteenth. For at the beginning of the siman, Pri Chadash brought the Yerushalmi: Can a resident of an unwalled town discharge a resident of a walled city? Let it come from this rule: anyone who is not obligated does not discharge; and "it shall not pass." Can a resident of a walled city discharge a resident of an unwalled town? Let it come from this rule: anyone who is not obligated does not discharge, or let it come from what Rabbi Chelbo said in the name of Rabbi Chiyya Rabbah, that all fulfill on the fourteenth, which is the time of its reading. This was not resolved. It appears that no one disagrees with Rabbi Chiyya Rabbah that a resident of a walled city discharges on the fourteenth; the question is whether, since he was not initially obligated, he is not called obligated, or whether, since after the fact he is obligated, he is called obligated. According to this, our question is resolved leniently, like every unresolved rabbinic question. What Pri Chadash wrote, that from our Gemara it implies that it disagrees with Rabbi Chiyya Rabbah, because it says, "the time of this one is not like the time of that one," one can say this was stated initially, and one should not multiply disputes. If so, Teveria and Hotzal initially read on two days and bless only on the fourteenth, not on the fifteenth, for he has fulfilled whichever way one looks at it, and it is forbidden to bless on the fifteenth. The Taz's answer is also unnecessary, and so too the reason that it is the reading time for most of the world is unnecessary, as we said. According to this, the law that emerges for us is that a resident of a walled city who read on the fourteenth is forbidden to bless on the fifteenth and reads without a blessing, like one reading from the Torah. If he read for a resident of an unwalled town, the resident of the unwalled town has fulfilled, because an unresolved rabbinic question is treated leniently. Also, many poskim, including Rashi, of blessed memory, hold that for villages, residents of towns would read, which shows that he is called obligated in the matter, since he is obligated in megillah reading. As for what the Taz asked against the Maharshal, what is the difference between a festival day and Purim, one can say that the blessing of Kiddush and the sanctity of the day are one matter, unlike the blessing of megillah and sending portions, which are unrelated. See Pri Chadash for an answer to this, and it is the Ran's answer. With Hashem's help, this will be explained in Magen Avraham, os 4, for we hold that a doubt is stringent.
The writer says: I have some thoughts here. For a common person like me, from whom the paths of wisdom are hidden, I became entangled in this analysis, and I said, I will speak and be relieved. Beforehand, I said, let me ask one small question, and this is its form.
Question. If one witness says it is forbidden fat of the gid or the like, and one witness says it is permitted fat, what is the law regarding eating it? What brought us to this is what is written in Sefer HaTerumos, rule 11, from Tosafos in Kesubos 27, and from the responsum of Rabbi Yehudah ben HaRosh, of blessed memory, brought by Beis Yosef in Yoreh Deah, siman 39, regarding two witnesses about a bubble at the bottom of the lung, which is a stringency of the Geonim and rabbinic; he establishes it on its presumption. This implies that where there is no presumption, we do not say that a rabbinic doubt is lenient. A doubt from the animal's lifetime may be another matter. At first glance, the reason is that this is called an established prohibition in such a case; see Shach, Yoreh Deah 110, in Kuntres HaSefeikos, law 21, and what I wrote in Pri Megadim there. In truth, in the Rambam, of blessed memory, chapter 8 of Shegagos, halachah 3, where one witness says forbidden fat and one says permitted fat, this is called an established prohibition; see there. Yet from the words of the Ran, of blessed memory, regarding uncertain towns, he wrote that we follow the majority, not that an even rabbinic doubt is lenient and they read on the fourteenth; see there. A reasoned argument, and Hotzal, are pious practice: Teveria, perhaps they are protected, and Hotzal, where they disagree about the tradition; even so, it is only pious practice, not law, and an unresolved rabbinic question is lenient. Contradicting witnesses should also be lenient even without a majority and without a presumption. It is possible that what he wrote, that it is pious practice, follows one reason, that we follow the majority; see there.
And behold. Beis Yosef in Yoreh Deah there brought the words of Rabbi Yosef at length, and his words require analysis. He brought proof there from a provisional guilt-offering, where we require an established prohibition; otherwise not, because a Torah doubt is stringent only rabbinically. Therefore in rabbinic matters it is lenient, unlike where the prohibition is established, which is Torah-level stringent, and therefore even rabbinically it is stringent. See Yoreh Deah 110 in Taz and Shach there. If so, when they disagree about a tradition, it is called established, as the Raavad, of blessed memory, wrote in chapter 1, halachah 3 of Shegagos, where one witness says forbidden fat and one says permitted fat. What Beis Yosef wrote, that if so even in such a case he does not bring a provisional guilt-offering, requires analysis. It is true that there he wrote that perhaps the intent of our teacher in the glosses is that it speaks of one witness regarding two pieces, but nevertheless he did not decide it there; see there. Also, what he wrote there, that Scripture says mitzvos, according to what Maharit and Pri Chadash in Yoreh Deah 110 wrote, we learn from there, because Scripture revealed regarding a provisional guilt-offering that specifically where the prohibition was established is it forbidden from the Torah; where it was not established it is only rabbinic, and from this, in rabbinic matters, where it was established, we are stringent, and where it was not established, it is permitted. See Lechem Mishneh there, where this can be reconciled.
