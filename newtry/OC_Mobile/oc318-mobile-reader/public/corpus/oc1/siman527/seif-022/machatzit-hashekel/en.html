(Seif katan 22) It is forbidden, etc. Since he cannot cook for himself, etc. Just as one may not cook for others on Shabbos when he cannot cook for himself, so too on Yom Tov he may not cook for others on that very day when he is fasting and has no personal need for the food.
And it cannot be permitted, etc., such as baking, etc. Pesachim 46 discusses separating challah from impure dough on Pesach Yom Tov. Since the challah is forbidden even to a kohen, Rabbi Eliezer says that one should not designate it as challah until after baking, for once designated it could not be baked because no one may eat it, while leaving it as dough would cause it to become chametz. Rabbi Yehoshua says one should designate it and not bake it, because even if it becomes chametz the owner does not violate bal yera'eh, since it belongs to the kohen. The Gemara explains Rabbi Eliezer's view that the owner does violate bal yera'eh because he could annul the designation and restore the dough to its untithed state. Rashi and Tosafot ask why he should not designate it and then bake it on the basis that he could annul the designation and separate another challah, making the first piece fit for him. They answer that since the problem can be avoided without relying on ho'il, by delaying designation, one may not rely on ho'il. Likewise, one who bakes from Yom Tov for a weekday is not liable according to Rabbah because unexpected guests could arrive, yet this does not make the act initially permitted.
One who fasts on Rosh Hashanah, etc. Rosh Hashanah is not exclusive; the same applies to other Yamim Tovim. Rosh Hashanah is mentioned because many people customarily fast then, as stated in siman 597.
In this case he agrees, etc. Pesachim 68b records a dispute concerning the verses “an assembly shall be for you” and “an assembly for Hashem.” Rabbi Eliezer permits either devoting the entire day to Hashem through learning and divine service, or the entire day to personal enjoyment. Rabbi Yehoshua requires half for Hashem and half for oneself, and the halachah follows him. One who fasts cannot fulfill the half devoted to himself, so Rabbi Yehoshua agrees that he must devote the entire day to Hashem. He may therefore not cook for others, because doing so interrupts divine service.
According to what he wrote, etc., it is not initially permitted, etc. According to this, there is no need for Mahari Weil's distinction between ho'il here and ho'il in the cases of challah or baking from Yom Tov for a weekday. One may never rely upon ho'il initially, since a D'Rabbanan prohibition remains.
It follows that even a maidservant, or a married woman, to whom the second reason does not apply because she is obligated to spend the day serving others and does not control her own labor, may not cook for her householder or husband while fasting. The first reason given by Mahari Weil still applies.
This is contrary to Levush in siman 597, who permits a fasting maidservant for the reason stated above. See what I wrote there.
And when Yom Tov falls on Friday, etc. He may not cook even for himself for Shabbos, even if he made an eruv tavshilin. The eruv alone cannot permit the Torah prohibition of cooking from Yom Tov for Shabbos. It works only because ho'il reduces the matter to a D'Rabbanan prohibition, as explained at the beginning of this siman. For one who is fasting, however, ho'il cannot apply, because even if guests arrive he may not cook for them, as Mahari Weil writes. For him, cooking from Yom Tov for Shabbos therefore remains forbidden according to the Torah, and eruv tavshilin alone cannot permit it.
Nevertheless, others, etc. The unqualified wording implies that others may cook for him even if he does not transfer ownership of his flour to them. Ordinarily, when one failed to make an eruv, his flour was also penalized and others may not bake or cook it for him until he transfers ownership. Here, however, he did make an eruv. He personally became forbidden to cook only because of the fast, so his flour remains permitted.
