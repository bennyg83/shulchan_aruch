(Seif katan 13) He cannot cook, etc. The second day is certainly a weekday according to the Torah, since we know the first day is the essential Yom Tov and observe sanctity on the second only because of ancestral custom. A further reason is that the Sages permitted only a nearby shevus, whereas the shevus here is remote. Magen Avraham writes at the beginning of this siman that according to the Torah Shabbos needs may not be prepared on Yom Tov, and eruv tavshilin alone cannot permit a Torah prohibition. We follow Rabbah's principle of ho'il, that the food could be needed for unexpected guests, leaving only a D'Rabbanan prohibition. The Sages permitted that prohibition through eruv tavshilin only where the shevus is close to Shabbos, namely cooking on Friday. They did not permit the remote shevus of cooking on Thursday when cooking can instead be done on Friday. Although Ran and Maggid Mishneh speak of a nearby Shabbos rather than a nearby shevus, the meaning is the same: they permitted the shevus for the nearby Shabbos, not for a distant Shabbos.
And as stated at the end of chapter 15 of Shabbos. Magen Avraham added this source; it is not in Ran. The Gemara explains that just as they sounded blasts every Friday to announce the onset of Shabbos, they did so when Yom Tov fell on Friday, to announce that Shabbos forbids even food preparation permitted on Yom Tov. When Yom Kippur fell on Friday they did not sound them, because there is no relevant difference between Yom Kippur and Shabbos. The Gemara asks that they should nevertheless sound them to publicize that Shabbos is stricter, since removing leaves from detached vegetables is permitted on Yom Kippur after Minchah. Rav Yosef answers that a shevus is not overridden merely to announce a permission. Rav Sheshes son of Rav Idi answers that a nearby shevus was permitted but a distant one was not: had the permission been needed presently, the shevus might be overridden, but on a Friday Yom Kippur the vegetables cannot be cooked until after Shabbos, so the blast would only teach a law relevant in a future year. The Gemara challenges this from Yom Tov after Shabbos, when no blast announces that immediate slaughter is permitted, and concludes like Rav Yosef. Although Rav Sheshes's answer was rejected regarding overriding a shevus to announce permission, his principle remains valid regarding a shevus permitted in order to impose a prohibition: only a nearby need was permitted. Initially, etc. This begins a new seif katan and refers to seif 14, which states that initially one should not rely upon the eruv for a later Yom Tov. Thus, setting it aside on Erev Pesach to rely upon it also for Shavuos is initially forbidden. Magen Avraham adds that even when it is intended for only one Yom Tov, setting it aside one or two days early is initially forbidden. Both rulings share one reason: we are stringent for both rationales for eruv tavshilin. Rava says it ensures that one selects a fine portion for Shabbos, while Rabbi Ashi says it teaches that one may not bake from Yom Tov for Shabbos without beginning beforehand. If one makes the eruv on Erev Pesach for Shavuos, he may forget it by Shavuos and fail to select a fine portion for Shabbos. The same concern applies when he sets it aside several days before Yom Tov.
