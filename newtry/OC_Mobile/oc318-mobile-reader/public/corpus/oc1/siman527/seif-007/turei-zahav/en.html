And it is a mitzvah upon every leading authority, etc. This is difficult, for at the beginning of the second chapter it is stated, 'send portions to one for whom nothing is prepared,' meaning one who did not make an eruv tavshilin. This implies that in the days of Ezra there was no leading authority who was required to intend to include those who had not made an eruv. Rather, it appears that the obligation is only to set up a means of help for one who forgot, not that people should rely on him initially.
That he intended to fulfill through him. This matter requires analysis. The Gemara says that Shmuel said to the blind scholar who had not placed an eruv tavshilin, 'Rely on me, for I make an eruv for all the people of my city.' The next year he saw that he was distressed and said to him, 'Why are you distressed?' When he explained, Shmuel said, 'You are negligent; for everyone it is permitted, but for you it is forbidden.' Rashi explains that Shmuel had no intent for intentional sinners and the negligent, who are not careful with the words of the Sages. This implies that if he had intended even for such negligent people, it would help. This is unlike the Rosh and Tur, who ruled that even if one relies on the leading authority of the city it does not help, since he did not fulfill the enactment of the Sages that each person make an eruv for himself. According to them, the passage is difficult. If the blind scholar had relied on Shmuel's eruv in the later year, why was he distressed on Yom Tov, since according to his view it helped, unlike the first year when he did not yet know that reliance on the leading authority helps? And if he did not rely at all the second time either, from where did the Rosh and Tur learn that one who relies is called negligent, since the Gemara does not discuss that? According to Rashi it is clear: he was not negligent because he relied, for relying does help when the leading authority intends to include him. Rather, the blind scholar did not rely even the second time, and was called negligent because he forgot twice; he should have been careful and quick to remember. Shmuel had not intended to include him. But according to the Rosh and Tur this remains difficult. One cannot answer that he was distressed on Yom Tov because he became worried then that perhaps relying does not help, and Shmuel answered him that it is forbidden because relying does not help. For then why did Shmuel say, 'for everyone it is permitted, but for you it is forbidden,' implying that he was worse than everyone because it happened twice? According to the Rosh and Tur the reason would not be that it happened twice, but that relying never helps. Nor can one say that this itself was the distinction between him and everyone else, that everyone else forgot while he relied, and one who relies is more negligent because he remembered and should have made his own eruv. For if so, why did Shmuel permit everyone else? Perhaps some of them also relied. Furthermore, why should he be called negligent for relying? He is only mistaken in halacha, thinking that reliance helps. This matter requires study in practical halacha, for according to Rashi and the Rashba, as the Beit Yosef cites, reliance helps if the leading authority intends so, and they are worthy to be followed. According to this, there is no mitzvah upon the leading authority to intend to include the public, as I wrote nearby from the Talmud, that in the days of Ezra it was necessary to say, 'send portions to one for whom nothing is prepared,' meaning one who had not made an eruv tavshilin; this proves that there was no mitzvah upon the leading authority to intend to include others.
