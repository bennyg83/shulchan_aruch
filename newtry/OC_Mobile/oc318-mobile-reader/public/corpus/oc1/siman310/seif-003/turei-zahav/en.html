"Returned to its permitted status." The reason is that there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos, since at bein hashmashot it was fit. In Yoreh De'ah, siman 266, I wrote that a scalpel used for a brit milah on Shabbos may not be moved after the brit milah, unlike Responsa Maharshal, which permits it on the ground that there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos. That principle does not apply here, because at bein hashmashot the scalpel was not fit to be moved. It was muktzeh, like a scribe's knife in siman 308. I brought proof from the pestle upon which meat was cut, in the first chapter of Beitzah, 11a, and from the spit upon which meat was roasted on Yom Tov, in chapter Ein Tzedin. These 2 objects possess an inherent muktzeh status: the pestle because one is particular about it and assigns it a fixed place, like the implements discussed at the beginning of Perek Kol HaKelim, and the spit because it is merely a piece of wood taken for use as a spit and has no status of a utensil, as Tur writes in siman 518. They are therefore even more restrictive than a kli shemelachto l'issur. Although at bein hashmashot they were designated for melacha needed on Yom Tov, after the Yom Tov use ended they returned to their prior muktzeh prohibition. The very same applies to this scalpel. At bein hashmashot it was muktzeh machmat chisaron kis, like a scribe's knife. It is obvious that a person is very particular not to use such a scalpel for another purpose lest it be damaged, and certainly he does not use it for eating. Therefore, although it is designated for the mitzvah of brit milah on the following day, once its needed use has ended it returns to the prohibition of moving that applied at bein hashmashot. I later saw in Yam Shel Shlomo, Perek Ein Tzedin, siman 16, that Maharshal maintained his position. He writes that the case is not comparable to dried figs and raisins, which are entirely unfit during their interim state because they become spoiled. A brit milah scalpel, however, is physically fit to eat with or for another permitted use even at bein hashmashot; the owner merely designates it for brit milah and not for another use. Therefore, he argues, it is not muktzeh, because in any event he has prepared it for Shabbos itself. A spit used for roasting is different because it becomes repulsive, while the scalpel does not remain repulsive if it is wiped clean. Nor, he argues, should its preservation for its special use return it to muktzeh machmat chisaron kis, because even at bein hashmashot it was designated for that same use. End quote. What I wrote above refutes all of this. The scalpel is exactly like the pestle and spit mentioned above and may not be moved except as needed for the brit milah. Afterward it should be put away in the same room in which the brit milah was performed. Maharil writes likewise. See also my discussion in Yoreh De'ah, siman 266.
