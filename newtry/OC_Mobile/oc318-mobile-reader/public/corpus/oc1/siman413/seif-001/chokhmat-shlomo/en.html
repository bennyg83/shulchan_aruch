Seif 1: One who makes an eruv for many people from his own food, etc. NB: See Tosafot Yom Tov, Pesachim chapter 5, who wrote that the rule that there is no breirah in Torah law is only rabbinic. With this one can resolve the Rambam's difficulty: since a kinyan peirot is not like ownership of the body, brothers who divided an inheritance are like purchasers from one another, so how can they bring bikkurim and recite? The Shaagat Aryeh answered that we are not concerned for appearing like falsehood, and the Mekor Chayim objected. According to Tosafot Yom Tov, however, before the Omnipresent it is not false, since by Torah law there is breirah; it appears false only before people, because rabbinically he must return it. Before people we are not concerned for appearing like falsehood. I also discussed the Ran in the name of the Ramban in Gittin chapter 3, that breirah applies only when one makes a condition about two matters. The community of Krakow asked: even with two alternatives, each side needs its own eruv, so each side is only one matter. The answer is that we follow the moment of acquisition; at one time of acquisition it must acquire either here or there, and therefore breirah applies. And see what I wrote elsewhere regarding the rule that in Torah law there is no breirah but in rabbinic law there is breirah, and regarding the difficulties from Tosafot in Temurah 30 and Yoma 55, which I resolved elsewhere.
Now regarding what is stated in Shulchan Aruch, one who makes an eruv for many people from his own food, etc. NB: In the Gemara at the beginning of Keitzad Mishtatfin, Rav Yosef says that one may make an eruv only for the sake of a mitzvah. The Gemara asks: is this not already taught? I find this difficult. Perhaps an eruv may be made even for a non-mitzvah purpose, and the Mishnah's cases of a mourner's house or a wedding feast are stated only because it is speaking of making an eruv for the whole city and of those who knew of it accepting it, which relies on breirah. Since we hold that there is no breirah, and one does not create a rabbinic doubt lechatchilah, perhaps only for a mitzvah may one create such a rabbinic doubt lechatchilah, but not for an optional matter. Yet for one definite person, where no breirah is needed, perhaps even an optional matter would be permitted. Why, then, does the Gemara ask? This proves that rabbinic breirah is not because of doubt, but is a definite resolution, as the Maharshal held; therefore there is no distinction between a mitzvah and an optional matter. According to those who hold that it is because of doubt, this requires great study.
