(Seif katan 2) He need not, etc. And perhaps if he prayed, etc. Meaning, although in seif katan 1 it was obvious to the Magen Avraham that if he prayed he may not eat even while it is still day, that is where he had not begun permissibly. Here, however, where he began permissibly, interrupted in the middle, and prayed, he is uncertain.
That Maharsha interrupted, etc. And perhaps, etc. Meaning, since the Beit Yosef's view is that the primary halacha follows the first opinion, as his normal sacred practice is to treat the first opinion he cites as primary, and the Rama also wrote that the custom follows the first opinion, it would be forbidden to be stringent and interrupt, for it appears as though he is driving away the King. Even for Krias Shema he did not need to interrupt, since he certainly began permissibly. Therefore he writes that perhaps Maharsha ruled not like the Beit Yosef and Rama, but like the 'some say,' which is the Rif's view, and therefore he had to interrupt according to the law.
It implies in Tosafot, Shabbos 9, etc. There the Gemara discusses Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi, who says that one may not taste anything before Minchah, and infers that even if he began eating he must interrupt. Tosafot write that this refers only to one who began in prohibition, but if he began permissibly he need not interrupt, for regarding havdalah too the language is that one may not taste before havdalah, and nevertheless Pesachim 105 teaches that if he began permissibly he need not interrupt eating. This is the law mentioned here in the Shulchan Aruch. From this it is proven that if he began in prohibition, then regarding havdalah too he must interrupt, since the same language is used. Although with prayer we rule that even if he began in prohibition he does not interrupt, that is because we do not rule like Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi. But regarding havdalah, where we do rule that it is forbidden to taste before havdalah, the rule remains that if he began in prohibition he interrupts. The reason is that prayer is D'Rabbanan, while havdalah is according to the Torah, as the Magen Avraham writes afterward. So too it appears from siman 271, seif 5 in the gloss, where one who forgot havdalah until after HaMotzi should eat first. Since he did not write that he should finish the meal first, it implies that he should eat only the slice of HaMotzi so his blessing not be in vain, and then interrupt and recite havdalah, as the Magen Avraham wrote there.
One must say that he holds havdalah is according to the Torah, and therefore its law is like Krias Shema, for which one must interrupt. If it were D'Rabbanan, its law would be like prayer, where we rule in siman 235 that he need not interrupt even if he began in prohibition.
According to the one who says havdalah is D'Rabbanan, and see above in the Magen Avraham, siman 296: if he washed his hands, meaning even if he already recited the blessing over netilas yadayim, he must nevertheless interrupt and recite havdalah, since there is an opinion in siman 166 that one need not be careful to avoid interruption between the blessing over netilas yadayim and HaMotzi. So wrote the Magen Avraham above in siman 235.
Since the entire night, etc. Meaning, although regarding Minchah we rule above that in such a case he need not interrupt, nevertheless havdalah, since its time is the entire night, is comparable to Maariv, which for this reason requires interruption.
And one should not rely, etc. Meaning, although elsewhere the Rama wrote above in siman 232 that one may rely on someone calling him, etc.
And because of havdalah, etc. Meaning that even according to the one who says havdalah is according to the Torah, and even where he began in prohibition, there is a distinction between whether he already read Krias Shema or not. For Krias Shema, if he began within the half hour adjacent to its time, it is called beginning in prohibition, as written above in siman 235. But for havdalah, according to the Magen Avraham, beginning in prohibition applies only if he began during doubtful darkness.
