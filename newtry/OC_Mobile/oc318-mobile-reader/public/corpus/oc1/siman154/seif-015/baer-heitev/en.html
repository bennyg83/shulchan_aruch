If the stipulation was that, should he wish, he could pay them their value and take the Sefer Torah, then even if he later agreed to give them the Sefer Torah, they are not considered to be in possession of it, since he retains the ability to remove their claim by payment. Magen Avraham. One who finds an object in the courtyard of a synagogue acquires it, because a courtyard acquires by virtue of its owner’s hand, and hekdesh has no hand. Mabit, part 2, siman 171, writes: If residents of a city had one synagogue and, because of circumstances, were compelled to divide into two congregations, all sacred utensils and Sifrei Torah are divided as follows. If certain objects are known to have been donated by a particular individual, he or his heirs may bring them to the synagogue with which he is affiliated. Objects whose donor is unknown are divided according to the number of males aged thirteen and above, for all have a share in them. If they cannot be physically divided, each congregation uses them for a period proportionate to its number. Magen Avraham writes: In my view, if the donor’s name has ceased to be associated with an object, then even when it is known who donated it, all have acquired rights in it, since the congregation may even divert it to a non-sacred use. It further appears to me that women and minors also have a share in all objects whose rights they inherited from their ancestors. See there. See also the lengthy discussion in Rosh, responsum 7. If a congregation fled and later resettled, and one member had taken sacred utensils, he must return them to the congregation, even if he does not wish to reside there, and even if he himself originally donated those utensils. Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 67. See also Responsa Avodas HaGershuni, siman 49. If one purchased a synagogue honor for a year, such as gelilah, and during that year the king expelled the Jews, then if the royal decree was already known at the time of purchase, even though efforts were being made to annul it, he should have stipulated accordingly. If he did not, he must pay. When he did stipulate, the condition must precede the act and satisfy all the laws governing conditions. Masas Binyamin, siman 64. See also Choshen Mishpat 334:1 in the gloss. It is possible, however, that hekdesh is different, for the hand of hekdesh has the advantage. Magen Avraham. Rashba writes: If Reuven built from his own property an aron kodesh for the synagogue and wishes to inscribe his name upon it, the congregation may not prevent him, for the Torah publicizes one who performs a mitzvah. One person built a synagogue, and the congregation wished to participate with him financially, but he refused, stipulating instead that it be identified with him and his descendants; his line later died out. See Sefer Chassidim, siman 503. See also Responsa Avodas HaGershuni, siman 49, on this matter. One person offered ten gold coins for a mitzvah, another offered fifteen, and the second bidder then withdrew. Tzemach Tzedek rules that the first bidder must take it for ten gold coins. Magen Avraham writes that this is not the accepted practice. Eliyah Rabbah discusses whether it is permissible to make a Sefer Torah covering, mantle, or cloth from shaatnez, and concludes that according to the view that it is forbidden, if one mistakenly made it, the consecration was made in error and it should instead be used as a paroches.
