It appears to me that if the condition was that if he wishes, he will give them its value and take the Torah scroll, then even if he afterward agreed to give them the Torah scroll, nevertheless they are not called the ones in possession, since he has the ability to remove them, as written there in Choshen Mishpat, siman 115; see there. If one found an item in the courtyard of a synagogue, he acquired it, and we do not say that the courtyard of a synagogue acquires for sacred property, because a courtyard acquires by the power of a hand, and sacred property has no hand. Agudah at the beginning of Me'ilah; see there, and see Shekalim, chapter 6. Mabit, part 2, siman 171, wrote: townspeople who had one synagogue and, because of some circumstance, needed to divide themselves into two, the law is that all sacred utensils and Torah scrolls are divided in the following way: if there are known objects that a particular person dedicated, he or his heirs may take them to the synagogue in which he is located; and what is not known is divided according to the number of people from age thirteen and upward, for all have a right in them. If they cannot be divided, each should use them for a time according to proportion. This is his wording. To me it does not appear so. If the owner's name has been forgotten from it, even though it is known who dedicated it, all acquired a right in it, for the community may change it to a mundane matter, as written in Yoreh Deah, siman 259, seif 3. It also appears to me that women and children also have a share in all the items, for they inherited the right from their fathers, as the Ran wrote in the chapter Bnei Ha'ir: even though there are many minor orphans, nevertheless the townspeople may sell the synagogue, for they are their guardians. This is his wording. The Ma'agalei Tzedek wrote in responsum siman 64: one who bought a mitzvah for a year, such as gelilah, and during the year the king expelled the Jews, if at the time of the purchase the king's decree had already been heard, even though they were trying to nullify the decree, nevertheless he should have stipulated, and if he did not stipulate, he must pay. When he stipulates, the condition must precede the act and follow all the laws of conditions; otherwise the condition is void and the act remains, and he must pay. This is his wording. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 334, seif 1, in the gloss. Perhaps sacred property is different, for the hand of sacred property is on top. The Rashba wrote in siman 581: Reuven had a house adjacent to the heichal of the synagogue, and he built it beautifully and wished to join it with the synagogue in order to expand the synagogue. The community prevented him because there were seats there that would be lowered from their status. They agreed that he should make the house into a heichal for the synagogue, and so he did, and he wants to write his name upon it. They cannot prevent him, for the Torah also writes and publicizes one who performs a mitzvah. This is his wording. See Yoreh Deah, siman 249, seif 13, and siman 253, seif 3. One who has a privy adjacent to a synagogue should see Responsa She'eris Yosef. It should be compared to a tree adjacent to a city, where we say he removes it and receives payment, for damage to the public is different from damage to an individual. So it also appears in Choshen Mishpat, siman 155, seif 22; see there. See Responsa Maharam, simanim 233 and 234, that if he has presumption and a claim, it is effective for a bathhouse and a privy. One built a synagogue and the community wanted to join with him financially, but he did not want this, so that it would be for him and his descendants by name, and his descendants died out. Sefer Chasidim, siman 503. In Rabbah, Parshas Acharei: if a person has no mitzvah fixed for generations, what benefit does he have? In Yoma 70, each person brings a Torah scroll from his house and reads from it to display its beauty to the public. Rashi explained: to show the beauty of the Torah scroll and the glory of its owners, who toil to beautify mitzvos. In Yoma 24: why do they draw lots and then draw lots again? In order to make the whole courtyard feel it. From here one can learn that it is better to pray Mincha by itself and then gather again and pray Maariv; but on weekdays they pray both together because of the burden on the community. And that we pray Mussaf immediately after Shacharis is because it is forbidden to establish a meal before Mussaf. One who prevents building a synagogue, even if there is another synagogue in the city, prevents the public from performing a mitzvah. Rivash, simanim 231 and 233; Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, 44 and 45. From the words of Re'em, part 1, siman 53, it appears that he holds that if the synagogue contains them, they are forbidden to separate, and Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 41, wrote likewise. See Rav Shmuel HaLevi, siman 5. Knesses HaGedolah wrote that the community may decree that ten should not pray outside the synagogue because of appearances before the nations, who will think they are many, and also because of individuals who do not pay the taxes and pray with ten elsewhere. One person wanted to give ten gold coins for a mitzvah, and another gave fifteen gold coins; afterward the second retracted. The first is obligated to hold him to ten gold coins. So wrote Tzemach Tzedek, bringing proof from the mishnah in the seventh chapter of Arachin, but this is not the practice.
