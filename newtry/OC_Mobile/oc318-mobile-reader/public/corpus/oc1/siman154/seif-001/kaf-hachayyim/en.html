1) [Seif 1] Likewise, houses and courtyards, etc. Our synagogue courtyards are no better than houses and courtyards. Perhaps, however, since people regularly pray in them whenever space is limited, they become sacred. Responsa Maharit, volume 2, Yoreh De'ah, siman 4; Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot HaTur; Magen Avraham, paragraph 1; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Chayei Adam, klal 17, paragraph 46. Mor U'Ketziah, however, takes it as obvious in this siman that such a courtyard has the same sanctity as a synagogue, and he questions Maharit; see there. Machazik Berachah, paragraph 1, cites him. See above, siman 151, paragraph 2. Accordingly, it is forbidden to use such courtyards as a shortcut. So too writes Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1. See above, siman 151, seif 5.
2) A garden, orchard, or platform surrounding the courtyard has no sanctity, even if it opens into the courtyard, and one may use it. If it opens into the courtyard directly opposite the heichal, then although the place is nonsacred, it is proper to be stringent and not behave frivolously there. Maharit there; Knesset HaGedolah there; Magen Avraham there; Eliyah Rabbah there; Chayei Adam there.
3) A room adjoining a beis midrash was not permanently designated as an annex to the beis midrash, but at times, when many people were present, they prayed there. They now wish to construct in that room a spring-fed mikveh for men and women. See Responsa Maharam Schick, end of siman 106, where he inclines toward leniency and permits it; see there. Orchos Chaim, paragraph 1, cites him. However, from what we wrote above in paragraph 1 in the name of Maharit and Mor U'Ketziah, it appears that this should be forbidden. Since people pray there whenever space is limited, the sanctity of a synagogue takes effect upon it. I later saw that Toras Chaim, paragraph 1, writes the same. He concludes that the straightforward solution is for the seven trustees of the town, in the presence of the townspeople, to sell that room and transfer its sanctity to the money. They may then buy it back, after which they may even make it into a bathhouse, as explained in siman 153, seif 9; see there. This applies specifically to a village synagogue. A synagogue in a large city has no remedy, as explained there in seifim 7 and 9; see there.
4) A synagogue is situated on an upper floor, while the area beneath it is used as a residence or for similar purposes. That lower area was never consecrated with synagogue sanctity, for only the floor of the synagogue and the area above it were consecrated. Later, people began praying below, and the trustees of the holy congregation protested and told them not to pray there, but they did not listen. Does synagogue sanctity take effect upon the lower area, making mundane use forbidden? See Responsa Lev Chaim, volume 3, siman 39, which concludes that there is no basis for concern about a prohibition there. Sanctity did not take effect, and it may be used for mundane purposes; see there. See also Petach HaDevir, volume 3, folio 307d, regarding this matter; see there. Orchos Chaim there.
5) Regarding the practice of placing firewood needed for heating a beis midrash in winter in the structure adjoining the synagogue or beis midrash that is called the palish, and the practice of every holy community to store in the palish the box used for grinding matzos, see Eshel Avraham by the holy gaon of Butchatch. He defends the practice and writes that it is included in the principle that the rabbinical court stipulates concerning communal property; see there. Orchos Chaim there.
6) There, in the gloss: Some say that it is not considered an aron, etc. This gloss belongs in seif 3, on the Mechaber's words, 'and a chest,' etc. It is placed there in the Levush as well, and the later authorities write the same. Olas Tamid, paragraph 1; Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 1.
7) There, in the gloss: But an aron built into the wall, etc. The same applies if it is built into a wooden wall, for it is considered merely a room. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2; Yad Aharon in Hagahot Beit Yosef; Siddur Beis Oved in Mekor Chaim, paragraph 2; Eshel Avraham, paragraph 2; Chayei Adam there, paragraph 47.
8) There, in the gloss: Which was made for protection, etc. If they made an aron kodesh from a natural rock formation that was originally attached to the ground, it has no sanctity even without the rationale that it was made for protection. Anything attached to the ground does not become prohibited, just as me'ilah does not apply to something attached to the ground. Or Zarua, Hilchos Beis HaKnesses, siman 386. See Sefer Minchas Pittim, folio 43b, regarding this matter; see there. Orchos Chaim, paragraph 2.
9) There, in the gloss: All the more so if the sefarim become damaged, etc. If so, it was never fit to serve as an accessory of sanctity, and the status of sanctity did not take effect upon it. It is not considered their accessory, but rather something that harms them, since it damages them. Beit Yosef in the name of the Mordechai; Magen Avraham, paragraph 3.
10) There, in the gloss: It is permitted to remove them from there. Magen Avraham, paragraph 4, writes: 'Meaning, it will have the law of synagogue sanctity.' His intent is that the phrase 'it is permitted to remove them,' used by the Rama, of blessed memory, is not exact. What prohibition could there be in taking the Sefer Torah from this aron and placing it in another aron? Rather, he teaches us the status of sanctity of that place. It is permitted to remove the sefarim from it, and the vacated place retains the law of synagogue sanctity, so that other synagogue articles may be placed there. This also appears to be the intent of the Levush, who writes, 'It is permitted to remove them for other uses.' He means that one may remove the sefarim and leave the place available for other synagogue uses. See Olas Tamid, paragraph 2, and Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 1, who struggled to explain the Rama's words; see there. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 4, similarly writes that all accessories of accessories have the law of synagogue sanctity, as stated in seif 6 in the gloss; see there. See below, paragraph 143.
11) Now, some make a wooden aron within a recess in the wall. One can say that it is an accessory of sanctity. Sometimes pieces of cloth are spread inside the wall so that the sefarim will not be damaged. If the cloth tears and is no longer fit for that purpose, it may be made into covers for the tables upon which people study in the beis midrash, to spread upon them in honor of Shabbos. One can say that the rabbinical court stipulates concerning them. According to the Taz, paragraph 7, whenever an item is no longer fit for its original purpose it is permitted. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 3. Toras Chaim, paragraph 2, similarly writes that a wooden aron built into a recess in the wall is the primary aron kodesh and is called an accessory of sanctity. But if it stands independently and is not built into the wall, it is like an aron kodesh built into the wall that was made only for protection. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, writes that the same applies to one built into a wooden wall, for it is considered merely a room. Toras Chaim rejects the words of Maharam Schick at the end of siman 80; see there. It appears to me that everything depends upon its intended purpose. If it was made to honor the Sefer Torah and not for protection, it is an accessory of sanctity. See below, paragraph 65. All this applies to those whose custom is not to make a case for a Sefer Torah, but only handles, so that they roll it in a cloth and, after the reading, store it in the aron kodesh. Those whose custom is to make a case for the Sefer Torah, covered with silk or with silver and gold fastened by nails, never remove the Sefer Torah from that case, even during the reading. After the reading they place it together with its case in the wall recess on the side facing the congregation, which is called the heichal. According to all opinions, that recess serves merely for protection, and its sanctity is like the sanctity of the synagogue. Yoreh De'ah, siman 282, seif 12, states that an aron or tower in which a Sefer Torah is placed requires genizah even if the Sefer Torah is placed there only while in its case. That refers to an aron in which the Sefer Torah is sometimes placed without its case, as is implied by the Beit Yosef there, and the Bach explicitly writes so there. This is unlike what the Gra writes there, paragraph 28. See the Beit Yosef there regarding the tower, which is a bench, and Be'er HaGolah there writes the same. This refutes the distinction written by the Gra; see there and examine it carefully.
12) The Radbaz writes in his responsa, volume 3, siman 530, that throughout all Jewish lands, from the rising of the sun to its setting, the custom is to place the Sefer Torah upright, and the custom of our ancestors is Torah. Although Rabbeinu Tam holds that it is preferable for it to lie horizontally, he follows his own view that a mezuzah too should be horizontal, like a Sefer Torah and the Luchos, as stated in Tosafos on Menachos 33a, s.v. ha. The Rambam and most of the commentators, of blessed memory, disagree with him. Just as they disagree with him regarding a mezuzah, they disagree with him regarding placement of a Sefer Torah, etc. Although the Sefer Torah in the Aron was horizontal, this is not difficult, because it was not positioned for reading, unlike our sefarim. See his full discussion. Petach HaDevir, paragraph 2, cites him and provides several additional supports. He writes that this is also Maran's view in siman 32, in the Beit Yosef and in the Shulchan Aruch there, seif 45, regarding the passages of tefillin, and in Yoreh De'ah, siman 289, seif 6, regarding a mezuzah. Maran rules like Rashi, the Rambam, and the Tur that it must stand upright, unlike Rabbeinu Tam. He questions the words of Adnei Paz, who writes that one must make a chest with the Sefer Torah lying inside it, because that is the respectful manner, etc.; see there.
