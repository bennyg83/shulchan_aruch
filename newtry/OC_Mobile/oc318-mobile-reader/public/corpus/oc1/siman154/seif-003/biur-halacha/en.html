Articles serving sacred objects, etc. Regarding whether the seven representatives of the city can remove the sanctity from a mantle or similar article, as they can with a synagogue under siman 153, seif 7, Magen Avraham writes there, seif katan 14, that no device can remove the sanctity of articles serving sacred objects. Eliyah Rabbah rules likewise. We wrote in Shaar HaTziyun there that this is evident from Ramban, Rashba, Ritva, and Ran at the beginning of the chapter Bnei HaIr. They struggled even to explain why the seven representatives can remove a synagogue’s sanctity. Ramban and Rashba explain that a synagogue’s sanctity is no more severe than that of other mitzvah articles, which may be discarded after they cease serving a mitzvah. That reasoning does not apply to articles serving sacred objects. Peri Megadim, however, notes in siman 153, Eshel Avraham, letter 14, that Rambam, chapter 10 of Hilchos Sefer Torah, implies that the city representatives can remove even the sanctity of articles serving sacred objects, at least when they intend to use the proceeds for a higher sanctity. The Tur, Yoreh Deah 282, adds that with the seven representatives acting in the presence of the townspeople they may remove the object’s sanctity even to drink beer with the proceeds, exactly as with a synagogue, where the buyer may use the building for mundane purposes and the proceeds may also be spent on beer. Beit Yosef writes there that Rambam agrees when the representatives act in the presence of the townspeople and merely abbreviated his wording. Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah, rules without qualification accordingly. The later authorities ask how this view answers Ramban’s objection. See Responsa Chasam Sofer, siman 38, who struggles with this. His further objection that Rambam contradicts himself by stating at the beginning of halachah 4 that articles serving sacred objects require burial, without distinguishing a sale, is not difficult, because Rambam’s words at the end of that halachah apply to everything stated earlier in the halachah. In my opinion, Rambam and Tur’s reasoning is straightforward. They hold that the authority of the city representatives regarding a synagogue rests on the principle that anyone who dedicates property does so subject to their authority. It is therefore comparable to a conditional dedication, which is effective. This resembles the Yerushalmi in the chapter Bnei HaIr, cited by the Rishonim, where Rabbi Yirmiyah saw people placing a tool in the ark and Rabbi Ami answered that they had presumably stipulated this from the outset. Under this reasoning there is no distinction between mitzvah articles such as a synagogue and articles serving sacred objects; the representatives’ authority applies to all. Meiri in Megillah likewise indicates that this is the basis of their authority. He writes that if the seven representatives sell a synagogue in the townspeople’s presence, it loses its sanctity even if they sell it in order to lower the proceeds to a lesser sanctity or spend them on mundane matters, and the building itself becomes mundane in the buyer’s hands for every use, because it was dedicated subject to that understanding and to their authority. Even according to this approach, however, the Tur’s juxtaposition of his father the Rosh’s ruling that an individual’s authority over his own property equals that of the city representatives is difficult. The Tur clearly implies that an individual may likewise sell articles serving sacred objects that he dedicated and remove their sanctity. One could say that when he dedicated them he did so subject to his own intent and discretion, no less than if he had made their status dependent on another individual’s decision, as Mordechai, siman 824, cites from Raavyah. Still, it is reasonable that his retained authority permits him to sell them and use the proceeds for mundane purposes, but not to remove the sanctity from the object itself whenever he wishes. Unless he stipulated explicitly, he presumably dedicated the object permanently. Practically, although Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch are lenient, and Raavyah cited by Mordechai, siman 826, also implies that a town’s sale can remove even the sanctity of articles serving sacred objects, it is difficult to be lenient against Ramban, Rashba, Ritva, and Ran. The Gra’s wording in seif 9 likewise implies that such articles cannot be lowered from their sanctity under any circumstances. See also Peri Megadim, siman 153, Mishbetzos Zahav, letter 15, who leaves the matter requiring study.
Specifically the item placed upon it, etc. See the Mishnah Berurah, where we cited Peri Megadim. Biur HaGra implies that anything touching the sacred object itself is considered an article serving sacred objects, even when used only for protection. According to this, Rama’s ruling that an ark built into a wall is not considered such an article because it is made for protection must mean that a Sefer Torah is ordinarily not placed in it without a mantle, so the ark does not touch the sacred object itself. See what we write below.
But an ark built into a wall and made for protection, etc. This ruling derives from Or Zarua and is cited by Mordechai and Hagahos Ashri. Or Zarua explains the Gemara’s phrase, “One might have thought that these were made merely for protection and not for honor; therefore it teaches us otherwise,” to mean that they were in fact made for honor. He derives that an object made for protection is not considered an article serving sacred objects. Or Zarua himself notes that the passage could be interpreted oppositely: one might have thought that because they were made for protection they resemble an ordinary storage room, and the Gemara teaches that they do not. Under that reading, even an article made for protection has the status of an article serving sacred objects. He nevertheless concludes that the first interpretation is preferable. I found, however, that Meiri and Rabbeinu Yerucham clearly follow the second interpretation and regard even something made for protection as an article serving sacred objects. According to them, perhaps even a recess built into a wall has that status. Alternatively, because it is merely a corner hollowed into the wall, it becomes subordinate to the wall itself and resembles an ordinary storage recess, having only the sanctity of the synagogue. This requires study.
