And the practice is to derive benefit, etc. Maharshal writes that placing other sacred books in the ark beside a Sefer Torah is not considered lowering its sanctity, because the ark remains in its sacred use. This is evident from the universal practice of placing invalid parchment sheets there, and the same should apply to Chumashim and other sacred books. It requires study, however, whether after removing the Sefer Torah one may place other books in the ark. Since no Sefer Torah is then present, perhaps this is a temporary lowering of sanctity. Bach proves that even temporary use is considered lowering from the Gemara’s teaching that one may wrap a Sefer Torah in a Chumash mantle, but not a Chumash in a Sefer Torah mantle. The first clause necessarily concerns temporary use; if the mantle were permanently redesignated for the Sefer Torah, the ruling would be obvious. Consequently, the prohibition in the latter clause likewise applies even temporarily. According to this, those who on Hoshana Rabbah remove all the Sifrei Torah and place a burning candle in the ark do not act properly, for the walls of the aron kodesh then serve the candle. One might answer that they do so in honor of the Torah, symbolizing “Torah is light,” meaning that when the Sefer Torah is absent the light of a candle is needed. This seems to be their intention, but it is insufficient because lowering the ark’s sanctity remains prohibited. The ark has this sanctity only when made to house a Sefer Torah permanently. If it was made only temporarily, as occurred in many places where synagogues were destroyed and a small temporary ark was constructed until Hashem enabled the community to build a proper aron kodesh, then once the permanent ark is made, the first one’s sanctity lapses. It resembles a synagogue rented for a limited period, discussed above, which lacks permanent synagogue sanctity. It may therefore afterward be used to store sacred books. In my opinion, this too is included in the presumed stipulation of beis din. Were I not afraid, I would suggest a further principle. The prohibition against converting a greater sanctity into a lesser one applies only while the object remains fit for the greater sanctity. If it is no longer fit for that use but can serve a lesser sanctity, it is preferable to employ it for that lesser sanctity rather than leave it unused and bury it. The proof is that worn Sefer Torah mantles are used as shrouds for a deceased person, which constitutes their burial. While still usable, this would certainly be forbidden. Once they require burial, however, this is their proper interment. Likewise, it is preferable to use an otherwise unusable ark for the sacred purpose of holding books rather than leave it entirely empty and without any sacred use.
