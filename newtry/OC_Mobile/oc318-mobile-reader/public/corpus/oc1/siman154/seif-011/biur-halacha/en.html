Wax candles, etc. See the Mishnah Berurah regarding converting an idolatrous house into a synagogue. Magen Avraham, citing Re’em, is lenient, while Eliyah Rabbah holds that Re’em never intended to permit making it a permanent synagogue. Know that this entire discussion applies only when the non-Jews did not place their idols there. In such a case Magen Avraham inclines to distinguish it from wax candles, which remain repulsive for a mitzvah even after nullification because they had acquired the status of idolatrous ornamentation. The house never acquired the status of idolatrous ornamentation or appurtenance, because no idol stood there; they merely bowed and prayed there to their idols. Magen Avraham also joins the consideration that a house is attached to the ground, but that is not his principal reason, since legally it is treated like detached property, and even an attached object used for worship is prohibited for a mitzvah, as the Gemara proves. If idols were placed there, however, the house acquired the status of an appurtenance of idolatry and was prohibited even for mundane benefit, as Rama rules in Yoreh Deah 145:3. After the idols are removed and the house becomes permitted for ordinary benefit through nullification, it nevertheless remains prohibited for a mitzvah, just as idolatrous ornamentation remains prohibited after nullification. It appears that Magen Avraham alluded to this by referring to siman 145, seif 3. One cannot object from Yoreh Deah 143:2, which permits initially constructing a palace or courtyard containing a dome upon which an idol will be placed. There the idol is not in the house itself but upon the dome, so the house merely serves the dome and is only an appurtenance of an appurtenance. A house specifically designated for placing idols within it and worshipping them is a direct appurtenance of idolatry, as Rama explicitly states in siman 145:3, and it is certainly prohibited for a mitzvah even after nullification. I saw one great authority incline to permit because their practice is to place the idol in a box or similar enclosure, making the house only an appurtenance of an appurtenance. His words are unconvincing. It is well known that many idols stand exposed on the floor or walls. Moreover, the core argument is unsound, because the house was nevertheless designated for idolatry. See Dagul Merevavah in the name of Tosafos, and Ritva there, who writes similarly. Know further that if their house of prayer was merely rented to them, there may be room for leniency even in this case, since they lacked the power to prohibit the house permanently. See Peri Megadim and Ein Yitzchak, Orach Chayim, siman 10.
