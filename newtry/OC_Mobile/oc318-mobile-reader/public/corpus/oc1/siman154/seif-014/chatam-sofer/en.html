There, seif katan 23. And there is no hand for sacred property. Note: as the Rashbam and Tosafot wrote in Bava Batra 79a, and the Rav of Bartenura and Tosafot Yom Tov in Me'ilah, chapter 3, mishnah 6. As to what Tosafot Yom Tov asked, that in any case there is agency for sacred property, one can say that where it causes loss to others, no. But this requires study: the power of an ordinary person should not be stronger than sacred property, as we say in Kiddushin 29a. In the Gemara there is no proof at all that there is no hand for sacred property, as Ramban wrote in his novellae there, that although it is acquired by sacred property, there is no me'ilah in it. It further requires great study: perhaps it can be speaking of a courtyard that is not guarded according to the will of sacred property, and there it discusses only the law of growths of sacred property and not the law of a courtyard. So it is also clear from mishnah 4, chapter 5 of Shekalim. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 268, and see Responsa Chasam Sofer, Orach Chaim, siman 44, regarding a householder who had a shop open to the public domain for pouring wine, where many Egyptians were commonly found, and his servant boy found a purse containing money, and it was known that it was lost by the Egyptians; whether the boy acquired it, or the householder, since his courtyard acquires for him. He concluded his responsum in these words: nevertheless, in our case we conclude that the servant boy acquired it, for we rule that the find of a worker or hired laborer belongs to himself, unless the householder said to him, 'lift this found object for me.' See Sema at the end of siman 78. As for what people say, that the custom is that the find of a servant belongs to the householder, nonsense should stop their mouths; there is no basis for this. Regarding what his honor wrote from the Shach at the end of siman 37, see what Beis Shmuel wrote at the beginning of siman 47, and understand this.
