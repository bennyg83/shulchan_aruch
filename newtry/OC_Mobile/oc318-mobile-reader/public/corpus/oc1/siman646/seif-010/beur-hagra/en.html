Seif 10: Its tip was severed, etc. The statement there, “and a date-like growth arose upon it,” applies specifically according to the first Tanna. We, however, rule in accordance with Rabbi Tarfon, as stated above.
And some authorities invalidate it, etc. They explain that Rabbi Tarfon's word “severed” does not mean that the top was cut off. Rather, it follows the wording of Toras Kohanim, Parashas Emor: Rabbi Yishmael says, “the fruit of a beautiful tree,” etc., and even two shoots and one that is not severed; Rabbi Tarfon says, etc. The same appears in the Yerushalmi there. In Rabbi Yishmael's statement the word “even” should not be read; rather, the text is, “and two shoots,” etc. Rabbi Abba bar Mamal asked Rabbi Ami: just as Rabbi Yishmael allows additional myrtles, should he likewise allow additional specimens of the other species? Rabbi Ami answered: Do you suppose that Rabbi Yishmael's position concerns beauty? We learned that Rabbi Tarfon says, “Even if all three are severed.” No person would say “even” unless he agreed with the preceding ruling. Rabbi Chaggai asked Rabbi Yosa: What did Rabbi Tarfon add to Rabbi Yishmael's words? He answered: Rabbi Yishmael does not regard a severed specimen as beautiful, whereas Rabbi Tarfon does regard it as beautiful. The explanation is that Rabbi Abba and Rabbi Chaggai initially thought that “severed” described a superior feature, namely the upper soft branches of the myrtle, which are its beauty and are the “shoots” mentioned by Rabbi Yishmael. Rabbi Tarfon called them “severed,” because anything broken while still soft is described as plucked. This refers to the soft branches that were plucked, as in the expression, “I shall pluck the tender shoot,” while “one that is not severed” means a branch broken from the hard trunk, for breaking is the term used when a hard object is fractured. Rabbi Ami therefore answered: Do you think Rabbi Tarfon meant this as a superior feature? If so, why did he say “even”? According to that reading, Rabbi Tarfon would require specifically all three to be severed and would not agree with Rabbi Yishmael. Rabbi Chaggai raised the same question, and Rabbi Yosa answered that “shoots” was stated as a deficiency. It means that the trunk is not whole, but a branch was cut from one side and another branch from the other side, and these are counted as two myrtles, while “one that is not severed” means that the entire trunk remains whole with all its branches. This explains the Gemara's statement that one unsevered myrtle is uncommon, meaning that it is uncommon for a myrtle to remain completely leafy in that form. According to the other commentators, however, such myrtles are very common. Baal HaMaor writes similarly. Ramban, in Milchamos, objects that according to this interpretation there is no reason for myrtle to differ from the other species. He therefore follows Rashi and Rif: Rabbi Tarfon validates a severed myrtle because its beauty lies in its leafiness. Furthermore, the Yerushalmi expressly discusses beauty, whereas the trunk itself has no greater beauty than the side branches. He explains “shoots” as branches whose tips vineyard workers customarily cut so that they will grow more, and Targum Yonasan likewise renders the phrase as having removed its top. The entire give-and-take concerning a severed myrtle addresses the preferred initial fulfillment. See there and Ran. Raam maintains that the severed myrtle mentioned in the Mishnah is valid only after the fact, as explained above in siman 645, seif 6, comparable to the conclusion in chapter 6 of Pesachim regarding remnants that became tamei, which establishes that they are invalid initially.
It is not called, etc. This follows from the statement, “and a date-like growth arose upon it,” because such a growth appears only upon the woody stem itself. Although regarding a lulav the phrase “its tip was severed” refers to its leaves, there the leaves rise to the top and are therefore called its tip. With a myrtle and willow, however, the end of the branch is sometimes bare, and the tip of the woody stem itself is called its tip. See Rosh.
