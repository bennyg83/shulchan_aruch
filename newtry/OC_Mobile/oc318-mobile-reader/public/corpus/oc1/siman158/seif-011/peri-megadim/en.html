What the Mechaber wrote. In seif 9, that one must be careful, requires analysis. See Tosafos, Sotah 4a, cited by Eliyah Rabbah, letter 21. They explain that one who habitually treats washing lightly is uprooted from the world through poverty, while one who occasionally transgresses may incur death, as expressed by the statement that a snake will bite him, and may also come to poverty. See there. The Mechaber combined all three consequences. Rambam, chapter 6 of Hilchos Talmud Torah, halachah 14, writes that one who treats a Rabbinic law lightly is placed under a ban, and Kesef Mishneh explains this as one who habitually treats it lightly. If so, why does the Mechaber single out the need for care regarding washing the hands? The same rule applies to all Rabbinic laws, where such a person is placed under a ban. According to Ran’s explanation that a ban is more severe than lashes for rebellion, and that one who uproots an enactment whose entire basis is Rabbinic is placed under a ban, one might explain that in washing the hands he treats lightly only the requirement for ordinary food, but not for terumah, and nevertheless he is placed under a ban. This is understandable. See Peri Chadash, Orach Chayim 496, who concludes that either a ban or lashes may be administered, whichever is appropriate, to one who transgresses or treats the law lightly. See Magen Avraham 496:1. Shach in Yoreh Deah 334 and Peri Chadash there discuss Kesef Mishneh’s difficulty. Peri Chadash explains that since washing the hands is fundamentally Rabbinic, one is placed under a ban. According to what I wrote, one may say that he treats it lightly only for ordinary food, where a second degree does not render a third degree, but washes for terumah. Why then is he placed under a ban if he is not uprooting the entire enactment? Therefore the Mechaber stresses that one should not think that because the Sages supported the enactment with a verse, as in Chullin 106a and Berachos at the end of the first chapter, it should be treated like a Torah law.
