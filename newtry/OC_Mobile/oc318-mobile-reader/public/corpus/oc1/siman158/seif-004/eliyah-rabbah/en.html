[5] Something dipped in liquid, etc. If an item is not ordinarily dipped or does not ordinarily have liquid upon it, then even if he dips it, his practice is disregarded relative to ordinary people. Likewise, one who inserts his finger into oil or honey and sucks it need not wash. Radbaz, siman 78. Taz, however, writes that one who eats honey must wash because he is likely to touch the honey with his hand. Shelah, page 81, writes that a confection made with honey requires washing. Bircas HaZevach to Menachos, page 31, writes that washing is unnecessary once the honey has congealed, and Radbaz, siman 101, rules likewise even when it congealed from cold. If it has thickened only slightly and still flows, it remains a liquid. Magen Avraham permits it because it is intended for eating. Taz rejects this, since an act rendered the honey into a liquid and mere intention to eat cannot override an act. Knesses HaGedolah and Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah cite authorities who hold that something dipped in a cooked liquid does not require washing, unless the liquid was not present while raw and emerged only through cooking. He concludes that when asked about cooked liquid he would say: I cannot decide; do as your heart desires. Olas Tamid writes that pickling is equivalent to cooking for this purpose. It is clear that liquid emerging after cooking is regarded as liquid according to all opinions, unlike Maharshal as cited by Taz and Magen Avraham in seif katan 10. This applies only if the liquid is one of the seven liquids. Derishah writes that even when the food is no longer in water, if it remains moist from the soaking water, washing is required. According to this, one would need to wash for cucumbers or salad even when they are no longer in water or vinegar. For plums and cooked fruits, however, it appears that even while they remain in their own broth no washing is required, since the blessing over the broth is Borei Peri HaEtz, showing that it does not have the status of a liquid. The same applies to confections fried in honey, where the honey is subordinate to the confection and the blessing is Borei Peri HaAdamah. Just as it is subordinate for the blessing, perhaps it is subordinate for washing as well, unless the confection is soaking in honey and one removes it from the honey by hand. If he takes it with a knife or utensil, there is no need to be stringent. Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah cites Radbaz that even when one eats something dipped in liquid with a utensil, washing is required. With a cooked liquid, however, even one who is stringent may be lenient when using a utensil, which somewhat accords with Derishah. Magen Avraham writes that Kesef Mishneh, chapter 1 of Hilchos Tumas Ochlin, implies that oil, blood, and milk that congealed are no longer liquids even if they later melt, with the exception of wine. This also appears at the beginning of chapter 3 of Taharos. Rambam and Bartenura there, however, make clear that melted oil again becomes a liquid, and Rambam writes likewise in chapter 9 of Hilchos Tumas Ochlin. See Bircas HaZevach to Menachos there. Rambam writes in chapter 1 that snow is neither food nor liquid; if one designates it as liquid, it contracts the impurity of liquids. Rash writes in chapter 3 of Taharos that frozen water is not a liquid, but when it melts it returns to the status of a liquid.
[6] Honey, etc. This means bee honey. Honey made from reeds, dates, or other fruits does not require washing.
[7] Blood, etc. This was mentioned unnecessarily, since consuming blood is prohibited. The blood of fish and grasshoppers is not considered a liquid. Perhaps the case concerns one who consumes blood for medicinal purposes.
[8] [Levush] Because this is not the usual manner, etc. Even one who drinks water from a river with his hands is not included in the decree. Magen Avraham, seif katan 11.
[9] [Levush] Because of dipping in oil, etc. Taz challenges Levush, arguing that Scripture itself teaches that these liquids are considered liquid. See there. In my humble opinion, he was not precise. Although Scripture teaches that they have the status of liquid, the prohibition here exists only because of the concern for terumah, and terumah applies only to wine and oil. This is readily understood.
[10] The end of a vegetable, etc. Taz questions Beit Yosef for saying that this teaches, etc., since the matter is an explicit Gemara later regarding the seven liquids, where the rejection states, “perhaps he immersed it,” etc. It is likewise explicit in Responsa Radbaz, siman 78.
