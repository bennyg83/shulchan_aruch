He therefore writes that one who pours a reviis over his hands all at once, so that the water does not become impure, does not require drying, as he concludes here: “The same applies to one who washes his hands,” etc. Maharshal writes concerning this: “He did not speak correctly, for drying is an enactment of the Sages because of repulsiveness, as Rashi explains regarding ‘their bread in impurity,’ that something repulsive is called impure. They enacted this only for washing of the hands, just as they enacted the requirement of a vessel and human force. Regarding immersion of the hands, however, which derives from immersion of the entire body and is D’Oraisa, they enacted nothing.” His words appear primary. The Gemara states in the name of Rabbi Abahu that whoever eats without drying his hands is considered as though he ate impure bread, as it says, “Thus shall the Children of Israel eat their bread impure.” Rashi explains that the verse teaches that something repulsive is called impure, for the verse there concerns a repulsive matter. If so, what is gained by pouring a reviis all at once? One cannot ask why one who immerses his hands does not require drying, for immersion is different, since it has the law of immersion of the entire body. It is obvious that one who immerses his entire body becomes pure even while still wet. The verse speaks only of something related to eating, as it says, “shall eat,” namely washing of the hands, and in that context the Sages were particular about repulsiveness. Levush writes that there are two opinions whether one who immerses his hands requires drying, since repulsiveness also applies when the immersed hands remain wet. This is incorrect. If so, all wet hands, even when pure, would be forbidden for eating. Rather, wherever the Sages instituted washing because of impurity, they were also particular that at that time there be no repulsiveness, and they supported this with the verse, “Thus shall they eat,” where we find that Scripture was likewise particular about repulsiveness. There is only one opinion here. Everyone agrees with the Baraisa that immersion suffices, as Beit Yosef writes, and Levush himself writes likewise. He merely states that Rabbi Abahu disputes the Baraisa, but such a statement should not be written. How could Rabbi Abahu, an Amora, dispute a Baraisa? This appears correct.
