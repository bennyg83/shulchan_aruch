1) [Seif 1] When one comes to eat bread, etc., he should wash his hands, etc. Washing the hands for non-sacred food was instituted because of the concern arising from terumah, and also because it is a mitzvah to obey the words of the Sages. Chullin 106a. Rashi, of blessed memory, explains: it was necessary because of the concern arising from terumah, since hands have secondary impurity and disqualify terumah. Secondary impurity does not affect non-sacred food. Nevertheless, because of the concern arising from terumah, so that those who eat terumah will become accustomed to washing their hands, the practice applies to non-sacred food. The Taz, paragraph 1, cites him. Tosafos there explain that the reason it is a mitzvah to obey the words of the Sages is that they instituted washing for cleanliness; see there. One recites the blessing over netilas yadayim even though the obligation is only rabbinic, for one may say 'and commanded us' regarding a rabbinic requirement, since the Sages supported it with the prohibition, 'You shall not deviate.' Tur. The Beit Yosef writes that since the prohibition of 'You shall not deviate' commands us to obey the Sages, when we fulfill their words we fulfill Hashem's mitzvah. This is the reason for all the blessings that we recite over rabbinic mitzvos. See Halachos Ketanos, volume 1, siman 275, who writes that the Sages decreed impurity even upon the hands of a one-day-old infant; see there. We cite this below in siman 162, paragraph 31; see there.
2) There: He should wash his hands, etc. Five matters require netilas yadayim, and their mnemonic is 'a current swept me away': Shema, food dipped in liquid, bread, prayer, and netilas yadayim. Avudraham, of blessed memory, in the blessing of Asher Yatzar; Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot HaTur, paragraph 75.
3) There: He should wash his hands, etc. One must wash the right hand first, so that the left serves the right. Recanati, parashas Eikev. Magen Avraham at the beginning of the siman. Shaar HaMitzvos, parashas Eikev, writes the same and gives a kabbalistic reason; see there. This implies that there is no difference whether one washes his own hands or another person pours for him. Darkei Moshe in Hagahot HaTur, paragraph 2, writes that at a meal one should wash the left hand first, so that the right hand of the attendant who pours will face the left of the person washing, and this was attributed to the Arizal, of blessed memory. This does not appear correct from the aforementioned Shaar HaMitzvos. We have only what R' Chaim Vital, of blessed memory, wrote in Shaar HaMitzvos in the name of the Arizal, of blessed memory, and nothing else. This is the common practice. One should also first take the vessel of water in his right hand, then give it to his left hand and pour from his left onto his right hand first, and afterward from his right onto his left, as written above in siman 4, seif 10. Machatzis HaShekel writes the same. See what we wrote on siman 4, paragraphs 34 through 37.
4) There: Even if he knows of no impurity upon them. The Rambam writes in chapter 6 of Hilchos Berachos: even if his hands are not dirty and he knows of no impurity upon them, he may not eat until he washes both hands. The Beit Yosef cites him; Olas Tamid, paragraph 2. One who has a wound on one hand and can wash only the other hand nevertheless recites the blessing over netilas yadayim, as we wrote above in siman 4, paragraph 7. So too writes the Peri Megadim in Eshel Avraham to this siman, paragraph 1, and Ben Ish Chai, parashas Tazria, paragraph 13. See below, siman 162, seif 10.
5) There: He should recite the blessing over netilas yadayim. The word netilah contains the letters of tal, numerical value 39, and NeHi. One should intend to draw the shefa from the three upper letters, Yod, Hei, and Vav, whose filled-out value, using alefs, is 39, to the final Hei, which is Malchus standing in NeHi. The initial letters of al netilas yadayim spell ani, whose numerical value is 130. The five Havayah Names also have the numerical value 130. One should intend to cause the value of 130 to prevail over the poor one and to sweeten it through the five Havayah Names. The Rashash, of blessed memory, in Sefer Nehar Shalom, folio 15b in the new printing. See below, paragraph 78; siman 160, paragraph 82; and siman 162, paragraph 2.
6) There: He should recite the blessing over netilas yadayim. If one recited the blessing over netilas yadayim and then changed his mind and decided not to eat, his blessing is not in vain. Ritva, novellae to Chullin 106; Machazik Berachah, Kuntres Acharon, paragraph 1; Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 1. Sdei Chemed, Asifas Dinim, maareches Berachos, paragraph 89, explains that whenever one's intention at the time of the blessing was for the mitzvah, even if he later changes his mind, we follow the time of the blessing. The blessing was necessary because he then intended to eat. He writes, however, that this can be questioned, for it appears that not all authorities agree. He concludes that it is nevertheless improper to act this way. One should compel himself to eat so that he does not enter into a concern of a blessing in vain, G-d forbid. Even the Ritva may agree that initially, when there is no compelling reason, one should not act this way; see there. See there further, where he cites a dispute regarding other mitzvos as well, when one intended to perform a mitzvah and recited a blessing, whether he may then withdraw. Therefore, one must initially take care not to cause his blessing retroactively to be in vain when he can fulfill the mitzvah.
6) Orchos Chaim states: washing before the meal is a mitzvah, while mayim acharonim is an obligation. What practical difference does this make? It gives mayim acharonim precedence over washing for a later meal. If one has just eaten and has no water for mayim acharonim, but he must eat another meal that day, such as the three meals on Shabbos, he should forgo the later meal and use the water for mayim acharonim before Birkas HaMazon, because he has already become obligated in the blessing. If he has scented oil or wine, he should clean his hands with it and recite the blessing. Beit Yosef; Olas Tamid, paragraph 2; Magen Avraham at the beginning of siman 181. Magen Avraham writes there that Orchos Chaim implies that if one wishes to eat and has only enough water for one washing, he should use it for washing before the meal and not for mayim acharonim, since he may not eat until he washes his hands. This requires study, for if so, in what way are mayim acharonim more stringent than washing before the meal? One must say that he may not eat unless he has enough water for both washing before the meal and mayim acharonim for that meal. Birkei Yosef there, paragraph 1, writes the same and comments that Eliyah Rabbah's position is not conclusive; see there. See there further regarding Magen Avraham's words. Magen Avraham nevertheless concludes there that nowadays washing before the meal takes precedence, even for one who is careful about mayim acharonim, because mayim acharonim is not as fully obligatory when Sodomite salt is no longer present. Eliyah Rabbah there, paragraph 1, writes the same. When Orchos Chaim says that he forgoes the later meal and washes for Birkas HaMazon, it appears to me that he means that he forgoes eating that meal with bread. After the fact, one may fulfill the third meal with another food, as written in siman 291, seif 5. He does not mean that one omits it entirely. See siman 163, seif 1, and Olas Tamid in siman 181, paragraph 6, and examine this carefully. Regarding Orchos Chaim's further statement, 'If he has scented oil,' etc., the Shulchan Aruch writes the same in siman 181, seif 9. See what we wrote there, with Heaven's help.
7) There: And he does not establish his meal upon them, etc. This implies that if he does establish his meal upon them, so that he recites HaMotzi over them as written below in siman 168, seif 6, he must wash his hands and recite the blessing over netilas yadayim. This also appears from the Beit Yosef, who, after considering the matter and presenting arguments in both directions, concludes that the Rashba's words imply that netilas yadayim is required; see there. The Levush, however, writes that if one establishes his meal upon them, he should wash his hands but not recite the blessing, because of the doubt. It is preferable for him first to eat an egg's volume of regular bread, so that he may wash his hands and recite the blessing over netilas yadayim, and then eat these foods; see there. The Shelah writes the same. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, cites the words of the Shelah and Levush and writes: 'I do not know who disagrees about this. Although the Beit Yosef initially questions the matter, he afterward concludes in the name of the Rashba that netilas yadayim is required, which implies that the matter became clear to him after he had questioned it. The Rambam likewise writes at the beginning of chapter 6 that anyone who eats bread over which HaMotzi is recited must wash, etc. The Bedek HaBayis and Mishmeres HaBayis explicitly write that washing is required when one establishes his meal upon it; see there. Therefore, he should recite the blessing over netilas yadayim.' The Taz, paragraph 2; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2; Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 2; Eshel Avraham, paragraph 2; and Mateh Yosef, volume 2, siman 18, paragraph 5, write the same. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 3, writes that this is the primary halachic ruling; see there. Zechor L'Avraham, paragraph 50, cites him. Machazik Berachah, paragraph 2, writes the same and rejects the words of Atzei Almogim, Nesiv Chaim, and Mor U'Ketziah, who hold that one should not recite the blessing; see there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1; the Gra, paragraph 5; Chayei Adam, klal 36, paragraph 2; and Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 2, also rule this way. Chesed LaAlafim adds that it is preferable also to eat an egg's volume of bread in order to satisfy all opinions, but if he has none or cannot eat bread, he should wash and recite the blessing; see there. Beis Menuchah, laws of netilas yadayim, paragraph 3; Kitzur Shulchan Aruch, siman 48, paragraph 1; Derech HaChaim, paragraph 1; Mishnah Berurah, paragraph 8. Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 7, however, writes that one who wishes to be stringent by not reciting the blessing or by eating bread together with these foods acts more properly; see there. Thus, this matter remains disputed. According to the rule that a doubt concerning blessings is treated leniently, even against the majority and even against Maran, of blessed memory, as we wrote above in siman 18, paragraph 7, it would have been proper to rule that one should wash without a blessing. Nevertheless, the common custom is also to recite the blessing, following Maran, of blessed memory, and the authorities who agree with him. We already wrote above in siman 7, paragraph 2, and siman 17, paragraph 2, that in a place with an established custom we do not apply the rule that a doubt concerning blessings is treated leniently, and they may recite the blessing; see there. The practical difference is that if a place is found where the custom is not to recite the blessing, one should not protest, for one can say that they follow the rule that a doubt concerning blessings is treated leniently. The quantity that constitutes establishing a meal upon pas haba b'kisnin will be explained below in siman 168, seif 6, with Heaven's help.
