2) There. A matter subject to loss is permitted, etc. Even a doubtful matter subject to loss is permitted. Maharshach, volume 1, siman 113; Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur; Olas Shabbos, subsection 1; Magen Avraham, at the beginning of the siman; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1. Eshel Avraham explains that the reason is that the laws of Chol HaMoed are rabbinic, so a doubt is treated leniently. Machatzis HaShekel and Levushei Serad write likewise. This implies that according to the opinion that the prohibition is under Torah law, one should be stringent. Nevertheless, Beis Yosef, siman 539, s.v. u'mah shekasav haRosh, writes: 'Since it is not certainly subject to loss, it is forbidden, etc.' See there. He writes this according to Rosh, who is among those who maintain that melachah on Chol HaMoed is prohibited rabbinically, as Rosh writes at the beginning of Moed Katan and Beis Yosef cites in siman 530. See there. This implies that even according to the rabbinic opinion, a doubtful matter subject to loss is forbidden. At first glance, however, this appears to contradict the mishnah in Moed Katan 18b, 'The following documents may be written during the festival, etc.' Tosafos there write that all these are matters subject to loss, lest the giver die, etc. Ritva writes likewise there in his novellae. This implies that we rule leniently in a doubtful case. The same appears from Responsa Rashba cited by Beis Yosef in siman 545. Rashba explains that the Tosefta permits one to write his accounts during the festival lest he forget and lose his money. Rashba is among those who maintain that melachah on Chol HaMoed is forbidden under Torah law, as Beis Yosef writes in siman 530, yet he permits a doubtful matter subject to loss. Maggid Mishneh, chapter 7 of Hilchos Yom Tov, law 3, likewise writes in the name of Ramban that produce must be brought in privately because the loss is uncertain rather than definite. See below, siman 538, se'if 2. This shows that he permits a doubtful matter subject to loss, and Ramban too is among those who hold that the prohibition is under Torah law, as Beis Yosef writes in siman 530. See there. It therefore appears that this does not depend upon whether the prohibition is under Torah or rabbinic law. Whenever it appears nearly certain that a loss will result, one may permit the work despite some doubt. When the concern is remote, one may not permit it because of the doubt. Chayei Adam, rule 106, subsection 5, writes that a doubtful matter subject to loss should be performed through a non-Jew. See there. Therefore, when possible, one should be stringent and have a non-Jew perform it. Likewise, when the concern is remote, if a loss may result, it should be performed through a non-Jew.
