[1] One who saw, etc. This excludes those who recite it every day. It is effective only if he recites it on the same day that he saw the dream. Toras Chaim, Bava Kamma, end of chapter 5. This does not appear correct to me. If so, why do all Israel recite it on Yom Tov? Did they all see a bad dream that very day? Rather, they must recite it for dreams seen on earlier days. So writes Magen Avraham. I am astonished, however, because Toras Chaim himself records the custom that all Israel recite it on Yom Tov and writes that they do not act properly, but only one who saw a dream should recite it. This also appears from the Gemara and the poskim, who say that he should conclude together with the congregation when they answer Amen, implying that he alone recites it and not the congregation. Moreover, Toras Chaim brings proof that it resembles fasting for a dream, which must be on that very day, and the wording, “I dreamed a dream,” likewise implies this. Magen Avraham also concludes that it is improper to recite it every day, and Taz writes likewise, except when one had a dream the preceding night. The practice that everyone recites it during Bircas Kohanim on Yom Tov therefore requires study. Magen Avraham writes in the name of Pitron Chalomot that it disagrees with the responsa of the Geonim concerning whether one may fast on Shabbos, reasoning that all the more so one may not recite Ribbono Shel Olam. Magen Avraham challenges this: fasting nullifies the dream like fire consumes flax, unlike reciting Ribbono Shel Olam. In my humble opinion, there is support for Pitron Chalomot from Responsa Maharam of Rothenburg, siman 603, which writes that one may fall on his face on Shabbos when he needs mercy, deriving this all the more so from fasting. See there. Nevertheless, one certainly should not recite it on Shabbos unless he had a bad dream.
