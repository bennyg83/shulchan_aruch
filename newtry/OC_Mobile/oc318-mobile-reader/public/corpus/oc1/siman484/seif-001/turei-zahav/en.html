Afterward he recites the blessing for each person. Although we rule that one cannot recite a blessing over enjoyment for others unless he partakes together with them, the blessing over the matzah and Kiddush are different, because they are obligatory. He may therefore recite them to discharge others even though he does not partake. The blessing over the vegetable is likewise considered connected to a mitzvah, and he may discharge others. The Rosh writes that since the Sages instituted the dipping as a means of arousing the children's attention, its blessing has the status of a blessing over a mitzvah. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, asks that the Gemara in the chapter Arvei Pesachim states that since the dipping serves only as a recognition, it is not a mitzvah. This is no difficulty. The Rosh does not mean that the dipping is truly a mitzvah. Rather, since the Sages instituted it as a means of recognition, it is obligatory and is therefore not treated like an ordinary blessing over enjoyment. Its law is consequently like that of a blessing over a mitzvah.
They themselves recite Birkat Hamazon. This means that he does not join them for a zimmun, since he did not eat together with them.
He should prompt them word by word. There is no concern here of uttering Hashem's Name in vain, because this is comparable to a minor who reads Hallel and the listeners repeat after him what he says. The Tur writes likewise in the name of the Rosh. One might ask that the Rosh rules in the chapter Sheloshah She'achlu that one who did not eat may discharge one who ate in Birkat Hamazon, because all Jews are responsible for one another. Why, then, is it necessary here to prompt them word by word? This is not difficult, because the Rosh writes there that by rabbinic law he cannot discharge them. The Semag, however, writes without qualification that he may discharge them, which implies that this applies even by rabbinic law. The Tur disputes this in siman 186.
The Tur writes that the Rif states, “He recites Birkat Hamazon for them,” but Rav Amram writes that there is no such institution, because if he recites Birkat Hamazon for them he will be unable to drink the cup associated with Birkat Hamazon in his own home. End quote. The Beit Yosef explains that since he recites Birkat Hamazon for them, he must eat a kezayis with them, and he will then be unable to eat and drink again in his own home. This is because it is forbidden to eat two meals on Pesach night, lest it appear that one is eating from two Pesach offerings. Rav Amram is discussing one who first conducts the Seder in other homes. According to this explanation, however, his statement that the person will be unable to drink the cup of Birkat Hamazon in his own home is not precise.
It appears that the Ra'avad holds there is no prohibition against eating twice and reciting Birkat Hamazon for them because it appears that one is eating from two Pesach offerings. The afikoman is eaten in place of the Pesach offering, and one eats it only once. Rather, the concern is that he will be unable to drink the fourth cup, over which Hallel is recited, because he would thereby add to the prescribed cups. Once he recites Birkat Hamazon a second time, he will already have four cups. Rav Amram therefore means that since he will recite Birkat Hamazon here over a cup, how can he later drink the cup of Birkat Hamazon in his own home? It would then constitute the fourth cup, leaving no cup for Hallel, which belongs over the fourth cup. This explanation is correct.
When the Tur later writes, “Rav Kohen Tzedek likewise says,” he means that Rav Kohen Tzedek agrees with the Ra'avad concerning the principal ruling, that there is no institution to recite Birkat Hamazon twice, although he gives a different reason. According to Rav Kohen Tzedek, it is forbidden to eat twice that night. Once the person has recited Birkat Hamazon the first time, his initial eating of matzah is concluded. Although he did not eat afikoman, the kezayis of matzah he already ate counts toward his obligation, as stated above in siman 477 in the name of Avi Ezri and the Ittur. What, then, is gained by eating a second kezayis of matzah later as afikoman, since he has already fulfilled his obligation? The Ra'avad does not give Rav Kohen Tzedek's reason, because when the person recites the first Birkat Hamazon he intends not to rely upon the first kezayis of matzah, but to return and eat again, and the later eating will constitute afikoman. The Ra'avad therefore gives the reason that he may not drink after four cups.
All this applies only when he recites Birkat Hamazon twice, whether according to the Ra'avad's reason or Rav Kohen Tzedek's reason. If he interrupts his meal, goes to another house, eats afikoman there, and recites Birkat Hamazon there, there is no prohibition at all, even according to Rav Kohen Tzedek, because this is certainly regarded as one continuous eating, as stated in siman 484 and as I wrote in siman 479.
The Tur further writes that perhaps one may even recite Birkat Hamazon to discharge them, since it is obligatory, just as with dipping the vegetable. The Beit Yosef understands that the Tur wrote this to explain the Rif. This is not so. Rather, it is the Tur's own view, at least in opposition to the Rosh, Rav Kohen Tzedek, and the Ra'avad, who recognized no such institution. If so, why not say that the Rif likewise does not hold this view, since his words, “He recites Birkat Hamazon for them,” may mean that they, and not he, recite it? The reason the other authorities did not agree with the Tur is explained by the Beit Yosef in the name of the Abudraham, who cites the Yerushalmi: Regarding every mitzvah from which a person is exempt, he may discharge the public, except for Birkat Hamazon, because Scripture states, “You shall eat and be satisfied,” teaching that the one who ate must recite the blessing. The Beit Yosef rejects this proof, reasoning that since Birkat Hamazon is obligatory on this night, it resembles the blessing of Hamotzi, through which one may discharge others on this night even though he does not partake.
There appears, however, to be another reason why the Rif, the Rosh, and the other Geonim did not agree with the Tur. There is a fundamental difference between Birkat Hamazon and the other blessings required on this night. One person may recite a blessing for another only when the blessing prepares for a mitzvah that the other person will subsequently perform. Thus, with Hamotzi, eating matzah, and dipping the vegetable, one person may properly recite the blessing and the other then performs the associated act. Birkat Hamazon, however, does not prepare for a mitzvah. The person has already eaten, and is now obligated to offer praise and thanks to Hashem for what he has already received. Another person cannot offer that praise on his behalf when no mitzvah will subsequently be performed through the blessing.
This is exactly comparable to the blessing recited upon seeing Jewish kings. Could one imagine that after a person has already recited the blessing upon seeing the king, another person who does not know the blessing may require him to recite it again on the second person's behalf? Certainly not. No proof is needed to reject such an idea. The same applies here. Since the person who is not proficient does not perform any mitzvah after the proficient person recites Birkat Hamazon, the latter's blessing would certainly be in vain.
It appears that the Tur himself was sensitive to this difficulty. He writes that since the Sages instituted four cups and designated one of them for Birkat Hamazon, it is obligatory like the dipping of the vegetable. He thereby attempts to answer the difficulty by identifying a mitzvah performed after Birkat Hamazon, namely, drinking the fourth cup. He does not base his ruling merely on the obligation to recite Birkat Hamazon for what was eaten. This proposed answer, however, is not valid. Birkat Hamazon has no inherent connection to the cup that will be drunk afterward. It is praise for the meal that has already been eaten. Only the blessing of Borei Pri HaGafen relates to what will be drunk afterward. The Sages merely arranged that the third cup be associated with Birkat Hamazon as part of the count of four cups. Birkat Hamazon was not instituted for the cup, unlike Hamotzi and the blessing over the vegetable, which are recited for the foods that follow. This matter appears clear to me, and I am astonished that our master the Tur did not address it, for it is reasonable and compelling.
