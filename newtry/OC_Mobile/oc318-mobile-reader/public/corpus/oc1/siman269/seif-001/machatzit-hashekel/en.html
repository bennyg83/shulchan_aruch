Seif katan 1, "he gives it to a minor to taste," etc. The positive commandment is fulfilled by Kiddush itself, even without wine, while the prohibition against eating before Kiddush is rabbinic. "Since some say that even an adult," etc. The words "that he fulfills his obligation with this Kiddush" refer to the Sar of Coucy cited by Mordechai. These 3 words, however, are found neither in Mordechai nor in Beit Yosef, who copied his words. The substance of Mordechai's statement is this. Rav said that those who recited Kiddush in the synagogue fulfilled the obligation of Kiddush but did not fulfill the obligation concerning the wine, meaning that when they return home and drink there they must recite Borei Pri HaGafen again because a change of place constitutes an interruption. Shmuel said that they did not even fulfill the obligation of Kiddush, because Kiddush is valid only in the place of the meal. Rav's statement that they did not fulfill the obligation concerning the wine, and Shmuel's statement that they did not even fulfill Kiddush, imply all the more that they did not fulfill the obligation concerning the wine. If they had not drunk from the cup in the synagogue at all, this would be obvious, because how could the earlier blessing exempt wine drunk only later after an interruption? We must therefore be speaking of a case in which they drank from the cup in the synagogue. According to Shmuel, how were they permitted to drink there if Kiddush is valid only in the place of the meal? It follows that drinking before valid Kiddush is nevertheless permitted according to this view. Thus the statement does not mean that they fulfilled Kiddush in the synagogue, for Shmuel explicitly says that they did not. Rather, it means that the Kiddush permits drinking, which is more lenient than eating. I also found that Eliyah Rabbah cites Rashba as permitting drinking before Kiddush, holding that only eating was prohibited before Kiddush. Bach writes that the wine should be given specifically to children who have not yet reached the age of education. This appears to assume that one may directly feed such a child something prohibited. Rama below, siman 343, does not rule that way. Ran, in responsum 82 in the name of Rashba, writes that if the wine is given to a child who has not reached the age of education the blessing is in vain, because he is not yet subject to education in blessings. Even where the custom is to give wine to the infant at a brit milah, this is because the blessing is being recited for the infant's own mitzvah. Kiddush is different. Therefore the wine should be given to a child who has reached the age of education. According to what Beit Yosef writes in siman 343 in the name of Rashba and Ran, however, regarding anything needed for a child, such as eating and drinking, one may give it to him directly, because the Sages did not decree in such a case. Even on Yom Kippur it is a mitzvah to feed a child, and we do not educate him even for short periods when the food is needed for his development, as explained in siman 616. There is also a distinction between feeding something intrinsically forbidden and feeding something permitted whose prohibition is caused only by the time. Tosafos make this distinction in Pesachim 106b. All the more so is there room to feed a child on Shabbos morning before Kiddush, for some authorities permit even an adult to eat then, as Raavad writes. "And there is a distinction," etc. If food is permitted because it is part of the child's normal development, why is it forbidden to feed him neveilah directly, since that too is food? The distinction is that neveilah is intrinsically forbidden. Even though eating is part of the child's development, it remains forbidden to feed him such food. Wine before Kiddush is different. The wine itself is permitted; only the time creates the prohibition because Kiddush has not yet been recited. They were lenient regarding such a lighter prohibition when it concerns the child's normal needs. "As Tosafos write in Pesachim," etc. Rabbi Yirmiyah bar Abba once came to Rav Asi's house, forgot, and tasted food before Havdalah. He nevertheless recited Havdalah afterward, because he held that one who had tasted may still recite Havdalah, and this is how we rule below in siman 299, seif 5. Tosafos ask that we say that even the animals of the righteous are not brought by God to stumble through forbidden food, all the more so the righteous themselves. Why, then, was he not protected from tasting before Havdalah? They answer that the food itself was permitted and only the time prohibited eating it. In such a case we do not apply that principle. This explicitly supports Magen Avraham's reasoning that a time-generated prohibition is lighter than an intrinsic prohibition. Raavad writes in chapter 29 of Hilchot Shabbos that tasting was prohibited specifically before the nighttime Kiddush, for the principal mitzvah is to remember Shabbos at its entrance. Daytime Kiddush is only supported by an asmachta. Thus there is an additional basis for leniency in the morning. "It appears to me that it is forbidden," etc. Meaning that since, by strict law, feeding the child is permitted, one should not impose a stringency upon children. Yet one must consider the concern that a child may become accustomed to the practice. Eruvin 40b discusses Shehecheyanu on Yom Kippur. It cannot be recited over a cup and drunk by the adult because he has accepted the fast; nor should the cup be given to a child, because he may become accustomed to drinking from such a cup even when he grows older. If so, why are we not similarly concerned when a child eats before Kiddush? "For something that is for his benefit," etc. The answer is that there is a distinction between something that merely constitutes the child's normal nourishment and something that is actually being done for the child's own benefit. Giving the Yom Kippur cup to a child would be for the adult's benefit, to avoid a blessing without tasting, so there we are concerned that he may become accustomed to it. Feeding a child before Kiddush, or feeding him on Yom Kippur when he needs food, is done for the child's own benefit, and there the Sages did not impose this concern. "And they give him to taste," etc. One must still explain why we do not fear that he will become accustomed to drinking Kiddush wine before Kiddush. Rashba writes that wherever the circumstance is not fixed, the Sages did not impose this decree. Sometimes guests are present and there is no need to give the cup to a child. Tosafos similarly write in Shabbos 139a. Hagahot Maimoniyot writes that if there is no child in the synagogue, an adult may drink the Kiddush and Havdalah wine. In Kiddush, it appears that he should drink the requisite measure, as stated in siman 273, seif 5, and Bach writes likewise. The one reciting Kiddush should intend that this Kiddush also serve for himself at home, since some authorities hold that he fulfills his obligation with it. Nevertheless, he may recite Kiddush again at home in order to discharge the members of his household, as stated in siman 273. "Where they eat and drink." This means in a house adjoining the synagogue, as stated in siman 273, seif 6. Eating inside the synagogue itself is forbidden, as stated in siman 151. "It is better to establish the practice." Rashba writes in responsum 37 that once the enactment of synagogue Kiddush was established, one should even seek wine for it, because it became a fixed communal enactment. In our countries as well, the privilege of supplying wine to the synagogue is purchased for money as a mitzvah honor. The custom should not be abolished, for many Geonim established it, as Rashba writes, and Taanis 24 also implies this. Tosafos write, regarding the Yerushalmi, that if one makes Kiddush in 1 place on condition that he will eat in another, this can be considered Kiddush in the place of the meal. Although our Talmud indicates that such a condition does not help, Tosafos explain that the Yerushalmi refers to 2 rooms in the same house, whereas our Talmud concerns going from 1 house to another. Ran, however, understands the Yerushalmi as permitting even from house to house, and therefore holds that the Yerushalmi disagrees with our Talmud and that we follow our Talmud. Even so, Ran's reading of the Yerushalmi can be combined as a supporting consideration with the fact that the person drank a revi'it. "And nevertheless he may," etc. One should not say that because he has already fulfilled his own obligation he can no longer discharge his household. Kiddush is different, and one who has already fulfilled Kiddush may nevertheless recite it for others, as explained below in siman 273.
