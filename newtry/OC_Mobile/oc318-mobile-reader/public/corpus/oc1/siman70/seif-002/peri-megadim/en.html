Know that in seif 4 the Mechaber writes, 'If he was occupied with communal needs and the time arrived,' while in seif 5 he writes, 'If he was engaged,' without saying that the time arrived. One could explain that seif 5 refers back to the preceding case, and only where he began permissibly does he not interrupt for these five activities. It would follow that if he began communal needs impermissibly, he must also interrupt, since he began unlawfully. See Kesef Mishneh and Beit Yosef here. Magen Avraham, seif katan 6, rejected this. Taz challenged Beit Yosef's second answer, because Rambam certainly maintains that regarding these five activities, even if one began impermissibly he need not interrupt as long as sufficient time remains. Rambam's commentary to the Mishnah explains that 'one interrupts for Krias Shema but not for prayer' is an independent statement applying to Torah study, whereas in the first clause one does not interrupt even for Krias Shema. This is unlike Rashi, who reads 'and one interrupts' with a conjunction and explains that Torah study is different because both this and that are study, so it is preferable that he recite Krias Shema and thereby fulfill Torah study. The Mishnah must concern one who began impermissibly, since the Gemara challenges it from lulav, where the case is certainly one who began impermissibly. Yet Rambam explains in the Mishnah that with a legal proceeding and similar activities one does not interrupt, contrary to Beit Yosef's second answer. Ran writes that if one began permissibly he does not interrupt, while the Mishnah in Shabbos concerns one who began impermissibly and teaches that one interrupts for Krias Shema, with the conjunction, referring also to the first clause. We discussed this at length in our novellae to Shabbos 9b and 11a. Beit Yosef also wrote that Kesef Mishneh implies that Rambam's statement, 'If he interrupted, he is praiseworthy,' follows Rabbi Zeira's statement in Sukkah. Yet there the lulav case is one who began impermissibly, and at the conclusion the case is where insufficient time remains, as Taz later wrote. Therefore Taz concluded that there is indeed an answer based on distinguishing whether sufficient time remains. The answer that Torah study is D'Rabbanan is certainly rejected, and there is no distinction. Rambam holds that even in Torah study, if one began impermissibly, he need not interrupt. For this reason one who does interrupt is praiseworthy, since one might otherwise have thought that a distinction exists between a Torah-level obligation and a Rabbinic obligation.
Nevertheless, the Mechaber's wording in seif 4, 'with communal needs, and the time arrived,' implies that if he began impermissibly he interrupts. Yet Kesef Mishneh wrote that this ruling was learned from intercalating the month, while Beit Yosef wrote that it is learned from the Yerushalmi, which compares communal needs to Torah study undertaken for the public. Regarding public Torah study it appears from chapter 9 and siman 106 that even if one began impermissibly he does not interrupt. In Shabbos 11a the Gemara challenges a baraisa: Was it not taught that just as one does not interrupt for prayer, so too one does not interrupt for Krias Shema? It answers that the baraisa concerns intercalating the year. Why did the Gemara not answer that one case concerns beginning permissibly? Evidently, the phrase 'just as for prayer' refers to one who began impermissibly, and the answer is that for intercalating the year one does not interrupt even when one began impermissibly. Why, then, did the Mechaber write 'and the time arrived'? Kesef Mishneh in chapter 2 of Hilchos Krias Shema, halachah 6, also writes that the statement refers back to the preceding case. If so, this is certainly true of communal needs, and the difficulty remains as stated.
