If he was engaged in communal needs, etc.—see Magen Avraham note 4, what Kesef Mishneh wrote in the name of Rambam, and consult Kesef Mishneh where he concludes regarding Rambam's words, and specifically in earlier generations his reasoning does not apply; and ostensibly why did Magen Avraham reject it outright—surely Magen Avraham in siman 38 proved from Rashi that if his main intent is only to profit he is not called one engaged in a mitzvah? And one could say that from Rambam's words it implies that in our generations we do not attribute his intent to Heaven's sake even when he does not profit from it—and on this Kesef Mishneh wondered; but if he profits from it and we know his main intent is only to profit, one must examine extensively according to Magen Avraham cited above whether he is called one engaged in a mitzvah to be exempt from Keriat Shema; and perhaps here it speaks when there is no one who will exert himself except him, which is preferable more so—and requires further study; and see above siman 38 in Biur Halachah where we proved that if his intent is for both of them he is called one engaged in a mitzvah.

If he was engaged, etc., and according to Ra'avad he interrupts, etc.—see Magen Avraham, who wrote that it speaks when he began after the half hour before amud hashachar arrived, for then it is forbidden to do any of these things according to Ra'avad, and the matter began in prohibition; therefore he interrupts in all these cases, for in a matter of Torah law, wherever he began in prohibition—even if he began [when permitted] he stops; and Rambam's reason is that he holds they did not decree on all these except near Minchah because that is common, as below siman 89 seif 7; therefore if the matter began in permission he does not interrupt; and likewise in Biur HaGra that this is Rambam's reason; and see Machatzis HaShekel and Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger, for it is proven from their words that if he began any of these after amud hashachar arrived, all agree he interrupts, for behold he began in prohibition—not as implied from Levushim of Sarad; and likewise accordingly, if he began any of these in the evening near the time of Keriat Shema of Maariv he must interrupt even according to Rambam; and some later authorities wrote that according to Rambam in all cases he need not interrupt after the fact once he already began if after he finishes there will remain time for him; and for practical halachah we have no practical difference here, for the agreement of most later authorities is as I wrote in Mishna Berurah note 23.
And what I wrote further in Mishna Berurah—that if he was engaged in melacha before bein hashmashos it is called that the matter began in permission—is found in siman 89 in later authorities that melacha is permitted until bein hashmashos; and likewise explicitly in Rambam chapter 6 of Hilchos Tefillah halachah 4; and I saw in the siddur of the Gaon of Lisa among matters forbidden before prayer at the end of note 4 that his words imply that ordinary melacha besides bath, haircut, and barber-surgery is like eating, which is forbidden half an hour before bein hashmashos [and what he wrote there note 5, "if he began doing melacha from melachos," certainly refers to haircutting and entering a bath, and the like, which the preceding note discusses so his words should not contradict each other—see that entry]—from Rambam and the other later authorities cited above it does not appear so at first glance. And what Darkei Chayyim wrote further at the end of note 6—that if he began after half an hour his law is like one who began in prohibition—does not apply to eating, for there even if he began when permitted he must interrupt; and barber-surgery and haircutting are certainly permitted before bein hashmashos; therefore it refers to other melachos and those similar in this to eating, which are forbidden half an hour before bein hashmashos; and it follows his approach at the end of note 4; and for practical halachah this requires further study, as I wrote.
