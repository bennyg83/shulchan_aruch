If he was occupied with eating, etc. It must refer to one who began eating impermissibly. If he began permissibly, the ruling in siman 235, seif 2, is that he need not interrupt. The case therefore concerns one who began impermissibly, yet according to Rambam he need not interrupt, because the Sages did not enact a decree regarding something uncommon, namely beginning a meal close to dawn, as explained in siman 89, seif 7. This proves that Rambam holds that even for prayer he need not interrupt. Were he required to pray, he would also have to recite Krias Shema in order to juxtapose redemption to prayer. Rather, he certainly need not interrupt. The Tur therefore writes in siman 89 in Rambam's name that he need not interrupt. The Beit Yosef writes there that this is a scribal error in the Tur, but in my opinion it is correct as I have explained. Although one must interrupt for lulav, as stated in siman 652, lulav differs because its time extends throughout the day, and eating and drinking during the day are common. This is unlike Krias Shema. The Bach rules that Rambam discusses one who began permissibly, while the Raavad discusses one who began impermissibly, and they do not disagree. This is how one should rule.
