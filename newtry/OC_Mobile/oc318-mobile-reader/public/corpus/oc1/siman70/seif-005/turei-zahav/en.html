The passage in Sukkah reads thus: one who came on the road and did not have a lulav in his hand — when he enters his house he takes it on his table — meaning if he forgot and did not take before eating he must interrupt the meal and take the lulav; if he did not take in the morning he takes between the evenings for the entire day is valid for lulav; and they challenge: you said he takes it on his table — to say they interrupt; and they raise a contradiction: if they began they do not interrupt — Rashi explained regarding Minchah prayer: we learned chapter 1 of Shabbat one should not enter to eat near Minchah until he prays; and if they began before Minchah and their meal extended and the time of Minchah arrived they need not interrupt their meal and pray — end quote. R' Safra — there is no difficulty: this where there is time in the day, this where there is no time in the day. R' Zeira — what is the difficulty — perhaps both where there is time; and perhaps this Torah law this rabbinic; rather Rava said: if there is difficulty, this is the difficulty — why did R' Safra need to answer; we learned 'when he enters his house he takes it on his table' — evidently he interrupts; and then it teaches if he did not take in the morning he takes between the evenings — evidently he need not interrupt; R' Safra — there is no difficulty: this where there is time, etc. R' Zeira — what is the difficulty — perhaps a mitzvah to interrupt; and if he does not stop he takes between the evenings; rather R' Zeira said: actually as we said initially — R' Safra needed to answer because of the difficulty from the outset; and if it is difficult for you this Torah this rabbinic — here on the second day of Yom Tov there are two rabbinic matters; and it is also precise from what it teaches 'one who came on the road' — for if you thought on the first day of Yom Tov — who would permit. and it appears to my humble opinion Rambam does not hold at all that there is any stringency in Torah matters in laws of interruption beyond rabbinic per R' Zeira's conclusion; and my suggestion is thus: whenever we have a difficulty and there are two resolutions — one is to distinguish Torah from rabbinic and the second is to distinguish there is time or not — certainly we prefer to distinguish Torah from rabbinic for this distinction the tanna need not remember for it is known by itself; unlike distinguishing there is time which is an external distinction the tanna did not mention at all — therefore it is strained; therefore initially we could have resolved Sukkah's difficulty on that of Shabbat and distinguished Torah from rabbinic — then the resolution of time or not would be void and have no place; unlike at the end where the main difficulty is from the second day of Sukkot which is also rabbinic on that of Shabbat in prayer — with this we are compelled to distinguish there is time or not — then the distinction of Torah to rabbinic is void — why distinguish gratuitously since there is no necessity, for the tanna of Sukkah stated his law even on the second day of Yom Tov as an inclusion that even in rabbinic matters he stops since there is no time; but in truth where there is time it is permitted even on the first day of Yom Tov which is Torah law — why stop as long as there is still time; therefore also in that of Shabbat that explains they do not interrupt for Keriat Shema since there is time — so are Rambam's words here; only he added that nevertheless one who fears and acts beyond the letter of the law and stops in Torah matters — behold this is praiseworthy; and Kesef Mishneh explained Rambam's words in what he wrote 'behold this is praiseworthy' — it is according to R' Zeira who said a mitzvah to interrupt, etc.; for if the mishnah where there is no time how is it appropriate to say merely a mitzvah to interrupt — behold it is an obligation since the mitzvah would be nullified; rather certainly the mishnah where there is time and therefore there is no obligation to interrupt but merely an optional mitzvah alone — and thus Rambam wrote 'behold this is praiseworthy.' And I wondered at the sight if these words came from a holy mouth — for in the conclusion mishnah Sukkah stands where there is no time — how could Rambam dispute the Talmud's conclusion; and further if mishnah Sukkah that stops at the meal is only for an optional mitzvah alone not a full law — the Talmud should have answered thus and there would be no difficulty at all from Shabbat that we learned 'if they began they do not interrupt' — there it states law; rather it is clear that R' Zeira certainly did not explain mishnah Sukkah as 'a mitzvah to interrupt,' etc.; rather all this is because we do not resolve mishnayot with the distinction there is time but Torah or rabbinic — automatically mishnayot deal where there is time; and the first part says a mitzvah to interrupt; but in the conclusion perforce mishnayot deal where there is no time — if so R' Zeira's explanation is rejected; and what he said 'a mitzvah to interrupt' — rather the first part deals where there is no time and the end where there is time.
