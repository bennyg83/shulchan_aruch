He finishes and afterward reads Kriat Shema—these are the words of the Rambam in chapter 2 of Hilchot Kriat Shema; and the Ra'avad challenged him as follows: Not so; rather, if he is ill he stops and reads, even though there is time remaining to read, because it is from the Torah; and if there is no time remaining, even for prayer he stops. And there is support for this matter in Sukkah, chapter A Stolen Lulav and a hint as well in chapter 40 of Berachos—thus far. The passage in Sukkah reads thus: One who was on the road and did not have a lulav in his hand—when he enters his house he should take it upon his table; that is, if he forgot and did not take it before eating, he must interrupt the meal and take the lulav; if he did not take it in the morning, he should take it between the evenings, for all day is fit for lulav. And they challenged: You said he takes it upon his table—to say that they stop; and they raised a contradiction: if they began, they do not stop. Rashi explained regarding the Minchah prayer: we learned in chapter 1 of Shabbat, one should not enter to eat near Minchah until he prays; and if they began before Minchah and their meal extended and the time of Minchah arrived, they need not interrupt their meal and pray—thus far. R' Safra answered: this [mishnah] where there is time remaining by day; this where there is no time remaining by day. R' Zeira said: what is the difficulty that R' Safra needed to answer? Perhaps both are where there is time remaining, and perhaps this is Torah and this is rabbinic. Rather Rava said: if there is a difficulty, this is the difficulty—that R' Safra needed to answer: we taught, when he enters his house he takes it upon his table—apparently he stops; and then it teaches, if he did not take it in the morning he takes it between the evenings—apparently he need not stop. R' Safra answered: this where there is time remaining, etc. R' Zeira said: what is the difficulty? Perhaps it is a mitzvah to stop, and if he does not stop he takes it between the evenings. Rather R' Zeira said: always as we said initially—R' Safra needed to answer because of the initial difficulty; and what challenged you, this is Torah and this is rabbinic? Here we deal with the second day of Yom Tov, which is rabbinic; this is also precise from what it teaches, one who was on the road—for if you would think the first day of Yom Tov, who would permit? And the Ra'avad holds that per the answer that this is Torah we rule—for R' Zeira concludes that here we deal with the second day of Yom Tov; and therefore R' Safra concluded: this where there is no time remaining; this where there is time remaining—he does not stop; but in Torah matters, even though there is time remaining, he stops. Therefore he challenged: for Kriat Shema is from the Torah; and therefore he wrote, Not so, rather he stops. And the Beit Yosef corrected the words of the Rambam in two ways: first, there is no need to contradict him to all viewers, uprooting the words of the Talmud from their implication; and the second way is per the Ran, that whoever began before his time of obligation—even in Torah matters he does not stop if there is time remaining; and this is evident from the language of the Rambam, for he wrote before this: he was engaged in Torah study and the time of Kriat Shema arrived, etc.; he was engaged in eating, etc.; and likewise what R' Zeira said, perhaps it is a mitzvah to stop—it is a law; therefore the Rambam wrote, if he is afraid, etc.; and likewise in Kesef Mishnah; and he concluded there to answer that what we learned in chapter 1 of Shabbat, they stop for Kriat Shema—is specifically according to Rabbeinu Tam, as they established it in the Gemara. And the Rambam holds that eating, judgment, and porridge do not stop at all, even for Kriat Shema, since there is time remaining; and likewise the Rambam in his explanation of the mishnah—thus far. And his words are very astonishing: how did he write that the main permission is in the mishnah of Shabbat that we learned, if they began they do not stop, neither for Kriat Shema nor for prayer—which deals only with one who began in permissibility before his time of obligation; for otherwise he would be obligated in Kriat Shema, which is from the Torah; and according to this view, the words of the Rambam would be as here; and if so, why does R' Safra need to answer this, that there is time remaining, etc.—let it emerge even if both are where there is time remaining? And there is a practical difference that the mishnah of Shabbat certainly deals with beginning in permissibility as stated; and the mishnah of lulav certainly deals with beginning in prohibition—for by day he sits at the meal; and since he did not answer thus, if so, necessarily also that mishnah of Shabbat that we learned, and if they began they do not stop, deals even in prohibition; and likewise the Ran himself explicitly so in both. And in both they conclude to distinguish between whether there is time remaining or not—and that is in rabbinic matters; but in Torah matters he stops even where there is time remaining, for that mishnah of lulav is on the first day of Yom Tov. And the Ra'avad's challenge returns to its place: why does he not stop for Kriat Shema? Therefore I say that the words of the great rabbi the Beit Yosef were written not with sharp investigation but in passing winter commentary—for the Ran, who distinguishes between beginning in permissibility or not, holds that the mishnah of Shabbat, they stop for Kriat Shema means also from within eating and judgment, since it is from the Torah, as with one engaged in Torah study; and not like the Rambam's explanation of the mishnah here, that in the mishnah they did not say they stop for Kriat Shema except regarding Torah study alone. And how did the Beit Yosef wish to reconcile their views? And it seemed to me that the Rambam does not hold at all that there is any stringency in Torah matters in the laws of interruption beyond what there is in rabbinic matters, according to R' Zeira's conclusion; and the suggestion is thus: whenever we have a difficulty before us and there are two answers—one is to distinguish between Torah and rabbinic, and the second is to distinguish between whether there is time remaining or not—certainly we prefer to distinguish between Torah and rabbinic, for this distinction the tanna need not remember at all, since it is known by itself; unlike the distinction between whether there is time remaining, which is an external distinction that the tanna did not mention at all; and therefore it is strained. Therefore initially, when we could have answered the difficulty of Sukkah on that of Shabbat and distinguished between Torah and rabbinic, the answer of whether there is time remaining or not would have been void and would have no place; unlike at the end, where the main difficulty is the second day of Sukkot, which is also rabbinic, on that mishnah of Shabbat regarding prayer—and in this we are compelled to distinguish between whether there is time remaining or not; then the distinction of Torah to rabbinic is void—for why should we divide gratuitously, since there is no necessity there, for the tanna of Sukkah who cited his law even on the second day of Yom Tov—for amplification, that even in rabbinic matters he stops when there is no time remaining; but in truth, where there is time remaining it is permitted even on the first day of Yom Tov, which is from the Torah—for why should he stop as long as there is still time remaining? Therefore also in that mishnah of Shabbat that he explained, they do not stop for Kriat Shema when there is time remaining—and such are the words of the Rambam here; except that he added that nevertheless, one who is afraid and acts beyond the letter of the law and stops in Torah matters—he is praiseworthy. And Kesef Mishnah explained the words of the Rambam in what he wrote he is praiseworthy—it is according to R' Zeira, who said it is a mitzvah to stop, etc.; for if the mishnah deals with where there is no time remaining, how is it relevant to say a mere mitzvah to stop—and is it not an obligation, since the mitzvah will be nullified? Rather certainly the mishnah deals with where there is time remaining; and therefore there is no interruption but a mere mitzvah alone—and this is what the Rambam said, "he is praiseworthy." And I was astonished at the appearance whether these words came from a holy mouth—for in the conclusion the mishnah of Sukkah stands where there is no time remaining; and how can the Rambam divide against the Talmud's conclusion? And further, if the mishnah of Sukkah that deals with stopping at a meal is only a mere mitzvah alone and not a full law, the Talmud should have answered thus, that it does not challenge at all from Shabbat that we learned, and if they began they do not stop—for there it states law. Rather it is a clear matter that R' Zeira certainly did not explain the mishnah of Sukkah as a mere mitzvah to stop, etc.; but all this is because mishnayos are not reconciled with the distinction of whether there is time remaining, but only with Torah or rabbinic—automatically the mishnayos deal with where there is time remaining; and the beginning states it is a mitzvah to stop, but in the conclusion it certainly deals with mishnayos where there is no time remaining. If so, R' Zeira's explanation is rejected; and what he said, it is a mitzvah to stop—only the beginning deals with where there is no time remaining, and the end with where there is time remaining. And according to what we said, it is fine that the Rambam did not come here to rule like R' Zeira, who said it is a mitzvah to stop—for if so he would not say he is praiseworthy but should have said "it is a mitzvah"; rather his intent is that from the words of the Talmud there is no necessity to be stringent in Torah matters as he wrote; but nevertheless, one who wishes to act beyond the letter of the law—he is praiseworthy, since in any case at the beginning of the passage it arose in mind to be stringent; and this is learned from what is written in many places, all day is fit—but the zealous precede to the mitzvah. This is what I wrote to settle these words of the Rambam; but for halacha it seemed to me per the Ra'avad, since the Rosh also wrote in Sukkah as his words; and Rashi wrote in chapter 1 of Shabbat on they stop for Kriat Shema—they stop from eating and from judgment, since Kriat Shema is from the Torah; and this is even though there is time remaining. And he answered from that of Eruvin 38a, according to R' Yehuda, that they do not stop from eating for Shabbat to sanctify the day, even though it is from the Torah—as the Ran wrote here, for there finishing eating is also for the need of Shabbat, as Tosafos wrote there; for otherwise it would be kiddush not in the place of a meal—as I explained here.
