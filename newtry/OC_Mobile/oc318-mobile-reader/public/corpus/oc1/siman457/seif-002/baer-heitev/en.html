He should not call it by that name. Rather, he should call it kuchen in the vernacular. See Maharil. It appears that the concern applies only when the owner names it as challah. If another person calls it that, there is no concern, since that person has no authority to separate terumah. See Magen Avraham.

To bake it. For it is not fit to be eaten.

To burn it. Sacred food may not be burned on Yom Tov.

In cold water. Magen Avraham writes that it requires study whether one may rely on this today, since even cold water that stands in the house for a short time becomes warm. Chok Yaakov permits it without hesitation. Pri Chadash concludes that if the water becomes somewhat warm, it should be replaced. See Yoreh Deah, siman 36.

A minor. One younger than nine years. See Darkei Moshe and Mahari Weil. Magen Avraham explains that we presume he did not experience a seminal emission, but if it is known that he did, even a one-day-old infant becomes impure through it. Chok Yaakov notes that he overlooked the Gemara at the beginning of Niddah, chapter Benot Kutim, and the Rambam, Hilchot Avot HaTumah 9, who rule explicitly that a boy under nine does not contract such impurity even if he lay beside a woman and experienced an emission.

Who immersed. It appears that if he afterward urinates and the water is divided or cloudy, he is impure. He should therefore eat immediately after immersion and not urinate in the interim. See siman 606 and Magen Avraham.

A kohen. We do not establish him as certainly a kohen, since perhaps his priestly status was disqualified. See Maharshal and Maharil. Shach, Yoreh Deah 332, writes that on Pesach the custom is to give it to a minor kohen, unlike during the rest of the year; see Magen Avraham for the reason. In these countries I have seen the practice to burn the challah in the kneading room on Erev Pesach or Chol HaMoed. On Yom Tov one should not designate it as challah until after baking, which is the preferable course. See Chok Yaakov.

Less. Taz writes that he should then not knead any further dough. If he kneads another dough smaller than the required measure, there is concern that the doughs may later be placed together in one vessel, thereby joining and becoming obligated, and he may eat untithed dough. Chok Yaakov writes that this is a remote concern.
