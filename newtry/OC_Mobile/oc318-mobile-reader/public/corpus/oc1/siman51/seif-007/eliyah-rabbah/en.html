(6) He must repeat, etc. This somewhat implies that he need repeat only the opening verse, unlike the implication of Levush. Rabbeinu Yonah's wording, cited by Beit Yosef as the source of this law, may also be interpreted this way. This requires study. This does not resemble the first verse of Krias Shema, where the verse says vehayu and reciting out of order is invalid, as will be explained in siman 63, seif 4. See also the beginning of siman 101.
(7) Because it is, etc., the end, etc. Maharil explains that from the verse “At the morning watch” through “Hashem shall reign” there are eighteen mentions of the four-letter Divine Name. Four times eighteen equals seventy-two letters, corresponding to the seventy-two-letter Name. In Pesukei DeZimrah we begin from Vayosha, omitting four Names that precede it. We therefore repeat “Hashem shall reign” and add the verse “For kingship belongs to Hashem,” completing the count. The fifth added verse is not included in the count, but explains “On that day Hashem will be One.” The writings of the Arizal say it is good to recite with the Song the verse “For Pharaoh's horse came,” etc. Levush writes that in Mi Chamocha Ne'edar the kaf of chamocha is strong. Otherwise, it could sound, Heaven forbid, like “Hashem is Michah,” referring to the man who stole the Name and made the calf, as alluded to in the verse, “Distress passed through the sea.” One may ask why he specifies the kaf in the second Mi Chamocha and not the first. The answer is that the first is not directly adjacent to Hashem's Name. Likewise, in “All my bones shall say: Hashem, who is like You,” the kaf is soft, because that verse was not said at the sea. In “They became still as stone,” the kaf is strong so that it not sound, Heaven forbid, like “they compared You to a stone.” In “This people You redeemed,” the gimel is strong. In “For He is highly exalted,” the gimel is also strong. These are exceptions to the general rule that the consonants beged kefes are soft after the letters alef-hei-vav-yod when no disjunctive factor intervenes. Radak writes this in his grammar. Abudraham writes that in yehallelucha selah the samech is strong; in shekachah lo the lamed is strong; in hodo al eretz veshamayim the final vav is pronounced with a cholam; in the list “the Canaanite, the Hittite,” the first three appear without a vav and the final three with a vav. Rabbi Shabsai Sofer's siddur writes that in ya'alezu the first sheva is silent and the second mobile, following the rule of two consecutive shevas. In pifiyos the peh is soft. In malcheihem the kaf is soft, and likewise every occurrence of malcheihem. In gadlu the gimel has a shuruk. In betziltzelei shema the mem has a kamatz because of the esnachta, though the ordinary word shema has a segol. In “Israel our father,” the word our father refers to Israel, meaning Yaakov our father.
(8) When he reaches “And You are our God.” Rama cites Rabbeinu Yonah at the end of the chapter Ein Omdin. I examined that source and found only the phrase “You alone are King, to You we give thanks” in Nishmas. Beit Yosef, Shulchan Aruch, and Levush below in siman 113 likewise mention only that. It must be that Rama understood the same rule to apply here. Lechem Chamudos, page 43, apparently did not see Rama's words here. Shelah writes that he received from his teacher Maharash that one should bow in Aleinu when saying “and we bow and prostrate ourselves,” for otherwise he establishes himself as a denier. The same applies during Selichos where the custom is to say “Come, let us bow and bend” and “Let us bow toward His holy sanctuary.” One cannot challenge this from “Every upright stature shall bow before You,” because there he does not speak about himself, whereas here he says “we shall bow.” As for why one does not bow at “we thank You,” it appears that the language of thanksgiving is different. See Shelah there and Taz, siman 113.
9) And the custom is to stand, etc. The siddur of Mahari Luria states that one should stand from Vayevarech David until 'You are Hashem, the God.' Kavanot HaArizal states that he would stand when the congregation reached 'Hashem reigns, Hashem reigned, Hashem shall reign,' even if he had not yet reached that passage; he would continue reciting the zemiros at his own place. Abudraham writes that these psalms allude to the Ten Utterances. Baruch SheAmar corresponds to 'In the beginning God created.' Mizmor LeTodah corresponds to 'Let there be light,' for all must thank Him for it. Yehi Chevod, which says, 'Hashem established His throne in the heavens,' corresponds to 'Let there be a firmament amid the waters,' for it is the glory of the Omnipresent that He separated His throne above. Ashrei Yoshvei corresponds to 'Let the waters gather,' for otherwise creatures would have no place to sit. Halleli Nafshi, which says, 'Who gives bread to the hungry,' corresponds to the fifth utterance, 'Let the earth sprout,' producing seed. 'Praise Hashem, for it is good to sing, etc.; He counts the number of the stars,' corresponds to 'Let there be luminaries in the firmament.' 'Praise Hashem, etc.; wild beasts and all animals, etc.' corresponds to 'Let the waters teem with a teeming living soul.' 'Sing to Hashem a new song' corresponds to 'Let the earth bring forth each living creature according to its kind.' This is a great kindness, for He does not desire that one species mate with a species not of its kind. 'Praise God in His Sanctuary; every soul, etc.' corresponds to 'Let Us make man.' Vayevarech David, which says, 'You rule over everything,' corresponds to 'Be fruitful and multiply, fill the earth and subdue it,' by which He gave humanity dominion over everything; yet we declare that, nevertheless, You rule over everything.
