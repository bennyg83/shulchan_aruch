(Seif katan 7) And they say, etc. The intention of “But we shall bless” is a prayer that Hashem's promise be fulfilled for us, that we merit the World to Come and bless Him forever.
Therefore, he writes that he said it at Minchah. The responsum states this briefly. Its full explanation is as follows: at Minchah there was no need to conclude with the verse “But we shall bless,” etc., because the reason of joining one Hallelujah to another does not apply, for no Hallelujah psalms are recited at Minchah. At Shacharit, by contrast, after Ashrei one recites psalms that begin and end with Hallelujah, and therefore Ashrei too concludes with a verse that ends with Hallelujah. Nevertheless, at Minchah he would also recite the verse “But we shall bless” after Ashrei. Since before Tehillah LeDavid he recited the verse “Fortunate are,” etc., which ends, “They will yet praise You, Selah,” he had begun with language of praise and therefore concluded with the verse “But we shall bless,” so as to end with language of praise. At Shacharit before Lamnatzeach, however, he did not conclude that Tehillah LeDavid with the verse “But we shall bless,” because he did not precede Tehillah LeDavid there with the verse “Fortunate are”; he did so only at Minchah, as Magen Avraham writes below. The reason the verse “Fortunate are” is added is that one must wait one hour before beginning to pray. Chazal therefore instituted the recitation of one chapter, which lasts for the required waiting period. Since the teaching that one must wait an hour is derived from the verse “Fortunate are those who dwell in Your house,” they instituted reciting “Fortunate are” before Tehillah LeDavid. This reason does not apply at Shacharit, when Tehillah LeDavid is in the middle of the songs of praise, and he therefore did not recite that verse then. The same applies before Lamnatzeach: he did not say “Fortunate are” before Tehillah LeDavid because it is in the middle of the prayer. He consequently did not conclude there with “But we shall bless,” either, because before Lamnatzeach neither reason applies: there is no joining of one Hallelujah to another, and he did not begin with language of praise, since he had not first said “Fortunate are.” See there for further differences. Our custom, however, is as stated: we follow Kol Bo's reason for reciting “But we shall bless,” which applies every time Tehillah LeDavid is recited. We likewise recite “Fortunate are” every time, because no distinction was made.
The second Mi Chamocha. This means the one in the Song at the Sea, namely, “Hashem, who is like You.” Michah's idol crossed the sea with them, and if one pronounced the kaf as rafeh, the phrase would appear, God forbid, to be a blasphemy, as though he were saying “Michah” immediately after Hashem's Name. Regarding the first Mi Chamocha, however (“Who is like You among the mighty”) although it too was said at the sea, it is not immediately adjacent to Hashem's Name, because the word ba'eilim intervenes, and there is therefore no concern. Likewise, regarding “All my bones shall say: Hashem, who is like You,” although it is adjacent to Hashem's Name, that verse was not said at the sea, and there is therefore no concern. It should be pronounced according to its normal rule, with a rafeh kaf, because it follows one of the letters alef, hei, vav, or yod.
The gimel has a dagesh, because if it were rafeh it would sound like a word meaning defiled and repulsive. Taz also writes that the kaf in “they were still as a stone” should have a dagesh, lest it sound like “they compared You.”
