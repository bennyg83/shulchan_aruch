Seif 7, in Rama: And they say the verse, “But we shall bless,” etc. Recently, the distinguished Rabbi Shmerel sent me his work Iyun Tefillah. Upon examining it I saw that he objected to the widespread custom in Pesukei DeZimrah of repeating the word Hallelujah and waiting with the extra repetition for the chazzan. He wondered how this can be permitted, since we may not divide any verse at a place where Moshe did not divide it in the Torah. Heaven forbid that one should challenge a Jewish custom. First, there are many customs that divide verses, as Magen Avraham records. It may be that these authorities hold that the rule applies only to the Torah, but not to the Prophets and Writings. This resembles the view of several poskim that the prohibition against reciting written Torah by heart applies specifically to the Torah. Moreover, the custom here is proper for another reason. Since the preceding psalm concludes with Hallelujah, when he repeats Hallelujah as the opening of the next psalm it may also be heard as a repetition of the final word of the preceding psalm. The prohibition against dividing a verse applies only when the interruption is recognizable. Here the division is not recognizable. One might object that repeating the word itself is forbidden, like one who says “Shema, Shema,” whom we silence. But that depends upon intent. If he actually intends to repeat the same declaration, it is forbidden because it appears to acknowledge two authorities, Heaven forbid. Here, however, his true intention is not repetition. The second Hallelujah begins the following psalm. Thus, in truth there is no repetition. Nor is it clearly a divided verse, since it can be understood as repeating the preceding word. There is therefore no basis to prohibit it. I found support in Beit Yosef, siman 51, who records that some Rishonim would repeat the final verse of each psalm. Others objected because it resembles saying “Shema, Shema.” The present custom appears deliberately designed as a compromise between the views. They repeat only the final word, which is simultaneously the opening of the next psalm. This neither resembles “Shema, Shema” nor clearly divides a verse. Biur Halachah in this siman explains that we say “But we shall bless Hashem” because otherwise there would be no need to connect the Hallelujah opening the following psalm with the preceding passage. His intention appears to be as I have written: without a concluding Hallelujah, one could not attach it upward because of the prohibition against dividing a verse. Since the preceding psalm concludes with Hallelujah, it may be attached upward for the reason stated. Fortunate is Israel, whose custom is Torah. The practice is proper. If they are not prophets, they are descendants of prophets. May my portion be with them in the World to Come. Amen.
