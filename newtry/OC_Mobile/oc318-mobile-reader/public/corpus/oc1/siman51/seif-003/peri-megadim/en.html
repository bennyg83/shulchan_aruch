And they say. See Taz. In Mi Chamocha the kaf is strong, and likewise in ka'even and ga'alta, even though they follow the letters alef-hei-vav-yod without an intervening disjunctive factor. The reasons given explain these exceptions. Sefer Daas Chochmah and Mekor Chochmah, siman 51, write that one should pause between bamayim and adirim. There is a tipcha under bamayim and the end of the verse at adirim, based upon the Gemara's phrase, “Let the mighty One come and exact punishment from the mighty in the mighty waters.” The word adirim refers to the Egyptians. The Midrash likewise says, “The mighty sank like lead in the waters.”
Regarding. For the laws and intention of answering amen, see siman 124, seif 6. I have a small pamphlet bound into my siddur in which I collected scattered laws concerning the synagogue. I shall copy several points here. Amen has two intentions. One is that I believe the blessing is true and certain. The second is a request and prayer that the petition be fulfilled. Thus, in the first three blessings of the Amidah, which are praise, amen means that it is true and established. One should not intend a request that the words be fulfilled, because that could appear, Heaven forbid, as though one doubts that Hashem revives the dead. For a blessing that is solely a request, amen means: may it be His will that the speaker's words be fulfilled. The blessing Atah Chonen begins with praise of Hashem and concludes with the request “Grant us,” etc. One should therefore intend both meanings. Shelah, page 254a; Magen Avraham, siman 124, seif katan 10; and Bach there.
Accordingly. In Kaddish, when the leader says, “May His great Name be exalted and sanctified,” and the congregation answers amen, the meaning is: may it be His will that the speaker's words be fulfilled in our days. Otherwise, it could appear as though one doubts that at the end of days Hashem will certainly be One. Why then do we answer a second time, “Amen, may His great Name,” etc.? The first amen refers to the leader's conclusion, “speedily,” etc. We also wrote there that one must pause between amen and Yehei Shemei Rabbah, and likewise between amen and Modim anachnu lach. Amen responds to the preceding blessing, while the following phrase is a separate matter. See Magen Avraham, siman 56, seif katan 7, and Shelah, pages 254 and 255. Amen should be pronounced with a full kamatz, with a perceptible alef, as though saying “I believe,” in the first person. Even when amen follows a request, this explanation remains appropriate: I believe Hashem will fulfill our requests if we pray wholeheartedly, for Hashem is near to all who call Him in truth. Eliyah Rabbah, siman 124, seif katan 13, writes that it is pronounced with a chataf sheva. Even so, one should not use a small kamatz, but a full kamatz. See Bach and Levush there.
