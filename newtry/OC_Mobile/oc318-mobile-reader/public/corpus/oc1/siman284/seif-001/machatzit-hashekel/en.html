Levush wrote, etc. Regarding the present practice of skipping, etc.: under Talmudic law one may skip within a prophet only so far that the time needed to reach the new place does not exceed the time the translator takes to translate the verse just read. Rashi explains that this protects the congregation's dignity, so they do not stand silently while the scroll is rolled to the new place, as in Megillah 24a.
It is possible to mark, etc. Before beginning the Haftarah, enabling him to find the place quickly without making the congregation wait. In Talmudic times all books were scrolls like a Torah scroll and could not be marked. This also explains why congregations do not write the Prophets as scrolls for Haftarah use: since our practice requires skipping, a scroll is impractical.
And it is stated in Gittin, etc. A difficulty remains: even if all the Prophets cannot be written, they could have written the Haftaros in our customary order, with the skipped portions already placed there, avoiding any need to skip while retaining scroll form. In answer, it is stated in Gittin that a Haftarah collection may not be written.
This follows the law in Yoreh De'ah that one may not write a scroll for a child containing only one or two passages, because the Torah was given complete and must be written in full rather than piecemeal. For the same reason, a Haftarah collection may not be written; all the Prophets must be written in full. Gittin says it is forbidden even to read from Haftaros written in violation of this rule.
For we rule that printing, etc. Since printing is available, the dispensation of 'It is a time to act for Hashem' no longer applies, and writing Haftaros alone reverts to being forbidden. According to this approach, one should not read even from printed Haftaros lacking the complete prophet, since a complete printed prophet is available; what difference is there between print and handwriting? Taz permits reading from our Haftarah collections, and this is the custom. Eliyah Rabbah writes that a Haftarah scroll should have only one roller at its end; at its beginning there should be no roller, only a blank section of parchment for rolling, as is the law for a prophet-scroll in the first chapter of Bava Basra.
Kol Bo likewise wrote, etc. Bach disagrees with Beis Yosef, who emended 'what you asked is impossible' to read 'what you asked is possible.'
Meaning, even, etc. Unlike Beis Yosef's strained explanation. Beis Yosef understood 'even without a scroll' to mean without a Torah scroll (that the Torah had not been read) and writes that this applies only where Torah was read, even from an invalid scroll; if no Torah was read at all, the Haftarah is not recited with blessings, as in the responsum attributed to Ramban. Magen Avraham explains 'without a scroll' to mean without parchment: a Haftarah written on paper may be read with blessings. Rashba did not discuss whether prior Torah reading is required. Accordingly, the law is not explicit where an invalid Torah scroll was read.
In some places, etc. Radbaz and Rabbi Avraham ben Chaim. These responsa are unavailable to me, but Responsa Maharam Padua 78 records that in some places at Yom Kippur Minchah they read three verses of Yonah in Hebrew, translate the entire portion into Greek, then read the three verses from Michah beginning 'Who is a God like You' in Hebrew and translate them into Greek. He concludes that they should not be protested. Although a Haftarah ordinarily requires twenty-one verses, where the custom is to translate it ten verses suffice, as Tur writes, because translated verses combine due to the congregation's burden. Thus ten Hebrew and ten translated verses total twenty; the final verse is repeated in Hebrew after its translation to conclude with Scripture, totaling twenty-one. At Minchah only three people are called and ten Torah verses suffice, as above siman 137; therefore ten Haftarah verses suffice, just as Rema in se'if 5 requires only fifteen on Yom Tov when five are called. Six Hebrew verses (three from Yonah and three from Michah) together with their translations total twelve and suffice. Greek has the law of Hebrew. Although Rambam says the Greek language was lost, see Beis Yosef's comments in Orach Chaim 690. Moreover, he holds that listeners fulfill the obligation in any other language they understand; see his lengthy discussion.
