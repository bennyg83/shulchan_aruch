1) [Seif 1] A haftarah from the Nevi'im relating to the parashah is read, etc. The reason is explained as follows. On one occasion, a persecuting government decreed that the Torah could not be read. The Sages instituted reading from the Nevi'im a passage relating to the parashah. They would read twenty-one verses from the Nevi'im, corresponding to the twenty-one verses that would have been read from the Torah, three for each of the seven people called to the Torah. They also recited seven blessings over the reading from the Nevi'im, corresponding to the seven blessings that would have been recited over the Torah. Even after the persecution ended and the Torah reading resumed, they retained the enactment that the maftir read twenty-one verses from the Nevi'im. He first reads from the Torah out of respect for the Torah. Were he to read only from the Nevi'im and recite a blessing over that reading, the honor of the Torah and the honor of the Nevi'im would appear equal. The maftir therefore reads first from the Torah and then from the Nevi'im. By his reading the Torah first, we see that the Torah is greater than the Nevi'im. The maftir consequently also recites seven blessings corresponding to the seven readers from the Torah. Bach; Taz, paragraph 1.
2) They instituted seven blessings for the maftir, corresponding to the seven people called to the Torah: three before the haftarah and four afterward. Of the three beforehand, two are the blessings over the Torah reading, and one precedes the haftarah, beginning Asher Bachar BiNevi'im Tovim, etc., and opening and concluding with Baruch. Of the four afterward, the first opens and concludes with Baruch. The pause upon reaching al kol devarav emes vatzedek does not indicate the end of a blessing. Only seven blessings were instituted, and if this were the end of a blessing, there would be eight. Rather, as stated in Maseches Sofrim, the custom was to recite words of praise and conciliation there. The second blessing is Rachem, which concludes with Baruch but does not open with Baruch, in accordance with the law of a blessing adjoining another. The same applies to the third, Samecheinu, which concludes, 'Blessed are You, Hashem, Shield of David,' as stated in the chapter Arvei Pesachim, Pesachim 117b: in prayer one says Matzmiach Keren Yeshuah, and in the haftarah, Magen David. The fourth is Al HaTorah and concludes, 'Blessed are You, Hashem, Who sanctifies Shabbos.' Tur. Taz, paragraph 1, cites this and writes that one should therefore not answer amen after emes vatzedek, since this is not the end of a blessing; see there. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, writes the same. Similarly, in the blessing before the haftarah, one should not answer amen after hane'emarim be'emes, since this is not the end of the blessing, but only after uviNevi'ei ha'emes vatzedek. Levush and Maharit Tzahalon, siman 73, write the same, as cited by Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 3. Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 1, writes the same.
3) Levush writes: Throughout my life I have wondered why I have not seen any community follow the law of writing the haftaros as a scroll, like a Megillah. It appears to me that a congregation does not fulfill its obligation at all through haftaros read from printed Chumashim, since they were not written according to all the laws of a Sefer Torah or Megillah. These are his words. Hagahas Yafeh Nof writes the same. I say, however, that the ancient custom is primary. Beis Yosef, siman 144, writes in the name of Terumas HaDeshen that nowadays we skip from one Navi to another because our haftaros are written in booklets and one can mark them and find the place quickly, etc.; see there. Gittin 60a states that it is forbidden to write a haftarah for the reason stated in Yoreh De'ah, siman 283, seif 2, but it was permitted because 'It is a time to act for Hashem; they have voided Your Torah,' meaning the community lacked the means to purchase complete books of the Nevi'im. Now that printing has been discovered and, thank God, books are inexpensive, writing separate haftaros should be forbidden, because we maintain that printing is fully equivalent to writing, as stated in Responsa Rema of Fano, siman 96, Mishbetzos Zahav, and Lechem Chamudos. One cannot object that they are printed on paper, because Shiltei Gibborim at the beginning of chapter 16 writes that other sacred books may be written with all kinds of pigments and do not require ink. The same applies to writing them on paper, as Tosafos writes there, and they likewise need not be rolled. This is also implied by Responsa Rashba, siman 487, which says that only regarding a Sefer Torah may no blessing be recited when it is written in booklets, but we are not particular about haftaros; see there. Magen Avraham at the beginning of the siman. Taz, paragraph 2; Olas Tamid, paragraph 1; and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 4, write the same. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3, however, writes that some halachic authorities certainly require parchment specifically, as stated in Responsa Rema, siman 134. Furthermore, handwriting is certainly superior to printing, and the Divine Names were not printed with sanctity as they are when handwritten. He concludes that those who lack a haftarah written upon parchment should not read from printed volumes containing only the haftaros. Since such volumes are regarded as written texts, it is preferable to read from a complete printed book of the Nevi'im, which every community can obtain; see there. Nevertheless, the custom is to be lenient and read the haftaros from printed Chumashim, as Mateh Yehudah writes at the end of paragraph 2. Some places, however, customarily write the haftaros upon parchment or gevil and roll them like a Sefer Torah. Each community may follow its established practice.
4) If a printed or handwritten haftarah lacks many words, the reader may recite the missing words by heart when doing so does not violate the rule that one may not recite written matters orally. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 4. See above, siman 49, and our words there, with the help of Heaven.
5) Even if the text was not printed according to the proper format for words that are written one way and read another, but instead places the reading within the text and the written form below in the margin, it may be used, although one should not produce a text this way ab initio. Chasam Sofer, siman 51; Pischei Teshuvah.
6) There: A haftarah from the Nevi'im is read, etc. Our Sages state in an aggadic teaching that a deceased person said he would not be saved from his severe punishment until his son recited Kaddish or read the haftarah from the Nevi'im. Rivash writes in Responsa, siman 115, that this is the source of the widespread custom for a deceased person's son to recite the final Kaddish throughout the twelve months and also read the haftarah from the Nevi'im. These are Rivash's words. A further custom has now spread in some places that each year, when the anniversary of a parent's passing arrives, the son reads the haftarah on the preceding Shabbos. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 1. See Yoreh De'ah, siman 376, seif 4 in the gloss. If two people have a yahrzeit during the week, the person whose yahrzeit comes first has priority. Thus, if one is on Sunday and the other on Monday, the person whose yahrzeit is Sunday has the right to maftir on the preceding Shabbos. If both are on Sunday, a father's yahrzeit takes precedence over a mother's. If both commemorate fathers or both mothers, a Torah scholar takes precedence. If both are Torah scholars, the one greater in wisdom takes precedence. If they are equal, they should compromise, but must not quarrel, G-d forbid, over this. A word is worth one sela, but silence two, and through this silence he will give his father greater satisfaction than by speaking and receiving maftir.
7) There: No fewer than twenty-one verses are read, etc. If a translator is present, even ten verses suffice, because each verse counts as two together with its translation, while the final verse counts as three because after the translator translates it, the reader repeats it a second time. Tur. See below, paragraph 29.
