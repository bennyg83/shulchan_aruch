He intended to acquire it. Tur, Beit Yosef, and Rosh imply that even if it belongs to a non-Jew, when the Jew intends to acquire it he does not fulfill his obligation, because it then becomes idolatry belonging to a Jew. Bach writes that the case concerns ownerless idolatry. If it has owners, even if the Jew intended to acquire it, he did not acquire it and it remains idolatry belonging to a non-Jew. This does not appear correct to me. We rule that a Jew cannot nullify a non-Jew’s idolatry because of the decree that he might first lift it and then nullify it, thereby making it idolatry belonging to a Jew, as stated in Avodah Zarah 42a. This explicitly proves that even when it has owners it is called idolatry belonging to a Jew. Tosafos on Sukkah 31 likewise implies this, for otherwise the Gemara’s question would be answered by saying that the case concerns idolatry with owners. The same is implied by Yevamos 104. The reason is that since the Jew bears responsibility for it, it is considered his, as we find regarding chametz. Beit Yosef at the beginning of Yoreh Deah 146 cites an even broader opinion, that even if he did not acquire it, once his name became associated with it, it can no longer be nullified. Although we do not rule this way, Bach’s proof from partnership is not valid, as Kiddushin 17 shows that through partnership he acquires it even before it reaches his hand. In any event, once he acquires it all agree that it is forbidden, even if the acquisition was through theft.
When it was nullified before Yom Tov. If it was nullified on Yom Tov, it had already been rejected when Yom Tov began and does not become fit again, because it is not within his power to repair it, since a Jew cannot nullify it. By contrast, where the defect is within his power to repair, even an item that was initially fit and then rejected is not permanently rejected, as stated in siman 646, seif 2. Although it is forbidden to reduce the berries on Yom Tov, it is nevertheless considered within his power to do so. This raises a difficulty from Yoreh Deah 28:11, where one who covered blood and the wind uncovered it must cover it again, because there is no rejection regarding a mitzvah. The ruling there should have been to cover only because of doubt, as here, for Avodah Zarah 46 compares the cases, and Tosafos on Sukkah 33 likewise writes that this is considered not within his power. It appears to me that Tur, Beit Yosef, and the poskim follow Tosafos in Avodah Zarah, who explain that Reish Lakish’s question concerns only the initial use. This resolves all the difficulties Tosafos raises in Sukkah. The law is therefore that after the fact he fulfills his obligation even without nullification, but initially he should not use it even after nullification because it is repulsive for sacred use, as stated in siman 154, seif 11. Accordingly, the ruling in siman 11, seif 8, that its wool is invalid, means only initially; after the fact he fulfills his obligation. The same applies to its horn as a shofar. The case there concerns idolatry belonging to a Jew, but living creatures do not become prohibited; nevertheless they are repulsive for sacred use.
