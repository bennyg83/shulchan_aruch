[9] Levush: Before shofar, etc. Beit Yosef writes this from Ran and Kol Bo. He then adds that Tosafos, Mordechai, and Hagahos Ashri write that Rava concludes in the second chapter of Nedarim that if one says, “Sitting in a sukkah is forbidden to me,” or even, “It is forbidden to me that I sit in a sukkah,” it is forbidden. Beit Yosef writes that the same would appear to apply to shofar if one says, “The sounding of a shofar is forbidden to me,” unless one distinguishes that sitting in a sukkah gives ordinary benefit even without the mitzvah, whereas sounding a shofar gives no benefit apart from the mitzvah. Beit Yosef’s reference to “they wrote there” plainly means the chapter “Ra’uhu Beis Din,” which he cited above; the words “in the second chapter of Nedarim” are part of the citation from Tosafos and Mordechai. This excludes the understanding of Taz and Olas Shabbos that Tosafos and Mordechai wrote this in the second chapter of Nedarim. They also understood that Tosafos and the other authorities agree with Ran and Kol Bo that if one says, “This shofar is forbidden to me for its sounding,” it is prohibited, while Ran agrees with Tosafos where one says, “The sounding of a shofar is forbidden to me.” In my humble opinion, one who examines their words will see clearly that according to Ran and Kol Bo all these formulations are forbidden, whereas Tosafos and the other authorities remain uncertain in all of them. The rationale that there is no benefit apart from the mitzvah applies equally where one says, “This shofar is forbidden to me for its sounding,” as is also implied by Agudah. Beit Yosef therefore rules in accordance with Ran and Kol Bo that all are forbidden, for one does not set aside their definite ruling because of the uncertainty of Tosafos and the other authorities. This is the primary ruling. Rabbeinu Yerucham, folio 48, rules likewise, Knesses HaGedolah cites the same from a gloss, and Darkei Moshe explicitly writes that Tosafos, Mordechai, and Hagahos Ashri are uncertain regarding Ran’s law. Taz writes that if one says regarding a particular person, “I shall not hear the sound of a shofar from him,” or, “His sounding is forbidden to me,” the vow certainly takes effect according to all opinions, because he can hear it from another person. This requires study, for Rama already writes in siman 589, seif 10, that if he says, “His sounding is forbidden to me,” it is prohibited even where no other person is available, because a vow takes effect upon a mitzvah. The first formulation, “I shall not hear the sound of a shofar,” is likewise forbidden in all circumstances, as Levush explains, because it negates the mitzvah in general.
