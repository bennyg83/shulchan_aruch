19) [Seif 2] One who steals a shofar and sounds it fulfills the obligation, etc. For it is stated in the Yerushalmi: What is the difference between a lulav and a shofar? R' Assi said: Regarding a lulav it is written, 'You shall take for yourselves,' meaning from your own property. Here, however, it is written, 'It shall be a day of sounding for you,' in any manner. The Rambam wrote in chapter 1 that the mitzvah consists only of hearing the sound, and the law of theft does not apply to a sound. The Beit Yosef cited his words; see there.
20) Ibid. One who steals a shofar and sounds it fulfills the obligation, etc. However, there is an opinion that a stolen shofar is invalid. This refers specifically to one who stole an already-fashioned shofar, which is forbidden. If he stole a horn and fashioned it, however, he owes only its monetary value, and it is valid, as explained in the Beit Yosef; see there. Ateres Zekeinim cited his words. A practical difference arises where another shofar later became available to him, or where he obtained permission from the owner from whom he stole the shofar. He should sound again in order to fulfill the obligation according to all opinions, but he should not recite a blessing, since we rule that b'dieved he fulfilled the obligation with a stolen shofar, even though he did not initially recite a blessing over the stolen shofar, as written in the following letter. See below, letter 24.
21) Ibid. One who steals a shofar and sounds it fulfills the obligation, etc. Nevertheless, one does not recite a blessing over it. Magen Avraham, s.k. 4; Eliyah Rabbah, letter 7; Chemed Moshe, siman 691, letter 2; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, letter 4; Chayei Adam, rule 140, letter 18; Mateh Ephraim, letter 4; Mishna Berurah, s.k. 9. Even if he acquired it through the owner's despair and a change of possession, it can be said that he should not recite a blessing. Eshel Avraham, letter 4. So too wrote Maharikash, that just as we say below at the beginning of siman 649 regarding a stolen lulav that even if he acquired it through a change he should not recite a blessing over it, the same applies here. However, Pri Chadash disagrees and wrote that this case is different, because the blessing is only over the hearing, and theft does not apply to hearing. Therefore, it is proper to recite a blessing over it under all circumstances; see there. This is also the view of Mateh Ephraim, ibid. Yet Mateh Yosef cited the aforementioned words of Maharikash and Pri Chadash and wrote that the primary ruling is not to recite a blessing; see there. So too, Nehar Shalom, letter 2, wrote regarding the aforementioned words of Pri Chadash that they are not compelling, and concluded: Nevertheless, for practical halacha, even if it were only a doubt concerning blessings, we are lenient; see there. So too wrote Mishna Berurah there, that even if there was the owner's despair and a change of possession, or a physical change, it is still not proper to recite a blessing. See above siman 25, letter 86, and below at the beginning of siman 649.
22) However, it is clear that even when one acquires a stolen shofar, he should not sound it if he has another one available. Is it fitting that the very object that became his accuser should become his advocate, as we say regarding a lulav in the Yerushalmi at the beginning of the chapter Lulav HaGazul? Pri Chadash; Mishna Berurah in Shaar HaTziyun, letter 19. Regarding the blessing, Pri Chadash appears to hold that one should recite it, and so too wrote Mateh Ephraim there. However, the view of the other later authorities is that one should not recite a blessing, as written in the preceding letter.
23) It appears that one may take another person's shofar without his knowledge and recite a blessing over it, as written in siman 649, seif 5. Although a shofar is somewhat damaged through sounding it, a tallis is also somewhat damaged through its use, and nevertheless it is permitted, as written above in siman 14. There is no concern that he will sound it excessively, as there is regarding sefarim, above siman 14, letter 31, for why would he sound more than is required for his obligation? Magen Avraham, ibid.; Eliyah Rabbah, ibid.; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, letter 5; Chayei Adam, ibid. Nevertheless, he may sound 100 blasts with it, since the custom is to sound that number. Mateh Ephraim, letter 5; Mishna Berurah, ibid. It is implied, however, that it is forbidden to take it without the owner's knowledge in order to practice, unless he knows that the owner does not object to this. Chayei Adam, ibid. The permission to take another person's shofar applies only to sounding it in the place where it rests. It is forbidden, however, to remove it from his house to the synagogue, or the reverse, for it is possible that he objects to this, and it constitutes theft, as written above in siman 14, seif 4, and in our words there, letters 19 and 22; see there. So too wrote Magen Avraham, siman 649, s.k. 16, regarding a lulav; see there. So too wrote Mishna Berurah in this siman, Shaar HaTziyun, letter 20.
