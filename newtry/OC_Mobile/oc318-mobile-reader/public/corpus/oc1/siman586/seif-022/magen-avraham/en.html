It may be sounded. Since it may be moved, one may fulfill the mitzvah with it. The Sages prohibited an item brought for a particular person only regarding eating and personal benefit, while mitzvos were not given for personal benefit. Tur, siman 655; Hagahos Ashri; and Mordechai, chapter 6 of Eruvin. In any event, it is forbidden to carry it beyond four amos, as stated in siman 515, seif 5. According to what I wrote at the end of that siman, if another Jew sent it and it was delayed and brought on Yom Tov, it is forbidden to move it. Levush asks that one may even initially instruct a non-Jew to bring it from outside the techum, since this is a double shevus. Why, then, was it necessary to state that it may be sounded? He answers that the case concerns a distance beyond twelve mil, which is prohibited according to the Torah. See siman 404.
If a non-Jew made a shofar. This requires study, for it is completely nolad, as stated in siman 501 and in Shabbos 29. It should therefore be forbidden to move it in order to fulfill the mitzvah, no better than a shofar covered by stones. See what I wrote in siman 655. Tur there likewise implies that if it may not be moved, it may not be used even to fulfill the mitzvah. R. Eliezer cited by Mordechai may follow those who permit nolad on Yom Tov. One must therefore say that the case here concerns a non-Jew who fashioned a shofar from his own horn, which is not considered nolad because its completion was in human hands, as stated in siman 292. The Gemara’s case concerns one who hollowed out a piece of wood belonging to a Jew. See siman 325, seif 4. All disqualifications that apply on the first day apply on the second day as well. Darkei Moshe and Rashba. This is unlike siman 649, seif 5, where items invalid on the first day may be taken on the second, because there they are at least valid on the remaining days of Sukkos, whereas here they are entirely invalid.
