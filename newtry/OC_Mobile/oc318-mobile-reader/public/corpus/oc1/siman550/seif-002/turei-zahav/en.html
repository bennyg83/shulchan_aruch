The Gemara asks: it calls them "fast" and calls them "joy," and Rava answers: when there is peace, i.e. when the Temple stands — joy; when there is persecution — fast, etc. He asks: what is the question? Certainly the intent is that what was fast before the second redemption shall be for joy after the redemption; and it appears this is his difficulty — it should have said "the fast of the fourth month... shall turn to joy," as Scripture said "and your God will turn the curse to blessing," and likewise in the piyyut "turn to profit... the fourth fast to joy"; and since Scripture says "shall be for joy," it implies the fast is not entirely nullified but becomes joy — and this is impossible; therefore he answers there is a time distinction and both exist. Beit Yosef explained the question thus: it should not have said "the fast of the fourth month" — "I am the month, the fourth," as in the question "I wept in the fifth month," etc. — but certainly the name "fast" remains as well. (1) Permitted in washing. I have difficulty from Megillah 5a: R' Yehuda bathed in the bathhouse of Tzipori on 17 Tammuz; Tosafot wrote: why was bathing relevant — even eating is permitted, as taught: "there is no danger and no peace" — if they wish they do not fast; one may say since our ancestors already accepted it upon themselves, presumably they accepted bathing too — end quote. Per what is written here that there is no prohibition of washing on the four fasts, what is R' Yehuda's novelty on 17 Tammuz? One may say from R' Yehuda's action we learn washing is permitted and only eating is forbidden — if so, what is Tosafot's question "even eating is permitted if they wish"? Perhaps they truly did not wish to cancel the fast but truly fasted per law and bathed per law. One may say per Beit Yosef in name of Ramban: all four fasts were initially decreed with all stringencies; but now, since they practice fasting in them and do not practice those stringencies, it implies initially they wished only to fast but not practice those stringencies; and since these depend on will, authority is in their hands — but from the origin of the decree they are surely forbidden in all — end quote; therefore R' Yehuda's case is resolved: the public held the stringency in all these matters including eating, and R' Yehuda permitted washing — thereby the decree was nullified even for eating; therefore Tosafot asked: if so R' Yehuda should have eaten to teach the decree was entirely nullified; they answered: likewise it was entirely nullified, but he accepted upon himself prohibition of eating and not prohibition of washing, as our ancestors accepted.
