The Gemara asks: the verse calls them fasts and calls them joy; Rava answers: when there is peace, namely when the Temple stands, they are joy; when there is persecution, they are fasts, etc. This is difficult: what is the question? Surely this is what it means: what was a fast before the second redemption will be joy after the redemption. It appears that this is the difficulty: it should have said "the fast of the fourth," etc., "will be transformed into joy," as the verse says, "the Lord your God transformed the curse into blessing," and as in the piyyut, "transform the fast of the fourth into relief and joy." Since the verse says "will be for joy," it implies that the fast will not be completely canceled but will be for joy, which is impossible. Therefore he answered that there is a distinction of times between them and both remain. The Beit Yosef answered that the question was this: it should not have said "the fast of the fourth" but rather "the fourth month," as in the question, "shall I weep in the fifth month," etc.; rather, certainly the name fast also remains.
(1) They are permitted in washing. I have difficulty from Megillah 5a: Rabbi washed in the bathhouse of Tzippori on the seventeenth of Tammuz. Tosafot wrote: If you ask, why mention washing; even eating is permitted, as it was taught: where there is neither persecution nor peace, if they want they fast and if they want they do not fast. They answer: since our ancestors already accepted it upon themselves, presumably they too accepted it; end quote. According to what is written here, that there is no prohibition of washing on the four fasts, what novelty is there in Rabbi's action on the seventeenth of Tammuz? One cannot say that indeed from Rabbi's act we learn permission to wash and that only eating is prohibited, for if so, what did Tosafot ask, that eating too should be permitted if they wanted? Perhaps in truth they did not want to cancel the fast, but did fast according to law, and Rabbi washed according to law. One may answer based on what the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Ramban, that all four fasts were initially decreed with all the stringencies, but nowadays, since the custom is to fast on them and not to practice those stringencies, it implies that from the outset this is what they wanted: to fast only, but not to observe those stringencies. Since these matters depend on consent, authority is in their hands; but from the essence of the enactment, they were certainly forbidden in all of them; end quote. Therefore Rabbi's case works well: people were treating all these matters stringently like eating, and Rabbi came and permitted washing; consequently the decree was nullified even regarding eating. Therefore Tosafot asked that if so, Rabbi should have eaten, to teach that the decree was entirely nullified. They answered that indeed it was entirely nullified, but he accepted upon himself the prohibition of eating and not the prohibition of washing, as our ancestors accepted.
