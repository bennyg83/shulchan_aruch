The Gemara asks: The verse calls them fasts and also calls them occasions of joy. Rava answers that when there is peace, meaning when the Beis HaMikdash is standing, they are occasions of joy, and when there is persecution they are fasts, etc. This is difficult. What is the Gemara asking? Surely the verse means that what had been a fast before the second redemption would become an occasion of joy after the redemption. It appears that the difficulty is that the verse should have said that the fast of the fourth month, etc., 'will be transformed into joy,' as the verse says, 'Hashem your God transformed the curse into a blessing,' and as stated in the piyyut, 'Transform the fast of the fourth month into relief and joy.' Since the verse instead says that it 'will be for joy,' it implies that the fast will not be entirely abolished, but will itself become an occasion of joy, which is impossible. Rava therefore answered that the verse refers to different periods, and both descriptions remain applicable. The Beit Yosef answers that the question is that the verse should not have said 'the fast of the fourth month,' but rather 'the fourth month,' as stated in the question, 'Shall I weep in the fifth month?' This wording indicates that the designation 'fast' will also remain.
(1) They are permitted to bathe. I find this difficult in light of what is stated in the first chapter of Megillah, folio 5a, that Rabbi bathed in the market of Tzippori on the seventeenth of Tammuz. Tosafos write: 'One may ask, why mention bathing? Even eating is permitted, as it was taught that when there is neither persecution nor peace, if they wish they fast, and if they wish they do not fast. One can answer that since our ancestors already accepted the fast upon themselves, presumably they also accepted it.' End quote. According to what is written here, that bathing is not forbidden on the four fasts, what was noteworthy about Rabbi bathing on the seventeenth of Tammuz? One cannot answer that we indeed learn the permissibility of bathing from Rabbi's conduct, while only eating is forbidden, because then what was Tosafos' question that eating too is permitted if they so choose? Perhaps they did not choose to abolish the fast, and in fact fasted as required, while Rabbi bathed permissibly. One can answer based on what the Beit Yosef writes in the name of the Ramban, that when the four fasts were originally decreed, all the stringencies were imposed upon them. Nowadays, however, since the people customarily fast but do not observe those stringencies, this indicates that from the outset they accepted only the fast and did not accept those additional stringencies. Since observance in these circumstances depends upon communal acceptance, the choice is theirs, although according to the original enactment all of those stringencies certainly applied. End quote. Accordingly, Rabbi's conduct is understood. The people had been observing the stringencies in all these matters, just as they did regarding eating, and Rabbi came and permitted bathing. The decree was thereby nullified even regarding eating. Tosafos therefore asked that Rabbi should have eaten, thereby demonstrating that the decree had been entirely nullified. They answered that it had indeed been entirely nullified, but he personally accepted the prohibition of eating and did not accept the prohibition of bathing, just as our ancestors accepted the fast upon themselves.
