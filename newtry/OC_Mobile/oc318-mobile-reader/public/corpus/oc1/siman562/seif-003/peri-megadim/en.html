From the seventeenth of Tammuz. See Taz. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 146, writes that two reasons are required regarding the Aseres Yemei Teshuvah, when the fasts are not completed: first, it is not customary to accept them formally, for they are already accepted by force of custom; and second, one may say that the intention is merely to cause oneself discomfort. Therefore, one who fasts only two or three days must accept the fast, since this is not the established custom, and consequently must complete it. The same applies from the seventeenth of Tammuz: although it is not customary to accept that fast formally, the notion of causing oneself discomfort over the past is inapplicable, and therefore the fast from the seventeenth of Tammuz is completed. This is difficult in light of Taanis 12a. Bach also objects that, regarding the destruction, causing oneself discomfort over the past is indeed relevant. Taz hints at a further difficulty. Certainly when Tishah B’Av is postponed to Sunday, no acceptance at Minchah is required, because the fast is already accepted and standing; see paragraph 2. Since no acceptance is required, only the rationale of discomfort remains. How, then, were they lenient there because it was their Yom Tov? We rule that Megillas Taanis was annulled, and fasting on those days is permitted. If the explanation is that they intended merely discomfort, discomfort alone is insufficient. He therefore answers that there are two kinds of discomfort. In that case, they did not intend at all that the observance on Sunday should be regarded as the Tishah B’Av fast, but only as a remembrance, not as a fast at all. By contrast, when one intends it to be a fast and to cause oneself discomfort, one may distinguish between distress over the past and distress concerning the future. This resolves Tosafos in Taanis there, s.v. ‘There,’ who ask that Megillas Taanis was annulled and fasting on those festive days is permitted. They answer that the authority there holds it was not annulled and that fasting was forbidden, and therefore they did not complete the fast. If so, the Mechaber’s ruling above in siman 559, seif 9, regarding one involved in a bris, etc., is difficult. Rabbi Elazar ben Tzadok held that Megillas Taanis was not annulled, so that day was their Yom Tov and fasting was forbidden, whereas for one involved in a bris there is no such rabbinically ordained Yom Tov. Taz therefore explains that Tosafos’ question from the annulment means that, since Megillas Taanis was annulled after the destruction and they were permitted to fast on those days because all joy had ceased, Rabbi Elazar ben Tzadok presumably ought to have fasted and completed the fast, thereby performing a mitzvah. Although one could say that, as a matter of law, because the fast was postponed they were lenient, as with one involved in a bris, Tosafos answer that Rabbi Elazar ben Tzadok held Megillas Taanis was not annulled and fasting was forbidden. Taz, however, explains that it may indeed have been annulled, but because the fast was postponed and the day had formerly been their Yom Tov, they were not required to fast at all. They observed only a measure of discomfort as a remembrance, not as a fast. Such a partial-day observance had never been prohibited to them, even before the destruction, and therefore it was not included in the annulment in a manner that would later make it a mitzvah for a distinguished person to fast. They therefore did not complete it, and the ruling in siman 559 is understandable. The matter still requires some study. Tosafos cite the phrase ‘to cause discomfort’ rather than ‘because it was our Yom Tov’; this may be somewhat explained according to the foregoing. See Magen Avraham, siman 559, seif katan 11, and siman 572, seif 2. With Hashem’s help, this will be explained there. See also Magen Avraham, seif katan 6.
