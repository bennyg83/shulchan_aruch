1) [Seif 1] Every fast, etc. Even a private fast. Beit Yosef. Levush, beginning of siman 564. Shulchan Gavoha, os 3. Mishna Berurah, os 1:
2) Ibid. Meaning, one did not complete it until the emergence of the stars. Although it is evident from the Gemara that bein hashmashos begins at the end of sunset, which is before the emergence of the stars, nevertheless, because people are not expert in determining the end of sunset, they practiced waiting until the emergence of the stars. Although it is evident from the Gemara that, were it not for the stringency of Tishah B'Av, its bein hashmashos would be permitted, implying that the doubtful period of other fasts is permitted, one can say that beginning a day is different from ending a day. At the beginning of the day, meaning entering the fast, its bein hashmashos is permitted, such as on erev Tishah B'Av. But when ending the day, meaning at the conclusion of the fast, one does not leave the fast because of a doubt until three stars emerge, at which point it is certainly night. Beit Yosef in the name of Hagahos Maimoniyos. However, Rabbeinu Yonah and the Rosh, at the end of the second chapter of Shabbos, wrote that bein hashmashos is permitted on a private fast even when ending the day; see there. Their words are cited in Biur HaGra. So too, we wrote above in siman 342, os 8, regarding Shabbos, that several poskim disagree with this and hold that there is no distinction between beginning the day and ending the day; see there. Accordingly, one must say that the primary reason we are stringent even regarding a private fast is that people are not expert in determining the end of sunset, as stated. So too wrote the Maamar, os 1. See above, siman 261, os 1:
2) The Magen Avraham wrote at the beginning of the siman that it is implied by the aforementioned words of the Beit Yosef in the name of Hagahos Maimoniyos that an individual who fasts through the night and the day need not cease eating at bein hashmashos. He nevertheless wrote that it is proper not to enter a doubtful period, and he should cease eating while it is still day; see there. So too wrote the Eliyah Rabbah, siman 564, os 1. See below, os 29:
3) Ibid., Rama's gloss. Meaning that he must see three medium-sized stars, but large stars do not suffice. Magen Avraham, s.k. 1, according to the gloss of Machatzis HaShekel, Levushei Serad, Eshel Avraham, Nehar Shalom, Nezirus Shimshon, and Chayei Adam, klal 132, os 10. Machatzis HaShekel and Levushei Serad wrote there that the reason is that large stars are visible even during the day:
4) Ibid., in the Rama's gloss. Three medium-sized stars. Although above, in siman 263, regarding Motzei Shabbos, the Shulchan Aruch ruled that one may not perform melacha until three small stars are visible, that is because they were stringent regarding Shabbos, since not everyone is expert in determining which stars are considered medium-sized, as explained there in the Beit Yosef and Magen Avraham. Regarding a fast, however, they maintained the basic law, and medium-sized stars suffice. Machatzis HaShekel. So too wrote Levushei Serad. What is written there, that the stars must also be consecutive, is because of the added time of holiness, whereas a fast, even Tishah B'Av, does not require an added period. Regarding Yom Kippur, one can say that medium-sized consecutive stars constitute the added period. Alternatively, one can say that they are stringent regarding a Torah law, whereas regarding a rabbinic fast they did not impose this burden. Eshel Avraham, os 1. Meaning, specifically regarding a rabbinic fast they did not impose this burden, and medium-sized stars that are not consecutive suffice. Regarding the fast of Yom Kippur, however, which is from the Torah, small consecutive stars are required, as on Motzei Shabbos. So ruled the Mishna Berurah, os 3, and this is reasonable:

7) Ibid., Rama's gloss. Nevertheless, the practice is to recite Aneinu even though one does not complete the fast, etc. This means that if he explicitly stated when accepting the fast that he would not complete it, then even if he fasted only until Minchah Gedolah, he recites Aneinu. If, however, he accepted a fast without qualification, he must complete it. If he did not complete it and ate, see siman 568, seif 1. If he ate before praying, meaning where he had explicitly stipulated, he does not recite Aneinu. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 157. Magen Avraham, s.k. 2. Eliyah Rabbah, siman 564, os 2. The Taz, s.k. 1, wrote that although one does not question an established practice, nevertheless, one who does not recite Aneinu on a fast that he does not complete avoids a possible blessing according to the majority of poskim. Although for an individual Aneinu is not an independent blessing, but is included within Shomea Tefillah, nevertheless, according to them the wording "the fast of our fasting" is inappropriate, since an unspecified fast means a complete day. Furthermore, according to the poskim who hold that Aneinu should not be recited, it constitutes an interruption in the blessing of Shomea Tefillah, as the Tur wrote in siman 563 regarding accepting a fast in Shomea Tefillah. It is therefore proper not to interrupt the prayer, and one who omits these two words, "the fast of our fasting," acts properly; see there. Bigdei Yesha wrote that even if he says "the fast of our fasting," he is not speaking falsely, for he did fast. End quote; see there. Machatzis HaShekel, s.k. 3, also inclines to this view; see there. See further in the following os:
8) The Mishbetzos Zahav wrote in os 1 that it is implied by the aforementioned words of the Taz that even if one did not accept the fast at Minchah, and even for a partial-day fast, he should recite Aneinu while omitting the two words. It is implied that it is preferable to recite it after the prayer, in Elokai Netzor, rather than interrupt Shomea Tefillah. He further wrote that during the Ten Days of Repentance, when people fast a partial-day fast until after Minchah Gedolah or the like, it appears that one may not eat until he first prays Minchah, just as with a bridegroom in seif 2. However, this would seemingly apply according to the view that he says "the fast of our fasting," so that he not be speaking falsely in the prayer after he has already eaten. If, however, he omits those two words, perhaps he may eat after midday without first praying Minchah, and afterwards, when he prays Minchah with the congregation, he may say Aneinu "on a day of distress," even though he has eaten, since he does not mention "the fast of our fasting," provided that he stipulated that he would eat after midday or that this was his intention. He left this unresolved; see there. Therefore, it appears that whether he stipulated that he would not complete his fast, or he did not accept the fast on the preceding day, which according to the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch is likewise not considered a fast, as written in seif 5, in all these cases it is preferable to recite Aneinu after completing the Minchah prayer, in Elokai Netzor, meaning before saying the final "May the words of my mouth, etc." after Elokai Netzor. It is also preferable not to say the two words "the fast of our fasting," thereby removing oneself from the dispute. Nevertheless, one should not protest against one who recites it in Shomea Tefillah, since this is already the accepted practice and many great authorities hold this way, as the Rama, of blessed memory, wrote in this seif and in seif 5; see there:
