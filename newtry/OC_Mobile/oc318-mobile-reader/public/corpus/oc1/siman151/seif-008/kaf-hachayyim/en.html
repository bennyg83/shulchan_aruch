8) There: And idle conversation. Sefer Pesach Einayim writes: 'One who speaks mundane conversation in a synagogue would be better off not coming at all, for he sins and causes others to sin. The Satan prosecutes and says, Today is too short for him to speak, so he waits until the time of prayer. This includes Torah students who engage in pilpul during prayer and disturb the congregation with their shouting. They do not act properly. Although the Sages said that Torah study is equal to all the mitzvos, there is a time for Torah study, etc. Furthermore, unlearned people will draw an a fortiori inference from them and will likewise not remain silent to hear the prayer from the shaliach tzibbur.' Siddur Beis Oved, paragraph 2, cites him. See Siddur Beis Oved there, where he discusses this at length. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1, similarly writes that one must rebuke anyone who speaks mundane conversation in a synagogue. This applies even when the conversation is not idle, for idle conversation is otherwise forbidden wherever it occurs, and one who engages in it transgresses at least a rabbinic positive commandment. Even speech needed for earning a livelihood, which is permitted outside, is forbidden in a synagogue. See Responsa Edus B'Yosef, volume 2, siman 31, where he explains the severity of speaking in a synagogue, particularly during prayer, its punishment, its judgment, and its rectification. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 4, cites him. The holy Zohar, parashas Terumah, folio 131b, states: 'Woe to one who speaks mundane words in a synagogue, for he demonstrates separation. Woe to him, for he diminishes faith. Woe to him, for he has no share in the God of Israel. He demonstrates that he has no God, that God is not present there, that he has no share in Him and does not fear Him, and he treats the supernal order above with contempt.' Shaar HaKavanos, folio 4a, states: 'I saw my master, of blessed memory, take the utmost care never to speak at all in a synagogue, even outside the time of prayer. He almost refrained even from speaking words of mussar, rebuke, and repentance, lest this lead to any mundane speech.' Magen Avraham, paragraph 3, cites this. See also what we wrote above in siman 132, paragraph 7.
8) On holy Shabbos there is an additional prohibition. Not only is mundane conversation forbidden even for one's own needs, but speech concerning the needs of the synagogue is also forbidden, as stated in the holy Zohar, parashas Vayakhel, folio 205b: 'Once the holy people enter the synagogue, it is forbidden to occupy oneself even with the needs of the synagogue, but only with words of praise, prayer, and Torah. One who occupies himself with other matters and worldly speech is a person who desecrates Shabbos and has no share among the Jewish people. Two angels are appointed over this on Shabbos. They place their hands upon his head and say, Woe to so-and-so, who has no share in the Holy One, blessed is He. Therefore, one must occupy himself with prayer, songs, praises of his Master, and Torah.' The reason is that the resting of the Shechinah in a synagogue on Shabbos is unlike its resting there on a weekday. One must therefore be especially stringent, and it is forbidden to occupy oneself even with synagogue needs. Shalmei Tzibbur, folio 12b; Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 1.
8) The Rokeach writes in Tikkunei Teshuvah that one who habitually speaks mundane matters in a synagogue, even outside the time of prayer, should fast for forty days and receive lashes each day. From then onward he should sit there with great reverence, pray with kavanah, and not speak at all in the synagogue. Chesed LaAlafim cites him there.
