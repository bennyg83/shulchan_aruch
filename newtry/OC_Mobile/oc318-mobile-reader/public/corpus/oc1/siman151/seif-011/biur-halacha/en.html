To use it - Eliyah Rabbah wrote in the name of the Bach, siman 153, that if he stipulated to pray there until a certain time and afterward not to pray there at all, he may use it after the time ends for whatever he wants. This requires study, for in this manner it is not considered designation at all, since he explicitly stipulated that it is only temporary; if so, even during that time itself the sanctity of a synagogue is not upon it. So too is proven from the Mordechai brought by the Bach. However, regarding levity, it appears proper to be stringent during the time. This is what the Gemara concluded [according to the Mordechai's explanation]: "nevertheless, one does not act with levity in them; and what is this? Accounts." Meaning, although by law such a designation, which is temporary, is not significant, nevertheless one should not conduct levity of accounts there, and all the more so laughter and mockery. Regarding eating, drinking, and other uses, it implies that one need not be stringent in this, as we wrote in siman 153 in Mishnah Berurah seif katan 51.
But while it is settled, etc. - There are several approaches to explaining the sugya that they were made conditionally: the view of Tosafot and the Rosh is that while it is settled, a condition does not help, but when it is ruined it helps; the view of Rashi and the Or Zarua is that even while it is settled a condition helps; and the view of the Ramban and the Ran, and so too the Rashba's view in his responsa, part 4, is that even when they are ruined a condition does not help to use them explicitly not in pressing circumstances, but where it is pressing, even while they are settled a condition helps. "Pressing" means when it is to feed poor people as a temporary need or to lodge them there. Now although the Mechaber copied as law that while it is settled a condition does not help, nevertheless it appears from the Taz's words at the beginning of the siman that where it is pressing, as above, one may rely on the Ramban that even while it is settled a condition helps. The law is with him, for this is also the view of the Ran and the Rashba; and one may also combine with this the view of Rashi and Or Zarua, who hold that even while settled a condition helps. Know further that even according to Rashi and Or Zarua, that a condition helps even while settled, nevertheless for actual levity, such as laughter, mockery, and the like, it is obvious that it does not help, as is explicitly proven in the Gemara; see there, for even accounts were forbidden because they too are levity.
A condition does not help - See Magen Avraham, who brought that this applies specifically to a condition for levity, such as eating, drinking, and entering in the sun because of the sun, but for other use a condition helps. This is difficult, for it is proven in the Rosh there that even to call a person from there a condition also does not help while it is settled; and how is this less than ordinary other use? Rather, certainly every use is forbidden while settled. Regarding his question from siman 154, where a condition helps for implements of sanctity, such as a cloth for a Sefer Torah and the like, perhaps one can answer that a synagogue is different, since it is a mitzvah for Israel to have a mikdash me'at wherever they are, as stated in siman 150, that they compel one another to build a synagogue. Therefore Chazal decreed that in a fixed synagogue, a condition should not help while it is settled to use it for mundane purposes; for if so, the name synagogue is not upon it at all, but it is like an ordinary house. This is unlike there: even if we say that the cloth does not have the status of an implement of sanctity, what concern is there in that? Know further that the Magen Avraham's question applies only according to Rashi's explanation and the other poskim who explain that "they were made conditionally" means that they may be used immediately, and nevertheless Tosafot and the Rosh hold that it does not help for the time it is settled; to this he asked from siman 154. But according to Rabbeinu Chananel's explanation that we have, there is no question at all, in my humble opinion. He explains "they were made conditionally" to mean that whenever he wishes, it returns to its original state [meaning, if the public wants this; and that is why he concludes that nevertheless they may not calculate public accounts in them, for this is at any rate forbidden because of levity. But other uses that are not disgraceful are permitted for the public to use when they wish, because then its sanctity is removed and it returns to its original state. According to his explanation, there is no difficulty from shelter from rain, for as long as the public has not consented to it, it still stands in its sanctity. It is reasonable that even according to him, although the public may use it for a use that is not disgraceful, so that it then becomes like an ordinary house, immediately when they return to pray in it, it again becomes sanctified with the sanctity of a synagogue]. If so, there is no question from siman 154, for there the condition is to use it while it remains in its sanctity, and for that one may say it helps.
But synagogues in Eretz Yisrael, etc. - See Mishnah Berurah. Regarding the very law that the Shulchan Aruch wrote plainly, that in Eretz Yisrael a condition does not help, one must examine it, for seemingly this law is not clear according to everyone, but only according to Tosafot, as just below. The Gemara does not mention at all that a condition does not help there; rather, it says that the synagogues of Bavel were made conditionally. If we explain that by default they were made conditionally, one can infer from this only to exclude Eretz Yisrael, where by default they do not stand on condition; but if they explicitly stipulated, why should it not help, as with all implements of sanctity where it is explicit in the Yerushalmi that a condition helps, as below in siman 154, and as the Magen Avraham asked in seif katan 14. Even according to what the Magen Avraham proved from several poskim, that "made conditionally" means that in Bavel they stipulated about them and not by default, nevertheless we have no proof that in Eretz Yisrael a condition does not help; rather, in Bavel it was their way to stipulate for whatever reasons they had, and in Eretz Yisrael they did not stipulate. The same would be true in all the Diaspora, that there was no condition upon their synagogues, and even in Bavel itself nowadays it is not clear that their synagogues are made conditionally. But if they explicitly stipulated, it should properly help everywhere, as with all implements of sanctity. In truth, it is not mentioned in any of the Rishonim that in Eretz Yisrael a condition does not help; this novel law is mentioned only in Tosafot. It appears that they wrote this only according to their view, that while it is settled the condition does not help and only when it is ruined; therefore they wrote the reason that the condition helps when ruined because "when the redeemer comes," meaning: since ultimately, when the redeemer comes and Israel are gathered from all the places where they were scattered among the nations to Eretz Yisrael, they will no longer need this mikdash me'at and they are destined to be ruined, and necessarily their sanctity will then automatically be removed, therefore the condition also helps for any time that it is ruined. This is unlike Eretz Yisrael, where its sanctity will never be nullified; therefore even when ruined a condition does not help, just as while settled. According to this, for the poskim who hold that even while settled the condition helps in pressing circumstances, such as to feed poor guests, one can say that there is no distinction between Bavel and Eretz Yisrael; even in Eretz Yisrael the condition helps if they stipulated, even while it is settled, for this matter. This also appears from Riaz, whose view also follows this approach, and who wrote that the reason there was a condition in the synagogues of Bavel is because guests are common; this implies that by law a condition helps everywhere, even while settled, but there it was their way to stipulate because guests were common. Although the Shulchan Aruch wrote plainly like the view of Tosafot, that a condition helps only when ruined, it is proven from the Taz at the beginning of the siman that we may rely on the view of the Ramban and his supporters, that even while settled a condition helps for the needs of the poor in pressing circumstances, as above; and if so, this law would apply even in Eretz Yisrael. More than this, one can say that even according to the Rosh and Tur, who hold like Tosafot that while settled a condition does not help and only when ruined, in this they may not hold like Tosafot, but rather that if they explicitly stipulated, it helps even in Eretz Yisrael when ruined [for by the essence of the law a condition helps for a synagogue as for implements of sanctity; only Chazal made a fence while it is settled, as above in Biur Halacha. But for what the condition helps in Bavel, the same should apply in Eretz Yisrael, for what difference is there?]. The Gemara mentioned that those of Bavel were made conditionally because there it was their way to stipulate, as above, and therefore the Rosh and Tur omitted this law that Tosafot wrote, and only the Mechaber copied it plainly. What emerges from this seif and all our words is that there are three distinctions: great levity, such as accounts, laughter, mockery, and the like, is forbidden even in Bavel and even with a condition, as stated explicitly in the Gemara, both while settled and while ruined. Mundane uses that contain no levity at all: it is proven from the Magen Avraham that a condition helps everywhere, as I wrote in Mishnah Berurah, and this too applies both while settled and while ruined. The discussion here concerns eating, drinking, and the like, which contain some levity: in Bavel a condition helps at least when ruined according to the view of the Shulchan Aruch, and according to several poskim even while settled, to feed poor people there when space is pressing. According to our words, even in Eretz Yisrael perhaps one may be lenient in all this.
