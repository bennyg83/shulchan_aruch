Paragraph 2: Under pressing circumstances; and the proof, etc. Magen Avraham means that the early authorities appear to hold four opinions. Shulchan Aruch copies Rambam's phrase 'under pressing circumstances,' implying that even a Torah scholar may eat or drink there only under pressure. Tur omits those words. Beis Yosef says Tur agrees that pressure is required, but omitted it because it is obvious; why would permission be granted without need? Bach says Rif, Rosh, and Tur, who omit the phrase, dispute Rambam and permit it even without pressure. Rema's gloss indicates that Ran holds a third opinion, distinguishing a synagogue from a beis midrash: pressure is required in a synagogue, but not in a beis midrash. Hagahos Ashri appears to present a fourth view, writing that Rav Adda and Ravina cited here by Magen Avraham were personally stringent in saying, 'A teaching requires clarity,' although it would have been permitted even while their places were inhabited; eating and drinking are permitted in synagogues and study halls even for people who are not Torah scholars. Thus, he permits even a non-scholar, apparently without pressure. Bach struggles to reconcile Hagahos Ashri with the baraisa on Megillah 28, which says like Shulchan Aruch that one may neither eat nor drink there. All this arose because they understood Rambam's 'under pressing circumstances' as an indispensable condition and did not consider distinguishing eating and drinking from other uses. Beis Yosef supports Rambam's requirement from the incident of Rav Adda and Ravina, who were standing, etc.
It appears to me that specifically, etc. Meaning that Rambam, Ran, and Tur do not disagree.
They would be interrupted from their study. Thus, every eating and drinking of a Torah scholar there is considered compelled, so that he not be interrupted from learning.
Ran writes concerning the incident of Rav Adda and Ravina: 'Although we say that Sages and their disciples are permitted, it appears to me that this refers to a beis midrash where they remain all day, as the expression bei rabbanan means the house of the Sages, but not a synagogue.' Rema understood Ran to dispute Rambam, who equates synagogue and beis midrash. According to Magen Avraham, however, there is no dispute. Rambam addresses a synagogue where they study, as was their practice in Talmudic times; eating and drinking were permitted so they would not be interrupted from study. Ran's language appears to address a synagogue where people do not study, and he therefore forbids eating and drinking there. The same would apply to sheltering from rain in such a case.
This is Rambam's meaning in saying that they may eat and drink 'under pressing circumstances.' The phrase is not a condition, because every eating and drinking by a Torah scholar in a beis midrash is compelled by the need not to interrupt his learning. Rather, Rambam gives the reason why eating and drinking were permitted. Rif, Rosh, and Tur consequently omit the phrase because it is not a condition; all their eating and drinking there is deemed compelled and permitted in every case.
This is also implied by se'if 4, meaning that it is permitted for a mitzvah, such as gathering to intercalate the year, etc. The Yerushalmi says they ate in a synagogue while intercalating the year, from which the poskim prove that a seudas mitzvah is permitted in a synagogue. What is their proof? Intercalating the year required great Torah scholars who were ordained, and Torah scholars are independently permitted to eat and drink in a synagogue. This question applies not only to the view permitting it without pressure; even according to the view requiring pressure, perhaps the Yerushalmi means that they ate there when compelled to intercalate there. Evidently, permission is granted only where they study in the synagogue. Since it specifically says they ate there while intercalating the year, implying they did not ordinarily eat there, it must concern a synagogue where they did not study; it was nevertheless permitted for the mitzvah of intercalating the year.
And eating and drinking are permitted to a Torah scholar in the plain sense; even without pressure. Thus, even if rain is not considered pressing circumstances, why should eating be different, when it is permitted without pressure? Evidently, all eating and drinking there is itself deemed compelled, as Magen Avraham writes.
Hagahos Ashri cited above also concedes this: permission is granted only to a Torah scholar.
