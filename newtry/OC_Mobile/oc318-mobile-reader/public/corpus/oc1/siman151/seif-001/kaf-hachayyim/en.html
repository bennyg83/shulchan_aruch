1) [Seif 1] Synagogues and batei midrash may not be treated with levity, etc. This is because they are called a minor Sanctuary, and it is written, 'You shall revere My Sanctuary.' Levush. See Megillah 29a. Through this sin, a synagogue is transformed into a house of idolatry, G-d forbid. Magen Avraham there, paragraph 1.
2) There: Levity, etc. It is forbidden to slaughter within them. Responsa of R' Moshe of Trani, volume 3, siman 84; Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot HaTur; Olas Tamid, paragraph 1; Magen Avraham there; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3; Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1. The Eshel Avraham writes there: 'I am uncertain regarding the courtyard before the synagogue. Perhaps it is permitted when the slaughter is needed for the kapparos of Erev Yom Kippur. This matter deserves great attention. It is forbidden in a women's section where people pray. Regarding the antechamber, even though people pray there, it was explicitly built conditionally; this requires further study.' Petach HaDevir, paragraph 1, cites him. See there, where he discusses this at length and writes that one may slaughter in a synagogue courtyard where people do not pray. If people pray there when space is limited, however, one may not slaughter there. He writes that the same applies to the open courtyard of a Talmud Torah where no one studies, either regularly or occasionally, because all study takes place in its rooms and the courtyard is used only for entering and leaving. It is permitted to slaughter there; see there. See below, paragraph 10, and the beginning of siman 154.
3) One may not demean a person in a synagogue, because this demeans the honor of the Shechinah. Maharil, siman 192; Pesakim U'Kesavim, siman 210; Responsa of R' Shlomo HaLevi, Yoreh De'ah, siman 8; Knesset HaGedolah there; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1.
4) One person sits and stretches out his leg; another sits while leaning on his hand; another stands and paces back and forth; another chats with his fellow about idle matters. Another diverts his attention and looks this way and that. These and other practices are neither seemly nor proper in the House of God. Sefer HaZichronos, folio 64; Knesset HaGedolah there; Olas Tamid there.
5) If idolatry was worshiped in it, even on a regular basis, there is no prohibition against praying there. Responsa of R' Eliyahu Mizrachi, volume 1, siman 79. There he strongly protested against one who wished to say that it was forbidden to pray in a synagogue in which the attendant was discovered committing an immoral act with a youth. Knesset HaGedolah there; Olas Tamid there; Ateres Zekeinim, paragraph 1. See below, siman 154, paragraph 82.
6) A person should not kiss his children in a synagogue, to demonstrate that there is no love like the love of the Holy One, blessed is He. Binyamin Ze'ev, siman 163, in the name of Sefer HaAgudah. So too writes Sefer Chassidim, siman 255; Knesset HaGedolah there. The Rama also writes this above, in the gloss to siman 98, at the end of seif 1. This implies, however, that one may kiss the hands of a Torah scholar, for this honors Hashem, as it is written, 'You shall fear Hashem your God,' which includes Torah scholars. Likewise, in a place where the custom is to kiss the hand of one's father or rav when he descends from an aliyah to the Sefer Torah, it is permitted, for this too is a mitzvah. So too writes Ben Ish Chai, parashas Vayikra, paragraph 11.
7) If one found an object in a synagogue courtyard, he acquires it, and we do not say that the courtyard acquires it for the sacred property. Agudah, beginning of Me'ilah, section 49; Magen Avraham, siman 154, paragraph 23; Eliyah Rabbah, end of paragraph 1. The Eshel Avraham writes in siman 154, paragraph 23, that the same law applies if one found it inside the synagogue itself; see there. This refers to an object that has no identifying mark, when he also did not see from whom it fell. Otherwise, he must announce it or return it, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 262, seifim 3 and 4; see there.
8) There: And idle conversation. Sefer Pesach Einayim writes: 'One who speaks mundane conversation in a synagogue would be better off not coming at all, for he sins and causes others to sin. The Satan prosecutes and says, Today is too short for him to speak, so he waits until the time of prayer. This includes Torah students who engage in pilpul during prayer and disturb the congregation with their shouting. They do not act properly. Although the Sages said that Torah study is equal to all the mitzvos, there is a time for Torah study, etc. Furthermore, unlearned people will draw an a fortiori inference from them and will likewise not remain silent to hear the prayer from the shaliach tzibbur.' Siddur Beis Oved, paragraph 2, cites him. See Siddur Beis Oved there, where he discusses this at length. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1, similarly writes that one must rebuke anyone who speaks mundane conversation in a synagogue. This applies even when the conversation is not idle, for idle conversation is otherwise forbidden wherever it occurs, and one who engages in it transgresses at least a rabbinic positive commandment. Even speech needed for earning a livelihood, which is permitted outside, is forbidden in a synagogue. See Responsa Edus B'Yosef, volume 2, siman 31, where he explains the severity of speaking in a synagogue, particularly during prayer, its punishment, its judgment, and its rectification. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 4, cites him. The holy Zohar, parashas Terumah, folio 131b, states: 'Woe to one who speaks mundane words in a synagogue, for he demonstrates separation. Woe to him, for he diminishes faith. Woe to him, for he has no share in the God of Israel. He demonstrates that he has no God, that God is not present there, that he has no share in Him and does not fear Him, and he treats the supernal order above with contempt.' Shaar HaKavanos, folio 4a, states: 'I saw my master, of blessed memory, take the utmost care never to speak at all in a synagogue, even outside the time of prayer. He almost refrained even from speaking words of mussar, rebuke, and repentance, lest this lead to any mundane speech.' Magen Avraham, paragraph 3, cites this. See also what we wrote above in siman 132, paragraph 7.
8) On holy Shabbos there is an additional prohibition. Not only is mundane conversation forbidden even for one's own needs, but speech concerning the needs of the synagogue is also forbidden, as stated in the holy Zohar, parashas Vayakhel, folio 205b: 'Once the holy people enter the synagogue, it is forbidden to occupy oneself even with the needs of the synagogue, but only with words of praise, prayer, and Torah. One who occupies himself with other matters and worldly speech is a person who desecrates Shabbos and has no share among the Jewish people. Two angels are appointed over this on Shabbos. They place their hands upon his head and say, Woe to so-and-so, who has no share in the Holy One, blessed is He. Therefore, one must occupy himself with prayer, songs, praises of his Master, and Torah.' The reason is that the resting of the Shechinah in a synagogue on Shabbos is unlike its resting there on a weekday. One must therefore be especially stringent, and it is forbidden to occupy oneself even with synagogue needs. Shalmei Tzibbur, folio 12b; Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 1.
8) The Rokeach writes in Tikkunei Teshuvah that one who habitually speaks mundane matters in a synagogue, even outside the time of prayer, should fast for forty days and receive lashes each day. From then onward he should sit there with great reverence, pray with kavanah, and not speak at all in the synagogue. Chesed LaAlafim cites him there.
9) There: One may not eat or engage in conversation in them, etc. One who is not a Torah scholar may not drink water in a synagogue or beis midrash. Responsa of R' Shlomo ben Tashbetz, siman 274. The current custom is that people drink water in the synagogue. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 2. This means that if they are sitting there to study it is permitted, for although they are not Torah scholars, it is allowed so that they will not have to interrupt their study.
10) There: One may not eat or drink in them, etc. The same applies to the courtyard when people sometimes pray there. The garden surrounding a synagogue, however, has no sanctity at all. Responsa of Mahari Bassan, siman 104; Machazik Berachah, paragraphs 1 and 2. See below, siman 152, paragraph 5, and above, paragraph 2.
10) There: One may not eat or drink in them, etc. The same applies to smoking a tobacco pipe there, for this too is included in levity, as R' Chaim Palagi writes in Sefer Chaim, siman 45, paragraph 5; see there. Yafeh LaLev cites him in Kuntres Yosher Levav, paragraph 5. See below, paragraph 13. Likewise, one may not perform work there. Rashi on Megillah 28a, s.v. mafshilin chavalim; Mishnah Berurah, paragraph 3. It is also forbidden to teach there writing and reading in other languages. Jewish script that is half Ashuri, which is called Rashi script, is permitted, and all the more so actual Ashuri script. Ben Ish Chai, parashas Vayikra, paragraph 4.
11) There: One may not enter them because of the heat, etc. One who flees in fear from an enemy pursuing him furiously with a drawn sword may hide inside a synagogue, for nothing stands in the way of saving a life. Nevertheless, once his mind has settled, he can rectify the matter by reading a little in the synagogue, as written in this seif: if one needs to enter them for his own needs, etc., he should enter and read a little, etc. He thereby avoids any concern of prohibition. Petach HaDevir, paragraph 3.
12) There: Torah scholars and their students are permitted, etc. The Taz, paragraph 1, writes that guests may eat there, for it is stated in siman 269 that guests eat in the synagogue, and Kiddush is thereby recited in the place of the meal. The Beit Yosef also cites this in this siman in the name of the Ramban. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 3, similarly writes in the name of R' Yeshayah the First, in Responsa Beit Yosef, siman 88, that even in a synagogue in Eretz Yisrael, guests may eat and drink there although they are not Torah scholars, for the prohibition was stated only regarding the residents of the city. Erech HaShulchan, paragraph 1, cites him and writes that this is also the view of the Sefer Mitzvos Gadol, positive commandment 29, and Orchos Chaim, folio 63, because it is considered a seudas mitzvah, which is permitted; see there. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2, however, questions the Taz's words, for Tosafos on Megillah 28a write that the case there concerns a room adjoining the synagogue, while eating in the synagogue itself is forbidden. Tosafos and the Rosh write the same at the beginning of Bava Basra. Furthermore, the Taz's statement that the Beit Yosef wrote this in the name of the Ramban is no proof, for that source concerns synagogues in Bavel that were built conditionally, where such use is permitted just as a condition is effective for an aron and sacred accessories in siman 154, seif 8. Magen Avraham in siman 269, paragraph 2, likewise writes that the case there concerns a house adjoining the synagogue, but eating in the synagogue itself is forbidden; see there. Birkei Yosef in Shiyurei Berachah to this siman, paragraph 3, also writes regarding the Taz's ruling that this does not appear to be the view of the poskim; see there. This is likewise the view of the Ran at the beginning of the last chapter of Pesachim, the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 29 of Hilchos Shabbos, the Meiri at the end of Megillah, and Tosafos and the Mordechai there. They maintain that even in a synagogue in Bavel, a condition is ineffective for this purpose until after it is destroyed; see there. See Responsa Rashbash, siman 274, where he discusses this at length and writes that a Torah scholar requires no condition, but for guests who are not Torah scholars one must explicitly stipulate this; see there. Erech HaShulchan cites him there. See also Shiltei HaGibborim on the Rif, chapter Bnei HaIr, paragraph 3, who writes in the name of Riaz that the Gemara's statement that the synagogues of Bavel are built conditionally refers only to guests, since travelers passing from place to place are common in Bavel; see there. This matter is therefore subject to a dispute. Some say that even in a synagogue in Bavel a condition is ineffective for this purpose until after its destruction; some say that it is effective even while the building stands; and some say that even in a synagogue in Eretz Yisrael, where a condition is ineffective, guests may eat there. Therefore, since the matter is disputed, guests should initially take care, whenever possible, not to eat in a synagogue, even outside Eretz Yisrael. In pressing circumstances they may rely upon the permissive opinions. See below, seif 11.
