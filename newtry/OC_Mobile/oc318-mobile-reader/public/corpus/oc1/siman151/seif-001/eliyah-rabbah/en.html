One does not conduct oneself in them, etc. A person must not treat the honor of the Shechinah lightly. Mahari Weil. In Eliyah Zuta I wrote that I found a note stating that when one sneezes in a synagogue people should not say 'health,' citing Maimoniyot. In Gan Nata a printing error attributes this to the Taz; it should say Eliyah Zuta. I have now looked and cannot find where Maimoniyot says this. On the contrary, the Rambam, chapter 4 of Hilchot Talmud Torah, and likewise the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh Deah siman 246, state this law specifically concerning a beit midrash, which implies that in a synagogue it is permitted. They add that the sanctity of a beit midrash is greater than that of a synagogue. Nor can the note mean only a beit midrash, because then it adds nothing to the Shulchan Aruch, which itself records the law in Yoreh Deah. Why would it cite only Maimoniyot and not the Tur and Shulchan Aruch? Furthermore, Kesef Mishneh and Beit Yosef there explain that the source is the Gemara in the chapter Elu Devarim, where it says that in the academy of Rabban Gamliel they would not say 'health' because it interrupted Torah study. What source is there for a synagogue? It is difficult to say that it similarly constitutes interruption of prayer. Moreover, even in a beit midrash the Shach there, citing the Perishah, writes that the prohibition applied particularly in earlier generations, when people never looked away from their books and certainly did not converse. Nowadays, when people are less careful in any event, they may say 'health.' Although the Taz there is stringent, his reason is honor of Torah; see there. Lechem Mishneh further asks that the Gemara establishes the baraita according to Beit Shammai, implying that according to Beit Hillel it is permitted, and he gives a strained answer. It seems to me that this can be resolved by first observing that the Rambam and Tur imply the prohibition is because of the sanctity of the beit midrash, which is surprising because the baraita explicitly gives the reason as interruption of study. This also answers how Rabban Gamliel's household could act according to Beit Shammai. They held that even Beit Hillel prohibits it because of the sanctity of the beit midrash. When the Gemara says the baraita supports Beit Shammai, that refers only to the stated reason of interruption of study; according to Beit Hillel there is another reason, the sanctity of the beit midrash. In any event, it seems proper to be lenient in a synagogue even while engaged in the prayer service and permit the interruption. See Sefer Chasidim siman 1011: if one finds an ownerless object in a synagogue courtyard, he acquires it, and we do not say that the courtyard acquires it for sacred property. Agudah, beginning of Meilah.
And some say that in a beit midrash, etc. This refers to Torah scholars and their students. Since they are permitted to eat or drink in a synagogue in pressing circumstances, they may do so in a beit midrash even without pressing circumstances, because otherwise Torah study would be disrupted. Other people, and apparently Torah scholars who are not studying there, are prohibited. This applies specifically to eating and drinking. Magen Avraham writes that other uses, such as entering merely to escape the rain, are forbidden even for Torah scholars, and all agree to this, because Torah scholars too are commanded to revere a sacred place. However, I saw in Semak, siman 66, that Torah scholars are permitted in all these matters, as stated in the Yerushalmi concerning the dust of the synagogue and the scribes and their students. Nevertheless, one should be lenient only in pressing circumstances. Even sleeping there in a bed would appear permitted in such circumstances, as implied by the Yerushalmi cited by the Ran concerning lodging donkey drivers and guests. But when there is a guard in the synagogue, one should not sleep there in a bed, as stated at the beginning of Tamid, though perhaps that case can be distinguished. I have seen the practice that when new communal officers or gabbaim are installed in the synagogue, ordinary people eat and drink there. Perhaps it is considered as though the synagogue was built on that condition because this is a communal and synagogue need, as in seif 4. This requires study. The Taz writes that guests may eat there, as stated in siman 180 regarding Kiddush, and says the Beit Yosef writes similarly in the name of the Ramban. I am surprised, because Tosafot Megillah 28 explains that the case there concerns a room adjacent to the synagogue, while eating in the synagogue itself is forbidden. Tosafot and the Rosh at the beginning of Bava Batra write similarly. Nor is the Beit Yosef's citation from the Ramban a proof, because it concerns Babylonian synagogues, which were built conditionally, where such use is permitted just as a condition can permit use of an Aron and other sacred appurtenances in siman 154, seif 8. Nevertheless, frivolous conduct remains prohibited, as explained in seif 11.
