Nevertheless, he himself must read, etc. Beit Yosef writes this in the name of the Rosh, because it is unreasonable for him to recite a blessing over the chazzan’s reading. This is difficult to me. The Tosefta and Yerushalmi, cited by Mordechai in the chapter Bnei HaIr, relate an incident in which Rabbi Meir read while seated in the synagogue of Tivon, gave the scroll to another person, and that person recited the blessing over it. The Yerushalmi asks: Can one person read and another recite the blessing? Rabbi Yirmiyah answers: From here we learn that one who hears is considered as though he recited. Why, then, should we not say the same here, that another person recites the blessing and the chazzan reads? This appears to support what Beit Yosef writes in the name of Agudah and Nimukei Yosef, that the blind person discussed in the chapter HaKorei Omed may not read from the Torah because one may not read it by heart. If, however, another person is appointed to open the scroll and look inside, while the blind person recites the blessing and stands beside him, it is permitted. Beit Yosef already cites authorities who prohibit one person from reading while another recites the blessing, in which case a blind person is disqualified from Torah reading, and authorities who permit it. He agrees with those who prohibit it, and rules accordingly in siman 139. Levush writes that he saw a blind person receive an aliyah in the presence of great authorities. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, writes likewise, except that he requires the blind person to be a Torah scholar and not an ignoramus. From the proof discussed above, it likewise appears to me that the matter is fully permitted.
