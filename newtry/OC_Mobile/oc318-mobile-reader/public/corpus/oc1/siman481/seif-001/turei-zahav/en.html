In the Tur he brings the words of Rabbeinu Hai regarding the fifth cup, as follows: one who wishes to drink the fifth cup shall say upon it the Great Hallel and leave yehalelucha and say it afterward (meaning: on the fourth cup he says Hallel and leaves yehalelucha on the fifth cup upon which he says the Great Hallel, and there he finishes yehalelucha); but now the disciples have come and said that on every cup one needs a blessing afterward as before it, and one says yehalelucha after Hallel and they conclude "the King Who is extolled with praises," and they drink the fifth cup and say upon it the Great Hallel and conclude in it "the soul of every living being" until "Who chooses." Beit Yosef expanded in explaining this matter, and it appears to me that the meaning is: certainly there is no concluding blessing on Hallel; rather initially the concluding blessing of "Who chooses" was also applicable to the Hallel that he said on the fourth cup, just as the final blessing that is on the vine is also applicable to the third cup — so too here with the concluding blessing; but since the disciples said that on the vine one needs on every cup, therefore also the concluding blessing of "Who chooses" is applicable only to the Great Hallel of the fifth cup, not to the Hallel of the fourth cup — you have no concluding blessing on Hallel; therefore perforce they concluded on the fourth cup with "the King Who is extolled" and on the fifth cup with "Who chooses," and he responded to this that it is not proper, for the Sages did not say two blessings — meaning two concluding blessings — but one of them; if so, why do we practice only on four cups and we conclude in yehalelucha "the King Who is extolled"? If one erred and concluded after yishtabach with "Who chooses," he should not again conclude after Nishmas with "the King Who is extolled," for behold even on two cups there would be only one concluding blessing — how much more so on one cup; rather he shall say the Great Hallel until the concluding blessing and no more — thus it appears to me, plainly.
