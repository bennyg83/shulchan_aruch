It is a mitzvah to eat, etc. For it is written, And you shall afflict your souls on the ninth of the month in the evening. It should have written, On the ninth of the month in the evening you shall afflict your souls until evening, etc. Since it says, And you shall afflict, etc., on the ninth of the month, it implies that they should fast on the ninth; but in truth Yom Kippur is only on the tenth of the month. Chazal received that, on the contrary, there is a mitzvah from the Torah to eat on Erev Yom Kippur. The Holy One, blessed is He, wanted to give reward for the eating as though they had fasted, for a mitzvah that involves distress is not comparable, as they said: according to the pain is the reward. Had it written, On the ninth of the month you shall eat, we would have reward only as one who fulfills His command through eating. Therefore Scripture changed and wrote the mitzvah of eating in the language of fasting, so that this eating should be considered before the Holy One, blessed is He, as though it were a fast, in order to give reward like one who fulfills a mitzvah through the distress of affliction. A person should reduce his learning on Erev Yom Kippur in order to eat and drink.
Even a fast for a dream. However, if he fears for himself and wants to fast, he should fast until the concluding meal, for certainly, according to the law, it is enough that he eat once. If he does not fast today at all, it is good that he fast some other day after Yom Kippur. If Erev Yom Kippur falls on Sunday, and on the preceding Shabbos he fasted a fast for a dream, for which he must observe a fast for his fast after Shabbos, he should not fast on Erev Yom Kippur; rather, he should delay it until after the festival passes. According to the Taz above, in siman 288, s.k. 3, he need not fast again after Yom Kippur, for Yom Kippur atoned also for this; one for whom fasting is difficult may rely on this. One who vowed not to eat meat except on Yom Tov: it was explained in siman 570 that he may eat on Erev Yom Kippur, for in human speech it is called Yom Tov. This is not only at the concluding meal, but even at Shacharis, for we are accustomed to eat meat also at Shacharis, and anyone who vows does so with the common custom in mind. But on the night of Erev Yom Kippur it is forbidden. This applies specifically to one who actually vowed. But one who did not vow explicitly, and merely has such a practice not to eat meat except on days when Tachanun is not said, may eat even on the night of Erev Yom Kippur; specifically at actual night, but not before then, even if he prayed Maariv.
