That he not interrupt between, etc. Olat Tamid asks that above, siman 177, it appears that only speech constitutes an interruption. He answers that speech is an interruption even for less than the time needed to say a short greeting, whereas silence requires longer than that. He concludes that the practical ruling requires study. In my humble opinion, perhaps here the restriction is only initially, whereas after the fact he need not bless again, and so rules Magen Avraham. Another possibility is that silence, even longer than the usual brief interval, is not an interruption unless during the pause he did not expect to eat or drink immediately, making it comparable to distraction of mind. There is some proof from Mateh Moshe and Knesset HaGedolah in siman 203, citing Sefer Chasidim, that if after blessing he is told that the seasonal turning point has arrived, he should wait silently until he knows with certainty that it has passed and then drink the water. This implies that even a longer silence is not an interruption. The same appears somewhat from seif 5 concerning one who blessed before the item was brought before him, and likewise from Riaz cited by Shiyarei Knesset HaGedolah. The matter still requires study. I later found that Birkat Avraham, page 174, states explicitly that even silence longer than the usual brief interval is not an interruption, and distinguishes between blessings of praise, such as the blessing on lightning below in siman 227, and blessings over benefit. See there at length.
[Levush] My master and teacher, etc. Shiyarei Knesset HaGedolah discusses at length that the word "and teacher" should be deleted, because "Peace to you, my master" alone is within the standard brief interval. I found the same in Maadanei Melech, chapter Merubah. I explained the law of hands touching the ground in siman 74.
[Levush] Whether in his right hand, etc. Solet Belulah writes that one should not stick the fruit over which he blesses onto a knife, because the right hand represents the primary side of life, and then two opposites are combined in one object. Sefer Chasidim, siman 109, writes that if one asks his fellow, "Pass me the sefer," he should take it with his right hand and not his left.
