One need not specify the sin. In the Tur, he first wrote the dispute whether one must specify the sin, and ruled like Rabbi Akiva, that one need not specify. Afterward he wrote: sins about which he confessed and did not repeat them, Rav Yitzchak Giyyat wrote that one should not confess except that if he stole or robbed and confessed over them the previous year, he should say in place of "I stole," "I acted corruptly," which is a general expression. This applies when one prays by himself, but when he says it with the public there is no concern, for it is impossible that there is not one among the public who stole or robbed. And my father and master wrote that he may confess over them even though he did not repeat them, end quote. His words are difficult one against the other, for at the end of his words it implies that one must specify, since even according to Rav Yitzchak Giyyat one must at least once say "I stole," while at the beginning he wrote that one need not specify. It seems to me that there is a distinction: at the beginning he speaks where no specific sin is clear to him, but he says the confession generally, regarding what a person normally does throughout the year. If he did not do this, his fellow did, and all Israel are responsible for one another. In this he ruled that one need not specify. But in the end he speaks of transgressions he himself did, and in this everyone agrees that he must specify that sin; they disagree only about a second confession. His conclusion is that he may confess a second time and mention it. Even in the Gemara one can say that the baraisa first brings the dispute about one who already confessed and did not repeat the sin, and afterward says, "and one must specify the sin," Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava says this and Rabbi Akiva says he need not specify. This is difficult: at first the tanna assumes simply that according to everyone one must specify, so what does it begin afterward, that one must specify, etc.? Rather, it is as I explained: at first it speaks where the sin is clear to him personally, and everyone agrees that he specifies; afterward it speaks of one who confesses generally, whether he must specify. Although it brings the verse of "gods of gold," and there the sin was clear, nevertheless not all the people sinned with the calf, for many did not sin with it. Rather, certainly because of mutual responsibility he attributed the sin to all of them, and we see nevertheless that he specified the sin. From this we learn for every confession that an ordinary person confesses, as it seems to me according to the Tur's view.
He has permission. This is difficult for me: according to this, what does the Gemara ask against Rabbi Akiva, who holds one need not specify, from the verse "and they made for themselves gods of gold"? He has permission to specify. In the Beis Yosef he wrote that according to Rabbi Akiva, in any event, one who does not specify his sins is more praiseworthy. According to this there is no difficulty, for Moshe Rabbeinu certainly ought to have acted in the more praiseworthy way. But according to what is written here simply, that he has permission, it is difficult. One can say that the Shulchan Aruch ruled here by way of compromise: according to Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava, there is a prohibition if he does not specify, and according to Rabbi Akiva it is more praiseworthy if he does not specify. He ruled that at least one has permission to specify, relying in this on Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava and granting permission to specify. This appears to me to be the Shulchan Aruch's view.
It is not called specifying. This requires study, for the Tur wrote explicitly that according to the one who holds one need not specify, one should not say Al Chet. The Rema should not have written that it is not called specifying, which implies according to everyone. Rather, he should have written that one should say Al Chet according to the view that one must specify.
