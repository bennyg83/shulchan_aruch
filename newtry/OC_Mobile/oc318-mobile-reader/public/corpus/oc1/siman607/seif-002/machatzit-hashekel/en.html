(Subparagraph 2) And if, etc. It implies that it speaks even aloud, for afterward he wrote, "and if he confesses quietly, etc.," implying that until now he spoke of aloud. Even so, he has permission to specify.
And it seems to me that specifically, etc. Meaning not as appears from the words of the Beis Yosef, that even with a sin that is not public, one may specify aloud, for he wrote generally in the Shulchan Aruch and did not distinguish between public and not public. This follows his approach in his Beis Yosef, and I need to set out the matter briefly. It is stated in Yoma 86b: one must specify the sin, as it is said, "Please, this people has sinned a great sin, and they made themselves gods of gold," the words of Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava. Rabbi Akiva says: "fortunate is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered." Rather, what Moshe said, "and they made themselves gods of gold," is like Rabbi Yannai, etc.: the silver and gold You increased for them, etc., caused them to make gods of gold. This is our reading. But the Rif and Rosh read: Rabbi Akiva says, he need not; rather, how do I fulfill "and they made themselves gods of gold"? Like Rabbi Yannai, etc. The Tur wrote that the law is that one need not specify, like Rabbi Akiva, for the law follows Rabbi Akiva against his colleague; and the Rambam ruled like Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava. The Beis Yosef wrote that the Rambam's reason is that Rav necessarily holds like Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava, for it is stated there: Rav Yehudah said in Rav's name, he raised a contradiction: it is written "fortunate is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered," and it is written "one who covers his transgressions will not succeed." It is not difficult: here with a public sin, here with a sin that is not public. When it is not public, "fortunate, etc., whose sin is covered." Rav Nachman said: here with transgressions between man and his fellow, "one who covers, etc., will not succeed," for by revealing it many will plead before the one he sinned against so that he forgive him; and here with transgressions between man and the Omnipresent, about which it says "fortunate is he whose transgression is forgiven, etc." Rav necessarily does not hold like Rabbi Akiva, for the sin of the calf was public and even so Rabbi Akiva needed to answer the verse "and they made themselves gods of gold" like Rabbi Yannai, etc. One must therefore say that he holds that even a public sin should not be specified; necessarily Rav holds like Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava, and therefore the Rambam ruled like him. The Beis Yosef's conclusion is: regarding practical law, since the Rif and Rosh agreed to rule like Rabbi Akiva, so we adopt. Nevertheless, it seems that if he wants to specify the sin, he has permission, for Rabbi Akiva did not forbid specifying; he only came to say that one who does not specify his sins is more fortunate. Perhaps Rabbi Akiva said this only where he specifies aloud, but quietly it is proper to specify so that he be more ashamed, and it is still "whose sin is covered" since it is not heard by people, end quote of the Beis Yosef. Based on this he ruled here in the Shulchan Aruch that one need not specify, like Rabbi Akiva, and nevertheless if he wants to specify aloud, he has permission; quietly it is more preferable to specify, as his conclusion in the Beis Yosef. But regarding this one must say that he retracted from what he wrote in his book, that aloud one is more fortunate when he does not specify. Rather, nevertheless, if he wants, he may specify aloud, though he is not so fortunate. In the Shulchan Aruch he wrote generally, "and if he wants to specify," meaning aloud, as the Magen Avraham wrote, "he has permission," and he did not conclude that if he does not specify he is more fortunate. This implies that he holds that in truth one who does not specify is not more fortunate than one who specifies. So too wrote the Taz. Perhaps in the Shulchan Aruch he relied on the reading of the Rif and Rosh, who copied Rabbi Akiva's language as "he need not specify," as above, implying that whether he specifies or does not specify is equal, and neither is more fortunate than the other. So too wrote the Chemdas Moshe. The Magen Avraham disagrees with this, and says that with a sin that is not public, according to everyone it is forbidden to say it aloud, as will be explained.
For certainly the amoraim do not disagree with the tanna'im. At first glance one should emend it to read: the amoraim do not disagree in the dispute of the tanna'im. For according to the Beis Yosef, Rav necessarily holds like Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava and not like Rabbi Akiva; and Rav Nachman, who holds that even a public sin should not be specified except that specifically a sin between man and his fellow should be specified, necessarily holds like Rabbi Akiva, for according to Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava, the sin of the calf was between man and the Omnipresent, and even so Moshe specified it according to Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava. Rather, he necessarily holds like Rabbi Akiva and explains that Moshe did not specify the sin of the calf except to give an excuse for Israel like Rabbi Yannai, as the Beis Yosef wrote. This is forced, that Rav and Rav Nachman should follow tanna'im.
Rather, Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava holds that before the Omnipresent, etc. And Rabbi Akiva holds even before the Omnipresent, etc.; and in this there is no distinction. That is, unlike the Beis Yosef, who holds that the dispute of Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava and Rabbi Akiva and the dispute of Rav and Rav Nachman are of one kind, but that Rav and Rav Nachman stated their matter according to everyone. Rav and Rav Nachman speak of one who confesses aloud, where people hear. In this, Rav holds there is a distinction: with a public sin, "one who covers his transgressions will not succeed," so that he should be ashamed and atoned; but with a sin that is not public he should not specify, for it is disgraceful, as they said, "one who specifies his sins is brazen." Rav Nachman distinguishes between transgressions between man and his fellow or between him and the Omnipresent. But Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava and Rabbi Akiva disagree when one confesses quietly before the Omnipresent and no person hears. In that case there is no distinction between public and not public, for everything is revealed and known before Him, blessed be He. Nor is it relevant there that he should be ashamed, for what difference is there whether he specifies or not? In both cases the shame is the same before Him, blessed be He, since everything is revealed before Him. Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava holds that one should specify because so we find with Moshe Rabbeinu. Rabbi Akiva holds there is no proof from Moshe, like Rabbi Yannai, and if so there is no proof that before the Omnipresent he should specify; therefore he need not specify, since shame is not relevant, as above. Since the dispute of Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava and Rabbi Akiva is about quiet confession, one may specify quietly: not only according to the one who rules like Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava, who says one must specify, but even according to the one who rules like Rabbi Akiva, nevertheless he may specify, only Rabbi Akiva holds that he need not, as the Beis Yosef wrote according to his approach. But aloud, the tanna'im did not speak, and one should rule like Rav, that with a sin that is not public it is forbidden to specify. So ruled the Raavad in chapter 2 of the Laws of Repentance, and according to the Kesef Mishneh this is also the Rambam's view, except that the Rif and Rosh ruled like Rav Nachman. The Magen Avraham's wording, "and Rabbi Akiva holds even before the Omnipresent, etc.," is not precise in the word "even," and this is easy to understand.
And in the Bach, unlike the Beis Yosef. See there, for the Bach also wrote similarly to the Magen Avraham. For practical law he holds like the Magen Avraham.
And there is a difficulty from what Rashi wrote in Chullin, etc. For we learned there: one who slaughters outside, and says he slaughters for the sake of something brought as a vow or gift, such as for the sake of a burnt-offering, his slaughter is invalid because of appearance. One who sees will think that now he consecrates it, and he is found slaughtering consecrated animals outside, and they will err that it is permitted to slaughter consecrated animals outside. Also, when they see that people eat from this slaughter, they will err that if consecrated animals were slaughtered outside, they are permitted to eat. Therefore his slaughter is invalid. But if he slaughtered for the sake of something that does not come as a vow or gift, such as for the sake of a sin-offering, it is valid, for there is no reason to say he now consecrates it as a voluntary offering, since it does not come as a voluntary offering, and those who hear know there is nothing in his words. Rabbi Yochanan said that if he was liable to bring a sin-offering and slaughtered and said "for the sake of my sin-offering," his slaughter is invalid, because those who see will say that he slaughtered it for his sin-offering that he is obligated to bring. Rashi asked: if so, even if he is not liable to bring a sin-offering, let it be invalid because of concern that those who see will say he is liable to bring a sin-offering. Rashi answered that there is no concern for this, for if he had sinned and become liable to bring a sin-offering, it would have a voice, etc.
And sin means inadvertent. This implies that even with inadvertent sin it is good to cover it and not publicize it, for even with inadvertence there is brazenness and disgrace if he publicizes, although it happened to him inadvertently, for "also without knowledge the soul is not good."
And one can answer that there it is different, etc. We see that he is not ashamed. With this his difficulty from Sotah is resolved. Also from the verse "whose sin is covered" one can answer this way, for we see that he violated the verse "whose sin is covered," since he said "for the sake of my sin-offering." If so, according to his mistaken thinking it is publicized in order that he be atoned.
There in Sotah, one can ask somewhat: from where does the Talmud know that the reason is not to embarrass? Perhaps the reason is as it is written, "fortunate, etc., whose sin is covered."
