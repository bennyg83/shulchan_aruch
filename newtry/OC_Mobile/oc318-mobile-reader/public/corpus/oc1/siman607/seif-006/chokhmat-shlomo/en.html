There, seif 6. "The whole congregation receives forty lashes after the Minchah prayer, etc." NB: I will copy here one branch of thought that arose in my mind; may it be that this teaching be fulfilled in us. The Sages said in Makkot 23 that all who are liable to karet and received lashes are exempted from their karet; this is the view of Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel. I saw in the book Orach Chayim on Pesachim, Frankfurt edition, in the introduction, that he brought the question of the Baal HaMaor against the Rambam, who ruled like Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel. It is proven, he argued, that his colleagues disagree with him, for it is explicit at the beginning of the chapter Elu Hen HaLokin that Rabbi Akiva holds that those liable to karet are included in forty lashes, for if they repented, the heavenly court forgives them. That is, lest you say how can we punish him with two punishments, he said that if he repents they forgive him and there are not two punishments. But if Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel were correct, in any case there would not be two punishments, proving that his colleagues disagree with him. Why, then, did the Rambam rule so? The Ramban answers his question that he does hold like Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel, and the meaning is this: since we hold that one liable to two death penalties is judged with the more severe one, why should they give him lashes? Let him be judged with the more severe, karet. Therefore he said that lashes are more severe, for with karet, if he repents, he is forgiven, whereas repentance does not help for lashes. In my poverty I say: although I am dust beneath their feet and am as nothing compared to the Baal HaMaor and the Ramban, nevertheless in this matter their words are not correct. First, what the Baal HaMaor wrote, that Rabbi Akiva came to explain why we punish him with two punishments, is not so. The rule that one may not obligate him for two punishments applies only in human court, for we derive it from "according to his wickedness," that for one wickedness you may obligate him and not for two wickednesses; this means the court cannot obligate him in two. But where one punishment is by human hands and one by Heaven, it is not considered two punishments. Also, the answer is not correct, for what does it help that if he repents they forgive him? In the end, perhaps he will not repent, and there will be two punishments. And the Ramban's answer, that Rabbi Akiva came to prove that lashes are more severe than karet, is astonishing, with due honor. For a moment the words of the Gemara in Ketubbot 33b escaped him, where it says: perhaps lashes are more severe even than court-imposed death, as they said, had they beaten Chananya, Mishael, and Azaryah, they would have worshipped the image. It is thus proven that lashes are more severe even than court-imposed death, where repentance also does not help. Why would Rabbi Akiva need the reason of repentance? True, the Gemara rejects this by distinguishing between a punishment that has a set measure and one that has no set measure, but it is still difficult: it should have brought support from this Mishnah, where Rabbi Akiva says that lashes are more severe because repentance does not help for them. This would prove that without this, lashes would not be more severe but lighter than karet; if so, against court-imposed death, where repentance also does not help, lashes are certainly lighter. Why did it not prove this from that Mishnah? Perforce, this is not the Mishnah's intent. Rather, the intent of the Mishnah appears as follows, with God's help. On the contrary, Rabbi Akiva's words accord with Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel. It is known from Yoma chapter 8 regarding the four divisions of atonement that for some sins repentance alone helps, while for others repentance alone does not help and suffering or death is required. For those liable to karet, repentance alone does not help; suffering is required. Therefore Rabbi Akiva says that those liable to karet are included in forty lashes, meaning that through lashes they are exempted from karet. He then explains how lashes can exempt from karet: if he had not been lashed, he would remain liable to karet even if he repented, because repentance alone is insufficient to atone for those liable to karet. But once he is lashed, then if he repents together with the lashes, the heavenly court forgives him. Therefore we give him lashes, for then it is in his power to repent and be exempted from karet. Thus, on the contrary, Rabbi Akiva holds like Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel, and the ruling of the poskim like Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel is well resolved. Examine carefully; this is good, with God's help.
There, seif 6, in the gloss. "Yom Kippur atones only for those who repent and believe in its atonement, but one who kicks against it and thinks in his heart, what does this Yom Kippur help, it does not atone for him." The language of the Yerushalmi, Yoma chapter 8 halacha 7, is as follows: One who says, "The olah does not atone for me," it atones. "It is impossible that it should atone for me," it does not atone. "Yom Kippur does not atone," it atones. "It is impossible that it should atone for me," it atones for him against his will. R. Chaggai son of R. Hillel said: not all from this; does a person say to the king, "You are not king"? This is the end of the text. Perforce this Yerushalmi follows Rabbi, who holds that whether one repented or did not repent, Yom Kippur atones. But the phrase "not all from this" has no explanation; see the Korban HaEdah, who was pressed on this, and below we will bring his words. In my humble opinion the Yerushalmi's intent is according to what we read in Shevuot 13, that Rabbi agrees regarding karet of the day; see there. This is the intent of the Yerushalmi: although Yom Kippur atones even without repentance, nevertheless "not all from this" means it does not atone for everything against his will, for it does not atone for karet of the day. Just as no person can say to the king, "You are not king," so too if one profanes the body of Yom Kippur by doing work or eating on it, it does not atone. This is clear in the Yerushalmi's intent. Or one may say more: the Yerushalmi's statement "not all from this" means that certainly one who denies and says Yom Kippur does not atone thereby commits a sin, for he does not believe in the Torah, which says, "for on this day He shall atone for you." Therefore Yom Kippur atones for him against his will for all sins in the Torah, but for this sin itself, that he does not believe Yom Kippur atones, it does not atone; this is like karet of the day. This is what the Yerushalmi says: "not all from this"; not for everything does He forgive, but "from this" law, meaning from this sin itself, it does not atone, for it is a great sin to say to the king, "You are not king." So too, for this sin itself, that he does not believe in Yom Kippur, it does not atone. This is well resolved; examine very carefully. The Korban HaEdah explained that nevertheless one who kicks against its atonement is not comparable to one who does not kick against it; see there. Besides the fact that this is forced, the sugya in the first chapter of Shevuot escaped him, that Rabbi agrees regarding karet of the day. Rather, the intent is as above: it certainly follows Rabbi, but one who kicks against Yom Kippur itself is not atoned, for that is like karet of the day, where Rabbi agrees that Yom Kippur does not atone. As for what is stated in Yoma, that one who says "I will sin and repent" twice is as Rav Huna explained, that once one transgresses and repeats it, etc., see its explanation in my work on the Torah, at the beginning of the year 5611, where I wrote about this in Parashat Bereishit; see there and examine carefully. Regarding what they asked against the Rambam, that he copied only one instance of "I will sin and repent," which is against the sugya of the Gemara, and regarding what the Gemara says there in Yoma, "let us say the Mishnah is not like Rabbi," and all that requires analysis in that sugya, see, with God's help, my work on the Torah from the year 5599, in the haftarah of Parashat Tazria, on the verse "and I will bow in the house of Rimmon." See there for correct matters and several laws that branch from this; see there and examine carefully.
