One need not - See Taz, Beis Yosef, and Bach about this. The Taz answered in another way. Even in the baraisa itself, Yoma 86, this is at first glance difficult: it first says sins about which he confessed on this Yom Kippur, implying that one must at least specify the sin, etc. In conclusion, according to the Taz, with a definite sin one must specify the sin on one Yom Kippur, and on the second Yom Kippur the law follows Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, whose teaching is measured and clean, and he repeats and specifies, as the Mechaber wrote in se'if 4. It implies that even with sins between man and his fellow, such as robbery and theft, even though the other forgave him and he returned the stolen item, he nevertheless confesses forever on Yom Kippur, for in any event he also sinned between man and the Omnipresent. But if he provoked his fellow with words, or a poor man pursuing a cake, and the like, one can say that since he asked forgiveness from his fellow and confessed on one Yom Kippur, he need not confess on a second Yom Kippur; or perhaps even on the first Yom Kippur he need not. This requires study. See Sefer Tosafos Yom ha-Kippurim. See in Sefer Tosafos Yom ha-Kippurim that the law follows Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov in a Mishnah and not in a baraisa, as in Chullin chapter 1 regarding blemishing the altar. Nevertheless, where his reason is compelling, the law follows Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov; see there.
