Some say. Minchah Gedolah. See Taz. In Pesachim 50b the Gemara asks, “Why specifically the eve of Pesach?” Rashi explains the question, and Bach explains that from the Mishnah's statement “until midday” alone we would not know that work is forbidden after midday. The Mishnah could have stated generally that in a place where the custom is to work on the eve of Pesach one may work, and we would know that this applies only until midday, since after midday labor is forbidden on the eve of a festival and on Friday as well. Because no Mishnah elsewhere states this law, the Gemara understood that it was obvious; see Bach. According to the Tur, the answer is straightforward: the Mishnah teaches that on the eve of Pesach labor becomes forbidden immediately after midday, whereas on Friday and the eve of a festival it becomes forbidden only at six and one-half hours. According to Beit Yosef's reading of Rashi, which has “nine hours,” the answer rejects the questioner's assumption: there, “from Minchah onward” means Minchah Ketanah, from nine hours onward, while the period immediately before that remains permitted. Rashi therefore explains “close beforehand” as the interval before nine hours, not merely the half-hour immediately preceding Minchah. Under this reading, it cannot mean that on Friday and the eve of a festival the half-hour before Minchah is also forbidden, for whenever the Sages prohibit labor at the time of Minchah, whether Minchah Gedolah or Minchah Ketanah, the preceding half-hour is treated like the time itself. Rashi therefore understands “close beforehand” to mean the interval from six until nine hours. Rashi's proof is that if the baraita meant Minchah Gedolah, it should have said that on Friday and the eve of a festival labor is forbidden from midday, because the half-hour before the prescribed time is certainly treated like the prescribed time. The distinction must therefore be substantial: on the eve of Pesach the prohibition begins at six hours, whereas on Friday it begins at nine hours, for an unspecified “Minchah” means Minchah Ketanah, the primary Minchah period; see siman 233. Rashi did not adopt the Re'em's explanation, for then the Gemara should not have said “here, from midday,” but rather “here, close to Minchah as well.” The phrase “close to Minchah” is not otherwise part of the discussion. Re'em, however, reads Rashi as saying “nine and one-half hours, close to Minchah,” meaning from the beginning of the ninth hour, and this is also the reading in Rashi printed with the Rif. According to him, the answer is that there, even the time close to Minchah, from nine onward, is permitted, while here even from midday it is forbidden. Rashi's proof is that if both refer to Minchah Gedolah, the Gemara should have said not “here, midday,” but “here, even close to Minchah.” Taz was dissatisfied even with Re'em's reading, because the unnecessary mention of “close” contributes nothing to the answer; it should simply contrast Minchah Ketanah there with midday here. Taz's approach may be explained in light of Ran: the question “Why specifically?” means that on Friday also the matter depends upon local custom throughout the period before midday, while after midday labor is forbidden, and the Mishnah should therefore have included Friday together with the eve of Pesach. Likewise, according to Rashi, it could have stated: in a place where the custom is to work on the eve of Pesach until midday, and on Friday and the eve of a festival until Minchah Ketanah, one may work; where the custom is not to work, one may not. This is Rashi's statement, “Why did it need to teach midday in order to inform us of this precise distinction?” The word “precise” is literal: the two laws could have been included together, Friday and the eve of a festival until Minchah Ketanah. The Gemara answers that they are not comparable. Here, on the eve of Pesach, the prohibition begins immediately at midday, whereas on Friday the half-hour from nine until nine and one-half is permitted by strict law and depends only on custom. Had the Mishnah said “until Minchah Ketanah,” one might have thought that the preceding half-hour was also prohibited. Bach, however, understood Rashi's word “precisely” differently, as though it said “this exact inference”; see there. The Tur's position fits especially well with Ran, for both cases extend until Minchah Gedolah: Friday therefore parallels the eve of Pesach before midday, where the matter depends on custom. According to the Gemara's second answer, however, one who works does not see a sign of blessing but is not placed under a ban. On that approach, a custom not to work before midday on Friday or the eve of a festival would not be binding, because the period after midday itself does not carry the severe prohibition of excommunication; such a custom would be mistaken and would require no formal release. See Ran. Ran also implies, as explained above, that on Friday there is an actual prohibition from Minchah onward, only without excommunication. Taz, by contrast, writes that there is no prohibition, merely that one does not see a sign of blessing. Shulchan Aruch cites without decision the views of Minchah Gedolah and Minchah Ketanah and does not state whether the preceding half-hour is permitted. According to the second answer, it may be said that the half-hour is forbidden, because every period close to the prescribed time is treated like the time itself. We are compelled to rule in accordance with the second answer, for according to the first answer the matter before midday on Friday would depend on local custom, yet no authority records such a rule. This appears to be the Mordechai's view when he writes that on Friday the time is Minchah Gedolah, namely half the day; see Eliyah Rabbah, note 3. On this reading, the person is rebuked and forcibly prevented from continuing. The phrase “does not see a sign of blessing” sometimes denotes no prohibition, but here an actual prohibition exists; otherwise the Gemara should have answered simply that there one does not see blessing, whereas here there is a prohibition. See Magen Avraham, where this will be explained further, God willing; and see note 2.
