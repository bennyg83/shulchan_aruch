Seif katan 1, One who performs labor, etc. The people of Beishan. In Pesachim 50b they asked Rabbi Yochanan whether they were permitted to travel on Friday from Tyre to Sidon. Rashi explains there that market day in Sidon was Friday. Since their fathers had been wealthy and had not needed that market day, they had adopted a stringency; but their descendants needed it because they were not wealthy, and they asked whether they were required to observe their fathers’ practice. In Shabbat 19a it states: “The Sages taught: One may not set sail less than three days before Shabbat,” etc.; “but from Tyre to Sidon it is permitted even on Friday.” Rashi writes: “Tyre to Sidon is only a journey of one day, and market day there was Friday, as stated in Pesachim, chapter Makom Shenahagu.” Rashi’s statement that Tyre to Sidon is a journey of one day cannot be literal. If it were, one who left Friday morning would arrive only toward evening, close to nightfall. Since market day was Friday, what benefit would there be in traveling there on Friday, when he could not arrive until night after the market had ended? Rather, Rashi means most of a day, with the greater part treated as the whole. Yet if commerce has the legal status of labor, so that it becomes forbidden at the same time as labor, their journey to Sidon would still have served no purpose. This is clear according to the opinion that labor becomes forbidden from Minchah Gedolah, six and one-half hours into the day: since they would arrive only after most of the day had passed, Minchah Gedolah would already have arrived and labor and commerce would already be forbidden. Even according to the opinion that the time is Minchah Ketanah, nine and one-half hours into the day, one would not undertake the journey merely for the short interval between Minchah Gedolah and Minchah Ketanah. Moreover, Rashi’s “journey of one day” must be imprecise, as explained, and cannot refer merely to the half-hour until six and one-half hours, Minchah Gedolah, for although this is more than half the day and thus most of it, Rashi would not therefore call it a journey of one day. It must mean that they remained on the road for several hours after midday. They would therefore arrive only when Minchah Ketanah had already come, or shortly before it, leaving almost no time for commerce. What benefit, then, was there in going? It must be that commerce remains permitted even during the time when labor is forbidden.
And this is also implied at the end of chapter 2 of Shabbat, etc. “To cause the city and its shops to cease.” The common understanding is that Magen Avraham proves his point from the teaching that six blasts were sounded on Friday: the first to cause the people to cease labor in the fields, and the second to cause the city and its shops to cease. They infer that labor was already forbidden from the first blast, while the shops remained open until the second. Eliyah Rabbah, Tosefet Shabbat, and Chemed Moshe therefore objected that the passage proves the opposite, because Rashi explains that the second blast caused the city to cease labor, as Magen Avraham himself writes in seif katan 4. Thus labor and the closing of shops began at the same time, from the second blast onward; the first blast merely caused those in the fields to stop, for the reason stated in Shabbat. In my humble opinion, this is not Magen Avraham’s meaning. According to their understanding, he should have cited the first blast as well, since his entire proof would be that although labor had to cease at the first blast, commerce remained permitted until the second. Their error arose because they treated commerce and an open shop as the same matter. In my view they are distinct: commerce means private trading, whereas a shop means selling publicly while the shop is open. See below, siman 539, regarding Chol HaMoed, where this distinction appears, and see also the laws of mourning. Thus, when Magen Avraham says commerce is permitted, he means privately, with the shop closed. He proves this from the second blast, which caused the city and its shops to cease, meaning that the shops had to close. The text in Shabbat states: “Six blasts,” etc.; “the first,” etc.; “and the shops were still open,” etc.; “they began the second blast,” etc.; “and the shops were locked.” Thus the second blast required only closing the shops. We do not find that it prohibited commerce, meaning even private trading. Had commerce itself become forbidden, the Gemara should have said so, and the obligation to close the shop would follow all the more. It must therefore be that private commerce remained permitted even after the second blast, when labor was already forbidden.
