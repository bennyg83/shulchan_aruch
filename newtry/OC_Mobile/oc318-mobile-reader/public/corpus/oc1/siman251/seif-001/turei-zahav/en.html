Some explain that it means minchah gedolah. In the chapter Makom Shenahagu it states: In a place where the custom is to perform labor on the eve of Pesach until midday, they may do so. The Gemara asks: Why specify the eve of Pesach? The same applies even on Friday, for it was taught that one who performs labor on Friday or on the eve of a festival from minchah onward will never see a sign of blessing from it. Rashi explains the question as follows: Why did the Mishnah teach that the matter depends upon custom only until midday, implying that after midday it is forbidden? Why was it necessary to teach midday in order to convey this inference, when labor is forbidden on every festival eve? The Gemara answers that there it is forbidden only from minchah onward, but shortly before minchah it is permitted, whereas here it is forbidden from midday. Alternatively, there he merely sees no sign of blessing, but he is not placed under a ban, whereas here he is also placed under a ban. Rashi explains that ‘from minchah onward’ means from nine and one-half hours into the day. According to Rashi, however, there is a major difference: on the eve of Pesach labor is forbidden from midday, after six hours, while on Friday it is not forbidden until minchah ketanah. What, then, was the questioner’s objection? It would appear that he assumed the Friday measure was likewise minchah gedolah. Why should we not retain that understanding in the Gemara’s answer? Moreover, why did the answer need to state that shortly before minchah is permitted? Who had mentioned ‘shortly before’? It appears clear to me that Rashi disclosed his intent when he wrote, in explaining the question, ‘Why was it necessary to teach midday?’ The question was not that the Mishnah should combine Friday and the eve of Pesach under one identical measure. Certainly Friday is measured from minchah ketanah, while the eve of Pesach is measured from midday. Rather, the Gemara asks that the Mishnah should teach each case according to its own measure, this one from midday and that one from minchah ketanah. Why did the Tanna formulate only the law of midday, which pertains solely to the eve of Pesach? The answer is that the measures do not correspond. On Friday, whose measure is minchah ketanah, the prohibition begins only at minchah ketanah, while shortly beforehand labor remains permitted. On the eve of Pesach, however, even the period shortly before minchah gedolah is forbidden, since the prohibition begins at midday, before six and one-half hours. Had the Mishnah taught the two together, one might have thought that Friday too is forbidden shortly before its stated minchah time. This, in my view, is Rashi’s clear and correct position, and there is no need for Maharshal’s emendation in Chochmas Shlomo. Maharam in Mordechai and the Tur, however, explain that Friday too is measured from minchah gedolah, six and one-half hours, while the eve of Pesach is treated more strictly regarding the preceding interval, for there the prohibition begins at midday. This approach is certainly straightforward according to the plain course of the Gemara. It appears, however, that Rashi declined to explain it this way because the chapter Arvei Pesachim likewise discusses Friday and festival eve regarding eating from minchah onward, and there the reference is to minchah ketanah. Presumably the same measure applies to labor. Beit Yosef cites Semag and Re’em as understanding Rashi in this manner. This, it appears to me, is the basis of the dispute. As a matter of law, since the consequence is not an actual prohibition but only the absence of a sign of blessing, one who relies upon Rashi has not forfeited the blessing, Heaven forbid.
