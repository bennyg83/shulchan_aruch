One who performs labor, etc. There is also an element of prohibition in this, although the prohibition is not as severe as other rabbinic prohibitions for which one would incur disciplinary lashes. This is stated by the Rambam in chapter 8 of the Laws of Yom Tov, by the Semag, and by the Or Zarua. Its source appears to be the opening of Pesachim, chapter Makom Shenahagu; see the Gemara's first answer there. Even according to the Gemara's second answer, it means only that he is not placed under a ban, but a prohibition still exists, for there is no reason to posit a dispute between the two answers. This is also implied by the Ran at the beginning of that chapter, as the Peri Megadim already notes. The Taz, however, writes that there is no prohibition, but only that one will not see a sign of blessing. According to him, we must say that the two answers dispute one another, and in a rabbinic matter the law follows the lenient view, particularly because it is the latter answer. Alternatively, even according to the first answer, although the Gemara uses the term “forbidden,” it means only that the Sages are displeased with him, not that there is a full prohibition. It is nevertheless surprising that the Taz did not mention the Rambam and the other authorities cited above. Regarding commerce, the Magen Avraham writes that it is not included in labor, but other later authorities, including the Maharam, Tosefet Shabbat, and Chemed Moshe, dispute this on the basis of proofs. See Peri Megadim, who explains that the Magen Avraham means only that according to all opinions one need not be stringent from Minchah Gedolah; from Minchah Ketanah onward, however, commerce has the same law as other labor. This follows from the Magen Avraham's own statement in seif katan 4 that the Gemara's second blast, which caused the city and its shops to cease activity, occurred at Minchah Ketanah. Machatzis HaShekel likewise implies that shops must close at the same time as other labor, although he is lenient regarding private commerce; see there. His discussion also refutes the proof of Nesiv Chaim. In any event, that proof is not compelling in light of the Rama's qualification, derived from the Or Zarua, that the restriction applies specifically when one performs labor regularly for profit. The proof cited there is from the Mishnah permitting ink to be soaked before nightfall and the other activities there that Beit Shammai permits only when they can be completed before Shabbat, whereas Beit Hillel permits them close to nightfall. The Perishah challenges this Mishnah and answers that the person merely begins close to nightfall and the labor continues by itself on Shabbat. But it is difficult to understand how Beit Shammai would permit it whenever there is enough time for completion, and Beit Hillel agrees to that. The correct resolution is that of the Or Zarua cited above. In practice, people have adopted a lenient custom regarding closing shops, apparently relying upon the Taz's view that there is no prohibition in any case.
And some explain: Minchah Ketanah. See Magen Avraham, who agrees with this opinion. Nevertheless, he implies that field laborers must cease at Minchah Gedolah. He thereby explains the Rambam's statement that the first blast, signaling the laborers to stop their work, had to be sounded at Minchah, as stated in Shabbat 35b. In our times this is not the practice. Perhaps the reason for the original enactment was that the laborers should return to the city and prepare the needs of Shabbat, whereas today each laborer's household prepares those needs. This resembles the ruling that when a person dies in a city, all residents are forbidden to work so that they will occupy themselves with the burial arrangements; where special societies attend to the deceased, everyone else is permitted to work. Nevertheless, anyone who fears Heaven should cease field labor at least from Minchah Ketanah, unless it is a matter involving loss, in which case it is no more stringent than Chol HaMoed. Many are stringent even regarding labor performed at home.

It would have been preferable to write that even soaking ink or dyes, or trapping animals and birds, all of which are certainly forbidden on Chol HaMoed when not required for the festival, are nevertheless permitted on Friday.
