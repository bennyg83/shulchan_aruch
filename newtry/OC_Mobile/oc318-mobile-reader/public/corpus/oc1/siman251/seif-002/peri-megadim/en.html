If the worker lacks food, he may take substantial payment. This implies that if he has bread and water, payment is forbidden; see Magen Avraham there. It may be argued, however, that on Shabbat one is commanded regarding enjoyment. Therefore, even if he has bread and water, if he lacks meat and fish with which to honor Shabbat, he may work. This explains the widespread custom of tailors working on Friday until close to sunset, even though the sources imply that there is a prohibition and that one must rebuke and forcibly stop the worker, as explained in note 1 and as implied by the Rama's language, “but it is forbidden to write for another for payment.” The prohibition therefore applies unless the worker is poor and has not prepared all appropriate Shabbat needs, including good beverages and cooked foods suitable to his circumstances. If he has prepared them, it is certainly forbidden, and from Minchah Ketanah onward he should be rebuked and forcibly prevented, because in a rabbinic dispute we are lenient and, under the accepted rule, where Shulchan Aruch cites “some say” followed by “some say,” the law follows the latter view.
