To repair his garments, etc. The same applies to sewing entirely new garments, as stated by Eliyah Rabbah and Peri Megadim, even though this is full-fledged labor and is forbidden on the eve of Pesach, as explained in siman 468. On Friday we are lenient; see their discussions. Their language implies that even skilled tailoring is permitted. Biur HaGra, however, derives the rule permitting garment repair from Chol HaMoed, where only unskilled work is allowed, implying that he limits the permission here likewise. The other later authorities do not appear to agree.
And he does not receive payment for it. If he sees that unless he delivers the garment for Shabbat the customer will reduce his agreed wage below the customary rate, or will cease employing him in the future, this may be considered a matter involving loss, similar to the ruling regarding Chol HaMoed in siman 539:13. There is an additional ground for leniency: Rabbeinu Yitzchak holds that if one began during a permitted period, he may finish even after midday on the eve of Pesach. Many authorities dispute him, and therefore the Rama in siman 468:9 rules against him. That ruling concerns the eve of Pesach. Friday is more lenient; therefore, in pressing circumstances, when the customer will bear resentment if the garment is not delivered for Shabbat, it appears that one may rely upon Rabbeinu Yitzchak and finish even after Minchah Ketanah, even for payment. Despite the several grounds for leniency stated here, a person of means who fears God should initially hasten his work and avoid reaching a situation in which he must continue until close to sunset, except where his financial circumstances are strained, as explained in Mishnah Berurah.
And one may take a haircut throughout the day, etc. See Biur HaGra, who strongly questions this ruling. In his opinion, one should refrain from Minchah onward even when no payment is taken, because haircutting is always skilled labor; see there.
Even from a Jewish barber. Since the discussion concerns haircutting, it is appropriate to mention a matter in which some ordinary people unfortunately stumble: the Torah prohibition against rounding the corners of the head. The Torah states, “You shall not round the corners of your heads.” Some authorities hold that the corners of the head are more stringent than the corners of the beard, because one violates the prohibition even when cutting them with scissors in the manner of a razor, meaning very close to the skin. The corner extends from opposite the hairline on the forehead down below the ear, to the place where the lower jaw protrudes and separates. One may not remove the full width of hair in this region, because it is all included in the corner of the head; below that point begins the corner of the beard, as explained in Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah 181. Both the person who cuts and the person whose hair is cut are included in the prohibition. Unfortunately, it is common for people to remove the sidelocks completely, close to the skin, without leaving anything, which raises a concern of a Torah violation. They should leave at least a minimal amount. Even more serious is the mistake of some young men who allow the barber to shave the hair beside the ear, because they think that “the corner of the head” means only the long lock commonly called a sidelock. This is incorrect, as explained above, and all opinions agree that removing the hair in that region is a full Torah prohibition. The prohibition also applies to the person whose hair is cut. Therefore, even if the barber is not Jewish, the Jew must warn him not to touch that area at all. Even in a bathhouse one may interrupt the barber to prevent him from shaving there, if he will listen, as explained in siman 84: one may speak there when necessary to separate someone from a prohibition.
A person should reduce, etc. The source is marked as the Yerushalmi, but careful examination there, and of the Midrash cited in Darchei Moshe, shows explicitly that they discuss Shabbat itself, not Friday. The Midrash should therefore read “except from Minchah onward,” as the commentators explain. Nevertheless, the Rama's ruling is correct, for the Gemara states that the Amoraim themselves interrupted their Torah study in order to prepare the needs of Shabbat.
