Seif katan 5: "And it appears to me that the reason for the permission," etc.; "and if so, all labors permitted on Chol HaMoed," etc. It appears to me that the explanation of his words is this: the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Kol Bo that it is forbidden for a Jewish barber to cut hair on erev Shabbat or erev Yom Tov from Minchah time onward, but perhaps one may permit haircutting as an ordinary act, no worse than Chol HaMoed, where we say that an ordinary person sews in his usual manner; end quote. The world customarily permits this. From his words it seems that at first he thought even an ordinary act was forbidden, and therefore wrote that perhaps one may permit it. The reason appears to be that here there are two issues: one because of labor, since the sanctity of Shabbat has already taken effect and one should not do labor, just as labor is prohibited on Chol HaMoed; and another so that he be free to deal with Shabbat needs. According to the first reason, ordinary labor may be permitted as on Chol HaMoed, but skilled labor should be forbidden, since on Chol HaMoed it too is forbidden, and even for Shabbat needs it should be forbidden as on Chol HaMoed. According to the reason that he should be free for Shabbat needs, if the labor itself is for Shabbat it should be permitted even if skilled, but if it is not for Shabbat even ordinary labor should be forbidden. This is what the Magen Avraham wrote: the reason for permission is that since it is for Shabbat it is permitted. On erev Pesach haircutting is forbidden, for there the prohibition is because of Yom Tov sanctity after midday, the time of the Pesach offering, and the reasoning that it is for Yom Tov does not help. It follows that even an ordinary act is forbidden, and the only permission is specifically haircutting on erev Shabbat, since it is in honor of Shabbat; but on erev Pesach it does not help that it is for Yom Tov, since the main purpose is to be free at the time of the Pesach offering. Therefore he wrote that cutting a non-Jew's hair, which is not for Shabbat, is forbidden, for in any event he will not be free for Shabbat needs. Even though on Chol HaMoed this may not be included in labor, it is still forbidden according to both reasons, for even on Chol HaMoed we require that it be for the festival. He then wrote that one may say erev Shabbat is lighter, and we forbid only where both factors exist, skilled labor and not for Shabbat, whereas ordinary labor is not a concern for preventing readiness, since it is only occasional labor. He then wrote: see siman 468, which proves that it is skilled labor, etc. Meaning, there they permitted haircutting before midday and forbade it after midday, and there the concern is not being free for Yom Tov, since it speaks of shaving for the festival; only the reason of Yom Tov sanctity remains, and this sanctity is no stronger than Chol HaMoed, where ordinary labor is permitted. Therefore there it must refer to haircutting as a skilled act, but an ordinary act is permitted. Accordingly, the main prohibition is not because of readiness but because of labor, and therefore ordinary labor is permitted; if so, there is no distinction whether it is needed for Shabbat. Any labor permitted on Chol HaMoed is permitted on erev Shabbat, even though on Chol HaMoed it must be for the festival; erev Shabbat is lighter in this respect. He further wrote that even if one takes wages it is permitted, for one should not say it is like Chol HaMoed, where permitted labors are only without wages. Erev Shabbat is lighter, for without wages haircutting would obviously be permitted since it is for Shabbat. The Kol Bo, who permitted an ordinary act because it is no worse than Chol HaMoed, must be speaking even for wages, for otherwise it would be obvious. This is also implied in Darkei Moshe, who wrote that a letter of greeting is permitted because it is no worse than Chol HaMoed; he did not distinguish whether it is for Shabbat. Thus anything not called labor on Chol HaMoed is permitted, and the concern that he will not be free does not apply to an occasional act such as a greeting letter. This also explains why Darkei Moshe gave the reason that it is no worse than Chol HaMoed, while here in the gloss he wrote that it is like occasional labor; both are needed.
A short path in explaining the Magen Avraham, slightly differently from above: the Beit Yosef wrote that the custom is to permit even a skilled act, and that the halakhah is uncertain; this must refer to taking wages, for without wages why should it be worse than repairing utensils, where we do not distinguish between skilled and ordinary work. The Magen Avraham explained that because it is for Shabbat they permitted it, unlike repairing garments, for here it is more recognizable. Perhaps even on Chol HaMoed, if there were no reason to forbid lest he enter the festival unkempt, haircutting for wages would be permitted even as skilled work because it is recognizable that it is for the festival. On erev Pesach after midday, however, haircutting is forbidden and it does not help that it is visibly for the following festival, since the prohibition derives from the sanctity of that time itself. At first this implies that even ordinary haircutting is forbidden, even without wages, and specifically on erev Shabbat, whose sanctity is because of the coming Shabbat, it is permitted when done for Shabbat. But on erev Pesach, whose prohibition is not because of the coming festival, it is forbidden. Therefore on erev Shabbat, when it is not for Shabbat, such as cutting a non-Jew's hair, it should be forbidden even as ordinary work. To this he wrote that one may say erev Shabbat is lighter than Chol HaMoed and ordinary labor is permitted even not for the festival, and the case in siman 468 also speaks of skilled labor. If so, all labors permitted on Chol HaMoed are permitted on erev Shabbat, even other labors not needed for Shabbat. He added that this is so even when taking wages, for haircutting also speaks of wages, and nevertheless the Kol Bo permitted an ordinary act. The proof from Darkei Moshe regarding a greeting letter is not about wages, but that labor permitted on Chol HaMoed is permitted on erev Shabbat as well.
