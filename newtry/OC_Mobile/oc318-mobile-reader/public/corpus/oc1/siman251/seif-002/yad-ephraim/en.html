Seif katan 5 ‘It appears to me that the reason for the leniency, etc. Accordingly, all forms of labor permitted on Chol HaMoed, etc.’ It appears to me that his words should be understood as follows. Beit Yosef cites Kol Bo as ruling that a Jew may not cut another Jew’s hair on Friday or on the eve of a festival from minchah onward, though perhaps ordinary, nonprofessional haircutting may be permitted, since it is no worse than Chol HaMoed, when an ordinary person may sew in his usual manner. Beit Yosef then writes that the world has adopted a lenient custom, and wherever the law is uncertain one should follow the custom. His initial assumption was evidently that even ordinary craftsmanship is forbidden, and he therefore suggested that perhaps it may be permitted. The underlying matter contains two distinct considerations. First, labor should not be performed once the sanctity of Shabbos has begun to rest, just as labor is forbidden on Chol HaMoed. Second, a person must remain free to attend to Shabbos preparations. Under the first consideration, ordinary craftsmanship may be permitted as it is on Chol HaMoed, while professional labor should be forbidden because it is forbidden on Chol HaMoed as well. Even when performed for Shabbos, it should seemingly remain forbidden, as on Chol HaMoed. Under the second consideration, however, labor performed for Shabbos may be permitted even when professional, whereas labor unrelated to Shabbos should be forbidden even when ordinary, because it prevents him from remaining free. This is the meaning of Magen Avraham’s explanation that haircutting is permitted because it is performed for Shabbos. Haircutting on the eve of Pesach is nevertheless forbidden, because that prohibition arises from the sanctity of the festival eve after midday, when the Paschal offering was brought; the fact that the haircut serves the festival does not remove that prohibition. According to this, even ordinary craftsmanship is generally forbidden, and haircutting on Friday is permitted only because it honors Shabbos. Cutting a non-Jew’s hair, which is unrelated to Shabbos, remains forbidden because the barber will not be free for Shabbos preparations. Although such work is not classified as labor on Chol HaMoed, it is still forbidden for both reasons, since even on Chol HaMoed ordinary labor is permitted only when needed for the festival. Magen Avraham then suggests that Friday may be more lenient, so that labor is forbidden only when both factors are present: it is professional work and it is unrelated to Shabbos. Ordinary labor, by contrast, may be disregarded under the concern that he remain free because it resembles merely incidental work. He then directs the reader to siman 468, which implies that haircutting is professional labor. There, haircutting was permitted before midday and prohibited after midday. Since the person is cutting hair for the festival, the concern that he remain free for festival preparations does not apply. The only remaining basis is the sanctity of the festival eve, which is no stricter than Chol HaMoed, for Magen Avraham writes in siman 468:3 that whatever is permitted on Chol HaMoed is certainly permitted on the eve of Pesach. Yet ordinary craftsmanship is permitted on Chol HaMoed. It follows that the case there concerns professional haircutting, while ordinary haircutting is permitted. Accordingly, the essential prohibition may not be the concern that he remain free, but the nature of the labor itself. Ordinary labor would then be permitted on Friday even when not needed for Shabbos, because Friday may be more lenient than Chol HaMoed regarding the requirement that the labor serve the festival. This explains Magen Avraham’s statement that all forms of labor permitted on Chol HaMoed are permitted on Friday. He further states that they are permitted even for wages. One should not compare Friday to Chol HaMoed, where even permitted labor is generally performed without pay, as explained in siman 542, because Friday is more lenient. Kol Bo’s permission for ordinary haircutting must itself concern paid work. If it were unpaid, the haircut would obviously be permitted because it serves Shabbos, and even professional repair of one’s garments is permitted when done without pay. Kol Bo’s comparison to Chol HaMoed therefore proves that ordinary haircutting is permitted even for pay. The same should apply to other forms of ordinary labor, even for pay. One cannot distinguish haircutting on the ground that its Shabbos purpose is evident, while other work lacks such an evident purpose. That distinction matters only if the prohibition is based upon the need to remain free. Under the present reasoning, however, ordinary labor is not prohibited on that account because it resembles incidental labor. The only possible prohibition derives from the sanctity of Shabbos itself, and regarding that concern there is no distinction between haircutting and other ordinary work. Since haircutting is permitted even for pay and Friday is more lenient than Chol HaMoed, other ordinary labor is likewise permitted. Darkei Moshe supports this by permitting a friendly letter because it is no worse than Chol HaMoed. He does not distinguish between work needed for Shabbos and other work. Evidently, anything not regarded as labor on Chol HaMoed is permitted on Friday, and the concern that one remain free does not apply to a friendly letter because it is ordinary, incidental work. This also reconciles the wording of Darkei Moshe, which permits it because it is no worse than Chol HaMoed, with Rama’s wording here, which permits it because it resembles incidental work. Both reasons are necessary: with respect to the sanctity of Friday it is permitted because it is no worse than Chol HaMoed, and with respect to remaining free for Shabbos preparations it is permitted because it resembles incidental work.
A shorter explanation of Magen Avraham’s words, somewhat different from the preceding discussion, is as follows. Beit Yosef writes that the custom is to permit even professional haircutting, and invokes the rule that where the law is uncertain one should follow the custom. This necessarily refers to paid work, for if it were unpaid, haircutting would be no worse than repairing garments, where no distinction is made between professional and ordinary craftsmanship. Magen Avraham explains that the leniency rests upon the fact that haircutting is for Shabbos and is visibly so. Perhaps even on Chol HaMoed, were haircutting not forbidden in order that one not enter the festival unkempt, professional haircutting for pay would be permitted because its festival purpose is evident. On the eve of Pesach after midday, however, haircutting is forbidden even when visibly performed for the coming festival, because the prohibition stems from the sanctity of that time itself. This appears to imply that even ordinary haircutting would be forbidden there, even without pay, and that only on Friday, whose sanctity derives from the coming Shabbos, may labor for Shabbos be permitted. Accordingly, cutting a non-Jew’s hair on Friday should certainly be forbidden, since it is not for Shabbos. On Chol HaMoed ordinary labor is permitted only when needed for the festival; all the more so should it be forbidden on Friday when unrelated to Shabbos. Magen Avraham therefore suggests that Friday may be more lenient than Chol HaMoed and that ordinary labor may be permitted even when unrelated to Shabbos. The prohibition of haircutting on the eve of Pesach in siman 468 must then concern professional labor, for otherwise it would be no worse than ordinary labor on Chol HaMoed when needed for the festival. Thus all labor permitted on Chol HaMoed may be permitted on Friday, even when it does not serve Shabbos, since there is no proof from the eve of Pesach, where professional labor is at issue, and Chol HaMoed does not prove that Friday requires a Shabbos-related purpose. This is the more plausible conclusion. Such labor is permitted even for pay, for the haircutting discussed by Kol Bo likewise concerns paid work, yet he permits ordinary craftsmanship because it is no worse than Chol HaMoed. Friday is therefore not only no stricter than Chol HaMoed, but more lenient in permitting wages. Darkei Moshe’s proof from a friendly letter is not intended specifically to establish permission to receive wages. Rather, it shows that labor permitted on Chol HaMoed is also permitted on Friday. Under the initial assumption one might have argued that labor not needed for the festival is more severe on Friday than on Chol HaMoed, but Darkei Moshe makes no such distinction.
