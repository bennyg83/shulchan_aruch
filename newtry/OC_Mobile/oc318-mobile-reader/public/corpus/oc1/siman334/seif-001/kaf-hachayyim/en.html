1) [Seif 1] If it is nighttime before the meal, etc. Whenever it is before eating, even at the end of the night, one may save food for three meals. In the morning, however, this applies specifically before the time of the meal, meaning until the end of the sixth hour. After the sixth hour, its time has passed and one may not save food for two meals even if he has not yet eaten. Bach. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 1, writes that Bach's ruling requires examination. Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 1, writes the same, because one remains obligated in three meals even after their proper times have passed, as stated in siman 291; see there.
2) There: He may save, etc. This applies even if he already has food to eat. Beis Yosef; Magen Avraham, paragraph 4; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 3. Whether he may also save for members of his household, or when he is fasting, is discussed below in paragraphs 21 and 22.
3) There: And what is fit for an animal, for the animal. Since one may not eat before feeding his animal, he may also save three meals of food for the animal. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 1. Tiferes Yisrael writes the same in Kuntres Niflaos on the Mishnah, chapter 16 of Shabbos, number 42; see there. Mor U'Ketziah writes likewise. Pischei HaDevir, paragraph 2, cites him and writes that this is a homiletic interpretation. In law, one may not save three meals for an animal that does not ordinarily eat three times a day, but only the amount it eats that day. The rule forbidding a person to eat before feeding his animal applies when both are hungry. He adduces support for this; see there.
4) There: In the morning, food for two meals. It is obvious that one may save two whole loaves for each meal. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 2. Regarding beverages, Tosefes Shabbos writes that even where wine is very common, one may save no more than enough wine for three meals, because we follow the majority of the world. Other beverages, however, may plainly be saved in the amount needed to drink throughout the entire day; see there. See below at the beginning of siman 335.
5) There: In the morning, food for two meals. Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 1, writes that even if one ate a small amount in the morning, he may save food for two meals, because that morning eating was not the principal meal, which is eaten at midday; see there. Pischei HaDevir, paragraph 1, agrees and writes that this provides strong support for those whose Shabbos practice is to eat a light snack of baked grain products upon leaving the synagogue in the morning, without washing their hands or reciting HaMotzi, recite the after-blessing comprising the essence of three blessings, and set the table at midday for the second meal. He subsequently cites Pri Chadash and Arizal, who hold that the meal should be eaten immediately after prayer, and concludes that every custom has support. One who eats a light snack in the morning must take care not to fill himself with delicacies and thereby become unable to eat the principal meal at midday. If he cannot restrain himself, he should change his practice, establish the meal in the morning, and eat delicacies to satiety.
6) There: Specifically members of the household, etc. Rosh writes this in the name of Rabbeinu Tam. Tosafos at the beginning of the chapter Kol Kisvei, however, writes in Rabbeinu Tam's name that this applies within the same house or courtyard. Ran writes the same in his name, and Rambam's wording also appears to imply this, as Maggid Mishneh notes. Bach explains that Rosh discusses separate houses like ours, while Ran discusses several houses standing within one courtyard, where all are considered one house. Tosafos discusses both and therefore writes 'in the same house or courtyard.' These are his words. See siman 535, which distinguishes between a house and courtyard. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 3.
7) There: Because there is concern, etc. Regarding a corpse, the Sages permit saving it lest one extinguish the fire, as stated at the beginning of siman 311, whereas here they forbid saving property lest one extinguish. Tosafos on page 44a explains that a person does not suffer over his property as he does over his deceased relative. If permitted to save property, his preoccupation with saving it may cause him to forget that it is Shabbos and inadvertently extinguish. He suffers much more over his deceased relative, and if not permitted to save the corpse he may deliberately extinguish. These are their words. Taz, paragraph 1, and Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 4, cite this; see there.
8) There: But nearby houses, etc. Their occupants are not so panicked. Tur; Levush.
9) There: Whatever they wish. No decree is relevant to them. Levush.
10) There: Whatever they wish. This applies specifically to food, whose moving is permitted, and specifically into an enclosed courtyard with an eruv, as stated in seif 10. Sacred writings, whose transfer into a closed alley entails a prohibited act, as stated in seif 17 in the gloss, may not be saved from houses near the fire beyond what is permitted from the burning house itself. Bach. Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, paragraph 1, however, writes that Bach's ruling is unconvincing; see there.
