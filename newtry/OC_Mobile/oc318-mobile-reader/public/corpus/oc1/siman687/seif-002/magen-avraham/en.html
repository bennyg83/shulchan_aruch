To hear the reading of the megillah. Publicly, Beis Yosef and Bach. See the end of siman 690.
For you have no, etc. It implies that he holds that even public Torah study is interrupted. But in the Gemara it implies that public Torah study is not interrupted, Keneses HaGedolah. See Radach in section 13, and see the end of siman 690. The Bach wrote that public Torah study means that of all Israel, and so it appears in Tosafos under "nevertheless, it is preferable, etc."; see there. Nevertheless, it appears to me that in any case they must read the megillah. However, an individual must interrupt his study and go read publicly; with many, this does not apply. Even if each individual studies on his own, it is called many, since each one interrupts, and so it appears in Tosafos.
As needed. It implies that he holds that although it has buriers, since they are not sufficient for its needs it is called an unattended corpse. So it also appears in the gloss at the end of siman 696, where he holds that the burial of his dead comes first. This requires analysis, for we say: I am certain that megillah and Temple service, megillah is preferable; unattended corpse and Temple service, unattended corpse is preferable; unattended corpse and megillah, what is the law, etc.? And it resolves that what we say, that an unattended corpse is preferable to Temple service, is specifically with a corpse that has no buriers; but the burial of relatives does not push aside the Temple service. So wrote Tosafos in Shevuos, page 30b. If so, it is obvious that megillah is preferable to the burial of relatives, for megillah is preferable even to Temple service. This can be rejected, but truth shows its way, as the Ramban wrote in Toras HaAdam, that it speaks specifically of an unattended corpse. However, the first clause, which says that an unattended corpse is preferable to Torah study, speaks of all dead; see there. So wrote Tosafos. See what I wrote in siman 526 in the name of Yam Shel Shlomo. So too Shiltei Giborim wrote: specifically when it is in a field and lying in disgrace, but in a city, megillah comes first. So wrote the Bach, and so it appears in Darkei Moshe in the name of Or Zarua, who wrote: the burial of a Torah scholar precedes megillah; end quote. His reason is that we say the honor of an individual Torah scholar is more stringent, implying that for every other person, megillah comes first. This is the main view. So too it appears in Berachos, page 20, that burial of relatives is not called human dignity when it is lying in a city; if so, it does not push aside megillah. So too it appears in the Re'em, at the end of section 62.
Where there is time. The same applies to circumcision and megillah. But in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 266, he inclined to say the opposite, that when there is time he should advance circumcision, and when there is no time he should read the megillah. It appears to me that the reason is that he can circumcise on the ninth day, Keneses HaGedolah. See the end of siman 693.
Where it is possible for him to read it. But if it is close to dark, he should read the megillah, for in any case it is possible to bury the unattended corpse at night. But if it is in a situation where it will be impossible to bury him afterward because of bandits, reading the megillah is certainly pushed aside, for an unattended corpse pushes aside the Temple service entirely, and the Temple service pushes aside megillah entirely. Nevertheless, this requires analysis, for the Gemara derives that an unattended corpse is preferable by saying that the Master said: human dignity is great, for it pushes aside a prohibition in the Torah. This is difficult: by the prohibition of "do not turn aside," a mitzvah is pushed aside entirely because of human dignity, as written in siman 13. Further, Pesach and circumcision are entirely pushed aside because of human dignity, even though it is possible to bury him afterward; nevertheless, we say that he should not lie in disgrace. All the more so for megillah, for no Torah mitzvah is pushed aside because of megillah, and nevertheless it is pushed aside before an unattended corpse; all the more so megillah should be pushed aside before it. The Re'em himself brought all these matters that we said, and Keneses HaGedolah wrote on Beis Yosef's explanation of the Re'em: the words of our teacher are astonishing, and I elaborated on them in a kuntres; end quote. He intended what I wrote. Therefore it appears to me that the Re'em came only to prove that the Rambam speaks only of precedence, since he says, "he buries him first and afterward reads," which implies that if there is time, yes, and if not, no. Meaning, it implies that the Rambam speaks where there is time; but if there is not time, he is not discussing it. Rather, megillah is pushed aside before every Torah mitzvah, and all the more so before an unattended corpse, as appears to me.
