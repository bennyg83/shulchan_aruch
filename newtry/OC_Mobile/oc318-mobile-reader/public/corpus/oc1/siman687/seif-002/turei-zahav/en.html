Except for a meis mitzvah, etc. The Rambam writes in this language: “One who encounters it buries it first, and afterward reads the Megillah.” The Mizrachi infers that the words “and afterward reads” are superfluous, and therefore teach that if there remains time to read afterward, he does so, but if not, he does not bury first. This is difficult for me. The plain conclusion of the Gemara is that a meis mitzvah takes precedence over Megillah, for the Master said, “Great is human dignity, for it overrides a negative commandment in the Torah.” The negative commandment is entirely set aside, as Rashi explains regarding the verses “You may not ignore it” and “you shall ignore,” which teach that there are times when one may ignore it, such as an elderly person for whom the act is beneath his dignity. Accordingly, regarding the reading of the Megillah as well, human dignity should entirely override the Megillah. This is all the more so, since Megillah is only from the words of the Sages. The extra wording noted by the Mizrachi does not appear to teach his conclusion. Rather, it follows what Tosafos wrote: “One might ask, let them perform their Temple service immediately and afterward read the Megillah by themselves. One can answer that it is preferable to read with the congregation because this provides greater publicizing of the miracle.” End quote. With this intent, the Rambam wrote that when both obligations are before him and there is ample time for both, one should not think that Megillah should come first because of the publicizing of the miracle with the congregation. He therefore teaches that we are not concerned with the greater publicizing in this case. Rather, he first buries the meis mitzvah and afterward reads the Megillah privately. However, the Rambam does not discuss a case in which there will be no time afterward. The law in that case is that the reading of the Megillah is entirely canceled, because human dignity overrides it. You can know this to be true. If one finds a meis mitzvah in a field and does not have a Megillah with him, would he abandon the body and go to the city to read the Megillah, although dogs might consume the body in the meantime? Could one possibly say that he should do so? This is certainly an untenable position. Apart from this, the words of the Shulchan Aruch here cannot stand, because they contradict one another. At first the Rama writes, “But if it is impossible to perform both,” which implies that the other mitzvah takes precedence over Megillah. At the end, however, he writes that this is only when it is possible to read afterward, which implies that when it is impossible to read afterward, Megillah takes precedence over a meis mitzvah, and all the more so over other mitzvos. Regarding Megillah and the Temple service, the Gemara states that Megillah takes precedence. It derives this from the words “family by family,” which include the families of kohanim and leviim, who suspend their service and come to hear the reading of the Megillah. The Ran writes that this applies only when they can complete the service afterward. If they cannot, they certainly do not suspend a Torah obligation because of the rabbinic obligation of Megillah. This is difficult for me. The Gemara also states that Megillah takes precedence over Torah study, and there the Torah study is entirely displaced. The study that one is obligated to undertake at that moment cannot be made up, because whatever he studies afterward is a separate mitzvah. Nevertheless, it is displaced by Megillah. The same should apply to the Temple service, and this follows all the more so because the Gemara states that Torah study is greater than the Temple service. The language of the Rambam copied here also indicates this, for he writes that all other mitzvos are certainly displaced by Megillah. This surely includes cases in which they are displaced entirely. There are certainly mitzvos whose fulfillment depends upon that specific time, and nevertheless, when they are displaced, they are displaced entirely. As for the Ran’s argument that the Temple service is according to the Torah whereas Megillah is rabbinic, one can answer that Megillah is among the words of the Prophets, and we rule that words of the Prophets have the status of words of Torah, as the Beit Yosef writes in siman 554 in the name of Rabbeinu Tam regarding Tzom Gedaliah. It is therefore proper to say that the Temple service is displaced. The Beit Yosef writes that Tosafos agrees with the Ran, because Tosafos states: “The kohanim suspend their service to hear the Megillah. One might ask, why is this considered suspending the service, since there remains much time after the reading in which to perform it? One can answer that once daylight has appeared, the time for the service has arrived, and because they set it aside for the reading, this is called suspension.” End quote. I do not see any proof from this that Tosafos agrees with the Ran. Tosafos addresses the language of the Gemara, which says that they suspend their service, meaning the ordinary daily time of service, which begins when daylight appears. Tosafos therefore asks that there remains time afterward throughout the day. However, if the service had not been performed until shortly before its final time and the reading of the Megillah was before them, it may indeed be that Megillah entirely displaces the service, since both cannot be fulfilled. The publicizing of the miracle takes precedence. This appears correct in my humble opinion.
And all this does not discuss, etc. This entire gloss is based on the words of the Ran and the Mizrachi. I have already written the difficulties with their position. My revered father in law, of blessed memory, also explained the words of the Tur according to the approach I stated above, and he wrote similarly in the name of the Maharshal: whenever the Sages state that one matter takes greater precedence, their intent is that the displaced obligation is entirely displaced, even when there will be no time to perform it afterward.
