Ibid., seif 2. “Torah study is suspended in order to hear the reading of the Megillah,” etc. Note: The Gemara says that the household of Rebbi relied upon the derivation from “family and family,” which includes kohanim engaged in their Temple service, etc. If Temple service, which is more stringent, is suspended, Torah study is certainly suspended. The Gemara asks whether Temple service is indeed more stringent than Torah study, citing the account of Yehoshua at Yericho. Regarding the continuation of that passage, “Yehoshua lodged that night,” and Rabbi Shmuel’s statement that Torah study is great, see my explanation in my work on Ein Yaakov.
I now see room to question the Gemara. Menachos 50, and Rambam’s ruling, establish that where the morning tamid was not offered, the afternoon tamid may be offered only if the altar had already been inaugurated. If the altar had not been inaugurated, the afternoon tamid may not be offered, because the altar is inaugurated only through the morning tamid. The morning tamid is therefore superior to the afternoon tamid. This is especially so according to Rebbi in Yoma 34b, who derives the afternoon service from the morning service. Tosafos there explain that the authority who derives the afternoon from the morning holds the morning service superior; if there are libations for only one offering, they are used in the morning. The verse “family and family,” which teaches that kohanim suspend their service to hear the Megillah, must refer to the morning tamid. The nighttime Megillah reading is not mentioned explicitly in the Megillah and, according to Turei Even, is only D'Rabbanan. Even were it of prophetic authority, it occurs at actual night, which is not the time for slaughtering and sprinkling the afternoon tamid, the central elements of the service. The verse must therefore concern the morning tamid. We may then say that Rebbi follows his own view that the afternoon service is derived from the morning and that the morning tamid is superior. The morning tamid may indeed be superior to Torah study, supporting the a fortiori inference. The episode of Yehoshua, however, expressly concerns the afternoon tamid, and Torah study may be more stringent than that service. What then is the Gemara’s question? This requires study. Examine carefully.
The Tur first writes that Temple service is suspended to hear the Megillah and then adds, “Even Torah study is suspended.” Derishah and Perishah wonder at the word “even,” since the Gemara indicates that Temple service is more stringent than Torah study. It cannot simply refer to public Torah study, which is superior to Temple service, because how could the Tur derive that from “family and family”? They offer a strained explanation. In my opinion, the matter can be resolved readily. Although an individual’s Torah study is less stringent than Temple service, this applies when he is not studying the laws of the Temple service. If he studies those laws, the Sages state that one who engages in the passage of an olah is regarded as though he offered an olah. The study then contains both Torah study and Temple service, and is superior to Temple service alone.
The Tur may have derived this from what I wrote in Sefer HaChayim to resolve the common question: What is remarkable about suspending Torah study for Megillah, when Megillah itself is Torah? I explained that a single intention cannot serve two distinct purposes. If one intends the reading as Torah study, it does not count as the mitzvah of Megillah; if one intends it as Megillah, it does not fulfill Torah study. I brought proofs there. The Tur’s wording is therefore precise. “Even Torah study” refers back to the Temple service mentioned first, meaning even Torah study concerning the Temple service is suspended for Megillah. This follows whichever way one reasons. If one intention can accomplish two purposes, such study contains both Torah and Temple service, while Megillah itself also counts as Torah; the only element lost is Temple service, and we already learned that Temple service is suspended for Megillah. If one intention cannot accomplish two purposes, then studying the laws of the service also constitutes only one thing: if intended as service it is only service, and if intended as Torah it is only Torah. Temple service has already been shown to yield to Megillah, and individual Torah study certainly does. The ruling therefore follows either way. This also supports what I wrote in Sefer HaChayim, that one intention cannot serve two purposes. The matter is resolved properly. Examine carefully. This is a sound approach, with Hashem’s help.
