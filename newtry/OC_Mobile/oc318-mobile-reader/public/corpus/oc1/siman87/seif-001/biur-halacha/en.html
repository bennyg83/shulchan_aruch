A chamber pot, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 2, regarding where a foul odor emanates from it. At first glance, the Magen Avraham’s leniency when one is always careful to pour in a reviis of water immediately is very difficult. According to Rabbeinu Yonah, a urinal vessel is prohibited on a Torah level even though urine itself is only prohibited Rabbinically. This must be either because it becomes more malodorous, so we treat it like excrement, as Chayei Adam writes, or because its designation for this use makes it like a beis hakisei. On either explanation its prohibition is Torah-level, as implied by Rabbi Akiva Eiger. How, then, does constantly adding a reviis of water help? A reviis of water neutralizes even a large quantity of urine only because urine is prohibited merely Rabbinically, as Rashi explains in Berachos 25b. Were its prohibition Torah-level, the water would not help at all. How, then, can it help for a urinal vessel, whose prohibition is Torah-level because of the filth absorbed within it? Surely a small quantity of water poured into it does not prevent the urine’s filth from being absorbed. This is similar to, and stronger than, the objection of the dissenting opinion cited in Baer Heitev. Perhaps one can answer that when we see that it has no foul odor, this itself proves that substantial filth was not absorbed into it. Its prohibition is therefore only because it was designated for urine and thereby treated as a beis hakisei. Since he always pours water into it immediately after urinating, he has not allowed it to become designated as a beis hakisei. This also resolves the preceding objection. Accordingly, if a foul odor emanates from it, it has the full law of a urinal vessel. This appears correct to me.
But if they are metal or coated earthenware. See Mishnah Berurah regarding porcelain vessels. Although Peri Megadim writes in siman 451 that for hagalah they are considered earthenware, this matter requires study. Concerning the Taz’s statement that, since siman 451 treats glass like earthenware, the same should apply here, Yeshuos Yaakov writes: I am astonished, for regarding glazed earthenware the cited poskim hold that it is not included in the law of a chamber pot, although it is certainly treated as earthenware concerning absorption of prohibited substances. The same applies to earthenware coated with lead, concerning which Chok Yaakov writes there, seif katan 64, that most poskim treat it as earthenware, yet here all agree that the coating is effective, as explained above. Rather, regarding a chamber pot, since it can be washed and is smooth, there is no concern. If so, the same may apply to porcelain, since it is smooth and does not absorb as much as earthenware. Nevertheless, in practice it is proper to be stringent. Peri Chadash is stringent even concerning glass itself. Although there we follow the later authorities who are lenient, here one should be stringent.
