(1) A chamber pot, etc. Even if it presently contains nothing, and even if it has been thoroughly washed inside and outside and is completely clean, without even a foul odor.
(2) For urine. If the vessel is also designated for another use, such as pouring wastewater into it, it is permitted when it contains neither excrement nor foul odor. Likewise, if each time one urinates into it he adds a reviis of water, it is permitted when no foul odor emanates from it. It appears to me that if a foul odor does emanate from the vessel, it has the full law of a urinal vessel, and one must distance himself four amos from the point where the odor ceases. See Biur Halachah.
(3) Like excrement. In front of him, as far as he can see; behind him, four amos from the point where the odor ceases. Many later authorities appear to agree with Rabbeinu Yonah that a chamber pot and urinal vessel are prohibited on a Torah level, even though urine itself is prohibited only Rabbinically. These vessels are more stringent because their designation for this use makes them like a beis hakisei. Adding water therefore does not help, even if they are filled to the brim. If one recited Krias Shema or prayed where such a vessel was within four amos, he must repeat if it was found in a place where he should initially have suspected its presence. Regarding other blessings, see siman 185:5 and Mishnah Berurah there, and apply that law here. If he found the chamber pot or urinal vessel within his full range of sight, he need not repeat after the fact, as explained in siman 81:2 and Mishnah Berurah there.
(4) Of glass. Taz questions this ruling, but most, indeed nearly all, later authorities agree to be lenient. They hold that these materials do not absorb, or at least do not absorb substantially, and therefore do not acquire the law of a beis hakisei.
(5) Coated. Some say this means what we call glazed; others say specifically coated with lead, not merely glazed. Some also require coating inside and outside, while others permit coating only inside. In practice, one should be stringent and require coating outside as well. When it is inverted over its opening, however, one may rely on the lenient opinions, as Eliyah Rabbah writes. Vessels called porcelain have the law of earthenware for other prohibitions, and hagalah is ineffective for them. It appears proper to be stringent here as well. See Biur Halachah.
(6) Thoroughly. Inside and outside, and it must have no foul odor. If a foul odor emanates from it, the odor is treated as one without a tangible source, since the vessel is clean and no longer has the status of a chamber pot or urinal vessel. One need distance himself only until the odor ceases. If the vessel was not washed, one must distance himself four amos from it, because tangible filth may remain upon it.
