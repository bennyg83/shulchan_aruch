It is permitted to recite, etc., since he distanced it, etc. See Mishnah Berurah concerning a bed ten tefachim high, where it is permitted to recite before the bed. Plainly, the same applies when he lies upon the bed, as proved by siman 79:2 and the Mishnah Berurah there. He must, however, take care not to extend a limb beyond the bed into the area of the chamber pot; see siman 76:4. Chayei Adam writes that a chest, screen, or similar object standing in the house, ten tefachim high and four tefachim wide, which separates between a person and excrement, should be treated stringently in accordance with Magen Avraham, who prohibits unless it extends from wall to wall. If, however, there was urine on the ground, or in a vessel that is not a urinal vessel, so that the prohibition is only Rabbinic, it appears that one may stand opposite the partition and recite or bless, but not opposite the open space, because a partition four tefachim wide is significant enough to permit opposite it. If the partition is wider than the open area, so that the standing portion exceeds the breached portion, it appears that even with excrement one may, in pressing circumstances, permit opposite the partition, provided he does not see the excrement. Likewise, a trench separating the two areas, ten tefachim deep, four tefachim long, and four tefachim wide from edge to edge, creates two domains. He further writes that the partition must be ten tefachim high and four tefachim wide from the ground throughout that height. Alternatively, even if within three tefachim of the ground it is narrower than four tefachim, such as when it stands on thin legs, we apply lavud. But an ordinary table or the like, which stands on legs and lacks a continuous width of four tefachim, is not a separating partition even though it is ten tefachim high.
Likewise, if he inverted, etc. Levush writes that if he placed the chamber pot or urinal vessel inside another vessel whose walls rise above them so that they cannot be seen, they are considered covered and it is permitted when there is no foul odor. Netz and Lechem Chamudos disagree and hold that this is ineffective unless the surrounding walls rise more than ten tefachim, as cited by Eliyah Rabbah. Peri Megadim writes that a broad roof overhang projecting from a house, called a porch roof, is not considered a separate domain from the public domain. Therefore, if excrement is outside it, one may not speak words of Torah beneath the overhang even when he cannot see it. See siman 76:8, where even in a place about which there is merely reasonable uncertainty one must be careful.
