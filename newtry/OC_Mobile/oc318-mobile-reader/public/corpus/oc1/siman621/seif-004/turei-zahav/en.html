In Mussaf he says. For the main service of the Kohen Gadol was only in the Mussaf of Yom Kippur, together with the offerings of the Yom Kippur goats. In the Tur he brought opinions that one should first say 'Ana Hashem,' and afterward say 'Ana vashem, please atone,' and so it is in the machzorim. It seems to me that the reason is that when one requests mercy, he says 'vashem,' meaning that he prays that He, may He be blessed, should save him through the essential Name, which is complete mercy without any admixture of judgment at all, as it seems to me. In the commentary to the machzor it is written that when he says 'Ana Hashem,' he did not mention the explicit Name but only the substitute Name of Adnus, and only the second time did he say the explicit Name. The beraisa in chapter Teref BaKalpi escaped him, for it says that he mentioned the explicit Name ten times, three times in the first confession. In the text 'Ana Hashem, I have sinned,' the Tanna in the Mishnah, and we in prayer, did not mention the Name of Adnus in our usual way with all Names. The reason is that this is only a narration of the words of the Kohen Gadol, not our own words. As for the fact that we say 'before Hashem you shall be purified,' although this too is a narration of the Kohen Gadol's words, there is no concern, because it is the language of the verse. So the Tur wrote in the name of Rav Saadiah Gaon, that it is proper to say 'before HaShem you shall be purified,' as with 'Ana HaShem.' The Ritz Giyus wrote: If one says 'before Hashem,' there is nothing wrong with this, for he is reading the verse, and we do not pronounce the Name by its letters. End quote. In this way we must explain what we say every day in the Yotzer prayer, 'Holy, holy, holy, Hashem Tzevakos.' This is only a narration of the words of the angels, and we should have said 'HaShem'; rather, because it is a verse, we say it as written in our reading. My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that when we say in the piyyut, 'he cried to Hashem, a sin-offering,' in the prayer of the Kohen Gadol, 'May it be Your will, Hashem, that this year be,' and likewise regarding the people of the Sharon, 'May it be Your will, Hashem,' etc., it is proper in these places to say the expression 'HaShem.' I have not found any author who awakened to this. In my humble opinion, specifically in a place where the Kohen Gadol would pronounce the explicit Name, namely in the act of the Avodah, we say 'HaShem' instead of Adonai, to indicate that in this mention there was a change; if we were to say Adonai, one would think the Kohen Gadol also said it that way. But in the prayer of the Kohen Gadol he did not mention the explicit Name, but only as in other prayers. This is as stated in chapter Teref BaKalpi: The Rabbis taught, ten times the Kohen Gadol mentions the Name on that day: three in the first confession, namely 'Ana Hashem, I have sinned,' 'Ana Hashem, atone,' and 'before Hashem you shall be purified'; three in the second confession; three in the scapegoat; and one in the lots, meaning when he says, 'for Hashem as a sin-offering.' Therefore there is no concern when we say the Name Adonai in the Kohen Gadol's prayer, for in any case it is not in vain, since through this we are aroused and pray and direct our hearts to what we too need in our time. We find similarly that in the text of the Kohen Gadol's prayer there is, 'a year in which You bring us up in joy to our land,' which the Kohen Gadol did not say, for he lived in Eretz Yisrael. Rather, we insert this because it is relevant to us. However, regarding what is written, 'he cried to Hashem, a sin-offering,' where the Kohen Gadol did mention the explicit Name, we should say 'for HaShem as a sin-offering,' as my teacher and father-in-law wrote. One can say somewhat even about this that it is the language of the verse, 'the lot that rose upon it for Hashem, and he shall make it a sin-offering.' But it appears that the best advice is to say here 'for HaShem as a sin-offering,' as it seems to me. It appears to learn from the words of the Ritz Giyus that anyone reading a verse may say the Name as Adnus, as he says it in prayer. One can learn further from the matters mentioned here that in a place where we do not want to mention the Name of Adnus, one should say 'HaShem,' and not as the masses say 'Adoshem,' for this is not a respectful manner toward Heaven; rather, one should say in the language of the Tanna, 'HaShem.'
