And some say that a non-Jew too, etc. — It implies we do not care about what he might come on Shabbat; and this is difficult, for we ruled in siman 365 seif 7 that if he intends to demolish on Shabbat it is invalid. And one may say that there it deals with one accustomed on Shabbat to become ruined, such as through a non-Jew accustomed to this; not so here, for in such a case if the non-Jew is accustomed to return to his house that day he is forbidden. And here it deals with one who went with intent to spend Shabbat there the entire day. And one could ask: if so, when a non-Jew comes on Shabbat why is it forbidden here — he changed his mind in that he came on Shabbat? And what is different from that which is written afterward regarding a Jew who changed his mind and came on Shabbat that he is permitted? Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Rosh the reason: since he already diverted his heart and will certainly not return, it is a Shabbat that was permitted and will not become forbidden again; so too we may say regarding a non-Jew who came on Shabbat when he did not intend on erev Shabbat to return here — and likewise if a Jew already diverted his attention we say "a Shabbat that was permitted," for here the matter does not depend on trustworthiness but on diverting his attention on erev Shabbat. Therefore it appears that in truth there is no difference between non-Jew and Jew except in our doubt on erev Shabbat whether he will come on Shabbat; and on this the tannaim disagree regarding a non-Jew on Shabbat, and Rama decided to be lenient regarding a gentile in doubt; and with this he ruled that if the non-Jew came on Shabbat he restricts, and we do not say here "once Shabbat was permitted," etc., since it was permitted only out of doubt. But if it was evident clearly on erev Shabbat that he would not come on Shabbat and he diverted his attention, but afterward changed his mind and came on Shabbat — even for a non-Jew one may say "once Shabbat was permitted it remains permitted," as with a Jew; and thus Rosh's words in the second chapter of "How they participate," and these are his words: if he returned to his house on Shabbat, some say he restricts, and they bring proof from a non-Jew — when he is not present he does not restrict, and when he is present he restricts. And further from nullifying domain — if he returned and removed items he restricts. And it appears to me: since R' Shimon permitted only because he diverted his attention and will certainly not return, it is a Shabbat that was permitted and will not become forbidden again; and one may say the same for a non-Jew who was destined to come on Shabbat, etc., end of his words — behold he differentiated only between one who diverted his attention and a non-Jew who did not divert his attention. Automatically, if it is clear to us also regarding a non-Jew that he diverted his attention, why should he restrict afterward? Therefore what Tur wrote at the beginning of the siman — "and he went while it was still day to spend Shabbat at his daughter's and even if he returned on Shabbat it is permitted" — means: he certainly diverted his heart on erev Shabbat from coming on Shabbat. And what he wrote afterward regarding a non-Jew — nevertheless if he came on Shabbat he restricts — this is when there was doubt on erev Shabbat. And it requires study to explain his wording thus.
