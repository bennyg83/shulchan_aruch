[6] Jewish kings, etc. The Gemara implies that even a blind person who knows that the king is passing recites the blessing. Magen Avraham. This requires study, however, from the wording of the Gemara: “Rav Sheshes began and blessed him,” which implies that he blessed the king in the manner of a greeting, and did not recite the blessing to the Holy One, blessed is He. This is also reasonable, since the Gemara states “one who sees Jewish kings,” and likewise with all blessings over sights, a blind person does not recite the blessing. The case above, at the beginning of siman 59, is different because he derives benefit. See there. Maharsha, however, in Chiddushei Aggados, plainly understood that he does recite the blessing. Responsa Radbaz writes that one recites the blessing over governors in their cities who are not subject to a king who can reverse their decisions from yes to no or from no to yes, and who execute judgment. He further writes that if one sees several kings or sages in one day, he recites a separate blessing over each one. Over the same king or sage, however, he recites the blessing only once every thirty days. We also hold that it is a mitzvah to make an effort to see kings. A kohen may even walk within four amos of a grave in order to see a king, since one may override a Rabbinic prohibition in order to see kings. Sefer Chassidim, siman 955, writes that if a king comes with a great army and splendor, a righteous person who went out to greet him once should not refrain from doing so another time if he hears that the king is coming with a greater army and greater honor.
