But he is obligated in sukkah. In the Gemara they ask: this is obvious. One might have said that since Rava said one who is distressed is exempt from sukkah, this one too is distressed; it teaches that only distress that comes on its own exempts, but here he is distressing himself and should settle his mind. The Rosh explained that one might have thought the mourner is more distressed in the sukkah than in the house, for a mourner wants to be alone and to sit in a place of distress and darkness in order to be occupied with his grief; it teaches otherwise. This is difficult: from where would we say such a poor reasoning, that for a mourner it would be considered distress not to be able to distress himself? If so, it would be forbidden to comfort mourners, since one distresses him by preventing him from being distressed. Furthermore, from where do we know this reasoning at all? Why not answer that with a mourner he has no advantage in the house more than in the sukkah, but one might have thought that since he is preoccupied he is exempt from sukkah; it teaches that we require something like dwelling, and he is not exempt unless he is saved from distress, as the Mordechai wrote nearby. Why did it need to distinguish between distress that comes on its own or not? And if you say it was obvious to the Talmud that the reason is not preoccupation, as it says there in the sugya regarding Shema for a mourner, 'if so, one whose ship sank at sea should also be exempt,' and one who is distressed is exempt only because we require something like dwelling, then the Gemara's question here about a mourner is obvious: he is obligated, since it is impossible for him to escape the distress. The answerer should have answered that there is a way to escape here, because he prefers being alone, like the Rosh's words. If so, why distinguish between distress that comes on its own or not? It is unnecessary; he should have answered briefly that there is a distinction with a mourner, who can be saved from distress, for that is the essential answer. If we say, on the contrary, that the truth is that the exemption of one who is distressed does not depend on being able to be saved, but he is exempt because of preoccupation, and although with other mitzvos we do not care that one is preoccupied with distress, as the Gemara says that a mourner is obligated in all mitzvos except tefillin, which are called glory, sukkah is different because it requires intent, as I wrote, then the answer works well: since the reason is preoccupation, one might have thought that a mourner too has the preoccupation of distress; it teaches that only distress that comes on its own, etc., but a mourner is not included in the distressed person exempt from sukkah. Yet this is difficult according to the Mordechai in the name of Re'em, who holds the matter depends on whether he can be saved. It appears to me that both are true, for there are two kinds of distress. One is when he is distressed specifically because of the sukkah, meaning the sukkah causes him distress when he sits there. Then exemption because of preoccupation does not apply; on the contrary, in this he remembers the mitzvah of sukkah more, since he is forced to sit in a place of distress for him, as stated in siman 625, that for this reason Hashem commanded to make sukkah in Tishrei and not in Nisan, because in Tishrei other people leave the sukkah, etc. In this case we say that he is exempt only if he has a place where he can be saved. But other preoccupation, where distress comes to a person from another side, such as illness or other bodily distress, where he has no more comfort in the house than in the sukkah, nevertheless he is exempt because of preoccupation, for he cannot have intent for sukkah. If so, one might have thought that a mourner too is exempt although there is no difference between house and sukkah; it teaches that this is not called preoccupation at all, for he should settle his mind and become obligated in the mitzvos of Hashem. And if you ask: if so, what does this teach? There is no distressed person exempt because of preoccupation except a sick person, and we already heard from the Mishnah that sick people and their attendants are exempt. One can answer that this itself is what it teaches: although it is impossible to be saved, and from the Mishnah I might have thought specifically with a sick person who has a difference between house and sukkah, but if he has no difference he is obligated, it teaches that nevertheless he is exempt since he is distressed and preoccupied. If so, we do not need that forced reasoning mentioned by the Rosh, that a mourner has distress in not being able to be distressed. What we have written is correct, with Heaven's help.
