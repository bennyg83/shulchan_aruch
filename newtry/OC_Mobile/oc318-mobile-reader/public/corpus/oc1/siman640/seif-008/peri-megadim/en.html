He concluded that a non-dangerously ill person, and bodily distress where his mind is unsettled, meaning like those with melancholy and the like, are exempt from sukkah even though it is impossible to be saved from the distress. Nevertheless, the essence of sukkah is to contemplate the wonders of Hashem, from the language of seeing, as in 'the father of Yiskah,' and one who is distressed does not pay attention. One might have thought that a mourner too is exempt for this reason, so it teaches that he must settle his mind, since he can settle it and it depends on him. Automatically, if one's ship sank at sea, he too is obligated. Only bodily distress from Heaven is exempt in such a case. What the Mordechai wrote, that if one cannot be saved he is obligated, means where the distress comes from the sukkah, such as sitting in the sun, fleas, cold, or rain, and he has no house to go to and be saved from it but would have to sit in the street or field. In such a case he is obligated in sukkah, although he is distressed, because it is somewhat because of the sukkah and he remembers the wonders of Hashem and the idea of exile, leaving permanence for the temporary. The word 'some say' is a scribal error and should read 'one might have thought.' What he asked, what does this teach us, means that a distressed person exempt because of preoccupation is specifically a sick person or bodily distress with unsettled mind, as written above, or one who has a painful wound in one place. If so, from the Mishnah we hear that a sick person and one distressed like him are exempt. But a sick person's attendants are exempt, whereas one distressed is exempt only himself, and automatically a mourner is obligated, and regarding every preoccupied person one could say it teaches that a sick person is exempt although he cannot be saved, while a mourner must settle his mind. See Rashi, 25b and 26a, where 'one distressed' means the sukkah distresses him; where he can save himself by going home, he is exempt because of 'in the manner of dwelling,' since in such a dwelling one leaves and goes away. The same applies to bodily distress that comes on its own, even though it is impossible to be saved from it.
