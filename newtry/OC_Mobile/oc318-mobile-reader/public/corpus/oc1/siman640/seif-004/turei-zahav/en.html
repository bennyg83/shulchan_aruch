One who cannot sleep, etc. This matter is a question in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 92, and he resolved it from the fact that a small sukkah, whose measure is seven by seven tefachim and which has no room to lie down unless one bends his legs, is nevertheless valid. I am astonished about this, for certainly it is great distress to lie that way all night in a narrow place. What question is there, and why did the Rema need to write this? If he comes to say that a person cannot escape sleeping in the sukkah because of cramped space, we should say to him: since you made a sukkah in a way that you cannot lie down, you did not even fulfill eating, for you knew that you could not lie down and would be distressed by lying, and making it initially in this manner does not help even for eating, as the Rema wrote nearby, based on the Mordechai. And if it speaks of a case where initially it was fit for lying down and afterward something happened that made it distant or narrow to lie down, then it is fitting to be uncertain whether he is exempt from sleep because he is distressed; but he answered nothing from the proof of a small sukkah. In a small sukkah, he made it from the outset in such a way that he would tolerate cramped lying, and with that in mind he made the sukkah. But here, where it had been fit and the distress of lying arose afterward, this certainly has the law of one who is distressed and is exempt from sleeping; how is it different from any other distress that arose? And if he teaches that it is permitted to make a sukkah in a way that he will suffer distress when lying down, that is obvious, for they explicitly said that it is permitted to make a small sukkah seven by seven, and all the more so if it is a little larger. I do not know how to resolve why Terumas HaDeshen and the Rema entered into saying that this is not called distress. Certainly it is called distress, and he is exempt from sleeping when this matter arose after it had not been so initially. He is exempt from sleeping unless he made it from the beginning with that in mind. This appears clear to me.
Except in a matter about which people normally, etc. Regarding what we find in Perek HaYashen, that Rav Yosef was sitting in the sukkah and splinters came through the wind, and he said, clear my utensils from the sukkah, and Abaye said to him, but we learned 'until the dish spoils,' and he answered, I am delicate, and for me it is like the dish spoiling, this shows that we follow the individual's disposition. One can say that all delicate people are equal in this, and regarding them they are considered many; one should not say that their view is nullified relative to everyone.
Only if he saves himself from the distress. For we require something like dwelling, and people do not remain in their dwelling in distress. So wrote the Mordechai in the name of Re'em. It is difficult: this is obvious. Why should he be exempt when he has no more distress in the sukkah than in his house? It should have said this in a case where he has no distress in either place: why should he cancel the mitzvah of sukkah for nothing? One can say that one might have thought that the reason one who is distressed is exempt is because the essence of the mitzvah of sukkah is to recall the matter of the sukkos, as it says, 'for I caused them to dwell in sukkos,' and this distressed person is occupied with his distress and does not put his heart to this, and therefore he is exempt. It therefore teaches that the reason is not this, but that we require something like dwelling, and a person does not live in a place of distress whenever he can be saved from it by leaving. So too with sukkah.
