A groom and his attendants, etc. This is the Rambam's language. He holds that what is stated in the Yerushalmi, that many acted stringently, means a measure of piety. The Tur concluded: my father, the Rosh, of blessed memory, wrote that the attendants are not called engaged in a mitzvah, and they are obligated in all mitzvos except prayer and tefillin; and the groom, although he is exempt from all mitzvos, is obligated in sukkah, because he can rejoice in the sukkah. These are his words. The Beis Yosef wondered why he needed to say that the attendants are not called engaged in a mitzvah, since the groom himself is engaged in a mitzvah and nevertheless obligated in sukkah, as he concludes. It appears to me that there is no difficulty. The Rosh wrote in this language: it appears that one should act like the Yerushalmi incident. This language implies that initially everyone should do this, meaning where it is easy to fulfill both mitzvos, sukkah and chuppah. But if it is not so easy, he is exempt. The attendants, however, are obligated in every case, since they are not so engaged in a mitzvah. The reason they are exempt from prayer is because joy commonly brings lightheadedness. Therefore the Tur concluded regarding the groom that he can rejoice, meaning that when he can do so easily, why should he not fulfill both? Terumas HaDeshen, siman 7, wrote: I remember in my youth at a wedding of the daughter of a great elderly Torah scholar, he and several Torah scholars were reclining at that meal, and the time for Minchah arrived at the end of its time. They had their meal before them and did not pray; only one stood to pray, and the others were somewhat astonished at him. These are his words. This is unlike the Rosh. The Beis Yosef in siman 232 brings this and writes about it: there is no substance to this reasoning. These are his words.
