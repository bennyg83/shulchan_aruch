For simchah exists only in the chuppah, in the place of the meal, and in a sukkah it is impossible to make a chuppah because of the groom's distress, since the place is open and he is embarrassed to play with his bride; this is from the Gemara. If so, nowadays, when we have a sukkah with four walls, he must sit in the sukkah, for we rule like Rava. Furthermore, the Rosh, Baal Halachos, and Tur obligate in every case. It speaks where he married on the eve of the festival; see siman 546 and Even HaEzer, siman 62, se'if 10.

Are obligated in the sukkah. This is not comparable to a wedding meal, which is a great mitzvah, to gladden groom and bride, and is called a mitzvah meal everywhere. But here it is only a mere custom. These are the words of Maharik. Meaning: even though the place is too narrow for them to sit there, and they cannot rejoice there so much, nevertheless they are obligated in sukkah. Although it is a mitzvah meal, as written in Yoreh De'ah, siman 265, se'if 12, nevertheless it is not so much of a mitzvah, for we do not find it explicit in the Gemara like a wedding meal, which is explicit in chapter 3 of Pesachim; see there. If so, ten people suffice for them. See Nachalas Shivah, who wrote that the meal made in Ashkenaz on the night before circumcision, where they invite the mohel, sandak, and other relatives and friends, is called a mitzvah meal regarding vows. This is not compelling. On the contrary, it is proven from Maharam, whom he brought, that specifically when they send gifts is the night before the chuppah called a mitzvah meal, implying that where gifts are not sent it is not called a mitzvah meal, as written in siman 558. If so, the same applies to the night before a circumcision; see there, page 53. Therefore one should be stringent regarding vows.
