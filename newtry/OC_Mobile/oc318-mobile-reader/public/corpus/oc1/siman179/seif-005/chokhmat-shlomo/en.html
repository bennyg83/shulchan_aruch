Seif 5. Guests in the host’s home who are served various fruits. Note: See Taz, who disagrees with this ruling, while Eliyah Rabbah decides like the Shulchan Aruch. In my humble opinion, the law follows Taz. Even if Tosafos intended what Beit Yosef understood, and as Peri Megadim notes this is the corrected reading in our editions of the Gemara, their own words are difficult. The principle that guests rely upon the host’s table applies during a meal, where one certainly need not have had specific intention. Even without such intention, one may eat whatever is brought during the meal. Only where one explicitly withdrew from further eating is more food forbidden. Since the diners rely upon the host’s table, their statement creates at most a doubt as to whether they withdrew, and they remain in the ordinary state as though they had not said, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon.” With fruit, however, if none of the original fruit remains before him, he must by law recite a blessing over newly brought fruit, unless we affirmatively say that he explicitly intended it from the beginning. What basis is there to say that reliance upon the host’s table creates a definite intention, as though he certainly had such fruit in mind? Perhaps it creates only a doubt or leaves the matter unspecified. The principal halachah therefore follows Taz. Eliyah Rabbah’s argument that in a doubt concerning blessings we rule leniently is astonishing. That principle applies when one is not dealing with a blessing over benefit, or even regarding an after-blessing after he has already eaten, since he has no other way to avoid the doubt. But regarding a blessing before eating, if there is a doubt, let him neither benefit nor recite a blessing. Examine this carefully.
