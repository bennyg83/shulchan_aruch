1) [Seif 1] If he finished his meal, etc., he may not eat, etc. It is permitted, however, to interrupt with speech between the final waters and the blessing. Kesef Mishneh writes this at the end of chapter 6 of Hilchos Berachos according to the Rambam, of blessed memory, and Magen Avraham, paragraph 1, cites it. According to Rosh, however, Beis Yosef writes in this siman that it is forbidden to interrupt even with speech; see there. Magen Avraham there holds the same view, as do Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1, Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1, and Zechor LeAvraham, under samech. We wrote likewise above in siman 166, paragraph 7; see there. Shaar HaMitzvos, parashas Eikev, similarly writes that one must be extremely careful not to interrupt at all between the final waters and Birkas HaMazon, even with words of Torah. It relates that a certain man once came before the Arizal and told him that for two days he had suffered severe pain in his shoulder. The Arizal looked at him and said that this pain came because he interrupted between the final waters and Birkas HaMazon by reading a chapter of Mishnah, thereby transgressing the Sages' statement that a blessing must immediately follow washing, referring to the washing of the final waters. Therefore, the word meaning 'immediately' was transformed into the word 'shoulder,' and he felt pain there; see there. See above, siman 166, and our words there, with the help of Heaven.
2) There: He may not eat, etc. The Shulchan Aruch distinguishes between washing one's hands and saying, 'Let us recite the blessing.' After washing his hands, all agree that he may neither eat nor drink until he recites Birkas HaMazon. After saying, 'Let us recite the blessing,' all agree that to drink he requires only a new initial blessing over what he wishes to drink. Regarding eating, according to Rosh he requires an initial blessing over what he wishes to eat, while according to Rabbeinu Yonah and Ran, even an initial blessing is unnecessary. The Tur, however, holds that washing and saying, 'Let us recite the blessing,' are equivalent. For both eating and drinking, he requires only an initial blessing over what he wishes to eat or drink; see there. Bach likewise writes that Rosh agrees with the Tur, contrary to Beis Yosef's understanding; see there. Taz, paragraph 1, and Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, write the same. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1, similarly cites several authorities who agree with the Tur; see there. Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 1. Therefore, since the matter is disputed, it appears that as a practical ruling one should initially be careful: whether he washed or merely said, 'Let us recite the blessing,' he should neither eat nor drink more until reciting Birkas HaMazon. If, however, for some reason he needs to resume eating or drinking, then if he washed, he must recite an initial blessing over what he wishes to eat or drink, afterward wash again, and recite Birkas HaMazon. That blessing also covers his first eating and drinking, in accordance with the Tur and the authorities who follow him. If he merely said, 'Let us recite the blessing,' but did not wash, he recites a blessing before drinking but not before eating, in accordance with Rabbeinu Yonah and Ran, because in cases of doubt concerning blessings we rule leniently. He should merely contemplate the blessing in his heart. See below, paragraphs 6 and 7.
3) There: If he said, 'Let us recite the blessing,' etc. Some say that pouring a cup for Birkas HaMazon, even without speech, is equivalent to saying, 'Let us recite the blessing.' Maharshal; Ateres Zekeinim, paragraph 1. For those who practice eating from the piece used for HaMotzi at the end of the meal, however, eating that piece at the meal's conclusion does not constitute withdrawing from the meal. Petach HaDevir, paragraph 1. See our words above in siman 167, paragraph 137, and understand this.
4) There: It constitutes a diversion of attention, etc. Tosafos in Berachos 42 and Pesachim 101, the Tur in siman 477, and Rosh in chapter 7 of Chullin imply that whenever a new blessing is required because of a diversion of attention, one must also wash his hands. The reason is that once he diverted his attention from eating, he presumably did not take care to guard his hands. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2; Chayei Adam, rule 44, paragraph 4. Even if he says he is certain that he guarded them, this is ineffective. Orach Chaim, paragraph 1, brings proof from chapter 7 of Taharos, mishnah 8; see there. He should not, however, recite a blessing over the washing, as stated above in siman 164, paragraph 16. Chayei Adam there writes the same.
5) There: Unless he first recites a blessing over it. Magen Avraham, siman 208, paragraph 28, writes that a concluding blessing is also required and Birkas HaMazon does not cover it, for this is no better than one who withdrew his hand from bread in siman 177, seif 2; see there. Eliyah Rabbah in this siman, paragraph 4, however, writes that one can distinguish the cases. There, even when he eats he remains withdrawn from bread, whereas when one formed a new intention, it is as though he returns to the bread. He writes that this appears to be the view of the Shelah cited by Magen Avraham there. The unqualified wording of the Shulchan Aruch likewise appears to indicate that only an initial blessing is required.
6) There: According to Rabbeinu Yonah and Ran, eating is different, etc. Beis Yosef writes in the name of Ran that the distinction between drinking and eating is that drinking has no fixed framework. Therefore, when they say, 'Let us recite the blessing,' he is considered to have formed a new intention and must recite another blessing. Eating, however, has a fixed framework, for sometimes a person sits for a small meal and it extends into a large meal. Consequently, even if he finished and even if he removed the food, he may eat until he washes his hands and need not recite a blessing; see there. Taz, paragraph 2, cites this and writes that if he returns only to drink, he may not drink without a blessing. If he returns to eat and this leads him also to drink, he certainly need not recite a blessing over the drink, for in this case it properly follows the eating; see there. Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 3, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 3, write the same. Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes there, however, that if he is thirsty because of his original eating, he must recite a blessing; see there.
