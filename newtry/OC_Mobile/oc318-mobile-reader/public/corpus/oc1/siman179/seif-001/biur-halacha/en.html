Until he recites Bircas HaMazon - See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote that several poskim hold that mayim acharonim is comparable to saying, “Let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” and that it suffices for him to recite HaMotzi over whatever he wishes to eat. Bach and Magen Avraham, however, write that ideally one should be careful in this regard. Upon examination, one certainly should not be lenient initially, for we find that many of the leading Rishonim are stringent. This is the view of Rashi, Rashbam, Or Zarua, who cites it in the name of his teacher, Rambam, Raavad as cited by Rashba, Rashba, Rabbeinu Yonah, and Raah in his commentary to the Rif in Berachos. The Gra likewise explains Rabbeinu Yonah as holding like Beit Yosef, unlike Taz, who writes that Rabbeinu Yonah agrees that a second washing permits further eating. Rashba too writes that the reason is that he has already prepared himself for Bircas HaMazon, implying that a second washing is ineffective. Raah writes that eating is forbidden even if he wishes to recite HaMotzi, because once he washed his hands for the blessing, he accepted upon himself the obligation to recite Bircas HaMazon. Several of these authorities are stringent even when one merely said, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” and prohibit further eating even with a new blessing, because he prepared himself for Bircas HaMazon and withdrew from the meal. Many authorities, however, are lenient in that case. Rif, Rabbeinu Chananel, and Rashi in Chullin permit eating after a new blessing, as do Tosafos, Rosh, and those who follow them; according to Ri and Ran, one may even eat without a new blessing. Nevertheless, after washing his hands one must certainly be stringent. This is also the Gra’s conclusion in his commentary, where he writes that after washing one specifically requires Bircas HaMazon.
And even if they removed the table - Meaning, they removed the bread and other food from the table, as Rashi explains there in the Gemara. This wording implies that removal of the table constitutes a stronger diversion of attention than saying, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon.” Accordingly, according to Rosh, who requires a new blessing after saying, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” all the more so after the table was removed. In truth, however, this is not so. That conclusion follows only the view of Rabbeinu Yonah and Ran, who understand the sugya in Berachos to concern only further eating. According to Rosh, by contrast, saying, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” is a stronger diversion of attention, while removing the table does not constitute a diversion at all, as is evident from the Gemara there, which concludes, “The halachah does not follow any of these teachings,” referring also to removal of the table, as Rashi explains. The Shulchan Aruch does not explicitly clarify Rosh’s view except regarding saying, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon.” It does not discuss whether, according to Rosh, a person who simply decided not to eat or drink any more, without saying those words, must recite a new blessing if he later changes his mind. Rambam explicitly rules that if one resolved in his heart not to eat or drink any more and then reconsidered, he must recite a new blessing even though he did not change his location. Beit Yosef cites his view as contrary to Ran and Ri. Rambam understands the Gemara’s initial statement, “One who finished may not eat,” and its later conclusion that the halachah does not follow that teaching, as follows: “Finished” means that he completed his meal, and the Gemara initially assumed that this itself constitutes a diversion of attention, since presumably one who finished his meal has withdrawn his attention. The conclusion rejects that presumption. But if one explicitly resolved not to eat or drink any more, that is stronger than merely completing the meal or removing the table. This explanation appears in Migdal Oz, and his words are very sound in Rambam’s view. Raah likewise holds that one who changed his mind not to eat and then returns to eating must recite a new blessing. It is possible that Rosh also interprets “finished” as having completed the meal, and understands the conclusion to mean that even after the table was removed we assume he still intended to eat if food became available. But if he explicitly withdrew his attention from further eating and drinking, perhaps Rosh too considers that stronger than removal of the table, like Rambam, and comparable to saying, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon.” I found that Beit Meir also raised this issue and ultimately inclines to say that according to Rosh an explicit diversion of attention is effective even regarding food and requires a new blessing. Shulchan Aruch HaRav rules likewise.
If he wishes to resume eating, etc. - See Peri Megadim, who writes that this is not limited to bread. The same applies to other foods. According to Rabbeinu Yonah and Ran, one who says, “Let us recite the after-blessing,” has not withdrawn from further eating. If he wishes to eat more, he may do so without a new first blessing.
He need not recite a new blessing, etc. - The Mechaber did not explicitly decide the halachah between the opinions. At first glance, it appears that he follows Rabbeinu Yonah and Ran, since they are the majority. Yet in siman 193, seif 1, the Mechaber rules without qualification like Rosh, that saying, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” concludes the meal, as Magen Avraham explains there in seif katan 2. Rosh is not alone in this view. Rambam also holds that once one says, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” he may no longer eat, and Rambam holds that even a new blessing does not permit it, as Beit Yosef cites. Tosafos in Berachos 42b, s.v. teikef, also hold this view, as does Or Zarua in sections 177 and 194, and Rashba in Berachos 48a, s.v. yahavu. I later found that Eliyah Rabbah cites many additional poskim who agree with Rosh, and writes that Rabbeinu Yonah is alone in this matter. Maamar Mordechai objects that Ran cites this view in the name of Ramban and Rabbi Meshullam, who follow the same position; see his lengthy discussion. Nevertheless, however one counts them, most poskim agree with Rosh that there is no distinction between eating and drinking. Eliyah Rabbah concludes that in practice, after saying, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” one should not eat until reciting a new blessing. The Gra, however, writes that the principal halachah appears to follow Rabbeinu Yonah and Ran, based on the episode of Shimon ben Shetach in Berachos 48a. See Tosafos in Pesachim 103b, s.v. keivan, who raise this difficulty and answer that this too was something he did on his own initiative, but the Gemara rejected his view for an independent reason. Rashba in Berachos 48a writes similarly. We therefore cannot decide the practical halachah. One should avoid eating after saying, “Come, let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” because this creates a doubt concerning a blessing, and Derech HaChaim rules likewise. Similarly, if one explicitly withdrew his attention from further eating, even without saying those words, it is very proper initially to avoid eating, since many Rishonim are stringent in that case, as explained above.
