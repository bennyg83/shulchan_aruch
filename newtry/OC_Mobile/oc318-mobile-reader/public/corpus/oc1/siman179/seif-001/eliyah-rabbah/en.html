Some say that one has permission, etc. Malbushei Yom Tov writes that this is Tur’s opinion and that Beit Yosef already rejected it. I say that Beit Yosef indeed rejected it entirely and wrote that according to all opinions, once one has washed his hands for mayim acharonim, he may neither eat nor drink at all until reciting Bircas HaMazon. He writes that Rosh rules this way, and Shulchan Aruch rules without qualification accordingly. I am astonished that Beit Yosef did not fully discharge his duty of investigation. I found in Baal Halachos Gedolos, page 10: If he wishes to drink water or taste something after washing his hands, he recites a blessing, drinks, and afterward recites Bircas HaMazon. This is explicit support for Tur. The same appears from Shibbolei HaLeket, page 19b, Sefer Tanya, siman 25, Rashi at the end of the chapter Elu Devarim, and explicitly in Sefer Tanya, page 34b, and Sefer Zikaron of Rabbi Yosef HaKohen, chapter Keitzad Mevarchin. I saw that Bach also disagrees with Beit Yosef’s statement that Rosh opposes Tur. He explains at length that Rosh agrees with Tur. Nevertheless, Bach and Magen Avraham conclude that a God-fearing person should neither eat nor drink, even with a blessing, after washing his hands. This too is astonishing. There is no doubt that had Bach seen the words of the great authorities cited above, he would have ruled in practice in accordance with Tur. It is even more astonishing that they did not see Rosh in the chapter Arvei Pesachim, cited by Beit Yosef himself below in siman 271. Rosh writes: Although he washed his hands, he need not recite Bircas HaMazon. Rather, he recites a blessing over whatever he eats or drinks, just as after saying “Let us recite Bircas HaMazon.” This explicitly shows that Rosh agrees with Tur. The same is implied by Tosafos there, page 42, Mordechai, Kitzur Piskei HaRosh there, and explicitly by Avudraham, page 133. Rashba goes even further and writes that saying “Let us recite Bircas HaMazon” is worse than washing mayim acharonim, because by speaking he explicitly accepted upon himself to recite the blessing. Therefore, as practical halachah, I rule in accordance with the “some say” recorded by Levush, that one may eat or drink after reciting the appropriate blessing. Nevertheless, if he does not wish to eat or drink further, he may not interrupt with speech. This appears correct to me. Magen Avraham and Bach also imply that even without speaking, one should not delay for the time required to walk twenty two amos. See above, siman 166, and below, siman 181.
