[Levush] Some say it is optional, etc. Malbushei Yom Tov says this is the Tur's view and that the Beit Yosef already rejected it. I say that the Beit Yosef indeed rejected it entirely and wrote that according to everyone, once one has washed mayim acharonim it is completely forbidden to eat or drink until Birkat HaMazon, and that the Rosh rules so; the Shulchan Aruch likewise rules without qualification. But I am astonished that the Beit Yosef did not complete his investigation. I found in Baal Halachot Gedolot, folio 10, that if one wishes to drink water or taste something after washing his hands, he recites a blessing, drinks, and then recites Birkat HaMazon. This is explicit like the Tur. I also find support in Shibolei HaLeket, folio 19b, Sefer Tanya siman 25, Rashi at the end of Elu Devarim, Sefer Tanya folio 34b, and Sefer Zikaron by Rabbi Yishmael HaKohen in Keitzad Mevarchin. I saw that the Bach too disagrees with the Beit Yosef's claim that the Rosh opposes the Tur, and he explains at length that the Rosh agrees with the Tur. Nevertheless, the Bach and Magen Avraham conclude that a God fearing person should not eat or drink even with a blessing after mayim acharonim. This too is surprising. Had the Bach seen the great authorities I cited, there is no doubt he would have ruled practically like the Tur. More surprising, they overlooked the Rosh in Arvei Pesachim, cited by the Beit Yosef himself below in siman 271, who explicitly writes that although one washed his hands he need not immediately recite Birkat HaMazon, but may bless over what he eats or drinks, just as when he says 'Let us bless.' Tosafot there, Mordechai, Kitzur Piskei HaRosh, and Abudarham all say the same explicitly. The Rashba's novellae go further and write that saying 'Let us bless' is more decisive than washing the hands because in the former case he explicitly accepted upon himself to bless. Therefore I rule in practice like the 'some say' cited by the Levush, that one may eat and drink after reciting the appropriate blessing. Nevertheless, if he does not wish to eat or drink further, he may not interrupt with conversation. Magen Avraham and the Bach imply likewise, and even without speaking one should not interrupt for the time needed to walk twenty two cubits. See above siman 166 and below siman 181.
[Levush] And he should first recite a blessing, etc. If the food is bread, he must also wash his hands. Tosafot Berachot 42 implies this.
[Levush] And it does not require, etc. The Bach writes that although the Rashbam and Rashba in Keitzad Mevarchin do not rule this way, one need not be stringent because their view is nullified against the majority of poskim. He apparently overlooked the Rashba in Kisui HaDam, where the Rashba explicitly rules like the majority that no separate final blessing is required. In Keitzad Mevarchin too the Rashba concludes that the Rif did not write otherwise.
[Levush] And some say that saying 'Let us bless,' etc. The Shulchan Aruch writes that according to the Rosh eating has the same law as drinking, whereas Rabbenu Yonah and the Ran distinguish eating. Its language appears to rule like Rabbenu Yonah and the Ran because the Rosh would be a lone opinion against them. Lechem Chamudot also implies this. I am astonished at the Beit Yosef, because Tosafot Berachot 42 explicitly writes like the Rosh in two places. I also saw the same in the Mordechai, Agudah, Rashba's novellae, Kol Bo, Tashbetz siman 314, Rabbenu Yerucham, Tzeidah LaDerech, Sefer Zikaron by Rabbi Yishmael HaKohen, and Abudarham. Rabbenu Yonah and the Ran are therefore certainly the minority. I am also surprised how the Beit Yosef knows that the Ran agrees with Rabbenu Yonah. In Kisui HaDam the Ran cites Rabbi Meshullam that regarding slaughter, when one changes his mind he must recite a new blessing. This is unlike one who sits and eats during a meal, where even if he decided mentally not to eat more he does not return and break bread again, because even if he finished and even if the table was removed he may still eat until he washes his hands. Eating has a fixed framework and a person may begin with a small meal and extend it into a large one, whereas slaughter has no fixed framework, so a changed decision requires a new blessing. The Ran may well hold that eating and drinking are both prohibited once one says 'Let us bless' or washes, while mere removal of the table is not a diversion. The distinction from slaughter is that slaughter lacks a fixed framework. The Beit Yosef understood the Ran as distinguishing drinking because drinking has no fixed framework, but I do not know where he found that. The Ran's words indicate no such distinction, as does the plain sense of the Gemara. Moreover, the Beit Yosef is forced into a very strained reading. According to my reading the Ran's discussion is straightforward. I later found in the Rashba's novellae to Kisui HaDam wording similar to the Ran but with a textual reading that seems to have caused the Beit Yosef's interpretation, and Maadanei Melech likewise implies that he had such a text. In my humble opinion that text is corrupt, because drinking is not the subject there at all. There is a further difficulty: the Ran speaks of saying 'Let us bless Birkat HaMazon,' which necessarily concerns a fixed meal. If he only meant drinking, we rule in siman 212 that no blessing is recited on such subordinate drinking, before or after. The Beit Yosef himself already wrote that drinking follows eating. If eating is permitted, drinking should be as well. The correct text is therefore the one before me, contrasting eating with slaughter. The Rashba's text should be corrected accordingly. I also find a clear proof, because otherwise the Rashba would contradict himself: in Keitzad Mevarchin he writes that even after 'Let us bless' eating is forbidden without a new blessing and that this is even more decisive than washing. The correct practical ruling is therefore that once one says 'Let us bless,' he should not eat again until reciting a new blessing. See below siman 196, seif 1. Magen Avraham at the end of siman 208 writes that a new final blessing is also required and Birkat HaMazon does not exempt the new eating, since it is no better than one who withdrew from bread in siman 177, seif 2. In my humble opinion one can distinguish the cases: there, even while he continues eating, he remains withdrawn from bread; here, a new decision to eat is comparable to returning to bread. This appears to be the Shelah's view.
[Levush] And drinking is clear, etc. According to this, it is difficult why a new first blessing should be required for drinking either. One must say the Levush understood the Beit Yosef this way. The Bach and Taz, however, write that the Beit Yosef contains a scribal error. It should read, 'Rather, it is difficult, since, etc.' It seems to me the better correction is, 'It does not appear correct to explain.' It also appears that the Levush emended the Beit Yosef in a way that produced his interpretation, and this is plausible because the Beit Yosef then writes, 'and so is the view of Rabbenu Yonah.' Note this well. I have already written that in any event the Beit Yosef's discussion here does not appear correct to me.
