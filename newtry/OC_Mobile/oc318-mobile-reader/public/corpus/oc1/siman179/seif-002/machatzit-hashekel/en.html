Seif katan 2, until, etc. The halachah does not follow, etc. Berachos 42a records that Rav Huna son of Rav Nachman holds that once one has finished eating, he may no longer eat, whereas Rav Pappa holds that even after finishing he may still eat unless the table has been cleared, meaning that the bread and all foods were removed. The Gemara concludes that the halachah does not follow any of these teachings, for whether he merely finished or even cleared the table, he may still eat. Rather, it follows Rabbi Chiya bar Ashi in the name of Rav, who said that there are three matters requiring immediate succession, one of which is that Bircas HaMazon must immediately follow mayim acharonim. Rashi explains that only after washing may one no longer eat. The Rosh writes that if one said, “Bring us the cup and let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” then according to Rashi and Rashbam, who explain Rav’s statement in Pesachim 103b, he may no longer drink until he first recites Bircas HaMazon. Some distinguish between eating and drinking, namely Rabbeinu Yonah and Ran, whose view is cited here in Shulchan Aruch, but the Rosh rejects that distinction. The Rosh in the chapters Kisui HaDam and Arvei Pesachim disagrees with Rashi and Rashbam. He holds that after saying “Bring us the cup and let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” one may not drink unless he first recites Borei Peri HaGafen, but he need not first recite Bircas HaMazon. The Rosh does not state explicitly what he holds after mayim acharonim, whether one must first recite Bircas HaMazon or whether a new blessing before the food or drink suffices. The Tur understands that even after mayim acharonim one need not first recite Bircas HaMazon. Beit Yosef, however, infers from the Rosh that after mayim acharonim he agrees that Bircas HaMazon must be recited first. The Rosh writes that only according to Rashi and Rashbam is saying “Bring us the cup and let us recite Bircas HaMazon” equivalent to mayim acharonim. This implies that according to the Rosh, who holds that this declaration does not require Bircas HaMazon first, it is not equivalent to mayim acharonim. Were the Rosh to hold that even after mayim acharonim one need not recite Bircas HaMazon first, the two cases would be equivalent according to him as well. It must therefore be that he agrees that after mayim acharonim one must first recite Bircas HaMazon. Shulchan Aruch therefore rules here unlike the Tur.
How could he contradict in the abridgment, etc. Although the Tur also authored the abridgment of the Rosh’s rulings, it would not be so astonishing if, while writing the Tur, the Rosh’s language cited by Beit Yosef temporarily escaped him. But while writing the abridgment, the Rosh’s work stood before him and he was condensing it. How could he write the opposite of the Rosh’s own conclusion while those words were before his eyes? Moreover, the Rosh himself writes in Arvei Pesachim, siman 7, regarding the Rif’s ruling. Although the halachah follows Shmuel that members of a group eating on Friday afternoon need not interrupt when Shabbos begins, but merely cover the bread, recite Kiddush, and continue their meal, the Rif writes that if they finished their meal before Shabbos and also washed mayim acharonim, they must first recite Bircas HaMazon and only afterward Kiddush. The Rosh challenges this: since Kiddush must be recited in the place of the meal, and the meal eaten before Kiddush cannot reasonably establish that status, they must eat again after Kiddush. Why, then, must they first recite Bircas HaMazon? They should cover the bread, recite Kiddush, and continue eating. He concludes, “Although they washed their hands,” etc. Thus the Rosh clearly holds that even after mayim acharonim one need not first recite Bircas HaMazon, and a new blessing before eating suffices, contrary to Beit Yosef.
The same is evident from siman 10, where the Rosh explains why Bircas HaMazon differs from other matters requiring a cup, such as Kiddush and Havdalah, in all of which Borei Peri HaGafen precedes the mitzvah, whereas here it is recited afterward. One might have expected the enactment to require Borei Peri HaGafen before Bircas HaMazon. It cannot be answered that Borei Peri HaGafen cannot be recited first because, had he wished to drink, he could have done so without a new blessing. That is incorrect, for once he said, “Bring us the cup and let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” he may no longer drink without Borei Peri HaGafen, and all the more so once he took the cup in hand for Bircas HaMazon. Rather, the reason is, etc. The Rosh’s words show that Borei Peri HaGafen suffices even after mayim acharonim and Bircas HaMazon need not be recited first. First, he mentions only Borei Peri HaGafen, not Bircas HaMazon. Second, if Bircas HaMazon had to be recited first and Borei Peri HaGafen afterward, what was the Rosh’s question that Borei Peri HaGafen should have been enacted before Bircas HaMazon?
That passage necessarily discusses one who already washed mayim acharonim, for the Rosh says, “all the more so once he took the cup in hand to recite Bircas HaMazon,” and at that point he had certainly already washed.
The same is implied by Beit Yosef, siman 477, who cites a responsum of the Rosh: If one forgot to eat the afikoman until after washing his hands for Bircas HaMazon or after saying, “Bring us the cup and let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” he should wash again, recite HaMotzi, and eat the afikoman. But if he remembered only after Bircas HaMazon, he should not eat it, since one should not add to the four cups. Thus the Rosh clearly holds that even after mayim acharonim, HaMotzi suffices and Bircas HaMazon need not first be recited. He does not mention reciting Bircas HaMazon, and if he did recite it, he could no longer eat, as the conclusion states.
“He should wash his hands again” means mayim acharonim.
And what he wrote in the chapter Kisui HaDam, etc. This refers to Beit Yosef’s proof cited above, from the Rosh’s statement that according to Rashi and Rashbam, saying “Bring us the cup and let us recite Bircas HaMazon” is equivalent to mayim acharonim. The meaning is not, as Beit Yosef understood, that in both cases Bircas HaMazon must first be recited. Rather, the Rosh is discussing the requirement that Bircas HaMazon immediately follow mayim acharonim. The issue there is not eating between them, but interruption by any other activity, comparable to the other cases of immediate succession listed there, such as adjoining redemption to prayer. The Rosh means that saying “Bring us the cup and let us recite Bircas HaMazon” is likewise equivalent to mayim acharonim in that one may not interrupt with another activity. This is specifically according to Rashi and Rashbam, who hold that if one wishes to eat, he must first recite Bircas HaMazon. According to them, the declaration is more than mere diversion of attention; it is a complete conclusion of the meal and is as though he has already begun Bircas HaMazon. Therefore he may not interrupt with any other activity. According to the Rosh, however, since the declaration does not require Bircas HaMazon first and is only diversion of attention, he may interrupt with another activity. Although according to the Rosh, after mayim acharonim one likewise need not first recite Bircas HaMazon, nevertheless mayim acharonim may still constitute a greater conclusion than the declaration.
And some distinguish, etc. One might ask: according to Magen Avraham’s explanation that the Rosh discusses only interruption by another activity, how does the Rosh’s concluding distinction between eating and drinking relate? Magen Avraham means that this follows the Rosh’s statement that, according to Rashi and Rashbam, the declaration is equivalent to mayim acharonim regarding interruption by another activity. From this we infer that according to the Rosh there is a distinction between the declaration and mayim acharonim regarding such an interruption. But regarding eating and drinking, the Rosh makes no distinction: in both cases one need not first recite Bircas HaMazon, and a new blessing before eating or drinking suffices. The Rosh then adds that some distinguish further: after the declaration, drinking requires a new blessing, but eating does not; after mayim acharonim, even eating requires a new blessing.
And Beit Yosef abbreviated his citation. Meaning, he did not cite the beginning of the Rosh’s statement, “Bircas HaMazon must immediately follow mayim acharonim,” but cited only: “The halachah is not so; rather, as long as he has not washed he may eat, and if he said, ‘Bring us the cup and let us recite Bircas HaMazon,’ it is equivalent to mayim acharonim according to Rashi and Rashbam,” etc. Therefore Beit Yosef did not consider that the Rosh might be discussing interruption by another activity, and instead understood him to be discussing one who wishes to eat.
Nevertheless, he holds, etc. Meaning, although Bach explains the Rosh differently from Magen Avraham, he agrees with Magen Avraham’s practical ruling.
This is implied by Tosafos in Berachos 42, etc. Tosafos there, Tosafos in Pesachim 101, and the Rosh in chapter 6 of Chullin ask concerning the account in Berachos 42. Rava and Rabbi Zeira visited the house of the Exilarch. After the table had been cleared, food was sent to them from the Exilarch’s house. Rava ate, but Rabbi Zeira did not, because clearing the table ends the meal. They ask: even after saying “Bring us the cup and let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” one need not first recite Bircas HaMazon; a new blessing before eating suffices. Rabbi Zeira should therefore have recited a blessing and eaten. They answer that perhaps the dish contained bread and required HaMotzi and washing, and it was burdensome for him to do so.
The Tur, siman 477, writes in the name of Avi HaEzri that if one forgot to eat the afikoman until after mayim acharonim or after saying, “Bring us the cup and let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” he should wash, recite HaMotzi, and eat the afikoman.
