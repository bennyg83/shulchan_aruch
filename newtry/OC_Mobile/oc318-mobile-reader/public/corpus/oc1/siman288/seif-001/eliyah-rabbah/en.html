[1] Until six hours, etc. This includes remaining without food for the sake of a fast, for even one hour is prohibited (Maharil). Accordingly, some communities act improperly when they fast on Shabbos until midday because of decrees against the community (Magen Avraham).
[2] [Levush] Or he prays, etc. Therefore, those prayer leaders who prolong melodies and do not leave the synagogue until after six hours do not act properly, especially in winter, and all the more so on Yom Tov. Rosh Hashanah is an exception, when this should not be prevented, as Bach and Yam Shel Shlomo, Beitzah 2:4, write, although the latter concludes that when Rosh Hashanah falls on Shabbos it is prohibited. Magen Avraham in siman 157 justifies the practice of sometimes prolonging prayer on Shabbos and Yom Tov by counting the six hours from the time people arise from bed. I say that we rely on Raavyah, whom Beit Yosef cites from Mordechai at first, that it is permitted when one is studying or praying. Similarly, Agur siman 401 cites as practical law only the beginning of Mordechai's words. I likewise found in the manuscript Mordechai Katan, and further in Gedulat Mordechai, that even Mordechai's final ruling like Rabbi Yehoshua applies to Yom Tov, while on Shabbos one may extend beyond midday. This also resolves the apparent contradiction in Raavyah. This seems to accord with the view of Mordechai Katan and Agur mentioned above. It is therefore surprising that the Shulchan Aruch ruled otherwise. I later saw that Bach in siman 242 also rules this way, contradicting what he wrote here, which is even more surprising. See siman 242, seif katan 6.
[3] [Levush] And whenever, etc. In Shibbolei HaLeket and Tanya, the source of this rule concerns the third meal, so it must be understood as not necessarily limited to that case, in order to permit, etc. Thus Rama explains the account that Rabbi Akiva would weep and say, "This is a delight for me." Taz objects and wonders that according to this, anyone who is distressed should weep on Shabbos, God forbid. He therefore explains that Rabbi Akiva's tears came from the intensity of his attachment to the Holy One, blessed is He. Thus we find in Zohar Chadash that Rabbi Akiva wept greatly when reciting Shir HaShirim because he understood the lofty matters to which the words reached. This is likewise common among those who pray with concentration. I found, however, in Responsa Binyamin Ze'ev, siman 210, that the incident cited by the Agudah concerned Rabbi Akiva weeping over the death of Rabbi Eliezer. Rama's explanation is therefore correct. Moreover, Rashi on Ta'anit 12a explains that a dream fast is permitted so that the person's bodily distress will be relieved, as stated in seif 3. The objection that everyone who is distressed should therefore weep is not compelling, because it is not the nature of every person for distress to leave his heart through crying, and it also depends on the nature of the distress. The heart knows its own bitterness, and if one seeks to act deceptively, etc.
