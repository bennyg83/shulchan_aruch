1) [Seif 1] It is forbidden to fast on Shabbos, etc. Even fasting for one hour with the intention of a fast is forbidden. Maharil, Hilchos Taanis. Rashi in Eruvin 41 writes the same. Therefore, some communities act improperly when they fast until midday on Shabbos because of decrees. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 1. Even a fast from meat and wine is forbidden, although there is no obligation to eat them. Beis Menuchah, paragraph 2.
2) There: Until six hours. This means including the sixth hour. Olas Tamid, paragraph 1; Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 1. We wrote the same above in siman 157, paragraph 3; see there. If he tasted something before Mussaf, as in siman 286, seif 3, he may delay eating beyond six hours. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1.
3) There: Until six hours. Although even on a weekday one should hasten to eat before six hours, because eating afterward is like throwing a stone into a wineskin, as stated above in siman 151, no prohibition applies on a weekday. On Shabbos, however, it is forbidden to fast beyond six hours. Therefore, chazzanim who prolong their melodies on Shabbos and do not leave the synagogue until after six hours act improperly, especially during winter, and all the more so on Yom Tov. On Rosh Hashanah, however, they should not be prevented, because some say one should fast on Rosh Hashanah. Although this is not our custom, it is proper to prolong the prayer even beyond six hours and not prevent them. Bach. Yam Shel Shlomo, Beitzah, chapter 2, siman 4, writes the same, but concludes that this is forbidden when Rosh Hashanah occurs on Shabbos. Magen Avraham in siman 157 explains the practice of sometimes prolonging the Shabbos and Yom Tov prayer by counting the six hours from the time people arise from bed. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2, writes: I say that we rely upon Raavyah, whom Beis Yosef cites initially in the name of Mordechai, who writes that one may delay while studying or praying. See there.
4) There: It is forbidden to fast on Shabbos, etc. See Responsa Rashba, volume 4, siman 262, who writes in the name of Rabbi Yitzchak ibn Gias that the prohibition against fasting on Shabbos and Yom Tov is from the Torah. This is also stated in Tanchuma, Bereishis. See there, where it states that honoring Shabbos is superior to one thousand fasts. For this reason, it overrides the rabbinic fast of Esther. Orach Chaim, paragraph 1.
5) Even one who fasts throughout the year for his sins may not fast on Shabbos. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur. This discusses a case in which eating does not harm him because of a change from his routine. Olas Tamid, paragraph 1.
6) The Yerushalmi teaches: Rabbi Chaggai said, 'Shabbosos and Yamim Tovim were given only for eating and drinking.' Rabbi Berechyah said, 'They were given only for engagement in Torah.' Tanchuma explains that they do not disagree. Rabbi Berechyah's statement concerning Torah study refers to laborers who engage in their work throughout the week and come on Shabbos to engage in Torah. Rabbi Chaggai's statement concerning delight refers to Torah scholars who labor in Torah throughout the week and delight themselves on Shabbos. Regarding Rava's statement that all agree that on Shabbos we require a portion 'for you,' as stated in siman 242, one can answer that there he speaks according to Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua. Alternatively, there he requires some delight, while here the discussion is whether to spend the entire day in delight. Beis Yosef. Rema cites this below in siman 290, seif 2 in the gloss. See Bach, siman 242, who discusses this at length and concludes that on Yom Tov, half the day is for Hashem and half for oneself, while on Shabbos most of the day is for Hashem and a minority for oneself; see there. He similarly writes in this siman: Above in siman 242, I concluded that even a Torah scholar needs only a minority of the day for himself, with most devoted to Torah. These are his words. See further our words above in siman 242, paragraphs 5 and 6; see there.
