7) [Seif 2] Some say that a person who is harmed by eating, etc. This applies if even an egg-sized or olive-sized amount harms him. If he can eat an egg-sized or olive-sized amount, he must fulfill the Shabbos meal, as stated below at the beginning of siman 291; see there. Regarding a delicate person who must fast on the day preceding Pesach so that he can eat matzah at night with an appetite: if the day preceding Pesach occurs on Shabbos, he may not fast. Rather, he should guard himself from eating his usual quantity so that he can eat the matzah at night with an appetite. Responsa Sedeh HaAretz, volume 3, siman 24; Beis Menuchah, paragraph 5.
8) There: For then it is a delight for him, etc. It is nearly forbidden for him to eat, lest he suffer on Shabbos. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 2; Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 2. One who is harmed by eating need not fast to atone for his Shabbos fast. The same applies to one whose ordinary practice is to fast every day: he need not fast to atone for his Shabbos fast. Mishbetzos Zahav there.
9) There, gloss: Likewise, one who finds delight in weeping, etc. An aggadic teaching relates that Rabbi Akiva's students found him weeping on Shabbos, and he told them, 'This is a delight for me.' It appears to me that his eyes flowed with tears because of his intense attachment to the Holy One, blessed is He. We similarly find in Zohar Chadash that Rabbi Akiva wept greatly when reciting Shir HaShirim because he knew the heights reached by its words. This is also common among those who pray with concentration. Rema's explanation that he weeps to release his sorrow is difficult, because according to it anyone who suffers, G-d forbid, could weep on Shabbos. Taz, paragraph 2. Rabbi Akiva's answer, 'This is a delight for me,' however, implies that whenever a person derives delight through his weeping, it is permitted. We also find that Rabbi Akiva later walked and wept over the death of his great teacher Rabbi Eliezer, as stated in Midrash HaNe'elam, parashas Vayeira. It also states there that he wept because his teacher cursed him to die an unusual death for failing to arrive earlier to serve him. I also found in the name of the Yerushalmi that Rabbi Akiva wept over the death of his eldest son and said that this was a delight for him. This accords with Rema. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 3. Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 3, similarly writes that regarding permission to weep, even if he does so to remove sorrow from his heart, it is permitted in accordance with the gloss. Since refraining from weeping would leave him distressed throughout the day, he may weep for one hour rather than suffer all day, contrary to Taz. Taz's question that this would allow every distressed person to weep on Shabbos is unfounded. The permission was not granted to everyone, but only to a person who knows about himself that weeping gives him delight. Note this well. These are his words. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3, writes the same.
10) There, gloss: To weep on Shabbos. See Sedei Chemed, Asifas Dinim, system of aninus, paragraph 24, regarding this matter. See further there in the name of Responsa Amudei Or, siman 76, concerning an onen on Yom Tov: may he weep over his deceased relative between death and burial? Since he is exempt from all mitzvos, he is presumably also exempt from the mitzvah of rejoicing on Yom Tov, for why should it differ from other positive mitzvos? The prohibition against weeping exists so that one not prevent the joy of Yom Tov, and our Sages have authority to exempt an onen from rejoicing on Yom Tov through passivity. It does not appear correct to classify weeping as an active deed. I relied upon the opinion of the halachic authorities that one who derives delight from weeping may weep, because I saw that without it I would have been even more desolate. Nevertheless, after the burial I directed my mind to fulfill the joy of Yom Tov during part of the day through joy in the heart. These are his words; see there. Orach Chaim, paragraph 1.
