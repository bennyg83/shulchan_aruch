9) There, gloss: Likewise, one who finds delight in weeping, etc. An aggadic teaching relates that Rabbi Akiva's students found him weeping on Shabbos, and he told them, 'This is a delight for me.' It appears to me that his eyes flowed with tears because of his intense attachment to the Holy One, blessed is He. We similarly find in Zohar Chadash that Rabbi Akiva wept greatly when reciting Shir HaShirim because he knew the heights reached by its words. This is also common among those who pray with concentration. Rema's explanation that he weeps to release his sorrow is difficult, because according to it anyone who suffers, God forbid, could weep on Shabbos. Taz, paragraph 2. Rabbi Akiva's answer, 'This is a delight for me,' however, implies that whenever a person derives delight through his weeping, it is permitted. We also find that Rabbi Akiva later walked and wept over the death of his great teacher Rabbi Eliezer, as stated in Midrash HaNe'elam, parashas Vayeira. It also states there that he wept because his teacher cursed him to die an unusual death for failing to arrive earlier to serve him. I also found in the name of the Yerushalmi that Rabbi Akiva wept over the death of his eldest son and said that this was a delight for him. This accords with Rema. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 3. Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 3, similarly writes that regarding permission to weep, even if he does so to remove sorrow from his heart, it is permitted in accordance with the gloss. Since refraining from weeping would leave him distressed throughout the day, he may weep for one hour rather than suffer all day, contrary to Taz. Taz's question that this would allow every distressed person to weep on Shabbos is unfounded. The permission was not granted to everyone, but only to a person who knows about himself that weeping gives him delight. Note this well. These are his words. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3, writes the same.
