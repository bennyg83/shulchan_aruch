And if. See Taz. Bach's view is that for medicinal use, even a small amount of oil in anigron is considered primary according to all opinions, and one recites only Borei Peri HaEitz. If one's intent is eating, and only a small amount of oil is added, Rabbeinu Yonah agrees that the anigron is primary. If one adds more oil than usual, although the anigron remains the majority, Rabbeinu Yonah requires Borei Peri HaEitz, whereas Bahag requires Shehakol. Bach deletes the word “much” from the Tur's statement that this is its medicinal manner, because one adds much oil. This also appears from the Mechaber's unqualified wording here. Rashi's statement in Berachos 36a that one with a sore throat requires much oil and therefore the oil is primary must be pressed to mean that he was addressing the permissibility on Shabbos. Taz, however, holds that even for medicinal use Borei Peri HaEitz is recited only when one adds more oil than usual. Otherwise not. There is proof for this, for if not, why did Rabbeinu Yonah not say that the Gemara mentioned a sore throat to teach that even a small amount of oil is primary? Know that this precision is substantial, for otherwise how did the Tur derive a dispute between Rabbeinu Yonah and Bahag? It is explicit in seif 1 that one follows the majority where two blessings are involved. Rabbeinu Yonah could be speaking where one already blessed Shehakol on the anigron or on another item and then mixed in oil. In that case his reasoning is clear: so long as the oil is not harmful and its taste is perceptible, why should he not recite Borei Peri HaEitz? The exempt item should not exempt the obligated item. Bahag, by contrast, could be discussing a case where both blessings are before him. Then specifically for medicine the oil is primary and he recites only Borei Peri HaEitz, but if it is sent for food one follows the majority and recites Shehakol rather than Borei Peri HaEitz. Yet the Tur found it difficult to say that Rabbeinu Yonah used the Gemara only by way of example. This indicates that Rabbeinu Yonah holds that even where both blessings are before him, the oil is primary, and the Tur agrees. According to Rashba's rule that one follows intent, the matter can be clarified further, and this may answer the question of Darchei Moshe and Perishah why Beit Yosef associated this with Rashba, since Bahag said the same. Rashba, as cited by Beit Yosef at the beginning of the siman, holds that one follows the majority. If so, the Tur's proof falls away. Several new rulings emerge from Taz's words: first, medicinal intent requires more oil than usual. Second, where a small amount is mixed into a majority, following the majority applies only when both blessings are before him; but if he already blessed Shehakol on the beer and wine is then mixed into it and its taste is perceptible, he recites Borei Peri HaGefen, for the exempt item does not exempt the obligated item. Third, even when the mixture was intentional, one follows the majority. This last point is difficult from siman 204 and Magen Avraham and Taz there, as I wrote in letter 1. All this requires study.
I will add. It appears that if oil is the majority and anigron the minority, all agree that one recites Borei Peri HaEitz, even Bahag, because in such a case it may be harmful and therefore treated as though eaten alone. See Chiddushei HaRashba in the name of Rav Hai, who distinguishes between weekdays, when medicinal intent must be explicit, since if left unstated it is not evident that he drinks it for medicine and indeed oil alone would be better, and Shabbos, when oil alone is impossible, so even without explicit intent one recites Borei Peri HaEitz. The wording of the Mechaber requires careful examination. Bahag wrote, “if his gums hurt,” implying that medicinal intent must be explicit, and Levush likewise says that he intends it for medicine. The Mechaber, however, writes without qualification about one whose throat hurts, perhaps following Rambam's wording. Yet Rambam concludes, “but if his throat does not hurt,” while the Mechaber concludes, “but if he does not intend it for medicine,” which teaches that even if his throat hurts, if he does not intend medicine he recites Shehakol. The implications conflict somewhat. This requires study.
