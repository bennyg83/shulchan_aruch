This is indeed the clearly correct ruling, and I see no basis for ruling that one should not recite Borei Peri HaEtz. Mahari wrote in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 29, that since Rashi wrote that one recites Borei Peri HaEtz on terimah, meaning fruit that was pounded somewhat but not completely crushed, this implies that on fruit completely crushed one recites Shehakol. Beit Yosef already challenged this at the end of siman 204 from Rambam, who rules that even if one mashed the fruit and made it into a paste, one recites Borei Peri HaEtz. He also brought proof from the Tur in siman 204, in his dispute with his colleagues, that everyone agrees that where the substance of the fruit remains, the fruit itself is primary. A further proof is the Tur in siman 205, who rules that even when fruit is cut into very small pieces, it does not lose its blessing. Here too, the Tur explicitly writes that Rabbeinu Yonah agrees with Rambam on this point. The inference of Terumas HaDeshen from Rashi is therefore astonishing. On the contrary, the Gemara proves the reverse. Rav Asi said that dates of terumah may be made into terimah but may not be made into beer. If heavily crushed fruit did not retain the law of terimah, Rav Asi should have stated the greater novelty, that it is forbidden even while the substance of the fruit still remains, though it has been heavily crushed. Instead, he prohibited making beer, where the substance of the dates is entirely gone and only their taste remains, as Tosafos writes in Berachos 38a, s.v. hai devasha. This is a strong proof that whenever the substance of the fruit remains, it still retains the status of the fruit itself. Nor can one infer otherwise from Rashi. The Tur likewise writes that the dates were pounded somewhat, yet then rules in accordance with Rambam that if they were made into a paste one still recites Borei Peri HaEtz. Rashi's phrase, “pounded somewhat,” appears merely to explain the word terimah and why it is called by that name. He explains that it is pounded somewhat, but not entirely crushed. This resembles targis in Berachos 37a, which Rashi explains as grain cut into thirds; in Greek, three is called tri. Rashi therefore writes that the term terimah denotes anything pounded somewhat but not completely crushed. This is a general lexical explanation. The Gemara mentions terimah here because dates were commonly prepared in this manner, but the same law applies when they are crushed thoroughly. Furthermore, in Nedarim, siman 217, it is stated that one who vows not to eat dates may nevertheless eat date honey. This implies that pounded dates remain forbidden so long as their substance remains. The same applies here. Moreover, the discussion below states that according to the Rosh, even the water in which fruit was cooked requires Borei Peri HaEtz if it contains the fruit's taste. Although the Rosh writes “it is possible,” here, where the substance of the fruit remains, the ruling is unquestionably so. It therefore appears to me that one who initially recites Borei Peri HaEtz on plum or cherry preserves has no concern at all and has acted correctly, since Borei Peri HaEtz is a more specific blessing than Shehakol, as explained below. A further proof is seif 13, which states that spices ground and mixed with sugar retain the blessing appropriate to those spices. Thus, even when completely ground, they do not lose their blessing. We likewise practice every day that when apples are cooked until completely crushed, one recites Borei Peri HaEtz, as stated in seif 12 regarding cooked fruit. They are certainly crushed through cooking, and there is no concern of a blessing in vain. So it appears to me.
