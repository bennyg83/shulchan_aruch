And if. See Taz. His view is that the ruling is not limited to fig beer. This also appears from Beit Yosef in the name of Rashba, that whenever there is a primary item together with a secondary one, the blessing follows the primary item. Magen Avraham, however, did not explain it this way. See also Levush and Eliyah Rabbah, who challenge this from Yoreh Deah 324:9. There it may be answered that rice is drawn after the water, whereas the Rosh implies that the reason is that taste is equivalent to substance by Torah law. One might argue that the five species are especially significant. Yet if so, even where they impart no taste they should retain their blessing, as in siman 208 according to Taz, seif katan 3. It must therefore be that for challah their importance helps only where taste is present. All the more so, for blessings, taste should be effective. Eliyah Rabbah answers that beer has its own blessing of Shehakol and wine has Borei Peri HaGefen, so one follows the majority, unlike there. Apparently the reason is that majority is stronger than taste, since capital punishments and similar laws follow majority, whereas taste as substance may be comparable only to half a forbidden measure according to some opinions. Nevertheless, the after-blessing remains difficult according to the view that Me'ein Shalosh is D'Oraisa. How can the Rabbinic Borei Nefashos of beer exempt the Torah-level Me'ein Shalosh of wine when taste as substance is D'Oraisa? I later saw that in siman 208, seif 9, regarding one of the five species mixed with other flour, if there is not an olive-volume within the time of eating a loaf, one does not recite Bircas HaMazon, even though the grain taste is perceptible. If so, all the more so here. Regarding a small quantity of wine mixed into other beverages, even if there is an olive-volume within that time, drinking requires a revi'is from beginning to end, as explained in siman 612:10. The simple resolution is that for blessings one follows the majority, and where there is a primary item with a secondary item, one blesses on the primary and thereby exempts the secondary. See siman 204:12. This will be explained further, with Hashem's help.
Still. This requires study from siman 204:6 and Magen Avraham and Taz there, who rule that wine diluted with water retains Borei Peri HaGefen so long as there are not six parts water. All the more so, when mixed with other beverages, if the taste of wine remains, why does the ruling here say to follow the majority? Perhaps one must distinguish between a mixture that occurred on its own and one in which wine was intentionally added to impart taste. Where wine was intentionally added to water for its taste, and it constitutes one-fifth of the water, one recites Borei Peri HaGefen. See siman 442 in Tur, Beit Yosef, Shulchan Aruch, and the later authorities, where there may be a distinction because those products are ordinarily made in that manner. Since the matter is lengthy and the paths of wisdom are hidden from me, I have abbreviated here. With Hashem's help it will be explained further in the introduction, in siman 204:6 and 12, and in siman 208:2 and 9.
What he wrote regarding fat, that even though it gives much flavor, follows his position in Yoreh Deah 98, seif katan 11, that fat is not considered an ingredient added for taste. When there is a large quantity of fat, however, one nevertheless recites the blessing appropriate to the primary food. Regarding blessings, one follows the principal item. Thus, one who eats whole wheat kernels with fat recites Borei Peri HaAdamah and not Shehakol.
