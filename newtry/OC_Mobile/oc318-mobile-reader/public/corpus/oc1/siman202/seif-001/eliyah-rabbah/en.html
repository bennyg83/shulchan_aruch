We learned: One who wishes to become pious should fulfill the laws of blessings. At first glance this is difficult, for it implies that one who does not fulfill them is merely not called pious, but is still not called wicked, although Scripture calls him a companion of a destroyer. Rather, the intent is that he should not rely on the general rule that one who recited Shehakol has discharged his obligation, but should study the laws of blessings and recite over each species the blessing appropriate to it. The writings of the Arizal state that one who routinely exempts himself with Shehakol is included among the boors cursed by Chazal. Sefer Chasidim, siman 852, writes that one who has a single nut should not bless and then crack it, lest the kernel be wormy or rotten and his blessing prove to have been in vain. Shelah writes that this applies specifically to a nut, for if it contains a worm it is wholly unfit. With other fruits, even if the inside has spoiled or become wormy, the blessing was not in vain because the outer flesh is also part of the fruit and the blessing takes effect upon it. Therefore it is preferable to bless over the fruit while whole, since regarding blessings a whole item is superior. Yaakov Levi adds that cracking the nut constitutes an interruption between the blessing and eating, like cutting bread after Hamotzi. According to this, even if many nuts are before him, he should crack them before the blessing. According to Sefer Chasidim's concern that it may be wormy, however, with many nuts there is no need to crack them, since we do not suspect that all are wormy; this is why Sefer Chasidim specified one nut. One may distinguish from Hamotzi, where in truth he does not fully cut the loaf. The reason there is that a slight cut in bread is not recognizable, but if the cut were recognizable he would not be permitted to do it; on Shabbos he does not cut at all, because this is not a complete interruption, as explained in siman 167, and it is preferable to tolerate this slight interruption in order to bless over a whole item. Nevertheless, Solet Belulah writes that one who wishes to peel a fruit should peel it and then bless, so as not to interrupt between the blessing and eating; perhaps the cases differ. He further writes that one should not bless over food or drink that is excessively hot or cold, because of concern for an interruption. For this reason, pious and meticulous people fill the kiddush cup before washing their hands. He further writes that one should select a clean, fine fruit free of worms for the blessing, because otherwise he may need to remove the pit and thereby enter a doubt regarding the after-blessing on account of the law of a complete creature. This does not appear correct, for why should he not bless while it is whole and then remove the pit without eating it? If he wishes to eat it with the pit, however, he should break it first, as will be explained in siman 210. Bach at the end of siman 206 writes that if one blessed over food and afterward the food became repulsive to him, he should eat a small portion even if it has spoiled, so that his blessing not be in vain; likewise, one should not bless over a nut until he sees its kernel. Mateh Moshe writes that one who is thirsty for water should not bless and afterward pour off some, but should first pour and then bless. Knesset HaGedolah explains that this is because of interruption. It appears more likely to me that the reason is degradation of the blessing; see below regarding pouring off havdalah wine. Mateh Moshe further cites Sefer Chasidim that if one blessed over water and then heard that someone died in the city, he should pour off a little and drink the rest. Knesset HaGedolah, however, quotes Sefer Chasidim as saying that he should drink a little and pour away the rest. I saw the text of Sefer Chasidim itself, siman 854, and it says that he should first drink and only afterward pour it away. Knesset HaGedolah writes that even according to those who explain the pouring of water after a death as a concern that the Angel of Death placed a poisonous drop into the water, he should nevertheless drink a little, for "one who guards a mitzvah will know no evil." Once he has drunk a little and the blessing is no longer in vain, he should pour away the remainder.

As to why the formula was not enacted as "by His utterance," corresponding to the Mishnah's statement that the world was created with ten utterances, and to the repeated scriptural "and God said," the reason is that "Bereishit" is itself an utterance, as it says, "By the word of Hashem the heavens were made." This teaches creation from absolute nothingness, which occurred on the first day, as Rashi explains that the word "et" includes all things, for all were created on the first day. Therefore the formula was enacted as "by His word," as Malbushei Yom Tov explains. Rabbi David Abudarham cites Rabbi Asher's question: why do all blessings say "Who creates," whereas here the formula is "through Whose word everything came to be"? Why not say "Who created everything"? He answers that since the blessing was enacted also over animals and the like, one cannot say that their primary creation was for this purpose, since Adam was originally not permitted meat. Further, it was enacted over wine that soured, bread that became moldy, and the like, all of which are not a perfection of creation but its deterioration. Therefore the Sages said "came to be" rather than "was created." The discerning will understand. Chochmat Manoach writes that the word nihyah should be pronounced with a segol under the yod, not a kamatz, because the latter is past tense, whereas most blessings were formulated in the present tense.
