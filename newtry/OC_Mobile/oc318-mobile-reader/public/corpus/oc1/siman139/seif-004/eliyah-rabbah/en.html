[6] All who read, etc. Although by strict law it would suffice for the first reader to recite the opening blessing and the last reader the concluding blessing, Chazal instituted a blessing for each reader because of those entering and leaving, lest they say that one reads without a blessing, etc. Taz challenges from this the Midrash Rabbah in Parashas Reeh, which states that one does not interrupt during the curses because of the blessing. His answer is strained. It appears preferable to explain as Tosafos Yom Tov writes at the end of the third chapter of Megillah. See there, and see Lechem Mishneh there.
[7] He should turn his face, etc. Bach concludes that it is proper and fitting not to turn his face at all, and that the Sefer Torah should remain fully open, because we are not concerned that people will say the blessings are written in the Torah. This was his practice, and the later authorities cite him. I found the same in Mateh Moshe, siman 227. Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes: “My practice is not to look at the written text, but to look elsewhere.” Paneach Raza writes on the verse, “He placed a veil upon his face,” that this teaches that when one comes from the bimah and from Torah reading, one covers his face. Shelah, page 254, writes: “I heard from Maharshal that those who are called to the Torah and bow during Bircas HaTorah should be instructed not to bow. Yet there was a custom that when one ascended to the Sefer Torah and saw the passage he would read, he bowed, not because he bowed during the blessing, but in honor of the Sefer Torah, and afterward recited the blessings.” In Lechem Chamudos, page 43, I found: “I say that all who bow at the beginning and end of Bircas HaTorah are mistaken.” See above, siman 51. Yet below, at the end of siman 150, the Levush rules that one bows. I likewise saw in Rokeach, end of siman 319, that Yehallu is recited when returning the Sefer Torah to its place because during Bircas HaTorah one bows in honor of the Torah. Yehallu is then recited to inform you that he did not bow because of divinity in the Torah, but because the Shechinah rests upon it, not because the Torah itself is a deity, for “His name alone is exalted.” Mateh Moshe, siman 256, cites this at length and concludes that from his words it is evident that the ancient custom was to bow during Bircas HaTorah, unlike those who say it is not a custom. This implies that the bow occurred during the blessing itself, for it is difficult to explain that he refers to before beginning the blessing. Nevertheless, it appears that he should not bow at the end of the blessing, because then it appears that he bows on account of the blessing, as explained above in siman 113. Responsa Penei Moshe writes that according to all opinions the concluding blessing is D’Rabbanan. The opening blessing is D’Oraisa according to Ramban, Rashba, and Sefer HaChinuch, while according to Rif, Rambam, and Rosh it is D’Rabbanan.
