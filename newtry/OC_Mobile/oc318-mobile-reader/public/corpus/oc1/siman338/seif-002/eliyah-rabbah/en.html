[3] To repair, etc. This requires study, because the Mordechai permitted only a rabbinic melachah, as stated by the Magen Avraham. There is no difficulty, because he compares it to heating water for an infant in Perek Hadar, where he writes that even a Torah melachah is permitted. It is no worse than lighting a lamp for a Shabbos meal in siman 276, seif 2. Musical instruments for the rejoicing of a bridegroom are even more significant, as the Bach writes. Moreover, this rabbinic restriction is not severe because one cannot generally repair a musical instrument himself, as stated in siman 339, seif 3, where the main prohibition of instructing a non-Jew is lest one do it oneself.
[4] Because of honor, etc. Otherwise it is prohibited. Nevertheless, regarding this law the practice has become lenient, as stated by the Rama. This implies that people are lenient even when it is not for the rejoicing of a bride and groom, whereas the Levush implies that the custom is limited to wedding rejoicing. So too it appears from seif 8. Shiyarei Knesset HaGedolah likewise writes that music is prohibited at a brit milah unless the musicians come on their own or were instructed before Shabbos. Yam Shel Shlomo in chapter 5 of Beitzah writes that outside a mitzvah context, we merely say to leave those who are lenient. The Maharil writes in Hilchot Eruvin that if there is an official order prohibiting instrumental music, the wedding should not be held without musicians, because instrumental music is the principal rejoicing of bride and groom; if it cannot be done in that place, they should go elsewhere. Responsum 132 of the Radbaz writes that perhaps the statement that there is no rejoicing of bride and groom without instruments applied in their lands, but in our lands there is rejoicing through meat, wine, and vocal song praising Hashem with songs and liturgical compositions established by the early authorities.
