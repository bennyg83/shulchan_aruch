“So that he should awaken the dawn.” It is very proper to be careful to say before each of the three prayers the request for mercy that is brought in the Yerushalmi (chapter “Tefillat HaShachar”): in the morning, “I give thanks before You… that You brought me out from darkness to light”; in Minchah, “I give thanks… that just as You merited me to see the sun in the east, so You merited me to see it in the west”; and at night, a “May it be Your will…”—just as I was in darkness and You brought me out to light, so too bring me out from darkness to light.
“It is a great principle in the Torah…” One who wishes to fulfill “I have set Hashem before me always” properly should hurry to fulfill what we will cite in the name of Sefer HaChinuch; because of their great value, many great authorities quoted them in their works. The Chinuch writes (in his introduction, and in simanim 25, 26, 417, 418, 432, 387) that there are six mitzvot whose obligation is constant, which never cease from a person even for a moment all his days; and every moment that he thinks about them he fulfills a positive commandment, and there is no limit to the reward. These are: (1) to believe that there is one God in the world… (2) not to believe in any god besides Him… (3) to unify Him (“Hashem is One”)… (4) to love Hashem—achieved through Torah and contemplation… (5) to have fear of Heaven upon one’s face so as not to sin, knowing that Hashem supervises all deeds even in darkness… (6) not to stray after the thoughts of the heart and the sight of the eyes (“and you shall not wander…”)—including heresy in thought and pursuit of physical desires without a proper purpose. I gathered the above from his pleasant words in brief; they are like eye-salve, and fortunate is one who fulfills them properly; see Chayei Adam, klal 1, who also elaborates on them.
“And he should not be ashamed…” See the Mishnah Berurah in the name of the Beit Yosef: the Beit Yosef was speaking only about a mitzvah a person does for himself when people mock him—then he certainly should not worry about their mockery and should not quarrel with them. But if he stands in a place where heretics rise up against the Torah and want to enact regulations in city matters that would remove the people from the will of Hashem, and he began with peace and his words were not accepted—in such a case the Beit Yosef was not speaking at all; rather, it is a mitzvah to hate them, to contend with them, and to thwart their plan as much as one can. David said: “Do I not hate those who hate You, Hashem? … With complete hatred I hate them…” etc.
