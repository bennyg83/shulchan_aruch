To take a haircut. This is prohibited lest the scissors break, requiring him to seek another pair, and the time for Mincha pass. It is possible that if he has two or three pairs, it is permitted. One should not take a child onto his lap close to evening, lest the child soil his clothing, and while he seeks water the time for Mincha may pass, or he may be delayed from reaching the synagogue. Sefer Chassidim, siman 18.
Close to Mincha. Meaning, half an hour beforehand.
To the bathhouse. Even merely to perspire is prohibited by decree, lest he faint.
To the tannery. Even merely to inspect is prohibited, lest he see that the hides have been ruined, become distressed, remain preoccupied with his distress, and fail to pray. This implies that the same law applies to any work or calculation of a similar nature.
Nor to judgment. Even if he has already heard their claims, he may not complete the judgment, lest he discover a new argument and overturn the ruling.
A small meal. This is prohibited lest it continue for an extended time. Mahariv writes in the laws of Pesach that the same applies to one who did not pray Maariv with the congregation: he may not engage in work or study until he prays, as stated in the first chapter of Berachos, “He eats a small amount,” etc. Regarding Shacharis, however, since the congregation is praying, we are not concerned that he will be negligent. For this reason, people customarily eat after the time of Mincha Ketanah, and additionally the shamash reminds them and calls them to the synagogue. Where there is no minyan, however, one may not study in the morning before praying. This is also stated above in siman 89, seif 6, unlike Bach's explanation in siman 431 of Mahariv's words, that the prohibition applies only to one who ordinarily prays in the synagogue but once failed to pray Maariv there, while one who always prays at home may study. The text plainly disproves this. Bach did not copy the end of Mahariv's words and therefore explained them differently. See there. The statement in Berachos that a person should not say, “I will first eat and drink, and afterward recite Krias Shema,” but should enter the beis midrash and recite Krias Shema, refers to the period before its time, namely the half hour preceding the time. Eating is then prohibited, but study is permitted so that he not sit idle for half an hour. So write Rabbeinu Yonah and Shelah. See Beit Yosef, siman 235. Once the time arrives, however, study is prohibited, for we rule like Rashi, as stated in siman 89, seif 6, unless he is in a place where there is a congregation.
And if he began. Even if he began after Mincha Gedolah, he need not interrupt when the time of Mincha Ketanah arrives. If he began close to Mincha Ketanah, however, he must interrupt even if sufficient time remains. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 5. One who is exempt yet interrupts is called an ordinary person. Lechem Chamudos, citing the Yerushalmi. The Yerushalmi challenges this from the law that a groom is exempt from Krias Shema but may recite it if he wishes. It answers that this follows Rabban Gamliel, who said, “I will not listen to you to cast off the yoke of the kingdom of Heaven even for one hour.” See siman 70, seif 3.
If there is insufficient time. Even if he began permissibly, he must interrupt.
To interrupt immediately. Even if much of the day remains, if he knows that his meal will continue until nightfall, he must interrupt immediately. This also appears from Rashi, Sukkah 38. See there.
From when he washes his hands. For one who customarily loosens his belt when eating, the meal is considered begun from when he loosens his belt, even before washing.
That a small meal is permitted. According to this view, our ordinary haircut is also permitted. Regarding a bathhouse, only the entire bathing process is prohibited, but merely perspiring is permitted. Regarding a tannery, only a large tanning operation is prohibited. Regarding judgment, only beginning a case is prohibited. Nevertheless, they agree that if one began close to Mincha Ketanah, he must interrupt even a small meal.
And some say that even. According to this view, even a small meal is prohibited close to Mincha Ketanah. If one began, however, he need not interrupt even a large meal.
Close to Mincha Ketanah. Nevertheless, once the time of Mincha Ketanah arrives, this opinion agrees that beginning is prohibited. Mordechai, however, is lenient even then and writes that the prohibition applies only to a large meal. A large meal close to Mincha Ketanah, meaning in the tenth hour, is prohibited according to all, and one should not be lenient. So writes Bach. Rashba cited by Rabbeinu Yerucham also agrees, unlike Bach's presentation. Agudah likewise writes that one may not rely on the synagogue announcement when beginning a large meal. Regarding the beginning of a court case and the other activities, however, one may rely on the synagogue announcement, because they do not usually continue so long. Furthermore, the Gemara says that drunkenness is uncommon. It is therefore obvious that regarding a meal, where drunkenness is common, one may not begin, for the synagogue announcement is ineffective for a drunk person.
And the time will pass. See what I wrote above, that even if much of the day remains, one must interrupt if he knows that his meal will continue until nightfall. Be'er Sheva writes that when a wedding takes place on Shabbos and the congregation does not leave the synagogue until after midday, they should first pray Mincha with the congregation and then eat, because a wedding feast is considered a large meal. A Shabbos meal eaten at home, however, is not considered a large meal.
