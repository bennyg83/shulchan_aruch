And made. See Taz. The principle is that Shehecheyanu is recited in one of two situations. One recites it when he experiences joy, even if the matter does not come at fixed intervals, because of the joy of the heart that Hashem has kept him alive and enabled him to acquire the item. This is why one recites it over new utensils, as explained in siman 223. Alternatively, one recites it over mitzvos that come at fixed intervals, even though they are decrees of the King and do not inherently bring such joy, because Hashem has kept him alive to reach this time. Regarding redemption of the firstborn, Bach writes that one must wait until the child is thirty-one days old. Yet even without this consideration, one recites Shehecheyanu whenever he has joy, just as with new utensils that bring him joy. Levush explains the matter this way here. Concerning circumcision, Yoreh Deah, siman 265, seif 6, where Shehecheyanu is not recited according to one view, I initially thought the reason was the infant's pain. I then saw that Levush rejects this. If so, why does one recite Shehecheyanu upon the death of his father when he inherits from him, as explained in Orach Chayim, siman 223, seif 2? Levush concludes that circumcision is common, while redemption of the firstborn is uncommon because it applies only to a firstborn son; see there. Bach's explanation also does not resolve circumcision, for one must wait eight days. What difference is there between thirty-one days and eight days? See Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 3 of Hilchos Milah, letter 4, who writes the same explanation as Levush.
And what Taz writes. Taz maintains that when a person performs a mitzvah for the first time as part of his entry into its obligation, he recites Shehecheyanu, such as one who becomes bar mitzvah or a minor who reaches the age of education in tzitzis. See Yoreh Deah, siman 28, Shach, letter 5, who leaves this matter requiring study. If one purchases a garment made from fox skins, fur, or similar materials, he recites Shehecheyanu. This is not comparable to Yoreh Deah, siman 28, seif 2, where one does not recite Shehecheyanu over slaughtering because he directly harms a living creature. Here he does not do so. Nor is it comparable to siman 223, seif 6, in Rama's gloss, where one does not say, “May you wear it out and replace it,” because additional animals would have to be killed. See Magen Avraham there. Here the situation is different, as it appears to me.
