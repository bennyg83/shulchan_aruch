1) [Seif 1] A stove constructed like a pot, etc. The meaning of a stove, a kupach, and an oven in Talmudic times is as follows. A stove and a kupach were made of earthenware like an earthenware pot, with their openings on top. They were not attached to the ground but were movable. A stove, however, had a long opening that could accommodate two pots resting directly upon its mouth, upon a cover over its cavity, or upon its rim. A kupach had room for only one pot upon its opening, and therefore retained more heat than a stove but less than an oven. An oven was also made of earthenware, but it was attached to the ground and its opening was likewise on top. It was narrow above and broad below, causing its heat to be retained within it more than in a stove or kupach, which were not broader below than above. This oven is called a baker's oven, because they would attach the bread to the oven walls. Ran writes that Rabbeinu Chananel explains that our ovens, whose openings are on the side, have precisely the law of a stove. Bach. Beis Yosef writes the same; Taz, paragraph 16; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 7. See below, paragraph 18.
1) There: And two pots are placed upon its opening, etc. The same applies within it: it is permitted to leave food there. Shabbos 37a and Rashi's commentary there.
2) There: Which is olive refuse. This is also Tur's wording. This implies that if it was fueled with sesame refuse, one may place a dish upon it and leave it until night even if continued cooking improves it. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 1, writes the same and explains that the decree is lest one stoke the coals. This concern applies only to material that produces coals, whereas sesame refuse does not produce coals. He adds, however, that some dispute this and forbid sesame refuse as well; see there. See below, paragraph 13.
3) There: To leave it upon the stove. Tur writes, 'To leave it upon the stove for the evening.' Levush writes the same. This implies that if it is intended for the daytime, it is permitted in every case, because he has diverted his attention from it and we are not concerned that he might stoke the coals. Beis Yosef, however, is uncertain about this. Since the Shulchan Aruch does not include the words 'for the evening,' it appears that he rules there is no distinction between food intended for the evening and food intended for the daytime. Bach rules this way. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1, similarly writes that as a matter of practical halachah it is logical to forbid it even when left for the next day. Since he could complete its cooking at night by stoking the coals, we are concerned that he might change his mind and eat it at night, and it is therefore considered needed for the evening; see there.
4) There: And it continues to contract, etc. This means that its volume diminishes, and is related to the expression 'shriveled breasts.' Perishah, paragraph 1. See Responsa Ginas Veradim, Orach Chaim, klal 3, end of siman 4, who writes that a coffee dish is harmed by continued contraction; see there. It appears that this applies specifically after it has been fully cooked, when leaving it longer would cause it to contract and deteriorate. Regarding whether coffee may be returned to the fire on Shabbos, see below, paragraph 11.
5) There: And it continues to contract and deteriorate, etc. Know that anything one may leave upon a stove that was neither swept nor covered with ashes may likewise be left upon a kupach or oven, even when it was neither swept nor covered with ashes. This applies even if the oven is enclosed and retains substantial heat, and even if the pot is close to the coals, provided it does not touch them. Beis Yosef. See below, paragraph 17.
