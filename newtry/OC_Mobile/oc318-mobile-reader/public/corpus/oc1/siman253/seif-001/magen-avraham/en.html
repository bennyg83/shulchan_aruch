"Further reduction." Meaning any food that a person is pleased to have dry out and further reduce. [Milchamot] "That it is raw." This means that in such a case it may be placed there immediately before nightfall. But if he placed it there earlier in the day, it is forbidden, because it will already have cooked somewhat before Shabbos and he must remove it after dark unless it became fully cooked before Shabbos. See siman 254. "That he covered the coals with ashes." It is unnecessary to cover them so thoroughly that no fire at all is visible. A minimal covering suffices. [Ran; Beit Yosef] Even if the coals later flare up again, it is permitted, because by initially covering them he demonstrated that he does not desire the coals. "A raw piece." Placing 1 raw piece into the pot permits leaving the entire pot there, because he thereby puts the food out of his mind until the following day. This applies only when the piece is actually raw close to Shabbos. "On top of it." The prohibition discussed by the Mechaber concerns leaving the pot on the stove where the heat is strong enough to create the concern that he may stir the coals. Placing it alongside is more lenient, as explained in the seif and in the authorities. "Garuf and katum." Garuf means that the coals were removed; katum means that they were covered with ashes. The purpose is to weaken the fire and show that he has no interest in intensifying it. "Fully cooked." Where the food is fully cooked and further reduction is detrimental, there is no concern that he will stir the coals. If further reduction improves it, the dispute stated by the Mechaber applies. "Ma'achal ben Derusai." According to the lenient opinion, once the food has reached the stage of ma'achal ben Derusai it may be left on the stove even when the stove is not garuf or katum. Our custom follows this lenient opinion, as Rama states. "An oven." Our ovens have the status of a kirah rather than the more stringent oven discussed by the Gemara, as the authorities explain. "If he forgot." Where one left the pot there inadvertently, the food is permitted when it was fully cooked, even if further reduction improves it. If it had only begun to cook and was not fully cooked, it is forbidden until after Shabbos. If he left it deliberately in violation of the law, it is forbidden. "A non-Jew returned it." Where a non-Jew returned the pot on Shabbos, its law is like one who forgot and left it. If a Jew returned it, its law is like one who deliberately left it. Where further reduction is detrimental, there is room for leniency because no benefit was obtained from the prohibited act. "Directly upon the coals." All agree that actually insulating a pot in coals is forbidden. When the pot merely stands upon the fire and is uncovered above, some authorities do not regard this as hatmanah, and this is the custom. One should preferably loosen it somewhat from the fire before Shabbos so that it can be removed without moving the coals. If this was not done and the pot must be removed on Shabbos, a non-Jew should remove it if available. If no non-Jew is available, a Jew may remove it carefully, taking care not to stir or move the coals. If a slight movement occurs unintentionally, it is permitted because it was not intended. Where coals are beneath the pot but the pot is not actually buried among them, removal is permitted when this can be done without stirring or arranging the coals. Where the coals surround the pot, however, Mordechai writes that a Jew should not remove it, because doing so necessarily moves the coals. The concern is not that he seeks to extinguish or kindle, but that removal itself will move them. See the related discussion in siman 254 and Tosafos, Yoma 44a.
