[Levush:] For the needs of the night, etc. This implies that for the needs of the following day it is permitted. Beis Yosef, however, forbids it, because he did not see anyone who wrote otherwise except the Kol Bo; see there. Therefore, the Shulchan Aruch did not write that the restriction applies specifically to the needs of the night, and the Bach likewise rules this way. The Bach questions Beis Yosef, arguing that the Kol Bo wrote this only where the stove was swept or covered with ashes. I examined the Kol Bo, folio 33, and the words of Beis Yosef are correct. The Kol Bo writes: 'It is permitted to leave it there, because people divert their attention from it, and additionally because they cover the coals with ashes,' etc. End quote. It is clear that according to the first reason it is permitted even without covering the coals. Nevertheless, as a matter of practical law, it appears that it is forbidden. Although one leaves it there for the following day, since it could finish cooking at night through stoking, we are concerned that he may change his mind and eat it at night, and this is considered a need of the night. Moreover, the Levush himself, in siman 254, se'if 8, states that it is forbidden. The fact that an indication of intent is effective where the coals were covered with ashes, and likewise regarding insulating food, may be distinguished. See Raavan, siman 338, and Rama at the beginning of siman 257. However, Beis Yosef cited above requires study, for in siman 254, regarding an onion, meat, and an egg, he likewise cites the Mordechai and accepts this reasoning that it is permitted when intended for the following day, and the context there implies that it concerns leaving food on the fire.
