To place upon it. This means upon the top of the stove. Its "top" refers to the thickness of the stove's walls, and certainly to the area directly above the stove's interior cavity. This applies even if the stove is covered with its cover and one places the pot upon the cover. So Rashi writes on folio 37, and so writes the Tur below in this se'if. See there in the Beit Yosef and Bach; the Magen Avraham in se'if katan 22 likewise concurs with the Bach. However, from the Rambam's wording in chapter 3, halacha 4, "one may not leave it upon the fire on Shabbat," see there, it would appear at first glance that placing it upon a cover is not included in "upon its top." Perhaps this resembles covering the coals with ash, where we say that he presumably diverted his attention from them and will not come to stoke them. See halacha 13, where the Rambam writes, "If he sealed the mouth of the oven with clay," which implies that even when it is covered there remains a concern that he may stoke the coals. There, however, the pot stands inside the oven, whereas here it stands above, upon the cover. It is possible that once he covered the stove and placed the pot above it, he has already diverted his attention from cooking. Nevertheless, the Rambam's position requires study.

To leave it upon the stove. This means for use during the night, as is the wording of the Tur. If his intention is to use it the following day, some poskim permit it, because we are not concerned that he will come to stoke the coals, just as in the case of a completely raw dish. See Eliyah Rabbah, and see siman 254, se'if 5, the Gra there, Magen Avraham there, se'if katan 20, and Eliyah Rabbah at the beginning of this siman. Strictly speaking, however, one may not rely upon this. Only with a completely raw dish, which cannot be eaten at all, is there no concern. Here, since it can be eaten, we are concerned that he may change his mind, decide to eat it, and stoke the coals. After the fact, one may rely upon this leniency, provided that he does not regularly act this way, as below in siman 257, se'if 1, in the gloss concerning insulation. This also appears from Peri Megadim, siman 254, Eshel Avraham, se'if katan 20.
And continued reduction is detrimental to it. The Gemara on folio 37b states: Any dish containing flour is harmed by continued reduction, except for turnips. Although turnips contain flour, reduction improves them. This is only where the turnips contain meat and he does not need the dish for guests. If they contain no meat, or if he needs them for guests, reduction is detrimental, because the meat crumbles through the reduction and is no longer discernible, and it is not respectful to serve guests in that manner. See Peri Megadim, Mishbetzot Zahav 259, who explains that even when the dish contains no flour, reduction is detrimental if it contains no meat. However, the discussion in Berachot 44b implies that reduction improves turnips even when they are cooked only with wood. This is obvious according to Shmuel, who states there explicitly that adding more wood improves them. Even according to Rav and Rabbi Yochanan, who mention meat and wine, their point is that these also improve the dish. Even were one to say that they require meat and wine as well, that would be to improve it further by completely breaking the strength of the turnip. All agree, however, that reduction itself improves the turnip by weakening its strength, even without meat. The Yerushalmi, chapter Kirah, likewise states that turnip heads are improved by continued reduction. It is also difficult to suppose that Rav Nachman, Shmuel's student, would disagree with him. We must therefore say that the Gemara's qualification applies specifically where the turnips contain flour. Rashi in Shabbat, however, somewhat supports the Peri Megadim, though his words can be reconciled. The Gemara further states that lafda, a dish of figs, porridge, and dates are harmed by continued reduction, even without flour. On folio 38 it states that cabbage, beans, and shredded meat are improved by reduction. See Rashi there and Gilyon HaShas in the name of the Arukh regarding the meaning of "shredded meat." The Gemara also states that hot water is harmed by reduction, while eggs are improved by it. So writes the Ramban in Milchamot, in accordance with the explicit conclusion of the passage. See Peri Megadim there, who raises a difficulty from the Yerushalmi at the end of the first chapter, which states that meat, onion, and egg are harmed by reduction, and he struggles greatly to reconcile it. In truth, it is clear from the straightforward meaning of the Babylonian Talmud that eggs are improved by reduction, and the Yerushalmi in chapter Kirah also states this in the name of Rabbi Yose. The passage at the end of the first chapter concerns food roasting directly upon coals, which becomes scorched through continued reduction, as the Rishonim explain. So too write the Penei Moshe and Gilyon HaShas there. Whenever one does not know with certainty whether continued reduction harms or improves a food, it is proper to be stringent, as stated by Eliyah Rabbah in siman 318 and Peri Megadim in siman 259. It is permitted to place a pot beside it. See the Mishnah Berurah for what I wrote in the name of some authorities. This is the view of the Magen Avraham as explained by Rabbi Akiva Eiger, who expresses surprise at the Dagul Merevavah for wishing to permit returning the pot. In truth, I am surprised at his objection. The baraita concerning two stoves explicitly states in its concluding clause that returning is also permitted. According to the Gemara's initial assumption that this case is included in placing a pot beside a stove, it necessarily proves that returning is also permitted. Although the Gemara rejects this by saying that there the second stove is elevated and air circulates around it, once the conclusion permits placing beside a stove, that distinction is no longer necessary. I found that the Ramban in Milchamot likewise brings proof from the concluding clause. I later found that the Tosefet Shabbat also raises the proof from that clause, though he answers with difficulty that according to the conclusion this is not considered placing beside the stove. In any event, there is no basis for surprise at the Dagul Merevavah, for he understands the matter as we have explained. Tosafot, s.v. "What is the law," also states that returning is permitted even according to the first opinion. What I wrote in the Mishnah Berurah, that some are lenient even to place it beside the stove initially, is found in Beit Meir, see there. Some support may also be drawn from the Rosh, chapter Kirah, siman 10, who writes that placing food near a bonfire is permitted because it resembles placing it beside a stove, see there.

Even if it was neither swept out. This requires consideration. The Talmud and the poskim use the expression, "it was heated with straw or rakings," implying that the fire had already burned. It is therefore prohibited with wood because wood leaves coals behind, creating concern that one may stoke them, whereas straw leaves no coals. Accordingly, if one placed a large quantity of straw that burns for a long time, perhaps it is prohibited to leave food there at least while the straw is burning. Alternatively, perhaps the Talmud merely described the usual practice, because people generally placed the pot after the fire had burned. The same law may apply while the straw is still burning. While there is an active flame, there is in any event no concern that he will stoke it, for why would he need to stoke it while it is burning? Stoking becomes relevant only after the flame dies down. The teaching may therefore mean that with straw, once the flame goes out, stoking will not help because even the ember is consumed. With olive refuse or wood, however, it is always prohibited, because he may stoke the coals after the flame has gone out. This requires study.

If a non-Jew returned it. This means that he returned it for the benefit of a Jew. The Levush writes that this applies only when it was done without the Jew's knowledge, for otherwise it is treated as intentional. This does not appear correct, because the reason for the ruling is that an act performed intentionally by a non-Jew for a Jew is treated like an inadvertent act by a Jew, as the Gra explains. There, the rule applies even when the non-Jew acted at the Jew's instruction. This is also evident from se'if 5 in the gloss, where food returned by a non-Jew is prohibited after the fact only if it had cooled completely, even when the non-Jew acted at the Jew's instruction, as the Gra explicitly explains there. So too writes the Shulchan Aruch HaRav.
Its law is like that of one who forgot and left it on the stove. See the Mishnah Berurah for the reason. Although returning food is prohibited even when done inadvertently, as stated immediately below, we are lenient here because that ruling itself is not entirely settled. The Hagahot Ashri is uncertain about it, as the Magen Avraham writes. See the Mishnah Berurah, where we wrote that even the Rama below agrees that if a Jew returned it, the food is prohibited even when he acted inadvertently. If a non-Jew returned it when it had been cooked to the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai, one might at first suggest leniency according to the Rama below, for he writes that its law is like that of one who forgot and left food on the stove, and regarding leaving food the Rama rules below that ma'achal ben Derusai is sufficient. This may be rejected, however, because regarding returning, all agree that it is prohibited. Even if, according to Chananya, the prohibition is only rabbinic, one may not have a non-Jew perform a rabbinically prohibited act. Even after the fact it is prohibited, as the Gra writes in his explanation below in se'if 5, in the gloss. We are lenient here when the food is fully cooked and further reduction improves it, despite the rabbinic prohibition when done by a Jew, because the Jew does not derive substantial benefit from the non-Jew's act, since the food was already fully cooked, as the poskim explain. Here, however, when it had not been fully cooked, the Jew clearly benefits from the non-Jew's act. This is certainly so according to the poskim who maintain that a Jew would violate a Torah prohibition in such a case, as discussed below in siman 318 in the Beit Yosef, see there. The Rama's ruling therefore applies here only when the food was fully cooked and further reduction improves it.

Accordingly, initially it is certainly proper to ensure that the food is fully cooked before nightfall and to remove it from the fire. If circumstances caused a delay, however, such as guests arriving before sunset and one needed to cook a dish for them, he may place it upon the metal support to cook even though it will reach only half-cooked by sunset.
