It is permitted. See Taz and Magen Avraham, se'if katan 6. Levush writes, 'even two adjoining stoves,' etc. In Eliyah Zuta, se'if katan 6, he adds that this must indeed be the law: if placing a pot beside the stove is permitted, why did the Mishnah formulate the case specifically as adjoining stoves? It could simply have taught that one may leave food upon a swept stove but not upon one that is not swept. Granted, were placing a pot beside the stove forbidden, the Mishnah would teach that where the pot is elevated it is permitted. Rather, this indicates that adjoining stoves are more stringent, perhaps because the pot receives heat both from the unswept stove and from the swept one. Teves Shor and Maamar Mordechai raise a similar difficulty. In my novellae I wrote that two stoves may indeed be more stringent. I also suggested that permission applies only when the pot is left on top of the swept stove, but not inside it. Although the expression 'upon it' can include inside it, the phrase 'on top of it' implies not inside, as I wrote in the discussion beginning 'Rav Chisda said in three cases'; see there. This explanation works only according to the view that sweeping out some of the coals is sufficient. On that view, two stoves, one of which is swept, are no worse than a stove partly swept in the place of the pot. According to the Mechaber, however, who rules that all the coals must be swept out and that partial sweeping is insufficient, what does the Gemara mean when it says that if the Mishnah teaches leaving food, it follows Rabbi Yehudah? Why could it not follow Rabbi Meir, with two adjoining stoves being more stringent than one stove? This proves that there is no additional stringency here. The Mishnah follows Rabbi Yehudah and mentions two stoves merely to present the case and provide a convenient sign. Taz therefore properly asks why Rama needed to write this law. It follows that with two stoves, placing the pot even inside the swept one is permitted. I discussed this at length there.
