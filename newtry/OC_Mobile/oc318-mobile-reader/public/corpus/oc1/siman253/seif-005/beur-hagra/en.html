Se'if 5, it is permitted to place, etc., because this is not the usual manner of cooking, etc. This follows what is stated in the gloss to siman 318, se'if 15: 'There are those who are lenient,' etc. This is the view of the Rashba, who writes in his novellae that placing food opposite a fire or in a first vessel is not forbidden merely because it is cold and uncooked. Rather, warming is prohibited in a place capable of cooking only as a decree lest one cook. Food that was already cooked, however, may be warmed even in a first vessel or opposite a fire, and even in a place where the heat reaches yad soledes bo, provided it is not placed directly upon the fire or directly upon a stove. This is evident from the statement, 'Anything that has already come into hot water,' etc. Although an object heated by fire has the same status as fire itself, and one who cooks with it is liable to bring a sin-offering, as stated in chapter 3 concerning rolling an egg, the Gemara nevertheless says that oil may be heated even to yad soledes bo, because that authority holds that oil is not subject to cooking. Salt likewise may be placed even in a first vessel, because it does not cook in a first vessel. It follows that even an entirely uncooked food, if it is difficult to cook, such as meat, may be placed in a first vessel or opposite a fire, even where it will reach yad soledes bo. The general principle is that wherever the item will not become cooked, whether because the prohibition of cooking does not apply to it or because it does not cook under those conditions, it is permitted in a first vessel or opposite a fire. Although the Sages forbade returning a pot to a stove that was not swept, and forbade initially placing it even upon a swept stove, notwithstanding that the food is hot and fully cooked, the reason there is that it appears like cooking, because that is the normal manner of cooking. The same applies to placing it directly upon a fire. A first vessel or a location opposite a fire, however, is not normally used by people for cooking in most instances, so the act does not appear like cooking but merely like removing the chill. It is forbidden only where the food will actually become cooked. From all this follows the ruling that one may place it upon another pot, etc., because this is not the usual manner of cooking and is no different from placing it beside a hot-water urn or opposite a fire. The first opinion in the Shulchan Aruch there, which forbids reheating a food containing liquid once it has cooled, is the view of the Rosh. He explains the Mishnah's statement, 'Anything that has already come into hot water,' etc., as referring specifically to dry food, and he derives this from the phrase, 'a decree lest it boil,' following Rashi's explanation. This presents no difficulty for the Rashba, for it may refer to bringing the food to a boil directly over a fire or upon a stove. The ruling there in se'if 4 concerning a cooked dish is likewise stated according to the Rosh, as explained above, but the proof is not conclusive.
But to insulate, etc. Even when the food is fully cooked, so that further cooking does not apply in this manner, insulation is nevertheless forbidden, as stated in siman 257, se'if 7: wherever we prohibit, etc. Since the Amoraim dispute leaving food on a stove in chapter 3 but do not dispute insulation, it follows that insulation is forbidden there under all circumstances. The same applies here.
And the same applies that it is forbidden, etc., for they permitted only. As explained above, returning is permitted only when the food is fully cooked. Even then, all the conditions of returning are required, and only an act of returning is permitted. This remains true even when further cooking causes the food to shrivel and is detrimental to it, as follows from the Gemara on 38a and from the statement of Rabbi Oshaya cited above. This is so even though the prohibition of cooking no longer applies, as explained above.

And she subsequently kindles the oven. Her intention in kindling the oven is not for the sake of the pot. Although the resulting heating is inevitable, it is only rabbinically forbidden, as Tosafot write in Kerisus 22b and Yoma 34b, and it therefore constitutes a double rabbinic restriction. It is true that an inevitable result is forbidden even in a rabbinic prohibition, as indicated in Beitzah 36b, and even though trapping that species is not ordinarily intended, and likewise in Shabbos 120b, where the Gemara states that even Rabbi Shimon concedes in such a case. Instructing a non-Jew is also forbidden with respect to a rabbinic prohibition, as explained above, and even when the non-Jew acts on his own, as shown by the passage in Beitzah and by the case of a non-Jew who comes to extinguish a fire in Shabbos 121a. Nevertheless, instructing a non-Jew to perform an act whose prohibited result is merely inevitable is permitted, because both elements of the prohibition are lenient. Terumas HaDeshen. This requires study, for the category of an inevitable result applies specifically where the result is not desired, whereas if the result is desired the act is forbidden by Torah law, as follows from Shabbos 103a, where the Gemara invokes the principle of Abaye and Rava and asks that he should be liable, and from Shabbos 133b, where Rava says, 'Even if you say,' etc. The distinction drawn between these prohibitions is also highly strained. It appears that the Rama did not rely upon this rationale alone, and permitted only where the food had not cooled completely. This is the meaning of his words, 'Therefore the custom is,' etc., namely, in such a case.
