And she subsequently kindles the stove. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 66, writes that even if the food cooled completely, there is room to permit it. The maidservant intends to heat the winter room, which the Sages permitted because everyone is considered ill with respect to cold. Although the hot food will certainly become heated by itself as a result, and she places it upon the stove with this in mind, nevertheless, when she places it there there is no fire in the stove, and her primary intention in subsequently kindling the fire is to heat the winter room. Although the heating of the food is an inevitable consequence, one may distinguish, regarding a rabbinic restriction, between complete intent and an unintended yet inevitable consequence. A similar principle appears in a responsum of Maharam cited by the Mordechai in the chapter Hazorek, regarding a privy built over a surrounding pit. End quote. Rama's words here imply that he permitted this only where the food had not cooled completely but retained some warmth. In truth, however, there is no distinction. Were it forbidden here when completely cooled, it should be forbidden even where some warmth remains, since even boiling food may not be returned on Shabbos to a stove that was neither swept nor covered with ashes, as was stated plainly above. Regarding the essence of this leniency, I do not understand the position of Terumas HaDeshen. It appears comparable to the ruling of Tur, Yoreh Deah, siman 113: 'Therefore, if the non-Jew knew that meat was in the oven, even if he heated it in order to dry a utensil there, the meat is forbidden, because we are concerned that he also intended to cook the meat.' End quote. Thus, even regarding food cooked by a non-Jew, a prohibition that has no basis in the Torah, they were stringent to suspect that perhaps he also intended to cook the meat. The cooking of the meat is at least secondary to drying the utensil, yet it is nevertheless forbidden. Here, the non-Jew certainly intends to heat the dish and places it there for that purpose. It is for this reason that a Jew may not place it there. Why, then, should we permit it because the stove is kindled afterward, merely because her primary intention is to heat the winter room? She also intends to heat the dish. Even if this were uncertain, it should be forbidden lest she also intended the dish, as in the other case, all the more so when that intention is certain. The proof Terumas HaDeshen brings from the Mordechai in the chapter Hazorek is not relevant. There, one has no intention whatsoever regarding where the waste falls; see there. Here, by contrast, she desires this result and the non-Jew performs an action for that purpose. Furthermore, it has already been established in this siman that anything forbidden for a Jew is likewise forbidden through a non-Jew, even after the fact, as Beis Yosef writes in this siman in the name of Rashba, responsum 68. Why should this case differ? It further appears to me that even Terumas HaDeshen permitted this only in the case addressed by his responsum. The question there states: 'There are people who have no separate ovens for baking and insulating hot food. They insulate it in the stove in the winter room. On Shabbos morning the maidservant removes all the hot food from the stove and places it upon the outer top of the stove inside the winter room, and then kindles the fire in the stove in order to heat the winter room,' etc. End quote. In that case, the prohibition is less severe, because the maidservant must remove the food from the stove in order to heat the winter room. When she places it at that time upon the outer top of the stove, it is not so apparent and definite that she does so for the subsequent heating. This differs from our situation, where we have a separate stove for insulating hot food, and on Shabbos she removes it from that stove and places it upon the stove in the winter room. Everyone sees that she does so for the purpose of heating it. What difference does it make whether the heating precedes her act or follows it? Therefore, it appears to me that a conscientious person should not rely upon this leniency.
