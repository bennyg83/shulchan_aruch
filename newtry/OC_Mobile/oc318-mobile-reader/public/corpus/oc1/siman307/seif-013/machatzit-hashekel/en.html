(Seif katan 13) Because, etc. This requires study, for in the third chapter of Eruvin, 39a, they state explicitly that this applies even within the techum. This is the correct reading.
It is not evident, etc. For they say there: Rav Yehudah said that when Yom Tov falls on Erev Shabbos, one may establish an eruv on Erev Yom Tov for Yom Tov in order to go toward the east, and on Yom Tov he may establish an eruv for Shabbos in order to go on Shabbos toward the west. The eruv is effected with his feet, by sitting there at twilight until nightfall, through which he acquires that place as his place of residence. The Gemara asks: But then Yom Tov is preparing for Shabbos, since on Yom Tov he establishes an eruv with his feet for Shabbos. It answers that he merely sits there at twilight and need not say, ‘My residence shall be in my present place.’ Since he says nothing, this is not regarded as preparing on Yom Tov for Shabbos. The Gemara then asks from that which was taught: A person may not walk to the end of his field to see what work it requires, nor may he stroll at the entrance of the province in order to enter the bathhouse after Shabbos, because it appears that he is preparing on Shabbos for after Shabbos, even though he says nothing. It answers: There, the matter is evident; here, regarding an eruv, the matter is not evident, for perhaps he is a Torah scholar, etc. Thus far are the words of the Gemara. It follows that even at the end of the techum the matter is not evident. How, then, could the Beit Yosef write that the reason it is forbidden at the end of the techum is that the matter is evident? These are the words of the Maggid Mishneh; see there.
And the reason it is forbidden, etc. This is a second objection to the Maggid Mishneh, who wrote that where the matter is evident, the prohibition is because of suspicion. Rashi, however, wrote that the reason is intrinsic to the law: anything that one may not do, etc., is included in ‘refraining from pursuing your affairs.’ Tosafot therefore asked that, for this same reason, it should also be forbidden here to wait until nightfall in order to detach fruit that is within the techum. Tosafot answered that since the matter is not evident, etc., the Sages were not stringent.
But to wait until nightfall at the end of the techum, although the matter is not evident, as stated above, is nevertheless forbidden. This is not because of suspicion, but because of ‘refraining from pursuing your affairs,’ for it is forbidden to prepare oneself on Shabbos for a weekday activity. Although within the techum, where the matter is not evident, the Sages did not forbid such preparation, they were nevertheless more stringent at the end of the techum. According to Rashi's rationale, the distinction within the techum between a case in which the matter is evident and one in which it is not evident must be that when it is not evident, it is considered no more than thought, which is permitted; whereas when the matter is evident, it is considered like speech, which is forbidden.
