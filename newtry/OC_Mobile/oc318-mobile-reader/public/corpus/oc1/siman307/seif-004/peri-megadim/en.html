The statement that some permit instructing a non-Jew to perform a Torah prohibition for the sake of a mitzvah does not raise the following difficulty: if instructing a non-Jew is prohibited only for discretionary matters, it should already be forbidden because of “refraining from pursuing your affairs and speaking of matters,” as explained in paragraph 1. The answer is that even hinting to the non-Jew is forbidden, as the Rama writes in seif 22. See Taz, seif katan 2, and Magen Avraham, seif katan 31. A hint also causes him to act as the Jew's agent. By contrast, under the prohibition of “speaking of matters,” thought and hinting are permitted, as stated in seifim 7 and 22. See what I wrote at length in paragraph 1.
The ordinary writer says: I have a small question here, which I shall present, and through it several other details will also be clarified. It is obvious to me that instructing a non-Jew on Shabbos is a shevus even where the act itself involves only an ordinary negative prohibition. For example, if a Jew's load is upon a non-Jew's donkey, it is forbidden to tell the non-Jew to lead it. Do not argue that this should be a double shevus and therefore more lenient, for it is all included in one decree, while the underlying prohibition of Shabbos carries kares and stoning. From this I derive the law discussed in Yoreh Deah 297:4 and Even HaEzer 5:14, based on Bava Metzia 90a. The Gemara resolves that regarding every prohibition involving a negative commandment, it is forbidden to instruct a non-Jew to perform it. We rule that a descendant of Noach is not commanded concerning castration, and the case of “those bulls,” etc., must therefore be prohibited because a Jew instructed the non-Jew. All the more so is a Shabbos shevus forbidden where the underlying act violates a negative commandment. It is also obvious to me that regarding other prohibitions, a double shevus may be permitted. The Gemara's question and resolution necessarily concerned instructing a non-Jew to violate an actual negative commandment, not a double shevus, and one cannot derive a stringency for other prohibitions from Shabbos, which is more severe. I remain uncertain, however, whether instructing a non-Jew is forbidden regarding an affirmative prohibition in other areas of halachah. The Rama's language in Yoreh Deah refers specifically to an act prohibited by a negative commandment. This implies that an affirmative prohibition, which is more lenient than a negative commandment, is not included. As the Sages state at the beginning of Yevamos, a negative commandment is more stringent than an affirmative commandment. We therefore have no source to prohibit it. Accordingly, after the sixth hour on Erev Pesach, it would be permitted to tell a non-Jew to purchase wine or the like for a Jew with the Jew's money and to keep the chametz in the non-Jew's possession until after Pesach. A non-Jew cannot serve as a halachic agent, and the chametz does not belong to the Jew. Whether one may instruct a non-Jew after the sixth hour depends upon the dispute whether one then violates bal yera'eh and bal yimatzei, or only the affirmative commandment of tashbisu. Most authorities agree that even in Rambam's view only the affirmative commandment applies at that time. The question is therefore whether the Sages did not prohibit instructing a non-Jew where the underlying prohibition is only affirmative. Yom Tov and Chol HaMoed may be different, because they are called Shabbos, as Tosafot write there in Bava Metzia.
And in the sacred words of Beis Shmuel, Even HaEzer 5, seif katan 19, he writes that the halachah does not follow Masas Binyamin, siman 96, who rules that a non-Jew may appoint another non-Jew as his agent, deriving this from the exclusion implied by “you also.” His proof is that one may sell an animal to a non-Jew even though the purchaser may give it to his agent to castrate. One who examines Terumas HaDeshen, siman 299, and the citation in Even HaEzer is a printing error, will see that the reason given is that the rule that there is no agency for a transgression may possibly apply even to a non-Jew. In addition, one can argue a fortiori that since we generally rule that a non-Jew has no halachic agency, a non-Jew certainly cannot appoint another non-Jew as an agent. This indicates that Terumas HaDeshen did not consider the matter certain and relied only upon the combination of both reasons. The Rama, however, in Choshen Mishpat 388:15, adopts the Tashbetz's reason that when the agent is known to be habitually prepared to commit the forbidden act, agency for a transgression does apply, because the principle, “Whose words should be obeyed, the Master's or the disciple's?” is then inapplicable. This reason alone found favor in his eyes; see Shach, seif katan 68. It therefore follows necessarily that in Even HaEzer, where the Rama permits the sale even where the non-Jew will give the animal to his agent, his ruling rests upon the rule that a non-Jew cannot appoint a non-Jew as a halachic agent, contrary to Masas Binyamin. Non-Jews in our time certainly do not generally observe all seven Noachide commandments and are presumed capable of violating them, so the rule of agency for a transgression would otherwise apply to them.
