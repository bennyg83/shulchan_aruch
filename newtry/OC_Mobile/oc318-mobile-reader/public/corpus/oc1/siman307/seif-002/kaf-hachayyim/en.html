12) [Seif 2] It is forbidden to hire workers, etc. This applies specifically for an optional purpose. For a mitzvah, one may hire workers. Riaz; see there. Erech HaShulchan, paragraph 2. Chayei Adam, klal 61, paragraph 2, however, writes that hiring is forbidden even for a mitzvah; see there.
13) There: Nor tell a non-Jew to hire, etc. This applies specifically to a matter involving at least a rabbinic prohibition. No decree was made regarding a matter that is only an extra stringency. Radak, bayis 22, room 7; Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur; Magen Avraham, paragraph 2; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 3.
14) There: Nor tell a non-Jew to hire, etc. It is forbidden even to tell him on Shabbos to hire workers after Shabbos. Chayei Adam, klal 62, paragraph 1. It is obvious that one may not tell a non-Jew to perform labor for him the next day. Responsa Radbaz, siman 684; Birkei Yosef, paragraph 4.
15) It is forbidden to tell one non-Jew to tell another non-Jew to perform labor. Rabbi Gershon Ashkenazi in a responsum cited in Responsa Chavos Yair, simanim 49 and 53. Mahari Ayash agrees in Bnei Yehudah, folio 67b. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 5. Shulchan Atzei Shittim, siman 1, similarly writes that when a Jewish person tells a non-Jew, and that non-Jew tells another non-Jew, this constitutes only one rabbinic prohibition. Responsa Shenos Chaim, simanim 38 and 175, writes the same. Tiferes Yisrael on the Mishnah, in Kalkeles Shabbos, laws of instructing a non-Jew, siman 5, however, writes that it is a rabbinic prohibition compounded with another rabbinic prohibition; see there. A practical difference concerns the law below in seif 5 that a compounded rabbinic prohibition is permitted for a mitzvah or great need; see there. Since most great authorities consider instructing one non-Jew to instruct another a single rabbinic prohibition, this is the ruling we adopt. Only when additional grounds for leniency exist may it be possible to rely upon those who permit it. See Sedei Chemed, principles, system of alef, paragraph 174.
16) Regarding the principal prohibition against instructing a non-Jew on Shabbos, whether it is a Torah or rabbinic prohibition, see Sedei Chemed, principles, system of alef, paragraph 164, who cites a dispute; see there.
17) There: Even telling him before Shabbos, etc. This implies that it is forbidden even to instruct him to hire workers on Shabbos to build during the week, although hiring is only rabbinically forbidden. This is the view of Rav Saadia, Rabbi Shmuel bar Baruch, Sefer Mitzvos Gadol, and Tur: telling a non-Jew on Friday is forbidden even regarding a rabbinic prohibition; see there. Responsa Rashba, siman 875, however, implies that on Friday one may tell a non-Jew to hire workers on Shabbos to build during the week. Rokeach, siman 88, and Rivash, siman 387, folio 297, write the same. Ri ibn Sasson, siman 156, similarly writes that this appears from Rambam's wording; see there. Radbaz, volume 1, siman 132, likewise writes that telling a non-Jew on Friday is forbidden only when one tells him to perform labor; see there. Erech HaShulchan, paragraph 3. See there.
18) I saw that Tashbetz, volume 2, siman 281, forbids telling a non-Jew to carry articles from one house to another without an eruv, even for a mitzvah and even when told before Shabbos; see there. This follows his own view that a compounded rabbinic prohibition was permitted only for bris milah, which overrides Shabbos, as he writes in siman 197, and not all authorities agree with Rambam; see there. According to our practice, however, that a compounded rabbinic prohibition is permitted for a mitzvah, it is permitted, especially when he is told before Shabbos, for some permit this even without a mitzvah. Radbaz similarly writes that by strict law one may tell a non-Jew on Friday to come play music the next day. Telling a non-Jew before Shabbos was forbidden only regarding labor, whereas here the Jewish person himself is prohibited only because of a decree. Some also permit telling a non-Jew to play music on Shabbos, and one should not extend the prohibition. See there. Although Rosh, cited by Beis Yosef in siman 517, forbids a similar case regarding pigeons for a Jewish person's needs, we rely upon these great authorities to permit it straightforwardly before Shabbos for a mitzvah. Even one who is stringent regarding a compounded rabbinic prohibition for a mitzvah would not be so stringent before Shabbos, especially in this case, for Hagahos Mordechai writes that in the public domain instructing a non-Jew is independently permitted because the article could be brought through human partitions; see there. See what is written in siman 655 in the name of Rabbi Yitzchak and Ritva, that instructing a non-Jew was not forbidden regarding a rabbinic prohibition that does not involve an action upon the object itself, such as carrying from one domain to another; see there. Erech HaShulchan there.
19) There: But after Shabbos he may say, 'Why did you not do it,' etc. His intent is that the non-Jew do it the following Shabbos. Sefer HaTerumah, Sefer Mitzvos Gadol, and Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 6, write this. Their words in their plain sense are difficult, because a complete Mishnah in the chapter Kol Kisvei teaches that if a non-Jew kindled a lamp on his own, a Jewish person may not benefit from it, and similar rulings appear below in siman 325. Ginas Veradim, klal 3, siman 21, questions this, followed by Rav Yaakov Castro; see there. Tosefta, chapter 14, teaches that when a familiar non-Jew acts for himself, benefit is forbidden because the Jewish person accustoms him to do so and he acts for the Jewish person on another Shabbos. How, then, could this be permitted? It therefore appears that one should be lenient only when the Jewish person's body derives no benefit, as Ginas Veradim writes, or when a lamp and fire already exist and the non-Jew merely adds to them, as Rav Yaakov Castro writes. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 6. Taz, paragraph 2, inclines to be lenient in his comments on Tur, but Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 2, questions him; see there. Accordingly, one should also not be lenient regarding any of Bach's permissive suggestions, such as telling a non-Jew, 'Wipe your nose,' so that he understands to remove the charred wick from the lamp; Knesses HaGedolah's suggestion in Hagahos HaTur, paragraph 2, of saying, 'The lamp is not giving light,' so that a maidservant understands and trims the wick; or saying, when one wants a book from a place without light, that the maidservant should bring another object from there, so that she kindles the lamp and he follows her and takes the book. Ikarei HaDat, siman 14, paragraph 100, writes the same in the name of Mor U'Ketziah; see there. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, writes in the name of Shiltei Gibborim in the chapter Shoel that all opinions forbid hinting on Shabbos itself for a Shabbos need. Magen Avraham, paragraph 31, writes the same, contrary to Bach; see there. See below, paragraph 64. Some permit only saying, 'The house is dark,' whereupon the maidservant independently understands to remove the charred portion, as stated below in paragraph 55.
