Until enough time has elapsed for it to be done. See Taz, and see Bava Metzia 90a regarding those oxen that a non-Jew castrated. Here, from one Shabbos to the next, the act could have been performed permissibly, and therefore the Sages did not impose a penalty. Castration, by contrast, can never be performed permissibly by a Jew, and therefore they imposed a penalty. Yet when one causes animals to mate in violation of kilayim through a non-Jew, no penalty is imposed. See Taz, Yoreh Deah 297, seif katan 2, who explains that castration is common and therefore the Sages imposed a penalty, whereas crossbreeding animals is not common. Beis Shmuel, Even HaEzer 5, seif katan 17, writes in the name of Derishah that with castration the prohibited act is recognizable, and therefore the Sages imposed a penalty, whereas with crossbreeding animals it is not recognizable, and therefore they did not. One cannot explain that they imposed a penalty for castration because crossbreeding an impure animal with another impure animal, or a pure animal with another pure animal, is only D’Rabbanan. An impure animal cannot conceive from a pure one; and perhaps they did not forbid the animal where the owner did not derive substantial benefit. This explanation is untenable, because Rambam necessarily refers to leading an impure animal together with another impure animal, which is D’Rabbanan, whereas causing them to mate is prohibited according to the Torah. See Shach, Yoreh Deah 297, seif katan 1. We have already discussed this at length in the general introduction; see there. This is not the place to elaborate. See also Rambam, chapter 6 of Hilchos Shabbos, halachah 8, who writes explicitly that although a Jew instructed a non-Jew to perform the act and receives disciplinary lashes, it is nevertheless permitted after Shabbos once enough time has elapsed for it to have been done. See Magen Avraham there, who writes that we have not found this prohibition in the Gemara; see there.
