(Seif katan 20) That he should not touch it, etc. The Agudah wrote: ‘My practice was, etc.’ We rule that an item brought from outside the techum is forbidden to the person for whom it was brought. Accordingly, the same should apply here: the person to whom the letter was sent may not read it, because reading it constitutes the benefit that is forbidden to him. If, however, the letter concerns matters necessary for personal welfare, it would be permitted. Because of the uncertainty, he did not wish to read it himself, lest it contain nothing necessary for personal welfare. He therefore instructed another person to read it. That other person may read it even if it contains nothing necessary for personal welfare, since it was not brought for him; nor does he derive benefit from reading it, since its contents do not concern him. If the other person saw that it concerned matters necessary for personal welfare, he would tell the recipient what was written in it. If it contained nothing necessary for personal welfare, he would remain silent and would not tell the recipient what it said. On the basis of this rationale, the Beit Yosef wrote that according to those who forbid it, although reading is forbidden as explained above, touching it was not forbidden. For we rule that food brought from outside the techum may be moved. Food is different because it is permitted to be eaten by someone for whom it was not brought, and since it is fit for some person, it may be moved. A letter, however, is fit for no one: the recipient may not read it, while others derive no benefit or purpose from reading it. Therefore, even moving it is forbidden. See there.
And now I have come, etc. For we are concerned that perhaps, etc. When one carries four amos in the public domain, or transfers an object from the public domain to a private domain or from a private domain to the public domain, if he performs both the lifting and the placement, he violates a Torah prohibition. If he performs only the lifting or only the placement, he violates a rabbinic prohibition because he has performed half of the melachah. If he stops in order to rest, this is considered a placement. If he stops merely to adjust his load upon his shoulder, it is not considered a placement, as written above in siman 266, seif katan 8. Therefore, when the messenger brings the letter into the private domain, a Jew may not take the letter from the messenger's hand before the messenger stops to rest, for the Jew would thereby perform the placement. Those who forbid taking the letter were concerned that he might take it from the messenger's hand before the messenger stops to rest; those who permit it were not concerned about this. See below, siman 347.
For there is no decree here, etc. And because of the concern that one may say, ‘Go and bring it,’ etc. This means that regarding a non-Jew who brings a gift to a Jew, Rabbeinu Tam writes that the reason it is forbidden is lest the Jew tell him to bring it. This concern is certainly inapplicable here, since the recipient did not know that someone would send him a letter. This is satisfactory according to Rabbeinu Tam's rationale. According to Rashi, however, the reason that something brought from outside the techum remains forbidden until enough time has elapsed after Shabbos for the act to have been performed is that one should not benefit from melachah performed on Yom Tov or Shabbos, as explained below in siman 515. One would therefore have to say that this authority considers Rabbeinu Tam's rationale primary. Yet below in siman 515 it appears that, for practical halachah, we consider Rashi's rationale primary; see there. We must therefore say that even according to Rashi, he may nevertheless concede that it is permitted here. As the Derishah writes, reading a letter is not a complete form of benefit, since the benefit is not recognizable at the time of reading, for the reader does not yet know whether its contents will be of any use to him. See there.
There is also no prohibition of muktzeh, for no person consciously set it aside from his use.
It is possible that those who forbid it hold, etc. This means as follows: With an object that remains within its owner's contemplation, since it is not muktzeh for its owner, it is not muktzeh for anyone, as written below at the end of siman 308. Here, however, the original owner set the letter aside from his use at the moment he sent it, while the person to whom it was sent knew nothing of it and therefore cannot be said to have had it in mind. It is consequently forbidden. The Magen Avraham writes similarly below, at the end of siman 515. If the letter belongs to a non-Jew, then the non-Jew is its owner, and an item belonging to a non-Jew does not require prior designation. It is therefore possible that he agrees that it is permitted. This is also implied below in siman 515.
Even Rabbi Shimon concedes in this case, as written in siman 498, seif 3. This follows the view of Maharshal there.
For if he is particular, etc. This means that he is particular not to use it because he does not want it to become soiled or the like. If, however, he is particular not to use it to cover a vessel because secret matters are written in it, it may be moved. After all, it is still physically possible to cover a vessel with it, and he can place the vessel together with the letter covering it in a concealed place where no eye will see it. See there.
