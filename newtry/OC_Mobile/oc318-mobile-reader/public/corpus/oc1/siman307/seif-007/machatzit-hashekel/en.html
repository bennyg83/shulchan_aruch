(s.k. 7) Slight, etc. According to one opinion, even a Jew himself may perform a rabbinic prohibition, and this is the Rambam's opinion cited below. See there in the Tur and in Magen Avraham, s.k. 12.
As stated there in seif 33, one who is groaning from pain may suck milk directly from an animal. In a situation of pain, where he is groaning, the Sages did not impose their decree against a rabbinic prohibition. Milking is a derivative of extracting, and sucking directly from the animal is considered extracting in an unusual manner. Although this is not an illness that affects the entire body and there is no danger to a limb, it is nevertheless permitted, because it is a rabbinic prohibition performed in an unusual manner, since people do not ordinarily suck directly from an animal.
These are the words of the Maggid Mishneh, etc. That is to say, everything written in this seif katan from the beginning, where Magen Avraham states, "But in the case of an actual illness, etc.," until this point, is from the Maggid Mishneh. The Maggid Mishneh wrote these words to resolve the Raavad's objection to the Rambam. The Raavad writes: "This is untenable. If there is a slight illness, the person himself may perform the act, for one who is groaning may suck milk directly from an animal on Shabbos, etc. They likewise permitted this in a case of financial loss, such as a drainpipe in which vegetation had grown, which one may crush with his foot." Magen Avraham cites the Maggid Mishneh's brief resolution and then explains it. The Maggid Mishneh writes: "The Rambam's intent here is that there is no illness affecting the entire body and no danger to a limb, but only some pain. This is why he writes, 'a slight illness or one who is in pain.' Furthermore, the act performed for him is done in its ordinary manner." End quote. It is implied from his words that his text read, etc. However, the Raavad's words imply that his text read, etc. This means that the Raavad necessarily had the Rambam's wording, "or if there is a great need for the matter." Otherwise, if his text had read only, "a slight illness or a mitzvah," and in place of "a great need for the matter" it had read, "or one who is in pain," what difficulty would the Raavad have raised from the fact that, in a case of financial loss, the person himself may perform a rabbinic prohibition, as we find regarding the drainpipe? Financial loss would have no relevance here, since the Rambam would not have discussed financial loss at all. Rather, the Raavad must have had the reading found in the Shulchan Aruch, "or if there is a great need for the matter." This means that, if we do not permit a rabbinic prohibition to be performed through a non-Jew, the Jew will suffer a financial loss. The Raavad therefore raised two objections. Against the Rambam's words, "a slight illness, etc.," he objected from the case of one who is groaning, where a Jew himself may violate a rabbinic prohibition. Against the words, "a great need for the matter," meaning a financial loss, he objected from the case of the drainpipe. The Maggid Mishneh, however, necessarily did not have the reading "a great need for the matter," but instead read "one who is in pain." He therefore needed to resolve only the objection from one who is groaning, not the objection from the drainpipe. For this reason, before addressing the Raavad's objection, he first explained the Rambam's words and wrote that the Rambam was precise in his language when he wrote, "a slight illness, etc." In the case of a full illness affecting the entire body, or even danger to a limb, a Jew himself may perform the act. He then addressed the Raavad's objection from one who is groaning. This is the conclusion of the Maggid Mishneh's words: "Furthermore, the act performed for him is done in its ordinary manner." This is unlike the case of one who is groaning. Although there too there is only pain, a Jew is nevertheless permitted to perform the rabbinic prohibition, because there it is performed in an unusual manner, whereas here it is performed in its ordinary manner. The Maggid Mishneh does not mention the case of the drainpipe at all. Although his explanation also implicitly resolves the objection from the drainpipe according to the Raavad's reading, "or if there is a great need, etc.," since the act with the drainpipe is likewise performed in an unusual manner, it is nevertheless impossible that the Maggid Mishneh had the reading, "or if there is a great need, etc." Had he read it that way, he would also have needed to resolve the objection from the drainpipe, and he should have mentioned it in his answer.
And in Hagahos Mordechai, etc. It writes concerning the drainpipe, etc. In the chapter Bameh Tomnin, Rabbeinu Baruch asks: Just as the Sages permitted a rabbinic prohibition in a case of financial loss, as with the drainpipe mentioned above and the other cases cited there, they should likewise have permitted one to tell a non-Jew to extinguish a fire. Yet the Mishnah teaches the opposite: when a non-Jew comes to extinguish a fire, one may not tell him, "Extinguish it," etc. He answers as follows: "The reason that all those cases were permitted in a situation of financial loss is that, if one does not permit the person to perform a minor prohibition, he may come to perform a major prohibition. With the drainpipe, if one does not permit him to crush the obstruction in an unusual manner, he may come to repair it in the ordinary manner." End quote.
And in Rivash, etc. Extinguishing is a melachah not needed for its own purpose. That is to say, according to Rabbi Shimon it is only a rabbinic prohibition. Nevertheless, the accepted halachah, without dispute, is that it is forbidden to instruct a non-Jew to extinguish a fire.
The Ran in the chapter Chavis likewise writes, according to one answer, etc. He explains why a rabbinic prohibition was permitted in the case of the drainpipe, whereas with a fire it is forbidden even to tell a non-Jew to extinguish it. He writes that the drainpipe is different because the act is a rabbinic prohibition performed in an unusual manner. He offers a further answer, that extinguishing is different, etc., because when the extinguishing is needed for its own purpose, namely to make charcoal or to singe the wicks, one would be liable according to the Torah.
Therefore, it appears to me, etc. According to Hagahos Mordechai, Rivash, and Ran, a rabbinic prohibition performed in its ordinary manner was not permitted even through a non-Jew, despite the fact that this is a double rabbinic prohibition, and even in a case of financial loss. This is likewise the opinion of Rashba in a responsum. Although the Rambam and Raavad are lenient, Magen Avraham does not consider it proper to rely upon them except in a case of substantial loss, and then only through a non-Jew. A Jew himself may perform the act only in an unusual manner.
