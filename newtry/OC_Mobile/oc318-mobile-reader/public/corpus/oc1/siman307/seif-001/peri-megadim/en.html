But per the Taz it appears the Rama's text was: but one who does not enjoy them is permitted to say them so his fellow may enjoy — and if so it should have said "even one who," etc. And in the Levush he emended to forbid telling them, as we emended before us (in the margin we marked "forbidden," which is not so, for before him z"l the text was "permitted to say them"). And the author of Beit Meir likewise wrote that the text is mistaken. But the Taz distinguished: if he tells only to fulfill his fellow's desire — forbidden; but if he intends that they should have pleasure — permitted. And it appears the Levush agreed with the Taz only when he has no pleasure himself and not that his fellows enjoy — only to fulfill their desire that they enjoy; certainly the teller has distress — forbidden. But if he has no distress and only acts so his fellow will enjoy — permitted. And I have thoughts on this matter, which I shall set forth, there's help:

Know that the prohibition of speech on Shabbat has three parts. (1) Speech that has no aspect of melacha, only that one's Shabbat speech not be like weekday speech — such as "I will do such-and-such tomorrow," etc., as Rambam wrote ch. 24 halacha 1 — they are not similar to melacha and do not lead to melacha, and are pursuing one's affairs if one speaks of them; these are explained in siman 306 and this siman, derived from Isaiah 58 "from pursuing your affairs and speaking a matter." Yet in Tosafot 150a s.v. "and speech" — "who forbids speech?" — their intent is that speech is certainly forbidden by several baraitot and mishnayot there: one may not hire workers, etc., and Rashi explained "from pursuing your affairs," etc., and they challenged — therefore he interpreted the verse; and he holds it is d'oraisa, as Tzitz Eliezer wrote siman 25 on oneg Shabbat (dealing with 242) that "for the mouth of Heaven has spoken" is like clothing the naked, etc., and they challenged: if so it is a mitzvah — why permitted? And they answered: in Torah speech your affairs are forbidden and heavenly matters permitted. And if one tells a gentile to do melacha for him — shevut di-rabbanan — from the verse it should be forbidden; one may say even telling him to light a candle to learn by him, which is heavenly affairs as below 276 and 476 s.k. 63, that in d'oraisa melacha it is forbidden to tell a gentile even for a mitzvah. And this is: what is forbidden for him to do today is forbidden to say "I will do tomorrow." All that has a side of permission through watchmen and the like is permitted in speech and occupation, as in seif 8 here and 306 seif 1; and even for a Torah prohibition one may say "I will go beyond three parsangs tomorrow," for techum outside is rabbinic — watchmen help only for rabbinic techum that does not apply by itself; and it deals with 253 s.k. 17 and is compelled in the Gemara by a fortiori to bring fruit — behold it is d'oraisa carrying, etc., and speech and occupation are equal — from this, even for d'oraisa, all that has permission is permitted. And if speech to one's fellow is forbidden, all the more to oneself — Magen Avraham letter 11 forbids even to oneself; only thought is permitted — so Tosafot 150a s.v. "what," see there; and it will be explained there. And since our sages said one may not multiply idle speech on Shabbat — so Rambam ch. 24 halacha 4 from Yerushalmi — and Tosafot 113b (Beit Yosef, Tur reconcile Rashi "nevertheless to speak" alone) — one may ask: idle speech unnecessary even on a weekday transgresses a positive commandment — "and you shall speak in them," not in idle matters; and women, who are not in Torah study, may speak idle matters; and the statement of R' Shimon bar Yochai "say: mother, today is Shabbat" — from the conclusion of poskim it appears this warning applies even to men; and for what Rabbenu Bechor wrote it is fine: idle speech to tell rumors that delight his fellow on a weekday is permitted, but on Shabbat since he has no pleasure — forbidden; see Yerushalmi and the commentator there, and there they said Shabbat has 35 shevut, 25 from speech. The second speech: telling a gentile — I explained that even for a mitzvah it is forbidden; and all that is permitted to tell one's fellow is permitted to a gentile — Magen Avraham letter 10. And if he tells him to do on Shabbat — there is rabbinic shevut lest he do it himself and appear as his agent and be liable — Rambam explained ch. 6, end; but if he told him to do melacha after Shabbat — there is only "pursuing your affairs and speaking a matter," and perhaps he is not liable for this; and in 306 we spoke of this. And even what could have permission through watchmen and eruv from reshut to reshut — nevertheless forbidden to say he should do on Shabbat, for they decreed — Magen Avraham letter 12. Shevut di-shevut in a place of mitzvah is permitted as 276. And so in 486 s.k. 21; one may say if outside the techum it is permitted to tell a gentile, since they derived it from the verse it is stricter than other shevut — and we gained this in 306 seif 9 forbidding a gentile to go outside the techum; and there it is a mitzvah matter — Magen Avraham there letter 17 — but from the conclusion of poskim it does not appear so. The third part: prohibition of telling an Israelite apostate — not the case before us; even an innkeeper who cooks for all travelers from his own food, and a guest lodged with him on Shabbat — there is no danger, for one may say shevut is permitted for illness without danger; and "before the blind" — since anyway he cooks many pots lest guests come, he also supports sinners — not two sides of the river. However, we wrote in siman 48 in Magen Avraham 1 that this matter is disputed in Rabbenu Bechor in Choshen Mishpat ch. 88 s.k. 15: if one told one who is warned to inform on the sender — the sender is liable, for "words are heard" applies here; and through his agency he is liable to stoning if possible — and for what the Levush wrote there it is gerama, therefore the sender is liable; and in Shach there he also challenged Rabbenu Bechor; and in Peticha Klalit we wrote from this; and it will be explained later on telling a gentile — here we cannot elaborate; it deals with 476 seif 1. And it will be explained in letter 4 below.
