It appears that the Rama's text read: “But one who does not derive pleasure from it may relate it so that his fellow will derive pleasure.” If so, however, it should have said, “Even one who,” etc. The Levush copied the text as, “It is forbidden to relate them,” as in the corrected reading before us. In the abbreviated gloss we noted “forbidden,” but this is incorrect, for the reading before the Levush was, “It is permitted to relate them.” Another authority likewise writes that the text is corrupt. The Taz, however, distinguishes as follows: if he relates the matters merely to satisfy his fellow's wishes, it is forbidden; but if his intention is that they derive pleasure, it is permitted. It appears that he agrees with the Levush only where the speaker himself has no satisfaction, nor is his purpose that his fellows derive pleasure, but merely to comply with their wish. Since relating the matter then causes the speaker distress, it is forbidden. If, however, he derives no satisfaction himself but relates it so that his fellow will derive pleasure, the speaker suffers no distress and it is permitted. See Eliyah Rabbah. In my humble opinion, this matter requires consideration, and with Hashem's help I shall set out my thoughts in detail.
Know that the prohibition of speaking on Shabbos has three categories. The first is speech that has no connection to melachah, but is prohibited because one's speech on Shabbos may not resemble one's weekday speech, such as saying, “Tomorrow I shall do such and such.” Rambam writes in chapter 24, halachah 1, that these matters neither resemble melachah nor lead to melachah, but concern attending to one's affairs and speaking about them. They are discussed in siman 306 and in this siman, and the Sages linked them to the verse in Yeshayahu 58, “refraining from pursuing your affairs and speaking of matters.” Tosafot on Shabbos 150a, s.v. “But is speech forbidden,” appears to ask whether speech is forbidden according to the Torah. Their intent is that speech is certainly forbidden, as is evident from several baraisos and mishnayos there, including the rule that one may not hire workers. Rashi explains this from “refraining from pursuing your affairs.” Tosafot therefore understand that the verse itself teaches the prohibition and that it is according to the Torah, as Tzemach Tzedek writes in siman 25 concerning oneg Shabbos, see siman 242, because the verse concludes, “for the mouth of Hashem has spoken.” Tosafot ask that, if so, why is speech for a mitzvah permitted? They answer that the Torah itself interprets the verse: “your affairs” are forbidden, while affairs of Heaven are permitted, both in action and in speech. One might ask that instructing a non-Jew to perform melachah is only a D'Rabbanan prohibition, whereas it should already be forbidden according to the Torah by “speaking of matters.” The answer is that this distinction applies even when one tells him to light a lamp so that one can learn by its light, which is an affair of Heaven. Nevertheless, as stated below in seif 5 and in siman 276, seif 3, one may not instruct a non-Jew to perform a melachah prohibited according to the Torah even for a mitzvah. The principle of weekday speech is that whatever one may not do today, one may not say that he will do tomorrow. Where there is some possible permitted manner, however, such as by means of guard huts or the like, speech and involvement are permitted, as stated in seif 8 here and siman 306, seif 1. This applies even where the act would otherwise involve a Torah prohibition. One may say, “Tomorrow I shall travel beyond three parsahs.” Do not say that guard huts are effective only regarding a D'Rabbanan techum, for this is incorrect. See Beit Yosef and simanim 263 and 317. The Gemara's kal vachomer concerning bringing produce likewise shows that even regarding a Torah prohibition, speech is permitted whenever a permitted manner is conceivable. Is only speech addressed to another prohibited, or is speaking to oneself also forbidden? See Magen Avraham, seif katan 11, who rules that even speaking privately to oneself is forbidden; only thought is permitted. Tosafot on Shabbos 150a, s.v. “What,” write the same, for otherwise the Gemara's question would not arise. Our Sages also said that one may not engage excessively in idle conversation on Shabbos. Rambam writes this in chapter 24, halachah 4, based on the Yerushalmi, and Tosafot cite it on Shabbos 113b. This raises a difficulty. Idle speech that serves no purpose is forbidden even during the week, for whoever engages in it violates the positive commandment of “you shall speak of them,” meaning words of Torah and not idle matters. Why, then, did the Sages specifically prohibit excessive idle conversation on Shabbos? One might answer that women, who are not obligated in Torah study, may engage in idle conversation. Yet Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai's statement, “My mother, today is Shabbos,” and the unqualified language of the poskim imply that this warning applies to men as well. According to the Rama's ruling, however, the matter is clear. During the week one may relate reports from which one's fellow derives pleasure. On Shabbos, when the speaker himself derives no pleasure from relating them, it is forbidden. See the Yerushalmi and its commentary. The Yerushalmi states that Shabbos contains thirty-five forms of shevus, twenty-five of which arise from speech. The second category is instructing a non-Jew. I have explained that this is forbidden even for a mitzvah. Whatever one may tell another Jew, one may also tell a non-Jew; see Magen Avraham, seif katan 10. If one tells the non-Jew to perform the act on Shabbos, this involves a D'Rabbanan shevus, lest one come to perform it himself, and because the non-Jew appears to act as his agent. One is liable to disciplinary lashes, as Rambam writes at the beginning of chapter 6 of Hilchos Shabbos. If, however, one tells him to perform the melachah after Shabbos, the prohibition is only that of “refraining from pursuing your affairs and speaking of matters,” and it is possible that disciplinary lashes are not imposed. We discussed this in siman 306. Even where a permitted manner could theoretically be created through guard huts or an eruv, it is nevertheless forbidden to tell a non-Jew to perform the act on Shabbos, because the decree still applies, as Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 12. A double shevus is permitted for a mitzvah, as stated in seif 5 and siman 586, seif 21. One might suggest that instructing a non-Jew to go beyond the techum is forbidden even for a mitzvah because the prohibition was linked to a verse and is therefore more stringent than other forms of shevus. This would explain siman 306, seif 9, which forbids instructing a non-Jew to go beyond the techum even though the matter concerns a mitzvah; see Magen Avraham there, seif katan 17. The general language of the poskim, however, does not support this distinction. The third category is instructing a Jewish apostate, who is treated like a non-Jew in this regard. The issue is not limited to the prohibition of placing a stumbling block before the blind. Consider an innkeeper who cooks his own food for travelers and happens to host on Shabbos a mildly ill person whose condition is not dangerous. One might argue that instructing a non-Jew is permitted for a sick person who is not dangerously ill, and that placing a stumbling block before the blind does not apply because the innkeeper cooks several pots in any event for possible guests. Nevertheless, in siman 304, on Magen Avraham seif katan 1, we wrote that the matter depends upon a dispute. According to the Rama in Choshen Mishpat 388:15, if one instructs a person known to act as an informer, the sender is liable, because the principle, “Whose words should be obeyed, the Master's or the disciple's?” does not apply to one habitually prepared to sin. All the more so here, where he acts at the sender's instruction, and it is possible that the sender would be liable to stoning. According to the Levush, however, the liability there is based on indirect causation because the resulting damage is certain, which would not establish liability for the sender here. The Shach there also questions the Rama. We discussed this in the general introduction, and with Hashem's help the laws of instructing a non-Jew will be explained further below.
