(Seif katan 12) Outside the Shabbos boundary, but within the boundary, etc. The Bach asks why the ruling specifies produce outside the Shabbos boundary. Even within the boundary it should be forbidden, since the produce may not be moved and therefore cannot be brought. He answers: “It may be said that within the boundary, since one may wait near nightfall for produce that is still attached to the ground, as stated in seif 9, even though he may not detach it and bring it, he may likewise speak to his fellow about it.” His intent is that Abba Shaul stated a general rule in the Mishnah: anything that I am permitted to speak about, meaning that I may speak of that matter, I am permitted to wait near nightfall for. The converse is likewise true: anything for which one may wait near nightfall may also be spoken about. Since one may wait near nightfall for muktzeh produce or produce attached to the ground when it is within the Shabbos boundary, one may likewise tell another person to detach the produce after Shabbos. However, the general rule that “anything that I am permitted to speak about, etc.,” together with its converse as stated above, applies to a matter whose only prohibition is its distance, because it lies at the end of the Shabbos boundary. It does not apply to something prohibited even within the boundary because it is muktzeh or attached to the ground. Although one may wait near nightfall for such an item, speaking about performing the prohibited act remains forbidden. It therefore appears to me that the text specifies produce outside the Shabbos boundary not to imply that produce within the boundary is permitted, for even within the boundary it is forbidden. Rather, it uses this wording because of the latter clause, which states that it is likewise forbidden to wait at the end of the Shabbos boundary, etc. There, the restriction applies specifically to something beyond the boundary, whereas within the boundary it is permitted, as stated in seif 9. The first clause therefore also speaks of produce outside the boundary.
It is obvious that even regarding an act, etc. It is forbidden to tell a non-Jew to perform it on Shabbos. In a case of this kind, permission was granted only to tell the non-Jew to perform it after Shabbos. The proof is from siman 325, seifim 8 and 10. If a non-Jew brought something from beyond the Shabbos boundary for a Jew, that Jew may not benefit from it. This is so even though a Jew could theoretically overcome the prohibition of the Shabbos boundary by means of guard huts, and the carrying itself could theoretically be accomplished by means of partitions. Nevertheless, even after the fact the item is forbidden to the Jew for whom it was brought, although he did not instruct the non-Jew to bring it. All the more so is it forbidden for a Jew to instruct the non-Jew to do so.
The words of the Bach require extensive study; likewise, what he writes that by means of a human partition, etc. Beit Yosef cites Hagahos Mordechai in the name of Raavyah, who writes that instructing a non-Jew is forbidden even regarding a Torah prohibition only where no permitted method can be found for a Jew to accomplish it, such as heating water. But where a permitted method is possible, as in a public domain where the item could be brought by means of a human partition, meaning that each person stands within three handbreadths of the next, thereby forming a valid partition, instructing a non-Jew is permitted. Where the matter is forbidden lest one confuse it with a public domain, instructing a non-Jew is forbidden even regarding a rabbinic prohibition, etc. Beit Yosef comments that the latter clause contradicts what was written immediately beforehand, that where the act is possible by a permitted method, such as bringing through a public domain by means of a human partition, instructing a non-Jew is permitted. This requires study. The Bach reconciles the passage as follows. The first clause discusses telling a non-Jew to carry an item through a public domain, even for a distance of one hundred cubits. This is permitted because a Jew has a permitted method of transporting it through the public domain, namely, if a human partition were present. Therefore, the non-Jew may bring it even when no human partition is actually present. The Bach adduces proof from the ruling in seif 8 that one may say to his fellow, “Tomorrow I am going to such-and-such a city,” since, were there guard huts, etc. Tosafot write that the same applies to telling a non-Jew on Shabbos to go to such-and-such a city. The same reasoning should apply here. The latter clause, however, discusses telling a non-Jew to carry from one courtyard to another when they did not make an eruv. A Jew is forbidden to do so lest he confuse it with a public domain and carry from a private domain into a public domain. In that case, a human partition provides no remedy, for what does the partition accomplish when the courtyards did not join in an eruv? Even where two courtyards adjoin one another and share a wall, and there is a window in the wall, if the two courtyards did not make a joint eruv, it is forbidden to carry from one to the other through the window. In such a case, instructing a non-Jew is likewise forbidden. This is the substance of the Bach’s words, with added explanation. Magen Avraham disputes his entire argument. The Bach’s assertion in the first clause, that whenever a Jew could act permissibly by means of a human partition one may tell a non-Jew to perform the act on Shabbos itself, is incorrect. Speech was permitted only when the non-Jew is to act after Shabbos, not when he is to act on Shabbos. This is demonstrated by the ruling in siman 325, as explained above.
Likewise, what he writes that by means of partitions, etc. That is, even according to the Bach’s own approach, his explanation of the concluding words of Hagahos Mordechai is incorrect. The Bach explains that they discuss a case in which it is forbidden to tell a non-Jew to carry from one courtyard to another when no eruv was made, because a Jew may not do so even by means of a human partition. Magen Avraham disputes this as well. Granted that in such a case a human partition does not permit the Jew to carry, instructing a non-Jew should nevertheless be permitted, because the prohibition against carrying from one courtyard to another without an eruv exists lest one confuse it with a public domain. Yet according to the Bach, instructing a non-Jew is permitted even in an actual public domain. One must therefore say that the Bach assumes that even with a human partition, a Jew may not carry from a private domain into a public domain enclosed by that human partition, and therefore instructing a non-Jew is also forbidden. Magen Avraham rejects this, because a human partition is a fully valid partition. “And it is explicit in the Gemara, etc., ‘Bring us this item tomorrow,’ etc.” A formulation of this kind is permitted because, were there a human partition, the act could be performed; but permission was not granted for the non-Jew to bring it on Shabbos itself, as explained above.
Likewise, what he writes in the name of Tosafot, etc. The Bach adduces proof for his first ruling, that one may tell a non-Jew to bring an item through a public domain even on Shabbos, from Tosafot. He understands Tosafot as commenting on the ruling in seif 8 that one may say to his fellow, “Tomorrow I am going to such-and-such a city,” etc., and as stating that the same applies to saying this to a non-Jew, as explained above. Magen Avraham writes that this is incorrect. Tosafot did not make their statement regarding the ruling in seif 8, but regarding the ruling in seif 7 that one may say to his fellow, “Do what seems proper in your eyes,” etc., as Magen Avraham cites from Tosafot in seif katan 10.
