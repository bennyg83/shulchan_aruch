(Seif katan 11) To such-and-such a city, etc. For otherwise, why does the Gemara say that the ruling is obvious? That is, why was it necessary to give the reason that there might be guard huts extending the permitted walking distance? It should be permitted simply because his words accomplish nothing, and the permission would be obvious.
This implies that without this it is permitted. Beit Yosef writes that the prohibition applies when one says this to another person so that he will prepare a horse for him, etc.; without that, it is permitted, because his words accomplish nothing. Beit Yosef derives this from the wording of the Rokeach, who forbids it because one cannot hire on Shabbos. What connection does the inability to hire have to the prohibition against saying, “I will ride tomorrow”? It must be that the statement by itself is in fact permitted, since nothing is accomplished through saying it. The prohibition lies only in his telling the other person to prepare a horse for him. It therefore follows from the Rokeach and Beit Yosef that the bare statement is permitted, provided that he is not telling another person to perform after Shabbos an act that is forbidden on Shabbos. It is possible that the Maggid Mishneh also agrees. His question, “What is it teaching us? It is obvious,” does not mean that in such a case there is no need for the rationale of possible guard huts. Rather, even this case requires that rationale, in accordance with the Rosh. His point is that it is obvious that possible guard huts suffice in such a case, and the Mishnah did not need to teach it. It must therefore be teaching that even when one says to his fellow, “Come with me,” so that his words do accomplish something, it is nevertheless permitted because of the possibility of guard huts. Even so, the Maggid Mishneh may agree that when one merely says regarding himself, “Tomorrow I will perform such-and-such an act,” without asking another person to do anything for him, it is still forbidden unless there is some manner in which the act could be permitted today, such as by means of guard huts or the like. See Tosafot. That is, Tosafot likewise imply that even saying, “Tomorrow I will perform such-and-such an act,” is forbidden. First, Tosafot on Shabbos 150a, s.v. “What difference is there,” address the Gemara’s question on the Mishnah: “A person may not hire workers on Shabbos, nor may he tell his fellow to hire workers for him.” The Gemara asks: What difference is there between himself and his fellow? Rashi explains that his fellow is also a Jew like himself and therefore cannot hire them. Tosafot ask: “Why not say that ‘his fellow’ means that he tells him, ‘Hire workers for me tomorrow’?” They answer: “If so, why mention his fellow? Even he himself may not say, ‘I will hire workers tomorrow.’ The statement that one may say, etc., ‘I am going tomorrow to such-and-such a city,’ applies only because, were there guard huts, etc.” Tosafot write the same explicitly there on 150b, s.v. “But one may wait at the edge of the Shabbos boundary,” as follows: “I know of such-and-such a place, etc. One might have thought that it is permitted to speak about one’s own plans, even to say that he will hire workers tomorrow, etc., because his words accomplish nothing. But according to Shmuel this cannot be so, for all such statements are forbidden, even concerning oneself, except where the act could be performed today, such as where there might be guard huts, etc.”
