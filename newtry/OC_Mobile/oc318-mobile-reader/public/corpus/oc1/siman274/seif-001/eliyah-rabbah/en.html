Two loaves, etc. If one cannot obtain two loaves, one whole loaf suffices. Kol Bo at the end of siman 31 cites the Mechilta that on Erev Shabbos the manna fell as two omers; from one omer they made two loaves, one for Friday morning and the second for Friday night, then the third for Shabbos morning and the fourth for seudah shelishit. In my humble opinion this is not a proof for one loaf at each meal, because perhaps Friday night and Shabbos morning they placed two loaves before them but broke only one. Only seudah shelishit, when one loaf remained, proves the point, as the Tur writes in siman 291. Still, according to the view that all three Shabbos meals are equal, it may be a proof. Shelah cites Kanfei Yonah that one should prepare twenty-six loaves corresponding to Hashem's Name: twelve Friday night, eight by day, four at Minchah, and two after Shabbos. At the three Shabbos meals one holds two and after Shabbos one. He gives mystical formulas and intentions. Therefore no one should do this unless expert in Kabbalah. There is no hint to it in the Gemara, chapter Kol Kitvei, and the Mechilta cited above does not imply it. Agudah at the end of Arvei Pesachim writes that two loaves are also used on Yom Tov. He asks that in VeAtah Kidashta we say Shabbos was "blessed above all days and sanctified above all times," and explains that perhaps manna actually fell on Yom Tov and its blessing refers to the special taste of the food. Rabbi David Abudarham explains that the blessing and sanctification of Shabbos refer to those who observe it, who are blessed and sanctified. Ibn Ezra explains blessing as added goodness, renewed strength and intellect in the body. I explained blessing as the fact that one lacks nothing even while refraining from work on Shabbos.
[Levush] In remembrance of the manna, etc. Therefore women too are obligated in lechem mishneh, because they too participated in the miracle of the manna, as Abudarham writes in his introduction.
In his hand, etc. Tashbetz siman 18 says that he holds the two loaves in both hands.
And he breaks, etc. Magen Avraham discusses this. Maharshal, Mateh Moshe, and Shibbolei HaLeket customarily broke both loaves. Perishah writes that this practice was specifically at the daytime meal, because the honor of the day exceeds that of the night. Nevertheless, one who follows our custom to break only one does properly, as appears from Rav Hai Gaon cited by Beit Yosef and Mateh Moshe.
