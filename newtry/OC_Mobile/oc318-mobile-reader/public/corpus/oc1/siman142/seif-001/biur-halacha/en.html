We make him repeat - See Mishnah Berurah. This is from Chayei Adam according to the view of the Shulchan Aruch. Although Responsa Terumas HaDeshen, siman 181, is not entirely certain about this, for he writes there only that it appears somewhat so, nevertheless the plain meaning of the phrase “we make him repeat” supports this conclusion. Accordingly, if they already completed the reading and afterward remembered that they erred in a word in a way that changes the meaning, then according to the Shulchan Aruch they would need to return and read that verse together with two additional verses, with a blessing before and after, just as where a verse or word was omitted, as in siman 282. Chayei Adam writes, however, that regarding the blessing we rely upon those poskim who hold that even where the meaning changed, one need not repeat after the fact. Although it is not truly clear that the Tur or the other lenient authorities hold that even where the meaning changed one need not repeat, as may be seen in the commentaries to the Shulchan Aruch, nevertheless in practice one should follow Chayei Adam. In any event, it is not clear that according to Rambam, who rules that we make him repeat, he would hold that even after the entire reading was completed one must return. Perhaps he means only that while one is still engaged in those verses, he should repeat them, but not that he has failed to fulfill the obligation even after the fact. Although Terumas HaDeshen writes that it appears somewhat that he has not fulfilled the obligation, that implication is insufficient grounds to repeat the reading with blessings. Know that it would appear that this ruling of the Shulchan Aruch applies specifically on Shabbos. On weekdays or at Shabbos Minchah, where we rule in siman 137 that even if one omitted an entire verse he does not repeat, the same should apply here. It is possible, however, that this is worse. The Yerushalmi in Megillah, chapter HaKorei Omed, halachah 1, states: Rabbi said, although you say that the Targum is not indispensable, nevertheless if the translator erred, meaning that he translated improperly, we make him repeat. Perhaps the same applies here: if he read words with different letters and thereby changed the meaning, it is more severe. Alternatively, there the translator publicly presented an incorrect explanation of the Torah, which could lead to harm, and therefore they required repetition. This requires study.
But if he erred, etc. - It is obvious that this applies only to an error in a letter. If he omitted an entire word, even if the meaning did not change, such as reading “ki hichbadti” instead of “ki ani hichbadti,” he has not fulfilled the obligation and must repeat, for nowhere do we find a distinction regarding an omitted word between a case where the meaning changed and one where it did not.
We do not make him repeat - Biur HaGra questions this even where the meaning does not change, such as Mitzrayim and Mitzri’im, and similarly where one erred between im and ve’im, for in his view one must repeat. Peri Chadash likewise rules that even if he read Haran instead of Aharon, with a patach but omitting the alef, or Mitzri’im instead of Mitzrayim, where the meaning does not change, he must repeat. He concludes, however, that if the error was not in the letters but only in the vowels or cantillation, and the meaning did not change at all, we do not make him repeat, though we rebuke him. It is reasonable that the Gra also agrees to this. Derech HaChayim went too far and ruled that even where the meaning changed, one who follows the lenient practice not to repeat should not be protested against, citing Eliyah Rabbah. It is not reasonable to be so lenient in a matter where, according to the basic law, one must repeat in every case, as the Gra demonstrated. It is sufficient for us to be lenient only to the extent that Rama was lenient.
