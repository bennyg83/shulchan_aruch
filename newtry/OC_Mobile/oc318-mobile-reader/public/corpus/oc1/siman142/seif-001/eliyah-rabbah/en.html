Even in pronunciation, etc. Hagahot Maimoniyot writes that the reader must not treat a mobile sheva as silent or a silent sheva as mobile, must not weaken a strong consonant, and must not strengthen a weak one. Maharam Mintz, siman 81, writes that the chazzan must study the weekly reading so thoroughly that all its grammatical details, accents, stress, and pronunciation are fluent in his mouth. He should read very deliberately and not quickly, lest he swallow a letter or word. On Shavuot the Ten Commandments should be read with the upper cantillation, which marks the divisions of the commandments rather than the ordinary beginnings and ends of verses. The first commandment begins with 'I am' and continues through 'those who keep My commandments,' all as one commandment; 'I am' and 'You shall have no other' were heard directly from the Almighty. The next verse begins 'You shall not take,' and because it is treated as one verse it has one melody. Certain vowels and consonantal strengths are read according to this cantillation. On an ordinary Shabbos in Parashat Yitro or VaEtchanan, however, the lower cantillation is used, marking the regular beginnings and endings of verses, and the vowels and consonants are read accordingly. This is the substance of Responsa Masat Binyamin, siman 6, which discusses the matter at great length. See Chizkuni on Parashat Yitro, Or Torah, and Magen Avraham siman 494. Responsa Chut HaShani, siman 42, relates an incident in which someone recited the blessing over the Torah, and before he began reading an error was found in that Sefer Torah. Another Sefer Torah was brought out, and the author ruled that the blessing must be repeated. His principal proof is from the Beit Yosef and Levush at the end of siman 142, who write that where two Sifrei Torah were already brought out together they are considered as though both were before him, implying that if the second had not yet been brought out a new blessing would be required. This contradicts the Taz in Yoreh Deah siman 279, who says no new blessing is required because at the time of the blessing the person presumably intended that if the first Sefer Torah proved invalid, the blessing should apply to the second. In my humble opinion there is no contradiction. The Beit Yosef and Levush discuss two different parashiyot, whereas here the subject is the same parashah. He intended to read this parashah, so what difference is there whether it is read from this Sefer Torah or another? Presumably his intent was to read it from whichever valid scroll was available. Even one who is lenient in the case of a lupine over which one recited a blessing and which then fell from his hand may agree here. There he could choose not to eat another lupine; here he has a mitzvah to read this parashah and certainly intends that if one scroll is invalid he will read from another. Moreover, one lupine may differ from another in desirability, whereas one Sefer Torah is equivalent to another for this reading. This refutes all of Chut HaShani's arguments; see there.

Responsa Beis Yaakov, siman 76, expresses astonishment at Rama, who derived this essential distinction, whether the meaning changed, from Piskei Terumas HaDeshen, siman 181, whereas it is explicit there that even when the meaning changes we do not make him repeat, for he writes that one who read Haran instead of Aharon has fulfilled his obligation. This is no difficulty, for Beit Yosef, citing Mahari Ibn Chaviv, writes that Aharon and Haran do not constitute a change in meaning. We must therefore say that Rama derived this distinction from Beit Yosef, who states it explicitly. His citation also to Piskei Terumas HaDeshen refers only to the ruling that one rebukes him even where the meaning did not change. I have seen, however, that Bach likewise rules that for any error we do not make him repeat. I therefore say that one should not protest against someone who does not repeat.
