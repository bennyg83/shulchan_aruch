1. Seif 1: One must be exact in writing the letters so that the form of none of them changes, etc. Regarding the forms of the letters, the Mechaber in Beit Yosef cites the booklet of Rabbi Yitzchak Baruch She'amar and other poskim. Know that some of the forms illustrated there follow Ashkenazic script, and there are differences between Sephardic and Ashkenazic writing. Noda BiYehudah, Yoreh Deah, siman 80, likewise writes that Sephardic script is distinct. See LeDavid Emes, siman 13, letter 3. Therefore, for each letter, after briefly citing Beit Yosef in the name of the earlier and later versions of Baruch She'amar, we shall give the Sephardic form as recorded by Mor U'Ketziah and cited by LeDavid Emes in that siman.
Alef. Its upper point should resemble a yod, with a small projection upon it. Its face and projection should turn upward. The leg of the yod should attach to the roof of the body in the middle of the roof, and the end of the roof should curve slightly upward behind it. The lower point should be distant from the head of the body by one and one-half pen-widths. The lower point should have a small projection below on the right, because its form is like a yod suspended by its crown from the body of the alef. The left projection of the upper point should align with the right projection of the lower point. Beit Yosef in the name of Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, the upper point of the alef is not a complete yod. Its head slopes and rises, and its leg emerges downward near its right side, though not from its very end, for its roof extends slightly behind it. It also does not touch the body at the exact center, but somewhat below the center. The lower point has no projection on its right, but it has a projection on the left emerging downward from the curve. LeDavid Emes there. According to the teaching of the Arizal in Shaar HaKavanos, discourse 2 concerning tefillin, all the alefs in tefillin should have the upper point in the form of a yod and the lower point in the form of a dalet.
If the upper yod was written reversed, then if it can easily be repaired, it should be repaired. Keses HaSofer, siman 5, concerning the form of the alef. This implies that if it cannot be repaired, it may remain as it is, where the matter concerns tefillin. Emes LeYaakov, general rule 5, letter 4, writes likewise. Pischei Teshuvah, siman 274, letter 5, cites Peri Megadim Avraham, part 1, siman 66, that if the alefs in a Sefer Torah were written with reversed yods, the scroll should not be invalidated for this reason. The same applies to shins and tzadis written with reversed yods. See there. Responsa Noda BiYehudah, second edition, Yoreh Deah, siman 171, rules likewise. See Emes LeYaakov there.
If the components of the alef were made like plain lines, the letter is invalid. It may not be repaired in a Sefer Torah, tefillin, or mezuzah because that would be writing out of order. The same applies to every similar case in which the components of letters must have distinct heads but were instead made like plain lines. Emes LeYaakov there. See what we wrote in siman 32, letter 106.
Beis. It should be square and should have a sharp projection above on the right, leaning toward the alef, and a thick heel below. It should also have a small staff above on the left side of its face, because its form is like a dalet within the throat of a vav. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, it has no sharp projection above on the right. Rather, it is somewhat rounded on the outside like a reish, but has an inner corner. Its leg attaches below upon its base near the right edge, though not at the very end, for the base extends beneath it slightly to the right by about the width of a pen. According to Radbaz in Magen David, its form is dalet-vav. LeDavid Emes there. Our custom here in Baghdad is not to make a small staff above on the left side of its face.
If the beis was made without a lower heel, meaning that the lower line does not protrude behind it, one should not invalidate it, provided it is not rounded. Pischei Teshuvah, siman 274, letter 5, in the name of Chasam Sofer, siman 265; see Emes LeYaakov there. If it appears like a kaf, it is invalid. If there is doubt, it should be shown to a child. Shulchan Aruch HaRav concerning the form of the beis; Keses HaSofer there; see Peri Megadim in the introduction to siman 32 concerning the form of kaf, and Keses HaSofer there, Lishkas HaSofer, letter 15.
Gimel. Its head and body should resemble a zayin. Its head should be thick, and the right leg should descend slightly lower than the left thigh. The thigh should extend thickly, at a slight angle and somewhat low. It should have three taggin upon its head. Baruch She'amar. Mor U'Ketziah writes that according to Magen David its form is vav-yod. For Sephardim its form accords with Baruch She'amar. LeDavid Emes there. The statement of Baruch She'amar that the thigh should extend thickly, slightly slanted, and somewhat low is explained as follows. They formerly wrote the bent nun with its lower base extending farther to the left than its head, sufficiently that if straightened it would become a final nun. They wrote the bent tzadi similarly, as explained below. If the nun or bent tzadi were written only as long as the other letters, then any adjacent letter would necessarily have to be spaced farther away above, as in this illustration: That is improper. The remedy is to make them longer, so that the next letter can also be placed close above, as in this illustration: They likewise formerly wrote the gimel with its left thigh extending farther left than its head, so that its form appeared like a bent nun, as Beit Yosef writes. It therefore also had to be made longer than the other letters. Our scribes, however, do not follow this practice. They follow Sefer HaEshkol and Rabbi Eliezer of Metz, as Keses HaSofer explains in Lishkas HaSofer, letter 7: the body is extended somewhat to the right, so it need not also extend to the left and need not be longer than the other letters.
Dalet. Its roof should be long and its leg short, for if the leg is longer than the roof it will resemble a final kaf. It should have a clear heel on the right; a sharp corner alone is not sufficient, because its form is like two closed vavs. The heel corresponds to the head of one vav, and the projection upon its face corresponds to the second vav. Its leg should descend at a slight angle opposite the gimel. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, the leg does not slant toward the right but is aligned with the roof. LeDavid Emes there.
Heh. It should resemble a dalet with a point suspended within it, like a dalet and yod. That point must not touch the roof above. The point should not be misshapen. It should be thin above, somewhat thicker below, and curve downward toward the right, but not toward the left lest it resemble a tav. The point should not be more than one pen-width from the roof, nor so close to the roof that an average person cannot distinguish the gap from a distance of four amos. The point should not stand opposite the middle of the roof, but on the left side opposite the end of the roof. A small staff should be made upon its face on the left side. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, the right leg is straight like that of a dalet, while in Ashkenazic script it extends slightly backward, also like a dalet. The suspended point is a short, straight line, somewhat thick in its middle and not curved at all. Its form is dalet-vav. Magen David and Matzas Shmurim give the form dalet-yod. The upper projection of the yod should be extremely thin, so that according to the mystical teaching it appears to touch while not actually touching. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there. If it was made like a reish with a point inside, it is valid. Emes LeYaakov, general rule 5, letter 4.
Regarding Beit Yosef's statement that the point should not be so close to the roof that an average person cannot distinguish the gap from four amos, Responsa Rema MiPano, siman 36, writes that this measure is not exact. Less is sufficient, provided an average person can clearly distinguish it while reading from a Sefer Torah lying upon the bimah. Magen Avraham, seif katan 1; Emes LeYaakov; LeDavid Emes; Beis Yosef Mechudash. After the fact, whenever it is separated from the roof, even by a hairbreadth, it is valid. It is invalid only when it actually touches. Bach.
If the roof of the heh was lengthened and its leg was written in the middle, it is invalid, specifically where the roof was lengthened excessively. A slight extension is not significant. If this heh is part of the Name Havayah, then even if it was lengthened excessively it should not be touched, because some authorities hold that even where the leg stands in the middle it is valid. See Magen Avraham, siman 32, seif katan 33. It appears to me that it should at least be shown to a child to determine whether it is read correctly. Keses HaSofer, siman 5, concerning heh. In other words, one should scrape away the left leg from where it was written and rewrite it at the end of the roof. Emes LeYaakov, LeDavid Emes, Beis Yosef Mechudash, Keses HaSofer, and Emes LeYaakov, general rule 5, letter 4. See Peri Megadim in the introduction to siman 32, at the end, concerning the form of heh. He writes that if the leg was made in the middle of the roof, one may scrape the roof until it aligns with the leg, and this is not engraving rather than writing. Emes LeYaakov there writes likewise in the name of Ezras LaSofer. See below concerning kof, under “If.”
If the heh was made with two inner legs, one may scrape away the middle leg even in a Divine Name. Emes LeYaakov there in the name of Mishnas Avraham. It may also be left as it is and is valid. Binyan Olam, siman 54, is uncertain about this, as cited in Mishnas Avraham and Emes LeYaakov. One should therefore be stringent.
If this heh was made with two legs on the right side, it is invalid because the form of the letter has changed. Likewise, if the heh was made like a vav with a point inside in place of a heh, so that it appears like a gimel, it is invalid. Emes LeYaakov there in the name of the cited authority. The left leg of the heh should be equal to the right leg, because Peri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, siman 32, letter 33, is uncertain whether to validate it when they are unequal. Repair would also constitute engraving rather than writing. The left leg should likewise not be far from the right leg and roof. Emes LeYaakov there.
Vav. It should be rounded on the right so that it not resemble a zayin. Although the head of a zayin projects on both sides, there is nevertheless concern that an undiscerning child might read it as a zayin and it would be invalid. Its head should be short so that it not resemble a reish, and its face should be level. If it were diagonal, it would not be complete script but broken cursive writing. Its leg should be long so that it not resemble a yod, and straight and even, not thin in the middle like broken script, but complete and whole. Its thickness should gradually diminish until it becomes pointed below. This alludes to a secret. Baruch She'amar.
The head of the vav should be somewhat slanted, with a projection upon it. According to Matzas Shmurim, the vav consists of body, head, and projection. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there. What is written in the name of Matzas Shmurim is likewise stated in Shaar HaKavanos in the cited discourse. This applies to tefillin but not to a Sefer Torah, as stated in Matzas Shmurim, page 31a, and Shaar HaKavanos. Shulchan Aruch HaRav and Keses HaSofer write likewise concerning the form of vav.
If the leg of the vav is as long as a final nun, even if its head is rounded, there is concern that a child may read it as a nun and it is invalid. Emes LeYaakov; LeDavid Emes; Beis Yosef Mechudash. Concerning the form of the shortened vav, see what we wrote in the laws of Sefer Torah, siman 274.
Zayin. Its head should be thick and square, projecting over its body on both sides. Its leg should not be long, lest it resemble a final nun. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, the head is not square but curves slightly upward on a diagonal. It projects over its body on both sides, but not equally, because the leg emerges on the right side near the end. In any event, it inclines from the center of the head toward the right. The upper beginning of the leg, where it attaches to the head, is very thin. It descends and becomes thicker, curves slightly backward, and then becomes thinner toward the front. The head of the zayin appears in the upper point of the alef together with its line. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes.
If the leg of the zayin was drawn from the side rather than the middle, but was then curved back toward the center in this manner: it is invalid. Chasam Sofer, Yoreh Deah, siman 269; Pischei Teshuvah, siman 274, letter 5; Emes LeYaakov there.
If it was made like a vav, even if taggin were added upon it, it is invalid. Shaar Ephraim, siman 81, cited in Pischei Teshuvah there and Emes LeYaakov.
Ches. Its two legs should be exactly like two zayins, not like a dalet and zayin. Shabbos states that if one removed the roof of a ches and made it into two zayins, and Menachos 29b says that expert scribes made the roof of the ches with a choter, meaning, “He lives at the height of the world.” Rabbeinu Tam explains choter as a camel's hump, meaning that the roof is raised in the middle. Rashi explains it as a staff on the left side. We follow both explanations. There should be one pen-width between the two zayins so that the ches is square, its length equal to its width. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, it is not formed from two zayins. Its roof is broad together with its right leg, like a reish, though some make it like a dalet. According to Magen David its form is dalet-vav or vav-dalet, meaning a reversed dalet. The left leg is a straight line, somewhat thick, with a thin head where it touches the roof above. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes. Our custom here in Baghdad is to form it from two zayins, as Beit Yosef writes in the name of Baruch She'amar.
What Shulchan Aruch HaRav and Keses HaSofer write, that according to the teaching of the Arizal the right leg should be like a vav in tefillin, and the section of the hump over the right leg should be thick while that over the left leg should be thin, is not mentioned at all in Shaar HaKavanos in the cited discourse that records the letter forms that differ in tefillin. This form is found only in Matzas Shmurim, page 31a, and Mishnas Chassidim, Tikkun Tefillin, chapter 1, letter 9. One should therefore not rely upon it, in accordance with Rashash in Nahar Shalom, page 60b, who writes that one should not rely upon kabbalistic works such as Chemdas Yamim and Mishnas Chassidim, but only upon the words of the Arizal and his disciple Rabbi Chaim Vital, upon whom the Rav placed his hand. See there, page 58a.
If one formed the ches from two zayins with a broad roof, meaning that the zayins were placed far apart and a broad roof was made over them, it is invalid because there is no hump. Magen Avraham, seif katan 3; Emes LeYaakov; LeDavid Emes; Keses HaSofer; Meleches Shamayim; Emes LeYaakov. Some permit it. Eliyah Rabbah, letter 22, in the name of Me'adanei Yom Tov; Chasam Sofer, Yoreh Deah, siman 258, cites this in the name of Me'adanei Yom Tov and Sefer Stam, Hilchos Tefillin, siman 25. In pressing circumstances, if no other Sefer Torah is available, one may rely upon Me'adanei Yom Tov and read from it. Chasam Sofer there. Likewise, another Sefer Torah should not be removed because of this. Keses HaSofer, Lishkas HaSofer, letter 11. Even according to the stringent authorities, this applies only if the roof was lengthened excessively. A slight extension is insignificant and the letter is valid. Emes LeYaakov there. Responsa Darkei Noam, Orach Chayim, siman 1, likewise implies this.
Likewise, if the ches was made like two dalets, or like a dalet and zayin, or like two vavs with a hump, some authorities invalidate it. In tefillin it cannot be repaired because of writing out of order. Others validate all forms of ches after the fact, and another Sefer Torah should not be removed because of them. Emes LeYaakov there in the name of Responsa Avodas HaGershuni, Zichron Yosef, Noda BiYehudah, first edition, Responsa Maggid MeReishis, siman 1, and Toldos Yaakov, cited in Mishnas Avraham. During the week all scrolls should be repaired. Emes LeYaakov there in the name of Noda BiYehudah, Keses HaSofer, LeDavid Emes, and Beis Aharon. Regarding tefillin, we cannot bring ourselves to invalidate them and consign Divine Names to genizah. I am therefore inclined to validate them after the fact. Emes LeYaakov there, citing Mishnas Avraham in the name of Be'er Mayim, siman 20.
If the ches was formed like a dalet and zayin, then if the hump reaches the middle of the line of the dalet, as in this illustration: one may, even in a Sefer Torah, tefillin, or mezuzah, scrape away a little from the middle of the dalet's line so that it becomes a zayin. This does not constitute engraving rather than writing. If, however, the hump reaches the beginning of the dalet, as in this illustration: one may not scrape the dalet. Even thickening the hump first toward the right over the dalet and afterward scraping in the middle is forbidden in tefillin and mezuzos. Emes LeYaakov there.
If the ches was made according to Rashi's view, it is valid. Beit Yosef in the name of a manuscript source; Magen Avraham there; Emes LeYaakov there; LeDavid Emes there; and Emes LeYaakov there. He should merely add a tag at the end on the left side, to indicate the Sages' intention that the ches lives at the height of the world. Shulchan Aruch; Emes LeYaakov there. If he did not make a tag on the left side, Peri Megadim, in the introduction to siman 32 concerning the form of ches, remains uncertain. Emes LeYaakov rules decisively that it is forbidden. Beit Yosef in the name of the manuscript source, however, implies that this is only an initial requirement. Rabbi Akiva Eiger likewise proves clearly that every aspect of the hump is only for preferred fulfillment and does not invalidate after the fact. If it can easily be repaired, however, it should be repaired, for that is treated like an initial case. Keses HaSofer cites this in Lishkas HaSofer, letter 10. Even according to the stringent authorities, it appears that in tefillin and mezuzos, if the tag was not made during the original writing, it may be added afterward without violating the requirement of writing in order, just like the point of the yod, because a child already reads it as a ches. See below concerning the form of yod.
A ches according to Rashi's view remains valid after the fact even if it was lengthened. Although it is not square, Magen Avraham there, Eshel Avraham there, Shulchan Aruch HaRav there, Emes LeYaakov there, LeDavid Emes there, Chasam Sofer there, and Keses HaSofer there all validate it. Although some invalidate a Rashi-form ches when it was lengthened, as Eliyah Rabbah and those who follow him, cited by Emes LeYaakov, the lenient view is primary because it has the majority of authorities. This is also our custom.
If a Sefer Torah contains some ches letters with a hump and others without a hump, this is not an internal contradiction, because even according to Rashi a ches with a hump is valid. Chasam Sofer there; Keses HaSofer there in Lishkas HaSofer, letter 10.
If a Sefer Torah contains a Rabbeinu Tam ches with a broad staff, one may not erase it and write in its place a broad Rashi-form ches, because that would be a reduction in sanctity. Chasam Sofer there; Emes LeYaakov there.
If ches letters are found in a Sefer Torah shaped like a reversed nun on the right and a bent nun on the left, with the hump above them in this form: they should be invalidated. Nevertheless, even in this case another Sefer Torah should not be removed. Keses HaSofer there in Lishkas HaSofer, letter 12; Imrei Shefer there.
If one made a hump upon other letters, such as a heh, tav, closed mem, or other letters, they should not be validated in that form. It nevertheless appears that they may be repaired even in tefillin and mezuzos, and this does not violate writing in order or constitute engraving rather than writing, because the hump is superfluous and anything superfluous is regarded as though removed. Imrei Shefer there.
Regarding the form of the hump, some make it like this: and some make it like this: Eliyah Rabbah, letter 9, in the name of Me'adanei Yom Tov. Eliyah Rabbah considers the first form primary, but Chasam Sofer, Yoreh Deah, siman 271, favors the second form. This is also our custom.
If one wrote a Rashi-form ches without a hump and afterward wishes to make a humped ches in that place, he may not scrape away the middle of the roof, thereby creating two zayins, and then make a hump over them. Emes LeYaakov there and general rule 11, letter 1. See what we wrote above in siman 32, letters 80 and 28.
Tes. Its right head should be somewhat long, because its form is like a kaf and zayin. Its head should incline slightly downward, since its body also extends beneath it diagonally. This improves the appearance of the kaf. The head should also be rounded above and unsuitable for crowns. If the first head were level and suitable for taggin like the second head, they would not pass over it to place the taggin upon the second head, just as one does not pass over mitzvos. For this reason, the yod-like heads on the right side of ayin, tzadi, and shin all face upward, while the point at the face of the head bends slightly inward. It should not be bent excessively. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, the right head descends inward and slopes greatly. Magen David gives two forms: kaf-zayin, or a reversed reish-nun. Its roof is low and sunken, as though broken. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there.
What Shulchan Aruch HaRav and Keses HaSofer write, that according to the tradition of the Arizal the left head of the tes should be a straight vav in tefillin, follows Matzas Shmurim, page 31a. According to Rabbi Chaim Vital in Shaar HaKavanos in the cited discourse, however, even in tefillin it should be like a zayin. One should not depart from his words, as explained above.
If he did not bend the point at the face of the right head inward, or did not make the left head like a zayin, it remains valid. It may be repaired afterward without violating the requirement of writing in order. Peri Megadim, introduction to siman 32, concerning the form of tes.
Yod. Its head and face should be level so that it appears as formal writing and not cursive script. It should have a small right leg, not a large one lest it resemble a reish. The leg should be curved to improve its form. It should have a tag upon its face and a small point descending below, as the Gemara says that even the point of a yod is indispensable. Rashi explains this as the right leg, which bends downward. Tosafos object that this is obvious, and Rabbeinu Tam therefore explains it as the left head of the yod bent downward. Rashi certainly does not disagree that the left head should bend downward. The Pesikta says: just as a yod has one point below, alluding to the wicked who descend, and one point above, alluding to their future ascent. This implies a point below and a corresponding tag above. The lower point should be shorter than the right leg lest it resemble a ches. For the same reason the leg of the yod should be bent. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, its head and face are not level. From its midpoint onward the head curves upward with some thickness until it narrows and forms a tag extending from itself. No point descends opposite it. As a general rule, all letter-heads resembling a yod are alike in this respect, such as the yod upon an alef, the heads of zayin, tes, lamed, ayin, tzadi, and shin. All these initial heads resemble a yod. The legs emerging from these heads do not extend from the middle of the yod-shaped heads, but mostly from their right side near the end. Some scribes draw them from the center, except for the left head of the tzadi, which differs from all of these. Matzas Shmurim writes that a yod has three points, agreeing with Baruch She'amar. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there. What is written in the name of Matzas Shmurim, that a yod has three points, is also stated in Zohar, Vayikra 11b, and in Shaar HaKavanos: “All yods should have three points, one above, one in the middle, and one below.” Ginas Veradim, general rule 2, siman 11, explains: one above is the upper point; one in the middle is the point extending downward from the upper point; and one below is the leg of the yod. Since the other points were already mentioned, it makes no difference whether the lower feature is called a point or a leg. Mishnas Chassidim, Tikkun Tefillin, chapter 1, letter 10, explains that the three points are the upper point, the body, and the leg. See there, letter 9, and chapter 2, letter 1. His view is implied by Zohar Chadash, pages 120 and 121a. Zohar, Vayikra 10b, however, implies like Ginas Veradim.
If one wrote another letter first and only afterward made the left point of the yod, Beit Yosef cites Agur that this is writing out of order and the text must be placed in genizah. In the name of Ri'as, however, he writes that in tefillin and mezuzos, failure to make that point does not invalidate because the letter still bears the name yod without the left point. The right leg is different: if it was not made initially, it cannot be added afterward in tefillin or mezuzos because the letter is not called a yod without that leg, and the repair would be writing out of order. The later authorities accordingly rule that if the left point of a yod was not made initially, it may be repaired afterward in tefillin and mezuzos without concern for writing out of order. Taz, seif katan 1, writes that the Mechaber's view is unlike Agur. Magen Avraham, siman 32, seif katan 36; Eliyah Rabbah, letter 20; Peri Megadim, Mishbetzos Zahav, letter 1, and introduction to siman 32 concerning the form of yod; Responsa Beis Yaakov, siman 26; Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Keses HaSofer, siman 9, letter 5; Emes LeYaakov there.
If he wrote a yod without Rabbeinu Tam's left point and afterward wrote the next letter, it is obvious that according to the stringent view this is writing out of order. The lenient ruling that one may repair it afterward only demonstrates that the letter already bears the name yod; all the more so, this means that writing subsequent letters creates a writing-order problem according to the stringent view. Peri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, beginning of siman 32.
If during Torah reading on Shabbos a yod is found lacking both of these points, another Sefer Torah should not be removed. The reading should be completed with the regular blessings, and after Shabbos the letter should be repaired. Emes LeYaakov there in the name of Beis Yaakov, siman 26; LeDavid Emes there; Beis Yosef Mechudash there, unlike Shulchan Aruch HaRav and Keses HaSofer.
A yod that lacks an upper and left projection should not be invalidated, because some authorities hold that no actual outward point descending below is required, but only upper and lower angularity rather than a rounded form. Chasam Sofer, Yoreh Deah, siman 265; Pischei Teshuvah, siman 274, letter 5; Imrei Shefer there.
If yods are found shaped like reishin, whether a child reads them that way or we ourselves clearly see that they resemble reishin, they are invalid. Emes LeYaakov there in the name of Mishnas Avraham. See also what we wrote above in siman 32, letters 96 and 97, concerning altered yods.
Bent kaf. It should be rounded on every side and have no corner. Its upper and lower faces should be equal. Baruch She'amar.
If it has an upper rear corner, it remains valid provided it is rounded below. Shulchan Aruch HaRav; this is also implied by Beit Yosef concerning the letter peh and by Darkei Moshe, letter 7. Peri Megadim, in the introduction to siman 32, writes that any kaf with a corner, whether above or below, is invalid. Keses HaSofer, siman 5, and Emes LeYaakov cite both opinions. Lishkas HaSofer, letter 15, writes that if such a kaf is found in a Sefer Torah during reading and cannot be repaired, one may rely upon the lenient opinion and need not remove another scroll. It appears that the same should apply to tefillin and mezuzos, treating them as after-the-fact cases to avoid consigning them to genizah. See what we wrote above concerning ches, under “Likewise,” and in siman 32, letter 65. If, however, the kaf has a lower corner, even though it is rounded above, it should not be validated. Lishkas HaSofer there.
Final kaf. Its roof should be short so that it not resemble a reish. Therefore, it should not be lengthened at the end of a line at all. Although Hagahos Maimoniyos explains that no letter should be lengthened, that is only an initial requirement. If done, it does not invalidate, for the accepted halachah in Israel is that in a get one lengthens letters in the phrase “according to the law of Moshe and Israel.” A final kaf, however, is invalid if its roof is longer than its leg. Ra'em writes that all final letters should initially be long enough that, if bent, they would become their corresponding bent forms. For this reason, a final kaf should not have an upper corner but should be rounded like a reish, because if bent it would become a bent kaf. The only difference between them is that one is straight and one bent. Baruch She'amar. Beit Yosef writes in the name of Agur that a final kaf should have a leg twice as long as its roof. Magen Avraham, siman 32, seif katan 26; Emes LeYaakov there; LeDavid Emes there.
If it was made with upper corners like a dalet, some invalidate and some validate. See Magen Avraham at the cited place; Emes LeYaakov there. It appears that initially it must be written rounded like a reish, but after the fact it should not be invalidated. LeDavid Emes there; Beis Yosef Mechudash there; Keses HaSofer there. This is our practice after the fact. Even one who wishes to be stringent may repair it in tefillin and mezuzos by adding ink to round it. This is not writing out of order because the letter still retains its form, as Peri Megadim writes in the introduction to siman 32 concerning kaf. This differs from Shulchan Aruch HaRav, who is stringent following Ri'as. Lishkas HaSofer there, letter 16.
Lamed. Its neck should be long like a vav. Its head should be rounded on the right and angular on the left, because the form of lamed is a bent kaf with a vav above it. For this reason, its tail should be thick and curve well toward its face, and it should be rounded behind on the right like a bent kaf. On the left, at the junction of its body and neck, it should have a corner at the end of the vav, which descends thinly into the form of the kaf. Its head should be bent toward its face. All this is an initial requirement. Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Keses HaSofer. It should have two points upon its head because of the mystical teaching. Beit Yosef writes in the name of Ri'as that the two taggin upon its head allude to kindness and judgment, and therefore the right one is larger. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, its head resembles a small yod. Its neck is upright and thin above, curving slightly backward at the point where it joins the point above it. Its body on the right is not completely rounded. Rather, below the neck it resembles a reish with a short leg, from which a diagonal line slopes toward the left. At its thin end it curves backward toward the right and becomes slightly thick. LeDavid Emes there.
If the neck was not lengthened like a vav but was made like a yod, some invalidate it. Emes LeYaakov there in the name of Rivash; LeDavid Emes there. What Mor U'Ketziah writes, cited immediately above, is not exactly the same. LeDavid Emes there. Others validate it. Emes LeYaakov there. See Meleches Shamayim, siman 26, letters 21 and 22 in Binah, and Emes LeYaakov in the margin, letter 4. Keses HaSofer there, in Lishkas HaSofer, letter 17, writes that if this is found in a Sefer Torah during reading and cannot be repaired, it should be validated. Likewise, if found in a Divine Name or in tefillin or mezuzos, it should be validated. Wherever repair is possible, however, it must be repaired. See Emes LeYaakov there in the margin, letter 4, who writes in the name of Mishnas Avraham that some maintain even the stringent opinion applies only where the neck was not made at the very end of the roof as required but slightly inward. If it was made at the very end of the roof, one may combine the yod with the width of the roof and regard it as a vav attached to the broad roof. This is our practice after the fact: if the neck was made like a yod at the end of the roof, we validate it even in a Sefer Torah.
If the vav-shaped neck was made like a plain line, see what we wrote above in siman 32, letter 106. If the vav upon the lamed is reversed in this form: its law is the same as a plain line. Emes LeYaakov there in the name of Mishnas Avraham.
If he wrote two vavs upon the lamed, this is worse than a heh with two legs and is invalid. If this occurs in a Divine Name that may not be erased, whether it can be repaired requires study. Emes LeYaakov there in the name of the cited authority.
Initially, one should not lengthen the lamed, because this damages the form of the letter, whose form is like a kaf and vav. After the fact it is valid. Emes LeYaakov there; LeDavid Emes there; Beis Yosef Mechudash there.
Gan HaMelech, siman 114, cites Radbaz that the kaf portion of the lamed must extend below until opposite its upper portion, so that it truly has the form of a bent kaf with a vav. The author disagrees and writes that the poskim do not require an exact kaf shape, but only that it somewhat resemble one. It is not indispensable. See there. Keses HaSofer cites this in Lishkas HaSofer, letter 18, and it is obvious that if one made it exactly like a kaf, this does not invalidate it. Lishkas HaSofer there. See Emes LeYaakov there.
Open mem. Its upper right side should be rounded, while below it should have a corner but not a heel like a beis. One should not round the open mem below, because the open and closed mem must be distinguishable. If the lower side were rounded, a child might confuse the closed mem with a samech, and the letters must be clearly recognizable. Its roof should be straight rather than rounded, and should extend until opposite its base so that it is square. Its form is also like a kaf and vav, and it should resemble the closed mem. For the same reason, the inner point should extend almost level with the roof, so that the gap between them does not become excessively wide because of the opening. The face of the closed mem is an upright vav, while the face of the open mem is a slightly slanted vav. Both have the form of a vav. The projecting tip should extend below the point for the length of two pen-widths, because the point and projecting tip together form a vav. It should reach opposite the lower base so that the letter could be closed. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, its roof is neither straight nor extended until opposite its base. Rather, it is rounded like the right head of a tes together with its leg, except that it is not bent inward as much. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there.
Closed mem. Its upper right side should be rounded. Below, it should have corners on the right and left, and it should be completely closed. Its roof should extend beyond the closure by the width of a pen, because its form too is kaf-vav, except that it is closed. Baruch She'amar. Some say that the upper right side should also be square. Beit Yosef there in the name of Agur. This is our custom.
If a closed mem is rounded below and a child reads it as samech, it is invalid. LeDavid Emes there; Beis Yosef Mechudash there, letter 44.
A closed mem whose upper side alone was widened to fill the line should ideally be repaired if this can easily be done. If such a form is found in the Divine Name Elokim, it should remain as it is and is valid. Noda BiYehudah explains there that the illustrated letter forms in Beit Yosef are not indispensable. Observe Sephardic script, which contains several differences from Beit Yosef's forms. Noda BiYehudah, Yoreh Deah, siman 80; LeDavid Emes there; Beis Yosef Mechudash there, letter 46. See Lishkas HaSofer there, letter 21.
Bent nun. Its head should resemble a zayin and should be short. Its base should extend well to the left, farther than its head, so that it not resemble a beis. Its neck should be somewhat long so that another letter may be placed close to its head. It should have a slight heel, because its form is like a zayin standing within a vav. Its lower portion should be rounded, because if it were straightened it would become a final nun. Likewise, all bent letters must be rounded below, so that if straightened they would become their final forms. Baruch She'amar. See what we wrote above concerning the form of gimel.
If a bent nun was made with a vav-like head above rather than a zayin-like head, Shulchan Aruch HaRav implies that it is valid after the fact. Emes LeYaakov writes this in the name of Mishnas Avraham. Keses HaSofer likewise writes so in Lishkas HaSofer, letter 23. LeDavid Emes also implies this. Our custom initially is to make it this way.
Final nun. The shape of its neck should be like a zayin, except that it is long enough that, if bent, it would become a bent nun. Baruch She'amar.
If it is short, it should be shown to a child. If the child reads it as a zayin, it is invalid. Shulchan Aruch HaRav there; Keses HaSofer there.
If it was made like a long vav, it is invalid. LeDavid Emes there; Beis Yosef Mechudash there; Keses HaSofer there; Emes LeYaakov there. This applies even if it has taggin. Shaar Ephraim, siman 81, cited in Pischei Teshuvah, siman 274, letter 5. Likewise, if the line was drawn from the side rather than the middle, but was afterward curved back toward the center in this form: it is invalid. Chasam Sofer, Yoreh Deah, siman 269; Pischei Teshuvah there; Emes LeYaakov there.
Samech. Its roof should be straight above. It should be rounded on three sides so that it not resemble a closed mem. Its roof should extend beyond the closure by the width of a pen, at least initially, because its form too is kaf-vav like a closed mem. The only difference between a closed mem and a samech is that the former has corners while the latter does not. Baruch She'amar; Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Keses HaSofer. In Sephardic script, the lower left side is not rounded. Rather, its left leg does not descend straight from above, but bends slightly toward the right near its end until it meets its base at a broad angle. Its base is therefore shorter. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there.
Ayin. Its first head should resemble a yod whose face turns slightly upward, for the reason explained concerning the letter tes. Its body should stand upright so that another letter may be placed close to it. The second head should be like an upright zayin rather than slanted, for the reason explained above. Baruch She'amar. Mor U'Ketziah writes that its first head resembles a yod from which a thin line extends slightly diagonally. Some draw the line from the body of the yod at its head, making it like a slightly curved vav. From the lower part of the line emerges a thick and broad base extending slightly downward on a diagonal. The zayin-like portion touches the upper thick part of the thigh above its midpoint. According to Magen David, its form is nun-vav-zayin. According to Matzas Shmurim, both lines are like vavs rather than zayins. LeDavid Emes there. What is written in the name of Matzas Shmurim is likewise stated in Shaar HaKavanos in the cited discourse. This applies to writing tefillin, as explained there and in Matzas Shmurim, page 31.
Bent peh. It should have corners above, both inside and outside. Below it should have no outer corner at all, only an inner corner, because its form is like a kaf. When it lacks a lower corner it retains the form of a kaf even though it has upper corners. It should be one and one-half pen-widths wide so that the inner point may hang within it without touching. It should have a projecting tip upon its face. The inner point should be rounded below on the left and suspended from that projecting tip, because the point and the continuation of the tip together form a vav. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, the inner point has a projecting tip on its lower left side, facing outward. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there. According to Magen David, its form is kaf-yod.
Final peh. It should have a small upper corner, but should extend backward like the bent peh, so that if bent it would resemble a kaf from behind. Baruch She'amar. Some say that the final peh should be rounded above, just as the bent peh is rounded below. Beit Yosef there in the name of Hagahos Maimoniyos; Shulchan Aruch HaRav there; Keses HaSofer there.
In both the bent and final peh, the inner point must touch the roof. If it does not touch and a child reads the letter as peh, it may be repaired as explained in siman 32, seif 25. Peri Megadim, introduction to siman 32, concerning the form of peh.
The inner point should touch nowhere except the descending line on its left face. Beit Yosef there in the name of Baruch She'amar; Levush; Emes LeYaakov there; LeDavid Emes there; Keses HaSofer there. It appears that in tefillin and mezuzos, if the point touches elsewhere only through a thin line and the form of the peh has not changed, it may be repaired like disconnected yods in a shin. See what we wrote above in siman 32, letter 108. Emes LeYaakov writes that if a small line emerges from the left side of the inner point and its tip touches the lower baseline, one may scrape away only that small line, without concern of engraving rather than writing or writing out of order. This requires study, because the form appears to have changed into a closed mem. If the inner point touches more broadly than at its attachment point, it is invalid. Emes LeYaakov there; LeDavid Emes there. This means that the reversed vav within it becomes like a plain line without a head, in this form: This is considered a broader contact than its ordinary attachment and may not be repaired in tefillin and mezuzos. Emes LeYaakov there. See above, siman 32, seif 18 in Rama, and our discussion there, letter 106.
If one made the peh in this form: with the inner point not extended evenly with the roof, Peri Megadim remains uncertain. Emes LeYaakov writes that one may thicken the reversed vav so that it becomes level with the roof. This does not violate the requirement of writing in order.
One must be careful in the final peh that the inner point not face outward, lest it resemble a tav. Beit Yosef in the name of Ri'as; Emes LeYaakov there; LeDavid Emes there; Keses HaSofer there. If one reversed the point outward, Peri Megadim remains uncertain concerning a final peh. If this occurred in a bent peh, Emes LeYaakov certainly validates it.
A final peh does not need a roof twice as long as its ordinary width. A minimal extension is sufficient. Peri Megadim there; Emes LeYaakov there. See what we wrote above concerning final kaf.
Bent tzadi. Its first head should resemble a yod whose face turns slightly upward, for the reason explained concerning the letter tes. Its thigh should attach at the middle of its neck, and one must be careful not to attach it too low, lest it resemble an ayin. Its second head should resemble a zayin. Its neck should be somewhat long so that another letter may be placed close to its head. Its base should extend well to the left, because the form of its body is like a nun. Its lower bend should be rounded like that of all bent letters. Baruch She'amar. Regarding the statement that its neck should be somewhat long, see what we wrote above concerning the form of gimel. In Sephardic script, the right head resembles a reversed yod and the left head a normal yod whose legs extend backward until they meet near one another at the head of the body beneath them. The body resembles a small nun or bent kaf, with a short roof. It appears preferable that the right head resemble the head of an alef if its leg were lengthened. Magen David likewise implies this when it gives its form as nun-yod. Matzas Shmurim gives the first illustrated form. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there. What is written in the name of Matzas Shmurim is likewise stated in Shaar HaKavanos in the cited discourse, and applies to writing tefillin as explained above. Our custom is to make the right yod reversed even in a Sefer Torah, following the Sephardic form cited by Mor U'Ketziah.
Final tzadi. Its heads should be like those of the bent tzadi, and its leg should descend below the point where the heads join, at least initially, so that it could be made into a bent tzadi. Baruch She'amar; Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Keses HaSofer.
If the yod and zayin components of a final tzadi are attached and from that point downward there is no extending leg at all, the law requires study. If, however, a leg of at least the measure of a yod remains, it is valid. Peri Megadim, introduction to siman 32, concerning the form of tzadi; Emes LeYaakov there.
Kof. Its roof should be level, and its rear leg should be well rounded, because the form of its leg is like a bent kaf with a separate leg suspended from it. The upper part of the suspended leg should be somewhat thick and gradually narrow, because its form is like a final nun. For this reason, its total length should be three and one-half pen-widths, so that it could be bent and made into a bent nun. It should not be lengthened more than this. The suspended leg should incline slightly toward the right leg. It must not touch the roof above, nor come so close to it that an average person cannot distinguish the gap from a distance of four amos. It should also not be farther from the roof than the thickness of a pen. A small staff should project toward the left upon its face. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, the rear leg is not deeply rounded in a circular form. Rather, it is shaped by two converging lines, the first thicker and straighter than the second. The suspended leg is thin at both its beginning and end, though somewhat thicker in the middle. The same applies to the left legs of the other letters: they are thin above where they emerge and attach to the heads of the letters, just as they are thin at their ends. The first illustrated form accords with Magen David, who depicts it as reish-nun. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there.
If the left leg touches the roof or the right leg, the letter is invalid. Keses HaSofer there. This applies even if the contact is extremely thin. Meleches Shamayim, general rule 27, concerning the form of kof. See what we wrote above in siman 32, letter 101.
If a kof is found in a Sefer Torah with its long leg positioned in the middle of the roof, Heichal HaBerachah cites a dispute between Radbaz and Gan HaMelech. It appears that if this is found in a Sefer Torah on Shabbos, another scroll should not be removed, and one may rely upon Gan HaMelech. Knesses HaGedolah and Eliyah Rabbah in siman 32, seif katan 40, likewise incline this way. Afterward, however, it should be repaired to satisfy Radbaz and Beis Yosef. LeDavid Emes there. It appears that the same applies to tefillin: if found in this form, they may be validated after the fact, as explained above in siman 32, letter 65, in this siman concerning the form of ches under “Likewise,” and in our discussion in siman 32, letter 126 concerning the form of heh.
A Sefer Torah in which the legs of all the letters kof touch their roofs should be repaired by scraping away the entire suspended leg and rewriting it. There is no concern that the text will appear mottled. Yachin U'Boaz, part 1, siman 24. See there. LeDavid Emes there.
Reish. Its rear side should be completely rounded so that it not resemble a dalet. Its face should be level so that it not resemble cursive writing. Its roof should be long and its leg short so that it not resemble a final kaf. Baruch She'amar. The reason its roof must be long is so that it not resemble a vav. Beit Yosef there.
Shin. Its first head should be like a yod whose face turns upward. The second head should likewise be a yod whose face turns upward, with a small projection upon it. The third head should be like a zayin, for the reason explained concerning the form of tes. The body should be drawn diagonally downward until opposite the third head. The middle head should join on the lower left side, so that all three heads are connected below at one point and stand upon one leg. That point should be sharp below like the kof and reish. In Sephardic script, the body extends from the first head near its end on a slight diagonal, or from its edge sloping downward like a vav curved slightly forward. The second head is identical in form and length, slanting forward equally above and below, so that its thigh reaches the place where the third head joins below. Opposite it, the first thigh remains distinct below, at the same distance as the heads are from one another. Afterward, the base is drawn beneath them from the first to the third head, with some width, though not as broad as the three heads together. The three heads are not all joined below at one place. Only the two left heads are joined together at an angle opposite the space between the middle and third heads. The first head attaches below to the base at a somewhat rounded point. The base is not completely straight. Magen David implies that the three heads are yods, showing that they are equal, and that its mnemonic is not the form mentioned by Baruch She'amar. It also implies that it has a broad base rather than a sharp point, because its form is three yods within a kaf or arch. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there. According to the teaching of the Arizal, all the shins in tefillin should have their three lines shaped like three vavs and not like three zayins. Shaar HaKavanos in the cited discourse; Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Keses HaSofer there.
If the middle head of the shin is shaped like a zayin, it is valid. If it is reversed, Peri Megadim validates it. Meleches Shamayim there. See what we wrote above concerning the form of alef under “If.”
If one wrote a shin with four heads, then even if a child can read it, there is no remedy by merely scraping away one yod so that three heads remain. He must first nullify the form of the letter and then complete it. Otherwise it would have the form of a ches. In tefillin and mezuzos, if he already wrote further, there is no remedy because the repair would be writing out of order. Peri Megadim there concerning the form of shin; Keses HaSofer, siman 8, letter 12.
Tav. It should be exactly like a dalet, and its left leg should resemble a reversed dalet. The end of the left leg should align with the end of the roof. The right leg should be short, because if it were long a child might confuse it with a final peh. The left leg should touch the roof above, because every letter must be a single complete body. Baruch She'amar. In Sephardic script, it is like a reish, and its left leg is like a reversed vav that bends below toward the left and then toward the right in the direction of its head. Mor U'Ketziah; LeDavid Emes there.
If the roof of the tav was extended more than appropriate and the leg stands in the middle, its law is the same as that of a heh and kof. Peri Megadim there concerning the form of tav; Keses HaSofer there concerning the form of tav.
If a tav is found with two legs in the middle, its form has changed and it is invalid. This is not comparable to a heh with two legs, discussed above under the letter heh, because there the additional leg does not touch the roof. Meleches Shamayim there in the name of Mishnas Avraham, citing Sefer Stam, siman 62.
If the left leg is shaped like a plain line, the letter is invalid because it resembles a ches according to Rashi's form. Meleches Shamayim there.
