And the lower point should initially, etc. The reader of this booklet should know that in several places I did not clarify, either in the text or in Shaar HaTziyun, whether a ruling applies only initially or also after the fact. This is for one of three reasons. Sometimes it is obvious that the ruling is only initial or, conversely, indispensable after the fact. Sometimes the matter has differing details, applying initially in one case and after the fact in another. For example, where a letter must be formed in a particular way so that another letter may be placed next to it, then if it was not formed that way and the resulting gap divides one word so that it appears like two words, the writing is invalid. If that letter stands at the end of a word, however, the writing may be valid after the fact. There are many similar cases, and I did not wish to lengthen the discussion. At other times I myself remained uncertain and left the matter without decision. Therefore, one should not rush either to be lenient or to rule stringently and cause another person loss unless he finds clear proof.
One should not be lenient. Peri Megadim writes this. Nevertheless, Rabbeinu Yerucham's words, cited under the letter kaf, prove that the main distinction depends upon the lower portion of the letter. It therefore appears that if a child reads it correctly, one may at least be lenient and add ink to repair it in tefillin or mezuzos, without concern that the writing is out of order.
And the right leg should be thin. Shulchan Aruch HaRav implies that this is only an initial requirement. Yet according to the reason Beit Yosef gives for it, this would appear indispensable, because the baraisa concludes regarding all the listed deviations that the writing must be placed in genizah. Perhaps the fact that the right leg extends lower than the left leg sufficiently distinguishes a gimel from a tzadi, unlike the form discussed by Baruch She'amar. According to this, extending the right leg below the left is indispensable, which may explain why Shulchan Aruch HaRav did not label it merely initial. Nevertheless, the practical ruling requires study. According to Rosh's explanation of that baraisa in Hilchos Sefer Torah, and see Rashi there, there is no proof that one must be exacting in this detail. Initially, however, one should certainly be careful because of the other authorities, as Beit Yosef writes similarly concerning the alef.
It should also not be very bent. Whether a greatly bent form is valid after the fact requires study. See Beit Yosef's reason from the Gemara's question, “Why does the leg of the gimel extend toward the dalet?” The Gemara similarly asks why the leg of the dalet extends toward the gimel, yet that feature is only an initial requirement, as will be explained below in Biur Halachah. In any event, repair certainly helps even in tefillin and mezuzos. The same applies if the left thigh is equal in length to the right leg, comparable to Peri Megadim's leniency concerning a final kaf made square.
Initially, its leg should be straight, etc. Beit Yosef's source is Shabbos 104a: “Why does the leg of the dalet extend toward the gimel?” It appears to me that this is only an initial requirement. The Gemara there says that one who corrected a single letter is liable, such as where he removed the crown of a dalet and made it a reish. Eruvin 13a likewise says that perhaps a fly will sit upon the dalet, etc. These passages suggest that a reish remains valid after the fact even if its leg is straight, though perhaps this proof can be rejected. Nevertheless, practically one should not invalidate it after the fact, based upon Taz above in siman 32, seif 16. See there and the concise rules of writing out of order that I copied from Peri Megadim.
And if. Know that this doubt arises only according to what we established as halachah in Biur Halachah, siman 32, seif 25, following Rabbi Akiva Eiger, that repair helps where a letter does not appear as two letters even when the separation is visibly recognizable. Peri Megadim holds that repair does not help in such a case in tefillin and mezuzos. For example, where the yod of an alef does not touch the alef, even though that detached yod has the independent form of another letter, the remainder of the alef has no independent letter name. Repair may therefore help so that the detached yod is no longer read as a yod but as part of the alef. Peri Megadim himself mentions this reasoning, though according to him it helps only where the separation is not clearly recognizable. We also established there that if the hump above a ches does not connect the two sides and the separation is clearly visible, repair does not help because it appears as two zayins. We do not say that the hump preserves the name ches. Our present doubt is whether, when the hump itself is continuous but does not touch one of the zayins beneath it, the form of a ches is still lacking because it is not attached on that side, or whether the continuous hump retains its status and prevents the zayin beneath it from being read independently, just as with the yod of an alef. This requires study.
It should not be bent too greatly. What is considered too greatly bent? Baruch She'amar, in his first alphabet, and Agur imply that this means when the bend touches the base of the tes. This requires study. Beit Yosef explains that it should not be bent too greatly because it would then resemble a peh entirely. Sefer HaTerumah and Hagahos Maimoniyos cite this language. They refer to the right half of the tes, for although the left side, a zayin, proves that it is not a peh, their view is that if part of a letter resembles another letter it is not complete writing. Sefer Yere'im interprets the baraisa in this manner regarding alefs made like ayins and similar forms. Maaseh Rokeach cites Maharanach, siman 1, likewise. Many authorities disagree, but this is not the place to elaborate. Yet regarding a peh, Baruch She'amar and Agur themselves write, and the law below follows them, that if the inner point touches either side, the letter is invalid. If so, the less the tes is bent inward, the more closely it would seem to resemble a peh. It must therefore be that even without touching the base, a very great bend is forbidden, and certainly if it touches. In either case, at least part of it resembles a peh. This also explains why Beit Yosef, Levush, and all the commentators on Shulchan Aruch did not define the phrase “bent too greatly.” Had they accepted Baruch She'amar's narrow definition, they would not have remained silent. Regarding the after-the-fact law, Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes “it should not initially be bent too greatly,” implying leniency after the fact. LeDavid Emes writes: “One should not bend the tes too deeply inward lest it entirely resemble a peh, for if its point touches its body inside it is invalid.” If Shulchan Aruch HaRav interprets “too greatly” as Baruch She'amar does, this would be a dispute: Shulchan Aruch HaRav validates and LeDavid Emes invalidates. The principal after-the-fact ruling, whether according to our explanation or that of Baruch She'amar and Agur, remains difficult. Sefer Yere'im, Sefer HaTerumah, and Hagahos Maimoniyos write that in this way it entirely resembles a peh, and explicitly explain this as the baraisa's statement not to make tesin like pehin. The baraisa concludes regarding all such cases that the writing must be placed in genizah. According to Rosh, however, who writes in Hilchos Sefer Torah in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that the right side of the tes should be bent so that if the letter is turned to the left it resembles a peh, the baraisa proves nothing concerning a slight or great bend. Its point is merely that although a tes resembles a peh from a certain angle, one should not actually make it a peh. Rosh also copied almost all of Sefer HaTerumah's language but omitted this distinction concerning a great bend, indicating that he disagrees. Tur and Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise merely say that the tes should be bent, without mentioning this issue. It follows that a greatly bent tes is disputed. Sefer HaTerumah, Yere'im, and Hagahos Maimoniyos invalidate it even after the fact, while Rosh, Tur, and Rabbeinu Yerucham permit it. Since this concerns a Torah law, one should be stringent. According to our explanation, however, “greatly bent” includes even where it does not touch below, and it is very difficult to invalidate after the fact against Baruch She'amar and Agur, who imply that without contact it is valid. Initially, it appears that one should repair it wherever possible. In a Sefer Torah, if it is found during reading, one should not remove another scroll because of this and may rely upon the lenient authorities. Afterward, however, he should erase enough of the tes to cancel its letter form and rewrite it, because otherwise the repair would be engraving rather than writing. In tefillin and mezuzos, where writing out of order invalidates, one should rely upon the lenient authorities. If the inward bend touched the base of the tes, then even in tefillin and mezuzos it is invalid, following LeDavid Emes. One should not be lenient against Sefer HaTerumah and Hagahos Maimoniyos. Perhaps Rosh also agrees that it is invalid because its form has changed. Even if a child reads it as tes, the letter is not surrounded by parchment at the point of contact, as explained below concerning the shin. Because of that alone, scraping at the point of contact would ordinarily help. But according to Sefer HaTerumah and Hagahos Maimoniyos, this is included in making tesin like pehin, and in every case the letter's form must first be nullified. That cannot be done in tefillin and mezuzos because of writing out of order.
The yod must also have a small crown above, etc. Levush implies that one may be lenient after the fact. In my humble opinion this requires study. Baruch She'amar, in his first alphabet, explicitly indicates that according to Rabbeinu Tam the missing lower point and missing upper crown are equally invalidating. The Mishnah's phrase “even the point of a yod is indispensable” may mention the point specifically to teach that even this smaller feature, smaller than the upper crown, is indispensable, as Beit Yosef explains. Moreover, Rosh in Hilchos Sefer Torah, Hagahos Maimoniyos, and Sefer HaTerumah are uncertain and write that “the point of the yod” may refer to the upper crown. See there. At the very least, even if one says its omission does not make the writing invalid after the fact, it must certainly be repaired initially, even in old passages. All the more so, the left-hand point must be repaired and not left lacking inside the batim.
He should scrape away the entire projection, etc. This surprises me. Since the letter appears like a small ches, its form has changed and it is invalid. The repair would therefore still be engraving rather than writing. One cannot answer that because its point is still missing it is not yet a valid letter, and that it becomes valid only when the point is added in writing. That works only according to Rabbeinu Tam, who holds the point is indispensable. According to Rashi, the letter is valid without the point and was created through scraping alone. Perhaps the halachah follows Rashi, for Rabbeinu Tam's ruling is not entirely settled among the poskim. We are required to consider his view stringently, but not use it as a basis for leniency. One also cannot say the case is merely one where the letter does not clearly resemble a small ches and a child reads it as a yod. In that case, the child's reading alone would suffice without scraping, as Tur rules concerning any doubtful letter. Why, then, did they write that the child's reading does not help? The correct ruling is therefore like the dissenting opinion cited in the text. The original responsum requires examination.
If a child, etc., perhaps, etc. I wrote “perhaps” although Peri Megadim states this regarding a final kaf, because the cases may differ. There the letter's length proves its intended form, whereas here that indicator is absent. Nevertheless, in pressing circumstances it appears proper to be lenient. Rema, cited by Beit Yosef under the letter nun, writes that all bent letters must be rounded below. This seems to include the bent kaf, implying that its principal identifying feature is its rounded lower portion. Darkei Moshe, cited at the end of the discussion of the letter zayin, likewise implies this. Furthermore, Beit Yosef writes under the letter peh that in Ashuris script the custom is initially to make the kaf angular above. How then could we say that under our practice even a child's reading does not help because the letter lacks its essential form? There is another proof from Rabbeinu Yerucham. He explains the baraisa's warning not to write beisin like kafin as requiring the scribe to be careful with the lower right projection at the rear of the beis so that it not appear like a kaf. It follows that the inverse warning, not to write kafin like beisin, likewise concerns the lower portion. All of Rabbeinu Yerucham's words appear in Rosh, Hilchos Ketanos, though Rabbeinu Yerucham clarifies that the reference is to the lower side. Accordingly, one may be lenient through a child's reading and adding ink.
If he lengthened its roof, etc. This is the language of Derech HaChayim in the laws of Torah reading. He does not mention the measure of twice the roof's length, because he holds that even regarding a kaf this measure is only for preferred fulfillment. For this he cites Darkei Moshe and Mordechai, who say concerning the letters generally that these dimensions are only initial requirements. Levush in siman 36 likewise implies this. See Rabbi Akiva Eiger's novellae to siman 32, seif 25. This differs from Taz in Yoreh Deah, siman 273, and Shaagas Aryeh, who adopts his stringency and invalidates even after the fact when the leg is not twice the length of the roof. Dagul MeRevavah in siman 32, commenting on Magen Avraham, seif katan 26, likewise invalidates even after the fact because he believes Beit Yosef in siman 36 proves this. His words are astonishing. One who examines Beit Yosef concerning the final kaf, in the second alphabet, will see explicitly that he mentions invalidity only where the roof is longer than the leg. When he writes that the leg should be twice the roof, this refers only to preferred fulfillment. I later found that Birchas Mayim, by the author of Seder HaGet in Even HaEzer, explicitly writes that they were stringent only where the roof is longer than the leg, but not where roof and leg are equal. All the more so, the requirement that the leg be twice as long was stated only for preferred fulfillment.
The scribes have adopted this opinion. Peri Megadim also writes that when the right leg of a heh retains the measure of a small letter, meaning a yod, it is valid. It appears to me that the same applies to other letters, such as dalet and lamed: if the right leg retains the measure of a yod, that suffices. Regarding lamed, his intent must concern the lower line, meaning that even if it was not extended below with a thin line of the length of a yod, the letter is valid. He cannot mean to be lenient concerning the central rounded line on the right, because without that there is no form of a lamed at all.
Like a kaf and vav. I wrote everything according to Beit Yosef and all the later poskim, excluding Peri Megadim, who writes that its form is like a nun and vav. There is no source for that. Perhaps he inferred it from seif 18, where Shulchan Aruch says to scrape away the projection until the form of a bent nun remains. This is no proof. Since that passage does not address the laws of forming letters, Shulchan Aruch may not have been exact concerning the preferred initial form, because a nun and vav are valid according to all opinions. Alternatively, he may have followed the Sephardic custom, which does not extend the upper line opposite the lower base, as LeDavid Emes writes. According to that custom the letter resembles a nun and vav. This is why LeDavid Emes writes that for them the mem resembles a tes. Rabbeinu Chananel famously writes that a tes resembles a nun and vav, as Beit Yosef records under the letter nun. According to our practice, however, scraping until the form of a kaf remains is sufficient. Levush and the order of gittin arranged by Maharam of Rottweil at the end of Even HaEzer likewise say to scrape until the form of a bent kaf remains. Taz writes there that a mem is written in two strokes, like a nun and vav. Beis Aharon already challenges him on this point.
It should reach opposite the lower base. If it does not reach downward and a child reads it as a mem, whether it is valid after the fact requires study. Perhaps this feature is not part of the essential form of the letter but only an initial requirement.
He should scrape away the excess. Nevertheless, if he extended it so greatly that a child no longer recognized the letter, it requires study whether scraping helps. See Rambam, chapter 1 of Hilchos Tefillin, halachah 19. Even if one says that with a heh in such a case it is valid, that case differs because according to several Rishonim that is the principal form of a heh, as explained above.
Peri Megadim left it unresolved. A contemporary scholar brought proof for those who validate from Shabbos 104b: if one intended to write one letter and two emerged, he is liable. According to Rabbeinu Chananel, cited by Hagahos Maimoniyos and Beit Yosef under this letter, the case concerns a tes: he intended to write a tes but did not attach its leg on the left, and it became a nun and zayin. If a nun whose head was made like a vav is not considered a nun even after the fact, why would he be liable for writing two letters? Presumably the nun within the tes was formed as other tesin are formed, with the neck extending from its side rather than its center. This proof may be rejected. According to the Gemara's conclusion, which says that the letters require crowns, the baraisa must refer to a case where he made the nun within the tes upright above and crowned it, unlike an ordinary tes, where the nun is unadorned and bent slightly inward. We may therefore also say that he extended the neck from the center of its head like an ordinary nun, not like the nun within an ordinary tes. That is why he is liable for the nun as well. Regarding repair of a bent nun whose head was made like a vav, I wrote in the text that repair helps even in tefillin and mezuzos. There is no concern of engraving because scraping alone does not validate it, and no concern of writing out of order because its essential form remains. Everyone commonly recognizes such a form as a nun. This follows from Peri Megadim's ruling regarding a final kaf made square. Even according to the authorities who invalidate it after the fact because they do not distinguish between final and bent kaf, they agree that adding ink to round it helps even in tefillin and mezuzos. It is still considered to retain the form of a kaf because people commonly write such kafin. Our case is all the more lenient. The Gemara explicitly requires the bent kaf to be rounded, and according to those who apply that form even to final kaf, a square top would seem to erase the name kaf. Nevertheless, Peri Megadim says repair helps because the letter is commonly written that way. Concerning a final nun with a vav-shaped head, at least there is no explicit source in the Gemara that repair should not help. All the more so, one should not be stringent concerning a bent nun whose head was made like a vav. I wrote that one should scrape only a little, because if he scrapes too much the form will be lost and later repair will be writing out of order. I also wrote that afterward he should add ink. If he first adds ink and makes the letter very broad, it may lose its essential form because people are not accustomed to such a shape, and later scraping from the left may not help. Know further that Peri Megadim treats final and bent nun alike even after the fact, evidently because both carry the name nun. He likewise tends to invalidate a final kaf made square. According to his reasoning, the same concern may apply to an open mem whose bottom was made rounded, since both open and closed forms bear the name mem. A closed mem made that way is certainly invalid because it falls under the warning not to make memin like samechin. Perhaps the same applies to the open mem. This requires practical study. Repair nevertheless helps even in tefillin and mezuzos, because its essential form remains even before repair.
And its right leg, etc., like a bent kaf. After the fact, even a slight inward bend appears sufficient. See Beit Yosef. Rashi on Shabbos 104a, s.v. “let him enter through this,” likewise implies this, as do Rabbeinu Yerucham and Agur, who write that the leg of the kof should be lengthened so that it not resemble a heh. All this implies that it remains open below and that at least a slight bend suffices. See Beis Aharon, who cites Radbaz that the principal form is a reish and nun. He discusses the matter at length and concludes: “Its form appears like a reish, except that the end of the reish should bend leftward like a lamed. The same appears from Magen David of Radbaz. To satisfy all opinions, one should make it bent below like a kaf, and this is the accepted custom. It should not be made like the form used by some scribes, with merely a lamed-like curve. That accords with no opinion and severs the plantings. Rather, it should be made as stated.”
