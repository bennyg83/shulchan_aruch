He does not now nullify what he has seen and removed, because he still wishes to eat from what he leaves over, and also wishes to fulfill the mitzvah of burning his own chametz the next day. He therefore does not nullify everything until the next day, after eating and after burning the chametz. To nullify it and let it be. See Bach, who discusses at length the dispute among the early authorities. According to Rabbeinu Chananel and those who follow him, nullification operates as ownerlessness. Maharik, responsum 142, however, writes that Rashi, Maimonides, and those who follow them hold that it does not operate as ownerlessness, but rather that one renders the chametz like dust. Kol Bo writes similarly. Accordingly, our formula, which does not mention ownerlessness, relies upon Rashi and those who follow him. Bach concludes that Rabbeinu Tam’s view is primary, and therefore writes that the proper formula is: “All leaven in my possession that I have not seen, have not removed, and do not know of, shall be nullified and shall be ownerless like the dust of the earth.” The later authorities agree. Maharik further writes that according to all opinions, ownerlessness declared privately is effective here, unlike ordinary ownerlessness. Rokeach, siman 266, writes similarly. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 273, seif 7, and Sema there, seif katan 11, and above, siman 247. Like the dust of the earth. Bach explains that this teaches that he does not declare it ownerless in the ordinary manner, since ordinary ownerlessness is incomplete until another person acquires it. He therefore specifies that he makes it ownerless like dust. His words are difficult. Ordinary ownerlessness is effective even before another person acquires it, although within three days the owner may retract, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 273. Regarding chametz, the prohibition makes it undesirable to him, and he has no wish to retract. The principal reason for saying “like dust” is therefore according to those who hold that nullification is not based upon ownerlessness. Even according to Rabbeinu Tam, whose view leads us to mention ownerlessness, we add “like dust” to remind ourselves that another Jew should not acquire the chametz. We declare it like dust in which no person should acquire rights. Why must one say “of the earth,” rather than merely “like dust”? Bach offers a strained explanation. It appears simple to me. If he said merely “like dust,” his intent might be to valuable dust, as in the verse “and he has dust of gold,” which is also called dust regarding covering blood. We therefore say “like the dust of the earth.” And if he said it in the Holy Tongue, “all leaven.” This is the version in most books. Some books read: “If he said it in the Holy Tongue, he fulfilled the obligation,” meaning even if he did not understand it. Olas Tamid explains this because the Aramaic formula is not literally Hebrew. My brother-in-law, in Eliyah Zuta, preserves the older reading. Rama calls the Talmudic wording “the Holy Tongue.” Shaar Ephraim attempts to object that the formula is Aramaic, but this is no objection. Relative to other vernaculars, the Talmudic language is called the Holy Tongue. I found explicit support in Agudah, first chapter of Pesachim. After recording the formula, it writes that one who does not know how to say it in the Holy Tongue should say it in German or another language he understands. This directly supports my brother-in-law’s interpretation of Rama. But in other languages he must mention each one separately. The same applies if he uses literal Hebrew. He must mention both chametz and leaven, as explained in Beit Yosef. Ordinarily, however, everyone recites the Sages’ established formula. One who changes it generally does so because he is unlearned and does not understand the established formula or Hebrew, and therefore says it in a vernacular. If he says words in a language he does not understand and has no awareness that he is nullifying the chametz, he certainly has not fulfilled the obligation. If he knows and intends to nullify it, he fulfills the obligation even without understanding every word, because nullification in the heart is also effective. The later authorities agree. There is no distinction in this respect between Hebrew and other languages.
