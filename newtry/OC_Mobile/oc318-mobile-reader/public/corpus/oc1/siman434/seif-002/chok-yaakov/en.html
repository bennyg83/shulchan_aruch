"That I did not see and did not destroy." Chametz that he did see and set aside for destruction is not nullified now, because he still intends to eat some of what he leaves over and also wishes to fulfill the mitzvah of burning his own chametz the following day. He therefore does not nullify everything until the next day, after eating and after burning the chametz. "Let it be nullified and become." See Bach, who discusses at length the dispute among the Rishonim. According to Rabbeinu Chananel and those who agree with him, nullification functions as hefker. Responsa Maharik, siman 142, however, writes that according to Rashi, Rambam, and those who agree with them, it does not function as hefker, but as rendering the chametz like dust. Kol Bo writes likewise. Thus our formula, which does not explicitly say "hefker," relies upon Rashi and those who agree with him. Bach discusses the matter at length and holds that the primary view is Rabbeinu Tam, and therefore concludes that it is proper to say: "Any chametz in my possession that I did not see, did not destroy, and do not know about shall be nullified and become ownerless like the dust of the earth." The later authorities agree. "Whether he understands it." This is the reading found in most books. Some books read: "If he said it in the Holy Tongue, he has fulfilled his obligation," meaning even if he does not understand it. Olat Shabbos writes likewise because it troubled those authors that the formula Kol Chamira is not literally Hebrew. My brother-in-law, however, in Eliyah Zuta, resolves the old text by explaining that the author calls the Talmudic language of Kol Chamira "the Holy Tongue." Shaar Ephraim in the addendum, page 113b, seeks to object that Kol Chamira is not Hebrew, but this is not an objection. Relative to ordinary vernaculars, the Talmudic language can be called the Holy Tongue. I found explicit support in Agudah, first chapter of Pesachim, siman 1. After recording the Kol Chamira formula, it writes that if one does not know how to say it in the Holy Tongue, he should say it in German or another language that he understands. This explicitly supports my brother-in-law's explanation of Rama. "But in other languages he must mention each separately." The same applies if one uses literal Hebrew rather than the established Talmudic formula: he must mention both chametz and se'or, as explained in Beit Yosef. Ordinarily, however, everyone recites the formula established by the Sages. A person who changes from it generally does so because he is unlearned and does not understand the established formula or Hebrew, and therefore says it in the vernacular. If he utters words in a language that he does not understand and has no awareness that he is nullifying his chametz, he certainly has not fulfilled the obligation. If, however, he knows that the statement is an act of nullification and intends to nullify his chametz, he fulfills the obligation even if he does not understand every individual word, because nullification in the heart is also effective. The later authorities agree. In this respect there is no distinction between Hebrew and another language.
