The Gemara explains that otherwise there would be no end to the matter, from courtyard to courtyard and city to city, because it is impossible that everyone should inspect simultaneously. Even within the same house we are not concerned, because there is a double doubt: perhaps no mouse entered, and even if one entered, perhaps it ate the chametz. This is all explicit in Pesachim 9. Accordingly, even if he did not nullify at all and the prohibited time has arrived, so that he can no longer nullify, he need not reinspect. Although the inspection is then required by Torah law because he did not nullify, the double doubt remains. Tosafos there, under the heading “So that the animal,” explicitly establishes the Mishnah even where no nullification occurred. Olas Shabbos, however, gives his own reason, that this is a Rabbinic doubt because bedikas chametz is Rabbinic. This implies that where the inspection is required by Torah law, such as where no nullification occurred, one must reinspect. He overlooked the Gemara’s discussion. He need not inspect. This applies specifically where the chametz was placed where children cannot handle it. Magen Avraham. He should cover it with a utensil. This implies that even initially it is permitted merely to cover it. Magen Avraham asks that during Pesach, if one finds chametz on Chol HaMoed, it must be burned immediately and covering it is insufficient, as stated in siman 446. He answers that there we are concerned that one may forget and eat it. Here there is no concern that someone will uncover it and afterward a creature will drag it away. On the contrary, people ordinarily cover things, as Rabbi Shimshon writes in the eleventh chapter of Parah. According to Rashi in Chullin 10 and Rambam at the end of chapter 9 of Hilchos Parah, who hold that people are as likely to uncover as to cover, it would follow that initially covering is prohibited. This cannot be correct. All the poskim imply that it is permitted initially, and this is explicit in the Jerusalem Talmud and Responsa Rosh, klal 14, siman 3. Tur’s wording, “if he covered it with a utensil,” and the Mechaber’s earlier conditional formulation are retrospective only because they discuss the later event of the chametz not being found. The covering itself may be done initially. Bach and Nachalas Tzvi strain unnecessarily on this point, and Bach’s attribution of the opposite view to Rashi is certainly incorrect. Magen Avraham’s basic question has a simple answer. During Chol HaMoed, when one finds chametz, he must in any event remove it, and since removal is permitted, why merely cover it? Those who are diligent hasten to perform mitzvos. Here, however, he wishes to retain the chametz for eating or to remove it the next day at the proper time. Moreover, here several uncertainties are present. Perhaps the person who uncovered it ate it. Even if a mouse dragged it away, perhaps the mouse ate it. We therefore do not initially concern ourselves that a person may uncover it, may not eat it, and then a mouse may come and also fail to eat it. All the more so are we lenient after the fact, particularly because bedikas chametz is Rabbinic. During Chol HaMoed, by contrast, there is concern that one may eat chametz during Pesach, which is a Torah prohibition. A mouse can enter. If we saw a weasel or mouse drag chametz away, one must reinspect even places where chametz is not ordinarily brought. This applies specifically when we did not see where it carried the chametz, and only to rooms whose doors are open. If the room is locked, inspection is required only in the place to which it was brought or in an open room, not in closed rooms. If the chametz was only a kezayis, which a weasel or mouse could carry through a hole or crack, we are concerned. Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah rules this several times. Bach, however, rules that we do not suspect that it was dragged through a hole.
