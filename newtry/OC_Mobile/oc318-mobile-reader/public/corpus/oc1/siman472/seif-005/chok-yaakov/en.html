Even if he is his father. The Kol Bo and Abudarham wrote the reason, that an ordinary father waives this honor for his son. According to this, it appears that where we know that the father does not want to waive it, the son should not recline. However, one may say that his view is disregarded relative to all people. The Bach ruled that in all cases the son should not recline unless his father gives him permission.
He does not need reclining. From this wording it initially seems that he does not need reclining, but there is no prohibition if he reclines. However, from the wording of the Rambam, chapter 7 of Hilchos Chametz, and the Kol Bo, it implies that it is forbidden for him to recline because of awe and honor of his rebbe. This is also clear in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 138. See the Bach, who explained the language of the Tur and the Gemara, which is like the wording of the Mechaber, in this way as well, and this is primary. See what I wrote in seif katan 9. See also the Taz and the Magen Avraham, who wrote in the name of the Maharil that anyone who reclines while exempt is called a commoner. He derived this law from the Yerushalmi in the first chapter of Shabbos. In truth, the Be’er Sheva wrote that this matter requires consideration, for many times we find that people are stringent in matters from which they are exempt. These are the words of the Magen Avraham. See my work Minchas Yaakov, question 15, where I also wrote that one can distinguish. Although below, at the end of siman 639, the Rav writes in the gloss regarding rain that if one does not leave his sukkah he is called a commoner, that case is different, since the Torah exempted him so that his body not suffer, for its ways are ways of pleasantness. Therefore it is forbidden for him to be stringent and afflict his body, all the more so on Yom Tov. This is also explicit in the responsum of Mahariv, siman 191, in the laws of Sukkos; see there. In other matters, however, perhaps it is permitted to be stringent where there is no side of prohibition. Yet Tosafot in Sotah 17b somewhat implies that even in such a case he is called a commoner, and this requires study.
Unless his rebbe gives him permission. Although the Beit Yosef wrote that whenever his rebbe sees and remains silent, it is as though his rebbe gave him permission, nevertheless here he writes specifically that he gives him permission, for otherwise how could he initially recline, perhaps his rebbe will be particular with him? In the manuscript it was written regarding the students of Rabbah, who did not know the law; therefore they reclined, and Rabbah saw and remained silent, thereby waiving it for them. But one who knows the law is forbidden to recline until his rebbe gives him permission. All this appears simple to me, unlike the Pesach Me’ubin, siman 172, who makes this a dispute; see there, but he was not precise. All this applies to a student in Torah, but a trade apprentice reclines in every case.
