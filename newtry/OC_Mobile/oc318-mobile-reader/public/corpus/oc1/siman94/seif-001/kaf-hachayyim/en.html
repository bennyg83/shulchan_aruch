(1) [Se'if 1] When he rises to pray, if he was standing in the Diaspora, etc. Berachos 30a teaches: A blind person, or one unable to determine the directions, should direct his heart toward his Father in Heaven, as it says, 'They shall pray to Hashem.' One in the Diaspora directs himself toward Eretz Yisrael; in Eretz Yisrael, toward Jerusalem; in Jerusalem, toward the Temple; in the Temple, toward the Holy of Holies; in the Holy of Holies, toward the place of the Kapores; and behind the Kapores, he should envision himself as standing before it. Thus all Israel direct their hearts to one place, 'the mound toward which all mouths turn.' Tosafos, Tur, and Beis Yosef rule accordingly. Rabbeinu Yonah explains that one in the Diaspora intends not only toward Eretz Yisrael, but also toward Jerusalem, the Temple, and the Holy of Holies; one in Jerusalem still intends toward the Temple and Holy of Holies. This is because the gate of Heaven is there and all prayers ascend through it [Chayei Adam]. Sifrei derives the law from Moshe's viewing the land: just as his distant sight helped bring blessing into Eretz Yisrael, directing one's face and heart toward it helps draw the Divine flow, which descends first there and then spreads outward [Sifrei Devei Rav]. Minchas Aharon adds that facing the proper direction serves as a sign to maintain, 'I have set Hashem before me always.'
(2) There: If he was standing in the Diaspora, he should turn his face toward Eretz Yisrael and also intend toward Jerusalem, etc. This wording implies that only his face must be toward Eretz Yisrael, while intention alone suffices for Jerusalem and the Temple [Prishah]. Kol Bo, however, implies that wherever one stands his face should be directed toward the Temple. Its statement that one in the Diaspora faces Jerusalem means Jerusalem together with the Temple, relying on its opening rule. The Gemara's phrase 'direct his heart toward Eretz Yisrael' thus means both face and heart, and Eretz Yisrael includes Jerusalem and the Temple, as Rabbeinu Yonah explains. Eliyah Rabbah understands Levush and Kol Bo likewise, and Chayei Adam also implies this.
(3) There: He should also intend toward the Temple. Through this intention it is considered as though we stand within it and pray, as Rabbeinu Tur writes in the following siman [Yafeh LaLev].
(4) There: If he was standing in Jerusalem, etc. If gentiles forced him into the Temple to perform work, and the time for prayer arrived while he stood between the Western Wall and the Holy of Holies, he should turn toward the Holy of Holies like all other worshippers. It is of no concern that his back faces the Western Wall [Radbaz 678; Yad Aharon; Birkei Yosef]. Regarding Birkei Yosef's citation from Pe'er HaDor of Rambam, see what we wrote above, siman 90, paragraph 41.
(5) There: If he stood behind the Kapores, etc. This means west of the Temple, outside the Western Wall [Magen Avraham; Yafeh LaLev]. Today it is forbidden to stand inside the Temple precinct, since all are impure through contact with the dead and we lack the ashes of the red heifer [Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Rambam, Hilchos Bi'as HaMikdash 3]. Now that the Temple is destroyed and we do not know the precise locations of the Cheil and the Azarah, one may not enter anywhere in the Temple area even if pure from every other impurity and bearing only corpse impurity. See Megillah 28a and Shemos Rabbah 2.
