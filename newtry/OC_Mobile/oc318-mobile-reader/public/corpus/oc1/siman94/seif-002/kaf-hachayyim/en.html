(6) [Se'if 2] If he prays facing another direction, he should angle his face, etc. Magen Avraham explains that 'angle' rather than 'turn completely' concerns a place whose custom fixes that direction, or one riding a donkey; Shulchan Aruch HaRav agrees. Maamar Mordechai explains it as one facing north to become wealthy or south to become wise, who must nevertheless angle his face toward Eretz Yisrael. Eshel Avraham objects that this would not explain why the Rema states what the Mechaber already taught. Neveh Shalom therefore explains that some circumstance prevents him from facing Eretz Yisrael fully; for example, the congregation has begun and no suitable place remains. He should at least angle toward Eretz Yisrael, which is easy. Without such cause he must face Eretz Yisrael; if he wishes to face north or south, he should only angle his face that way.
(7) There: He should angle his face, etc. Taz writes that this remedy helps only one facing north or south. One who mistakenly began facing west should not turn sideways, for then he would face north or south; neither need he interrupt and uproot his feet to face east, since one opinion holds the Divine Presence is everywhere and may be relied upon after the fact when others are not then facing east. Eliyah Rabbah says it is better to turn south, which also inclines toward Eretz Yisrael. Yad Eliyahu requires interrupting and moving to face east; later authorities dispute this. Maamar Mordechai argues that whenever correction is possible it should be made by turning and moving his feet slightly; walking during prayer is allowed when necessary, as in siman 104, and here he barely moves. Tikkunei Zohar forbids praying westward, the direction called 'behind.' Those east of Eretz Yisrael pray west because the Temple is in that direction and the Divine Presence never left its Western Wall. Those west of Eretz Yisrael may not face west. If one mistakenly did so, he should turn east; if north or south inclines toward Eretz Yisrael, he may turn there instead. This necessary turn is not an interruption. If he remembered only after completing the prayer, he need not repeat it, as implied by Rambam at the beginning of Hilchos Tefillah 5.
(8) There, gloss: We who turn our faces eastward, etc. Since prayer must face east, the custom is to place the sanctuary containing the Torah scroll on the eastern wall. Even if it was placed on another wall, one must still face east in prayer [Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Chakan HaLevi; Knesses HaGedolah; Olas Tamid; Magen Avraham; Eliyah Rabbah; and others].
(8) If one prays in a gentile home and idols stand in the east, it is forbidden to pray in that direction; he should face another direction even though it is not east [Chayei Adam 22:10; Kitzur Shulchan Aruch 18:10]. See below, paragraph 36, and above, siman 90:23.
(9) In a synagogue where the Aron Kodesh was placed on the southern side and the entire congregation faces it, Yad Eliyahu permits an individual to face east without concern for arrogance or hostility. A great authority there disagrees: when praying with the congregation, he should face their direction. Chesed LaAlafim sharply criticizes those who place the Aron on another side merely for beauty. This is not beauty but disgrace. One wishing to beautify the mitzvah should, where possible, place the Aron and eastern wall toward the southeastern direction; that is beauty and honor to the Omnipresent, judging according to the holy Torah rather than human appearance.
