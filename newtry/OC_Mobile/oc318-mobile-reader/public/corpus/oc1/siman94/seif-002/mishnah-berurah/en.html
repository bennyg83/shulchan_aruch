Toward one of the other directions. For example, if he wishes to become wise or wealthy, for Chazal said: one who wishes to become wise should turn southward; to become wealthy, northward. Or, for example, if he is riding on a donkey and cannot turn himself toward Eretz Yisrael, nevertheless he should turn his face somewhat, to fulfill what is stated, "and they will pray to You by way of their land."
And we who turn, etc. Since one must pray toward the east, the practice is to set the ark in which the Sefer Torah is kept into the eastern wall. If he cannot set it in the east, he should set it in the south, but in any event not in the west, so that the backs of the people not face the ark. However, even if they set the ark in another wall, one who prays must turn his face toward the east.
Our faces to the east. If he stood facing north or south and they reminded him, it is forbidden for him to uproot his feet to turn eastward; rather, he should bend his face toward the east, and that suffices, even if he is praying with others whose faces are toward the east. If this is impossible, or he is standing with his face to the west, he should direct his heart toward the Holy of Holies and not uproot his feet. However, if he is praying in a synagogue, where the matter is conspicuous, the Peri Megadim inclines to say that he should turn himself toward the congregation, and so ruled the sefer Shulchan Shlomo. A synagogue in which they placed the Aron Kodesh toward the south of the world and everyone prays toward the Aron Kodesh, namely toward the south, even though this is improper, as above in seif katan 9, nevertheless one who comes to pray there should pray toward the direction in which the congregation prays, but should turn his face somewhat toward the east.
Opposite midday. This wording is not precise; rather, it means approximately half an hour or an hour afterward, for then the sun has already shifted considerably from its place. All this is estimated at the time of the tekufah of Nisan or Tishrei, or one week before it. See the Biur Halacha, where I copied the wording of the Levush on this. See the later authorities, who all agreed to the words of the Levush, that in our countries, since we do not sit exactly west of Eretz Yisrael but also incline to its north, they should position the synagogue wall so that it inclines slightly toward the southeastern side of the world, for then it will stand directly opposite Eretz Yisrael, the Beis HaMikdash, and the Holy of Holies. So too, every country according to its location should position the synagogue according to its direction. In the sefer Lechem Chamudos he justified the common practice, but nevertheless it also appears from him that it is better to do as the Levush says. If one happens to be in a place where the wall is exactly opposite the east, he should turn his face toward the southeast; see there. However, if it happens that he is standing and praying on the southern side of the Aron Kodesh, he should not do so, for then he will appear as turning his back and not his face toward the Aron Kodesh; see there in Lechem Chamudos. The Peri Megadim wrote that in places where they make the wall exactly toward the east, it is good to make the rabbi’s place north of the Aron Kodesh, so that he can turn southward and become wise, for from the south he cannot turn further south, as above. In places where they make it as the Levush says, it is proper to seat the rabbi to the right of the ark, unless this will remove a dispute; then he should not be particular.
He should turn his face somewhat, etc. Rashi explained the opposite, that he should stand toward the east and turn his face somewhat to the south or north; and this is our practice, and it is correct, so that his standing should not differ from that of the congregation. The Peri Megadim wrote that in places where they follow the Levush, meaning that the wall faces southeast, one need not turn southward, for it is already so in any case; and it is also impossible to turn northward, for it appears as turning his face away from Eretz Yisrael.
