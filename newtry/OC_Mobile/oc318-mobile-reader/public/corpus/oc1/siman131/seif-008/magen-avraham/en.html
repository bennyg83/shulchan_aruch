To prostrate. If one inclines somewhat to the side, it is permitted, as stated at the beginning of this siman.
With full extension. The prohibition applies specifically when one’s face is pressed against the ground. If one merely bows during prayer, it is permitted even on a stone floor. So appears from Rivash. See Responsa Mahari ben Lev, part 1, siman 53, which prohibits marble stones, while Knesses HaGedolah permits them. Nefilas Apayim, however, is prohibited even when one’s face is not pressed against the ground, since his intention is not merely to bow but to fall upon his face, as explained above. See the Tur. It appears to me that a brick floor is not prohibited, since Scripture says ‘stone,’ and a brick is not a stone, as it says, ‘The brick served them as stone.’ This is also implied by Rambam, at the end of chapter 6 of Hilchos Avodah Zarah, and by Mahari ben Lev, part 1, siman 53.
And they should do likewise on Yom Kippur. Meaning, they should incline onto their sides.
If they spread coverings. Meaning, if they spread grasses to interpose between themselves and the floor, then prostration is permitted with an inclination. Some texts read, ‘or if they spread,’ meaning that in that case it is permitted even without inclining. The prohibition without an inclination applies specifically upon the ground, lest a stone floor lie beneath it and the ground was built over it. Earth is not considered an interposition, since the ground of a house is legally continuous down to the depths, as stated in chapter 15 of Keilim. Grasses, however, are considered an interposition. Rambam writes likewise in Hilchos Avodah Zarah, and this is the primary ruling, unlike Rivash, siman 412; see there. This implies that on Rosh Hashanah grasses are unnecessary, because people do not fall upon their faces but merely kneel and bow during Aleinu. On Yom Kippur, however, they fall upon their faces during the Avodah service.
