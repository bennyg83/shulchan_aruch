Not in a mourner’s house. Beit Yosef, in the name of Shibolei HaLeket, explains that the reason is the verse, ‘I will turn your festivals into mourning.’ Accordingly, this applies throughout all seven days, since mourning is compared to a festival, namely the seven days of the festival. It is also explained there that other confessions and supplications are not recited in the mourner’s house. Nevertheless, it appears to me that a distinction should be made. Nefilas Apayim should not be recited by the other worshippers at all, either in the mourner’s house or after they leave, because it was established at the beginning of the siman that it must be recited immediately after the Amidah. Other supplications, however, such as VeHu Rachum, should certainly be recited by the other worshippers in their own homes after leaving the mourner’s house. Why should they be exempt? It is a personal obligation that we accepted for every Monday and Thursday, as stated in siman 134. So it appears to me.
And not in a groom’s house. In this case it appears that even after one returns home he need not recite supplications such as VeHu Rachum, since he was in the synagogue at the time of prayer and the joy extended to him. The same applies at a bris. This differs from a mourner’s house, where omission is not because the mourner’s sorrow extends to those present. The mourner himself is not exempt because of sorrow, but because the Attribute of Judgment rules over him, and it is improper then to arouse that attribute through Nefilas Apayim, which likewise alludes to judgment, as Levush writes. The others therefore should not arouse the Attribute of Judgment there, but after leaving they remain obligated. However, according to what was written in seif 1, that one may not speak between the Amidah and Tachanun, the same should apply automatically to VeHu Rachum, since it is recited between the Amidah and Tachanun. Since it cannot be recited in its proper place, immediately after the Amidah, perhaps it should not be recited afterward either. Rama writes that this exemption applies specifically on the wedding day, as Beit Yosef writes in the name of Shibolei HaLeket. This is difficult, for all seven days of celebration are called a moed with respect to a groom, as stated in the name of Riva at the beginning of siman 132. Why, then, should Tachanun be recited on the other days when the groom is present? On Rosh Chodesh Nefilas Apayim is omitted because it is called a moed, as Beit Yosef writes in the name of Shibolei HaLeket himself, and here too all seven days are called a moed. It therefore appears to me that throughout all seven days the groom should not come to the synagogue, so that the congregation will not omit Tachanun because of him.
Specifically at Shacharis. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, writes that Bach reached this conclusion because Hagahos Maimoniyos states that Nefilas Apayim is not performed during the Yotzer prayer on the day of a bris. This implies that it is omitted specifically during Yotzer, but not at Minchah. This is no proof, because it may mean only that at Minchah it is recited when the congregation is not praying in the infant’s presence, whereas in the infant’s presence it is omitted even at Minchah. Maharshal ruled accordingly, and this is the practice in Brisk of Lithuania. It likewise appears proper to be lenient, since Nefilas Apayim is optional, as Rav Natrunai writes in the Tur. I have seen this practiced in Krakow as well.
