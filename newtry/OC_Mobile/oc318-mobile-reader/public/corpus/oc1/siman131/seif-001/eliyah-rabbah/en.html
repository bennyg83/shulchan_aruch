One should not speak, etc. This is because the prayer does not prevent Nefilat Apayim. So writes the Beit Yosef. His precise wording implies that if one did speak, nothing further is required and he may still fall on his face, although the supplication is not accepted as readily. So writes Olat Tamid. See above siman 51, seif 7 and seif katan 2. The Rama writes that ordinary conversation is not a concern unless one turns to other matters. Sefer Chasidim writes that one may pray in one place and perform Nefilat Apayim in another. The Seder HaYom writes that one should recite the chapter 'To You, Hashem, I lift my soul.' The later authorities write in the name of the Zohar that after completing Rachum VeChanun one should say this chapter and intend to surrender his life to death because of his sins. The Zohar warns very strongly that this intention must be wholehearted; otherwise, God forbid, one causes premature death to himself, because Scripture says that one who speaks falsehood cannot stand before Hashem. Magen Avraham writes that this is apparently why this chapter is not recited in these lands. Abudarham writes that some do recite it because David composed this chapter of Tehillim alphabetically, but three letters are absent, forming a word meaning emptiness, alluding to the emptiness of deficient repentance. In Ayalah Sheluchah I found that one who says this chapter every day does not see the face of Gehinnom. It appears that outside the specific time of saying Rachum VeChanun, the Zohar's concern does not apply.
The left side, etc. The left side is the side associated with the angels' support, and therefore one must humble oneself there. Kol Bo, siman 19, gives another reason: one assumes a posture of freedom, because through Nefilat Apayim all his sins are forgiven when he confesses and falls on his face, as Moshe Rabbeinu did when he said that he remained upon the mountain. Kol Bo further writes that one leans left in remembrance of the daily offering, which was slaughtered while lying on its left side. Mateh Moshe writes that this recalls Yitzchak Avinu at the Akedah, who lay on his left side. Some have the custom to lean right because of the verse 'His right hand embraces me.' Yet the Beit Yosef, citing Shibolei HaLeket, writes that this very reason indicates one should lean left, because the Shechinah's right side is opposite the person's left when the Shechinah faces him. The Beit Yosef writes that this is the accepted practice. The Rama's statement that in honor of the tefillin one should lean right at Shacharit appears to conflict with this. I found in Shibolei HaLeket that there is no concern for degrading the tefillin, because their place is high on the arm over the muscle; when one lies on the left side, the tefillin remain above while the forearm is below. Since he bends toward the heart, every pressure of the heart upon the tefillin increases their function as a remembrance. Sefer Tanya writes similarly. Therefore I say that one should at least follow the compromise proposed by the Levush and Bach, unlike the Taz and Magen Avraham, who reject it without sufficient reason. Mateh Moshe writes in the name of Maharil that a chazzan sitting with his back to the south should fall toward the right so that he bends his head toward the bimah, with the mnemonic 'His right hand embraces me.' Olat Tamid, following Magen Avraham, rules that he should lean left. To me this requires study. Anyone who examines Maharil will see that he follows those whose practice is always to lean right, based on 'His right hand embraces me.' Thus he writes that the chazzan should sit facing such that his back is to the south. The same would apply if he happened to sit with his back to the north: according to Maharil he would still lean right, even though this would be away from the bimah. But according to us, who rule that one without tefillin leans left, the chazzan should ideally sit with his back to the north, and even if he sits with his back to the south he should still lean left. The Rama and all the early poskim do not distinguish based on leaning toward the Ark. Perhaps Mateh Moshe independently reasons that where the chazzan sits directly before the bimah with his back to the south one may rely on those who lean right out of honor for the Ark. This requires study. In any event, it seems to me that he speaks specifically of the chazzan sitting directly before the bimah. Otherwise every congregant seated on the east, south, or north sides of the synagogue should have been instructed to lean toward the Ark, as Shiyarei Knesset HaGedolah writes in the name of Mahari Bruna. Why specify the chazzan? Clearly it is as I explained, unlike Magen Avraham; see there. Sefer HaManhig writes that one should cover his face with a tallit or cloth and not with his own hand, because his sins are written there.
A little while seated, etc. The Shelah, folio 255, writes that he saw a worthy chazzan who would say the words 'And we do not know what to do' aloud. He said 'And we do not know' while seated, and 'what to do' while standing, and said that this was a received tradition. Know also that the correct wording is 'He is appeased and reconciled,' whereas those who say 'and is enticed' are incorrect, because the Ramban in Parashat Mishpatim writes that enticement refers to inducing someone by means of falsehood.
While seated, etc. However, if one finishes the Amidah and the chazzan immediately sits for Tachanun, or begins Barechu, or on Motzaei Shabbos begins Vihi Noam, he should remain in his place and cover his face according to all opinions. So writes Olat Shabbat. See above siman 123, where it is written that the kabbalists agree to this, unlike Magen Avraham. It seems to me that the same applies when one says Tachanun without Nefilat Apayim, where there is no requirement to sit. It also seems to me that if another person is praying directly in front of him, he may not sit, as explained above in siman 102.
