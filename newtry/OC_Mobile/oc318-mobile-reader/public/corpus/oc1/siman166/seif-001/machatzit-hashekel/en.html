Seif katan 1: Some say, etc. Nevertheless, it is forbidden, etc. This opinion is that of Rabbi Yoel and those who agree with him. They hold that the statement that a blessing must immediately follow washing of the hands refers specifically to mayim acharonim, not to the first washing. They were challenged from the Mishnah in Berachos, chapter Eilu Devarim: Beis Hillel say that one first pours the wine and afterward washes his hands. One Baraisa explains that Beis Hillel hold this because a blessing must immediately follow washing of the hands. This proves that even regarding the first washing, the blessing must immediately follow the washing. See Taz in the name of Bach.
Magen Avraham, and likewise Bach, hold in accordance with Tosafos in Pesachim. Tosafos ask: according to the Gemara's conclusion there in accordance with Rav, that one may wash his hands and afterward recite Kiddush, contrary to Rav Bruna, and we are not concerned for diversion of attention between washing and HaMotzi, the ruling of Beis Hillel that one first pours the wine and afterward washes remains difficult. Tosafos answer that pouring wine is different because it is mixed with hot water and requires precision not to make it deficient or excessive, and therefore involves a greater diversion of attention than Kiddush. Rabbeinu Tam alternatively explains that the case there refers to a weekday. Magen Avraham writes that Rabbi Yoel too may accept Tosafos' first answer. In truth, regarding pouring wine or a similar activity, Rabbi Yoel concedes that it constitutes diversion of attention and that one must wash his hands again and recite the blessing.
And Agudah writes in the name of Rabbeinu Tam, etc. Perhaps he will divert his attention, etc. According to this explanation, the concern applies only initially, lest he divert his attention. After the fact, however, if he knows that he did not divert his attention, he need not wash again. Magen Avraham does not mean that Rabbi Yoel, who is the first opinion in the Shulchan Aruch, also holds this way, for how then could the Shulchan Aruch write that one need not be careful about interrupting? He must at least be careful on a weekday. Rather, Magen Avraham means that even according to the second opinion, which requires caution, one can say that Kiddush and Havdalah do not constitute diversion of attention.
The same applies to Havdalah, etc. In truth, this explanation, cited in the name of Agudah quoting Rabbeinu Tam, is also written by Tosafos there in Pesachim in the name of Rabbeinu Tam. Magen Avraham cited Agudah because Tosafos did not mention permitting Havdalah. One might have thought that only Kiddush was permitted, because we rule that Kiddush must be recited where the meal is eaten. There is therefore an independent reason that Kiddush does not constitute an interruption, since it is connected to the meal. Rabbeinu Tam's reasoning also applies more strongly, for we are not concerned that he will divert his attention, leave, and fail to eat immediately, since he must eat close to Kiddush. This does not apply to Havdalah. Magen Avraham therefore cited Agudah, who explicitly mentions that the same applies to Havdalah. Agudah's proof that Havdalah has the same law as Kiddush for this purpose is that the ruling in Pesachim, that one who washed his hands may recite Kiddush afterward, was stated in the name of Rav, yet Rav holds there, on 100b, that Kiddush may be recited away from the place of the meal, although we do not rule like Rav on that point.
