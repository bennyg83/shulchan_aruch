Some say one need not be careful. Nevertheless, one may not mix hot water into a cup between washing and HaMotzi, because this requires attention to the proportions and creates a significant diversion. The same applies to similar tasks. Rabbeinu Tam writes that on a weekday it is forbidden lest one become distracted and leave, whereas on Shabbos, when the table is already prepared, one may wash, recite Kiddush, and eat. The same applies to Havdalah.
It is proper to be careful. Shelah writes that before washing each day one should pray to Hashem for sustenance. If he forgot until after washing, he may interrupt for this prayer. The common practice is to recite Tehillim 23, “Hashem is my Shepherd,” between washing and HaMotzi. Maharshal writes that the host should allow the greatest person to wash last. If water was brought to him first, however, he should not refuse, since it is improper to say, “I will not interrupt, but others may interrupt.” He should wash, sit, and remain silent. If asked a Torah question, he may answer. Bach forbids even Torah speech and considers sitting itself an interruption. Accordingly, the greatest person may refuse to wash first, because an interruption by the one who will break bread is more problematic than an interruption by the others.
Twenty-two cubits. Tosafot derive this measure from the metzora, who placed his hands on the offering near Nikanor Gate and the animal was then taken to the altar, a distance of twenty-two cubits. This was still treated as immediate juxtaposition of semichah and slaughter. Nevertheless, other sources imply that even a short walk from place to place may constitute an interruption. Therefore one should avoid any unnecessary interruption between washing and HaMotzi. Rama’s ruling in Yoreh Deah, siman 19, concerning blessing four cubits before entering the slaughterhouse, requires further study; the source in Agudah appears to concern movement within one building, like moving from one corner to another.
