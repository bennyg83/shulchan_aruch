Even an incidental sleep. This is unlike Tur and Rosh, who permit an incidental sleep. Beit Yosef asks why Rosh and Tur did not cite the Gemara's measure for an incidental sleep, taught by Rami bar Yechezkel as the time required to walk one hundred amos. He answers that another baraisa states: one who enters to sleep during the day may either remove his tefillin or leave them on. This baraisa disagrees with the measure of one hundred amos and instead holds that daytime sleep is permitted because it is ordinarily incidental, as stated in Sukkah. This is extremely difficult. Rashi explains even that second baraisa by saying that during the day a person does not ordinarily sleep together with his wife and does not ordinarily sleep a fixed sleep. Thus, that baraisa also depends upon the distinction between incidental and fixed sleep. What difference exists between the incidental sleep in that baraisa and the incidental sleep in the baraisa of Rami bar Yechezkel? Furthermore, in the chapter HaYashen in Sukkah, daytime sleep is described as sixty breaths, as Shulchan Aruch states above in siman 4, seif 16, and this was King David's practice. Who says that sixty breaths and the time required to walk one hundred amos are not the same measure? It appears to me that we are entirely unable to measure the time required to walk one hundred amos, as Beit Yosef writes in the name of Raavad. Therefore, one should be stringent and rule that he may not sleep while wearing tefillin at all. Rosh and Tur, however, did not see fit to be stringent in this way. The Torah was not given to ministering angels or only to exceptionally learned people. Since the entire prohibition is merely a Rabbinic safeguard, we follow the lenient approach. A person may therefore sleep an incidental sleep according to ordinary human practice. This is comparable to Tur in siman 47, citing Rosh, that sleeping while resting one's head upon his arms is considered incidental sleep. This appears to be Tur's position. Shulchan Aruch, however, rules stringently here like Rambam. Beit Yosef further cites Rosh and Rabbeinu Yonah: One might ask how an incidental sleep is permitted, since one may not divert his attention from tefillin. The answer is that distraction applies only when one engages in frivolity and laughter. When he dozes, there is no distraction, because he forgets worldly vanities.
