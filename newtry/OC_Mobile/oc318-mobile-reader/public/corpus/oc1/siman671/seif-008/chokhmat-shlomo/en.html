Do we say that since he does not fulfill the Chanukah obligation with it, the suspicion remains in its place? Or perhaps, granted, if he did not light at all at this entrance, people will say that since he did not light here, he also did not light there. But if they see that he is lighting outside, only the reason it does not help is, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write below, because one who sees says that he lit it for his own need. If so, this is understandable where he lit only this one, for perhaps even the one who sees considers that it may be for a Chanukah lamp, but since the mitzvah of the Chanukah lamp is to publicize the miracle, we require a clear and revealed publicity; since here it is not publicized, for perhaps he took it for his own need, it is not considered publicizing the miracle. This is understandable where he lights only this one. But if in truth he lights in another place, and there is only a concern of suspicion that people will say that since he did not light here, he did not light there either, that is understandable where he does not light at all. But if he lights, only he holds it in his hand, then at least it is possible that he holds it for the Chanukah lamp, and there is no suspicion, for the one who sees considers that perhaps it is for the Chanukah lamp. Although he has not fulfilled the obligation with this, nevertheless one can properly assume that he lit at the other entrance and that there it is not himself standing there, and it is certainly resting in its place according to law. It appears to me to resolve this doubt from what the Tur and Shulchan Aruch wrote below in siman 675: if one lit the Chanukah lamp inside and brought it outside, he has not fulfilled the obligation, because one who sees says that he lit it for his own need. The later authorities wondered, for this was needed in the Talmud only if we say that placement performs the mitzvah, but according to what we hold, that lighting performs the mitzvah, it is already clear in the Talmud without this that he has not fulfilled the obligation, because we require lighting in its place. Why then did the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write this reason? According to the above, it works well. One can say that the Tur teaches this because there is a practical difference regarding a courtyard with two entrances, where one must light on both sides. If he lit on one side according to law, and on the second side he lit inside and brought it outside, has he fulfilled the requirement or not? The Beis Yosef asked why here we are concerned for suspicion, while regarding a synagogue above in siman 90 we say the opposite. He answered that there, there is no financial loss, but with a Chanukah lamp there is financial loss; see there. If so, it is proven that where there is no financial loss, suspicion does not apply. According to this, if we did not know that when he lit inside people might say it was for his own need, but only said that certainly it was for the Chanukah lamp and it does not help because we require lighting in its place, then regarding two sides there would be no concern. Although here it was lit inside and brought outside, they would say that in the other place it was lit properly; here there is no financial gain, since we see that in the end he is lighting. It would then be exactly like the synagogue, and they would say that there he lights properly and would not suspect that there too he lit inside and brought it outside, since there is no monetary gain in that. But when they used the reason that perhaps one will say that he lit it for his own need, financial gain again applies and there is suspicion: they will say that since he did not light here, he did not light there either. Therefore it does not help. This is a practical difference for a courtyard with two entrances if he lit inside and brought it outside. Do not ask why the Tur then wrote the main reason that we require lighting in its place, for there is a practical difference in the opposite direction regarding the blessing. Since he extinguishes it and lights it again, if we require lighting in its place, he has not fulfilled anything at all and must recite the blessing again. But if by law lighting inside would have helped, and only because of a decree he must go back and light it, he would not need a second blessing, as the Rama wrote here in the name of the Ran, that where it is only because of suspicion, a blessing is not needed. One might have been forced from this to say that the Tur does not hold like the Ran, but rather that even where it is because of suspicion a blessing is needed, and then according to either reason a second blessing is needed. Although in the end he must go back and light it because of concern that one who sees says he took it for his own need, nevertheless there is a practical difference: if placement performs the mitzvah and he has not yet done anything, he must recite the blessing again; but if it is only because of concern that one who sees says it is for his own need, it is only because of suspicion, and for suspicion one does not need a blessing. Why did they not write this practical difference? Perforce, it would seem that the Tur does not hold like the Ran, but holds that even because of suspicion one needs a blessing. This implies that it was more obvious there than here that one should be concerned for suspicion. According to the Beis Yosef and those who agree with him, the suspicion here is stronger than there; if so, why there does the Talmud not ask from where we know that we are concerned for suspicion, while here it does ask? It is explicitly implied here in the Talmud that we say that people will say: since he did not light at this entrance, he did not light at that one either. It is proven that only because of this proof will they say so, 'since he did not light here'; without such proof there is no room for suspicion. Now, the Magen Avraham wrote above in siman 90 that even if a person has a nearby synagogue, it is a mitzvah to go to a farther one because of reward for steps; see there. Therefore, granted, here, where there is no reason to light at this entrance more than at that one, since both are equally open to the public domain, what difference is there between this one and that one? Therefore people will say: since he did not light here, etc. But there, with a synagogue, if there are two entrances and he walks before this entrance, then this entrance is close to him according to the way he is currently walking. One can properly say that this is why he does not go through this entrance, but adds more steps to go to the other side and enter through the second entrance, because he wants to receive reward for steps. Just as one who has a nearby synagogue should go to the farther one, so too if one entrance is close to him, it is a mitzvah to go through the farther entrance to receive reward for steps. It is therefore not applicable to say: since he did not enter here, he did not enter there either. Thus there is no room for suspicion there at all. This is clear; analyze. According to this, it appears to me as a new law that what the Talmud and poskim wrote, that a courtyard with two entrances should light at both, refers to where both are equally open to the public domain, so that the two sides are equal. But if one side opens to a place where many people are very common, and one side opens to a place where many people are not so common, as we find in many streets and marketplaces where on one side many people are found and in the other marketplace many people do not pass so much, in such a case he need light only at the entrance that opens to the place where many people are very common. In this case it is not applicable to say that since he did not light here he did not light there either, for the Talmud concludes that according to Rav Yosef we are not concerned for suspicion of the world at large, but only for suspicion of the people of the city. If so, they know that on the other side more people are common than here. Therefore it is more reasonable to light there and not here than to do the opposite. In this case it is comparable to a synagogue, where we are not concerned for suspicion. This is clear and correct in my opinion as a matter of law.
