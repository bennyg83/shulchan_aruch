A minor, etc. It appears obvious that according to the Mechaber, if for some reason the father did not purchase tefillin for his son, such as because the son could not keep them in purity or the like, then once the son becomes thirteen years and one day old the mitzvah of chinuch no longer rests upon the father. The son is now an adult and personally obligated in all the mitzvos. If he is poor, all of Israel are obligated to provide for this need, as Peri Megadim writes in seif katan 4.
To purchase for him. It requires study whether a father who has two sons must purchase tefillin for each one, or whether he fulfills his obligation by first training one son and then, after that son's prayer, giving them to the second son. Likewise, if he has one son and can borrow tefillin for him, or give him his own tefillin for Krias Shema and prayer throughout the entire period of chinuch until he becomes an adult, perhaps this suffices. It is possible that the baraisa's wording, “His father purchases tefillin for him,” specifically reflects their time, when people wore tefillin all day and it was uncommon for one person to lend his tefillin to another. Nowadays the situation differs. Proof may be brought from the fact that the baraisa does not use this expression regarding lulav. I later found that Bach somewhat implies our reasoning, though his words can be rejected. The matter requires study.
And some say, etc. Peri Megadim writes: “I do not understand. If he is thirteen years old, what obligation remains upon his father? He is an independent adult. If he is poor, all of Israel are commanded to sustain him.” He suggests that the case may be one of a thirteen-year-old who has not developed two pubic hairs. According to this, perhaps the father remains obligated in chinuch until the son's majority of years if he has not developed two hairs. Even though at that stage the son is obligated by law because of Rava's presumption, as cited in Shaarei Teshuvah, one might still say that the father's obligation of chinuch does not end so long as it is not known that the son developed two hairs. This is exceedingly forced. See below, siman 225, seif 2, where when the son turns thirteen the father recites the blessing “Who has exempted me,” meaning from the mitzvah of chinuch according to Magen Avraham there. None of the authorities qualify this as applying only when it is known that he developed two hairs. Clearly, for the Rabbinic obligation of chinuch we may rely upon Rava's presumption. The truth is that Peri Megadim apparently did not see Sefer HaIttur, part 2, which had not yet been printed in his time. HaIttur's words show that, according to his view, this law was not stated in the Gemara concerning a Rabbinic enactment at all. He implies that there was no such enactment. Rather, the issue concerns the Torah obligation: a thirteen-year-old is not obligated in tefillin unless he knows how to guard them. HaIttur brings proof from the Mechilta on the verse, “You shall observe this statute,” and this applies even if he developed two hairs, just like the obligation of mezuzah mentioned there. See there. Regarding Peri Megadim's question why the father is involved, see Birkei Yosef, where the matter is resolved. All of this follows HaIttur's approach. We, however, rule like all the other poskim, as Beit Yosef writes, that the Gemara's law was not stated regarding an adult but only regarding a minor. Nevertheless, common practice follows HaIttur stringently by not having a child put on tefillin.
That this minor specifically, etc. Meaning that at that stage the law depends upon whether he knows how to guard his tefillin. This is HaIttur's approach, as explicitly stated there. Our present custom not to be exacting about this may be explained in two ways. Perhaps Rama accepted HaIttur's practice only to the extent that a child should not put on tefillin before bar mitzvah, but not regarding the additional requirement of demonstrated ability to guard them. Darkei Moshe somewhat supports this. Alternatively, all of this applied specifically in their time, when tefillin were worn all day and guarding them was difficult. Nowadays they are worn only during Krias Shema and prayer, and we ordinarily assume that when a child becomes thirteen he knows how to guard them, as explained in seif 2, unless we specifically know that he does not.
And this is the custom. See Mishnah Berurah. Peri Megadim inclines toward Bach's ruling that at least a twelve-year-old child who studies Gemara and understands may put on tefillin, for such a child may certainly be relied upon to guard himself from the matters discussed immediately below.
