(1) It is permitted to make a fire. Although the Torah states, “Only that which may be eaten by every person, that alone may be prepared for you,” we rule that since kindling was permitted for food preparation, it was also permitted for purposes other than food preparation, provided that the benefit is common to all people and is needed on Yom Tov. Some say that every form of bodily benefit is included in “that which may be eaten by every person,” provided that it is common to all people.
(2) Some say that it is forbidden. This applies specifically when done by a Jew, but all agree that it is permitted through a non-Jew. The custom is to be lenient even when a Jew does it, as explained below.
(3) To warm the winter room, etc. This means that no food is cooked there and the fire is made only for warmth.
(4) For sometimes the cold is not severe. Meaning, when the cold is not severe enough that ordinary people are generally troubled by it. If Yom Tov happens to be very cold, as sometimes occurs on Pesach or Sukkos, all agree that it is permitted. Even when it is not cold enough to require a fire for warmth, if fatty foods that were cooked on the stove will congeal because of the cold, all agree that one may heat the winter room and place them there so that they do not congeal, since this is an actual food preparation need.
(5) Rather, only for pampered people. Since this benefit is needed only by pampered people, it is not considered common to all.
(6) Like a bathhouse and incense. This refers to the law in se'if 2 that one may not heat water for washing the entire body for this reason, and to the law of incense in se'if 4 below, whose prohibition is likewise based on this reason.
(7) And the custom is to be lenient. Since the primary act of kindling for protection from the cold is a permitted melacha because it is a benefit common to all, we are not particular whether the cold is severe or mild.
