The five grain species. They are wheat, barley, spelt, oats, and rye. What we call tatraki is not included. See what I wrote in siman 454.
That he boiled or crushed them. The opening implies that it discusses boiling without crushing. This is difficult, for in seif 4 he writes that when whole kernels are boiled one recites Borei Peri HaAdamah. Perhaps here it refers to boiling them until they become mashed. This is also implied by Levush, seif 4; see there, and below, seif 7. Rabbeinu Yonah writes: “Specifically when the wheat was crushed and adhered together, becoming like a cooked porridge; but if he cooked whole wheat and the kernels remain whole, he recites Borei Peri HaAdamah.” This must mean that when the wheat was crushed and adhered, it adhered through cooking, for crushing alone certainly does not make it adhere. “If he cooked the wheat whole” means that he did not remove the husk, and therefore the kernels remain intact and do not adhere through cooking; the blessing is then Borei Peri HaAdamah. Rambam’s wording, “if he split or crushed them,” implies that “crushed” applies even if he did not split them but merely removed their husk. This is also implied by his statement concerning rifos, namely barley whose husk has been removed, and by what is written in seif 4. The Gemara, however, does not teach rifos but only “split,” which according to Rashi means that each kernel was divided into two, as stated at the end of chapter 2 of Moed Katan. Accordingly, Bach’s ruling that the barley or wheat called whole gersten should be eaten only during a meal, as written in seif 4, applies where they did not adhere through cooking. If they adhered through cooking, one recites Borei Minei Mezonos, provided that they were thoroughly mashed, as Tosafos writes. The same law applies to whole gritz. See Shelah. See also what I wrote in siman 205, seif 2, that the liquid has the same law as the food itself.
