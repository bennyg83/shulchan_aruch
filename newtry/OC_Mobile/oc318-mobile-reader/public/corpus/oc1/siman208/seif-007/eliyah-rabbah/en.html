[9] [Levush] In the Ashkenazic language, rice, etc. Emek Berachah and Shelah, page 92, write that since some explain the terms in the opposite manner, identifying orez as hirz and dochan as rice, every person should take care to eat them only during a meal. If one must eat them outside a meal, because of the doubt he should recite Shehakol over both. Bach likewise rules stringently for this reason. In my humble opinion, there is an additional reason for stringency. I found in Shibolei HaLeket and Sefer Tanya, in the names of Rav Sherira Gaon and Rav Yehudai Gaon, that Borei Minei Mezonos is recited only over the five grain species, not over rice or millet. See there. Shelah further writes that this stringency applies only when they were thoroughly mashed in cooking. If they were not mashed and the kernels remain whole, whether rice or millet, one may initially eat them outside a meal and recite Borei Peri HaAdamah. Bach, however, is stringent even when they are whole, because Beit Yosef is uncertain how to interpret Rabbeinu Yonah. See there. I found in Kol Bo, page 19, in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah: “Even if one ate it whole as it is,” and so forth. Thus, it is explicit that there is no distinction regarding rice between completely whole kernels and mashed kernels. This is astonishing regarding Beit Yosef and all the later authorities. It is therefore proper to be stringent in accordance with Bach. Perhaps for this reason Shulchan Aruch states the law without qualification and does not distinguish whether it was mashed, whereas Rama and Levush do distinguish, though they did not formulate the matter correctly. The later authorities write that tatraka, namely buckwheat, always receives Shehakol and Borei Nefashos according to all opinions. Magen Avraham writes that regarding a dish made from oats called whole gritz, into which much water is placed so that it is fit only to be drunk as soup, perhaps the water is not subordinate to the kernels. One should then recite Borei Peri HaAdamah over the kernels and Shehakol separately over the water, since the main intent is for the water. See above, siman 205, seif 2.
[Levush] The majority is rice, etc. Although Bach disagrees and Olat Tamid cites him, Shiyarei Knesset HaGedolah agrees with Shulchan Aruch and Levush, and Magen Avraham rules likewise. Taz's objection is no objection at all. It appears to me that even when the mixture is exactly half and half one recites Borei Minei Mezonot, because the Rosh and Tur say only that when the majority is another species one does not recite Borei Minei Mezonot, since it is not considered like one of the five species. This implies that when they are equal one does recite Borei Minei Mezonot.
[Levush] Eaten by itself, etc. Malbushei Yom Tov writes that in his main text the Levush placed this ruling where the issue is bread made from millet and other legumes, and that he does not know who the authorities are behind the "some say" cited there. Lechem Chamudot, page 46, similarly writes that Rabbi Mordechai Yafe omitted the law of millet bread and instead wrote about eating millet itself, which requires study both as to why and from where he derived it. In my humble opinion, he omitted explicit mention of millet bread because he relied on the end of the seif, "even if he made bread from them," which applies to millet as well; this is why he says it is not as significant as rice bread. Yet his law regarding millet eaten by itself is indeed difficult. It seems to me that he inferred it from Rabbenu Yonah cited by Beit Yosef, who writes that one recites Borei Minei Mezonot over rice even when whole, but over millet one does not recite even when it is whole. Since he says simply "does not recite" rather than "does not recite Borei Minei Mezonot," the Levush understood that no blessing is recited at all. It is unreasonable to say this of cooked millet, so he interpreted it as raw. One can also explain that Rabbenu Yonah refers back to the preceding statement and means only that Borei Minei Mezonot is not recited, while Borei Pri HaAdamah is recited. I found, however, in Kol Bo in the name of Rabbenu Yonah that one recites Shehakol over whole millet. In Tashbetz, siman 322, I saw a ruling that one recites Borei Pri HaAdamah over raw whole millet, and I found the same in Rabbi David Abudarham, page 132. This appears primary in practice. Kol Bo concerns cooked millet.
