(Seif katan 10) Until it became mashed; these are the words of Beit Yosef, etc. The marginal source on Rama’s gloss states, “Beit Yosef in the name of the Rosh and Rabbeinu Yonah.” Magen Avraham objects that Beit Yosef states the opposite. It must therefore be Rama’s own ruling, and Magen Avraham asks why Rama disagreed with Beit Yosef without proof.
For there is a distinction between, etc. Rabbeinu Yonah writes, according to the opinion that millet has the same law as rice, that cooked millet likewise requires Borei Minei Mezonos first and Borei Nefashos afterward. Yet the Baraisa mentions only rice, not millet. Rabbeinu Yonah therefore resolves that there is a distinction, etc. The Baraisa mentions rice because its law is definite: even when the grains remain whole, one recites Borei Minei Mezonos.
Nevertheless, one may distinguish between wheat, etc. Rabbeinu Yonah’s phrase “whole rice” retains its plain meaning, namely, rice that did not become mashed, and one nevertheless recites Borei Minei Mezonos. To answer Beit Yosef’s question from wheat, which specifically requires mashing, Beit Yosef writes that wheat and rice differ because rice is ordinarily cooked in this manner. This is what Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 2 in the name of Bach.
Even when it was not cooked extensively, its blessing does not differ from the blessing it would require had it been cooked extensively and become mashed.
And Bach writes that when it did not become mashed, the matter is doubtful. Meaning, apart from the uncertainty whether the grain is millet, as explained in seif katan 9, there is an additional uncertainty when it did not become mashed.
