It appears to me that the same applies to raw cucumbers, which may not be salted, for people regularly make them into pickled vegetables, and salting is effective for them, so they are like a radish; the same applies to anything similar. However, if one immediately adds oil, it is permitted, as according to Rabbi Yosei. It is permitted to put salt on pieces of fowl or fish and afterward put water or wine on them. Raw meat or fish may not be salted on Shabbos so that it should not spoil; see the end of siman 500, which implies that even where there is loss it is forbidden. Tosafot and the Rosh in Chullin 14 wrote that even if one wants to eat it raw as it is, it is forbidden to salt it. Although there is no tanning with foods, as written at the end of siman 500, nevertheless it is forbidden because it appears like tanning. The Ran in Chullin wrote similarly, because salt prepares the food and fixes it, as stated regarding radish. All the more so there is reason to forbid salting raw meat, for he fixes it thereby, and all the more so if it would be forbidden to him without salting, for that is an even greater fixing; see what I wrote at the beginning of siman 314. The Rashba also wrote this, and see there, where they wrote that one who salts meat in order to store it for the road is liable to bring a chatas. This requires study, for in the Gemara it appears that the halacha follows Rava, who exempts, since he is later, and so the Rambam ruled in chapter 11. It appears to me that it is permitted to place the meat into vinegar in order to eat it, as written in Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 67. It is clear from their words that one may rinse meat in order to eat it. The Porat Yosef asked why this differs from immersing vessels, which is forbidden because one fixes the vessel, as written in siman 323, seif 7; here too it should be forbidden. He labored to answer this, but did not see what is written there in seif 8, that it is permitted to rinse a cup. The same applies here: since the prohibition is not in the body of the meat, but he is merely rinsing it because of visible blood upon it, it is permitted. It appears that it is forbidden to rinse meat when the third day falls on Shabbos in order that it not become forbidden for cooking, for since he does not want to eat it that day, it is forbidden to rinse it, as written in siman 323, seif 6, and siman 302, seif 3. So too in the seventh chapter of Pesachim, even on Pesach they did not permit rinsing the innards, since it is not needed for that day. I found written that one gaon permitted this, as stated in seif 11 regarding vegetables, that since they are fit to eat that day, one may water them just as one may move them. But it appears to me that it is forbidden, for he fixes the meat by softening it, as written above in the name of the Ran; with vegetables, however, he fixes nothing. Furthermore, according to what is written in siman 308, seif 31, that meat is forbidden to move, the same should apply that it is forbidden to put water on it. Furthermore, even mere preparation for a weekday need is forbidden, such as bringing wine for Motzaei Shabbos, and certainly in a case like this. It is not comparable to vegetables, since perhaps he will eat some of them that day, whereas with meat it is evident that it is for weekday use. Therefore one should not be lenient; see siman 497, seif 2. Even through a non-Jew one should not be lenient, for there is not such a great loss, since he can eat it roasted; see siman 307, seif 5. In Maharil, Laws of Pesach, he wrote that were it not that the great authorities expressed hesitation in the matter, he would have said it is permitted to rinse them, for there is some need, lest they become chametz and the vessel be spoiled, since even for muktzeh one may make an arrangement, such as overturning a vessel over it. Nevertheless, his heart did not allow him to be lenient, and therefore it is good to rinse them through a non-Jew. See siman 459. In any event, here there is no loss, for it can be eaten roasted.
