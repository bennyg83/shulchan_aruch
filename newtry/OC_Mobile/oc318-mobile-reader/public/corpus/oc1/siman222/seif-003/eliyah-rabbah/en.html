[Levush] That a person is obligated, etc. This is not itself the reason that one blesses over bad tidings, for why would it apply specifically to evils one sees? I found in Rabbi David Abudarham a reason for good tidings: since Scripture says, "Let me hear joy and gladness; let the bones You crushed rejoice," praising such tidings, we should thank Hashem for them. For bad tidings, since it says, "A bad report dries the bones," and Scripture disparages such news, we must justify the divine judgment over it. Sefer Chasidim, siman 382, writes that if because of poverty a man had to marry a wealthy woman whom he did not desire, he recites both HaTov VeHaMetiv and Baruch Dayan HaEmet. Mateh Moshe writes that for all tidings, if the reporter is not trustworthy or did not see the matter himself, one should not bless at all. Likewise, if Reuven was robbed and hears from a trustworthy person that the robber was captured and will return everything, he should not bless until the property is actually returned. He supports this from the incident of Avraham's servant and Avraham's prophecy. Rabbi David Abudarham writes that if weeds grew among his grain or he had a daughter when he wished for a son, he should not bless Dayan HaEmet, because the wheat did not transform into weeds and the son did not transform into a daughter. If the Creator chose not to give him all wheat or all sons, one does not bless over what was never given, only over something that was given and then spoiled or lost.
With joy, etc. He should think that all of it serves as atonement for his sins, as Talmidei Rabbenu Yonah write.

The Gemara in Berachot 60, which asks for a verse, therefore requires explanation since the Mishnah already derived it. One may distinguish the cases.
