[1] That he should turn it over, etc. If the corpse was alive at bein hashmashot, the bed may later be moved because it did not become a base for muktzeh (Rosh). Baal HaMaor permits even if the corpse was already dead at bein hashmashot, because a bed does not become a base for a corpse, since the corpse need not be placed specifically upon a bed and could be placed on the ground (Magen Avraham).
[2] A corpse lying in the sun, etc. Or in a place where the relatives cannot remain sitting beside it (Agudah, chapter Kirah). Responsa Beit Yaakov, siman 151, asks against the opinion cited by Rama in Yoreh Deah siman 373 that a Kohen may not remain in a house with his deceased relative merely to guard the body because the prohibition was waived only for burial. Here, preserving the corpse from degradation appears more stringent than burial, since burial is prohibited on Shabbos while moving a corpse from sun to shade to prevent degradation is permitted. There is no difficulty. Moving the corpse is only rabbinic, and even carrying it into a public domain has a rabbinic aspect as stated in seif 2, whereas burial is a biblical melachah. Even burial through a non-Jew differs from a double rabbinic prohibition of movement. Moreover, Ramban gives the reason, cited by Magen Avraham seif katan 13, that the honor of the deceased requires avoiding the impression that Shabbos was desecrated for him. Nekudot HaKesef writes that the custom is for a Kohen to remain and guard. Tosafot in Nazir writes that when no Jew is available to move a corpse from sun to shade, a Kohen may do so. Beit Yaakov's lengthy objections there therefore do not stand.
[3] Within the same domain, etc. Bach is lenient to carry it from a private domain into a karmelit, and Shiltei Gibborim cites Riaz similarly. This accords with the opinion in seif 2 that permits even before the corpse has begun to decompose, etc.
