One should not recite, etc. Lechem Chamudos writes in the name of a manuscript: Reuven and Shimon each drink his own wine, and Reuven later gives Shimon his cup to drink. It requires study whether Shimon recites the blessing, since Reuven too benefits from it, or whether he does not recite it because only Shimon has experienced a change of wine. Mordechai, at the end of the chapter HaRoeh, cites Tosafos: “The Rif ruled that a lodger and a guest do not recite HaTov VeHaMeitiv, because this is not a benefit since the wine is not theirs.” Beit Yosef explains that only the guest does not recite it, while the host does, and he disagrees. Bach rules like Mordechai, as does Shiltei HaGibborim in the chapter Arvei Pesachim. In my humble opinion, it is difficult why Mordechai mentions both the lodger and the guest; he should have mentioned only the guest. It appears that “lodger” means the host, as Tosafos explain in the second chapter of Avodah Zarah. The Rif follows his own view, for Tur, siman 221, writes in his name that one recites HaTov VeHaMeitiv over rain only if he has a partner in that field; otherwise he recites Shehecheyanu. Although others also benefit from the rain, the person and his companion must share ownership in the same thing. The same applies here: they must be partners in the wine. A guest has no share in it, and even the host does not recite, for how can he say “Who bestows good upon others” when there is no benefit to others in something that is not theirs? This is also implied by the Gemara's wording, “where there are members of a group drinking with him,” meaning specifically members of one group. His wife and children, because he is obligated to support them, are considered as though they have a share in it. Therefore one should be stringent, since siman 221 rules like the Rif. This is especially so in a doubt concerning blessings. Although one might say that when he gives wine to the guest it is as though the guest has a share, one should nevertheless be stringent, because the guest has no share in the actual cup the host drinks. Partners are different, for we rule that there is no retroactive clarification. Nevertheless, it appears that if the host places the pitcher on the table, as is done at large meals, everyone is equally included in the benefit. It is then as though they all have a share, and they recite HaTov VeHaMeitiv. If they do not drink together, but each drinks in a separate room, it is obvious that the blessing is not recited. Tosafos on page 43 state that when they tell a man, “Your wife has borne a son,” he recites HaTov VeHaMeitiv even though his wife is not with him. That is because they are partners in one thing, namely the child. Here, however, the wine one drank was not drunk by the other, so they must drink as one group. Tosafos cite the case only to prove that each person may recite the blessing independently. See there.
