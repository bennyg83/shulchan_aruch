[Levush] For him with wine, etc. The Levush erred here, because the blessing requires specifically that other people be present with him, as stated below in seif 3. Magen Avraham there gives the correct explanation. If he recited Birkat HaMazon over the second wine, he need not recite HaTov VeHaMeitiv separately, because he already said HaTov VeHaMeitiv in Birkat HaMazon.
[Levush] On the table, etc. Lechem Chamudot, folio 72, writes that if both wines were prepared for him to drink now, even if they were not actually on the table, and he intended to drink from both, he does not recite HaTov VeHaMeitiv. If they were not prepared before him, even if he intended to drink both, he does recite it. The source of this distinction is the Rama's own reasoning, intended to prevent a contradiction in the Shulchan Aruch between the present ruling and seif 3. In my humble opinion there is no initial contradiction. Although ideally one recites the blessing over the superior wine, if he mistakenly recited it over the inferior wine, the present case addresses a situation where it was not known which wine was superior. The Bach likewise challenges the Rama. I also found in Sefer Zikaron by the gaon Rabbi Yishmael HaKohen that one recites HaTov VeHaMeitiv even if both wines were before him from the beginning.
[4] One is new, etc. Nachalas Tzvi writes that this applies when he first drinks new wine and afterward old wine. But if he first drinks old wine and afterward new wine, he does not recite HaTov VeHaMeitiv unless he knows that the new wine is as good as the old, since ordinarily new wine is not as good as old wine. Tashbetz, siman 296, writes that throughout the thirty days of fermentation it is called new wine. Kol Bo writes likewise.
[5] [Levush] Inferior to the red wine, etc. Rama writes likewise. Bach objects that only according to Rabbeinu Tam, who requires the blessing even over inferior wine, would one recite it even when the second wine is much inferior to the first. According to our practice, however, when one knows the second is inferior, one does not recite the blessing if it is much inferior to the first. Nachalas Tzvi greatly erred in understanding Bach to mean that one recites the blessing only when he does not know that it is inferior. He therefore wrote that since white wine is healthy, it is certainly superior. This is imprecise. Bach agrees that even when one knows it is inferior, the blessing may be recited; his objection concerns only a wine that is much more inferior. Regarding Bach's principal objection, it appears to me that there is no difficulty. He did not read Rama and Levush carefully. They wrote “even though it is more inferior,” not “much more inferior,” to the point that it can be drunk only with difficulty. The phrase means simply that it is inferior, while “more” relates to the first wine: it is more inferior than the first. This is also implied by Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah and is explicit in Mordechai, chapter Arvei Pesachim, for one who examines it. This is also unlike Taz. See there; there is no need to elaborate. If he first drank white wine and afterward red wine that is superior, Bach writes that it is obvious that he recites HaTov VeHaMeitiv. Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah is uncertain whether he recites it when he does not know whether the red wine is superior to the white, and concludes that it appears he should not. Olas Tamid and Magen Avraham rule likewise. I am surprised that they did not mention Lechem Chamudos, page 72, who rules unequivocally that he should recite it.
