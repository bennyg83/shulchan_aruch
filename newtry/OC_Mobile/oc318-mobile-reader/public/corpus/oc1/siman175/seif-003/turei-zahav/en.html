Initially, however, he should recite Borei Peri HaGafen only over the superior wine, which automatically exempts him from HaTov VeHaMeitiv. This appears to be how Rama reconciles this seif with seif 1. Yet the wording of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in seif 1 does not support Rama's explanation, for they write, “Rather, if he initially had two wines,” implying at the beginning of his drinking, when he intends to drink both. Bach explains that seif 1 concerns one who transgressed and recited Borei Peri HaGafen over the inferior wine; he then recites HaTov VeHaMeitiv over the superior wine. All this is intended to reconcile the rulings of the Shulchan Aruch. In my humble opinion, however, the Tur holds that initially one may recite Borei Peri HaGafen over the inferior wine. Why is this different from seif 3, which follows Mordechai and requires the preferred item first? There is no difficulty. Where there is only one praise of the Holy One, blessed is He, it is proper to arrange it over the preferred item. Here, however, there are two praises, one for the creation of wine and one of HaTov VeHaMeitiv. It is certainly preferable to offer two praises. He should first recite over the first wine the blessing appropriate to it, and this itself is preferable to a person. Proof may be brought from siman 211, seif 3: If before him are an item requiring Borei Peri HaEtz and an item requiring Shehakol, Borei Peri HaEtz takes precedence. Yet since we rule that minimizing blessings is preferable, as stated in Yoma, chapter Ba Lo, because of an unnecessary blessing, one might think he should recite Shehakol and thereby exempt the fruit. Necessarily, since Borei Peri HaEtz is a distinguished, specific blessing, we do not eliminate it, and it is not considered unnecessary. The same applies to HaTov VeHaMeitiv. This follows Rashbam, cited by the Tur, who limits HaTov VeHaMeitiv to where one knows clearly that the second wine is better. According to Tosafos, cited afterward, the blessing is recited even where quality is unknown, because it praises the abundance of wine, as Beit Yosef explains in their name. Only where the second is certainly inferior is this praise inappropriate; in uncertainty, the praise of abundance applies. Accordingly, Mordechai's ruling does not challenge the Tur at all. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch in seif 1 discuss a case where he is uncertain which wine is better. When two wines are brought before him, two obligations immediately arise: the blessing over wine itself and the blessing over the abundance of wines. He therefore recites the blessing over one for the wine itself and HaTov VeHaMeitiv over the other. Mordechai in seif 3 discusses a case where one is known to be inferior. The obligation arising from abundance does not initially apply; only the blessing over the wine itself applies, so he recites it over the superior wine first and is thereby exempt from HaTov VeHaMeitiv. This appears to me the correct understanding of the Shulchan Aruch and the practical halachah: there is a distinction between uncertainty and certainty. One who is uneasy about reciting two blessings in a case of uncertainty should remove one wine from the table, recite Borei Peri HaGafen over one, and afterward recite HaTov VeHaMeitiv over the second. In this way he satisfies all opinions.
