1) [Se'if 1] Earthenware pots, etc. This does not apply only to pots. The same law applies to bowls used by pouring from another vessel and to utensils used as a second vessel. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1; Mekor Chaim, subsection 1. In other words, although they are not used directly over fire like pots, they must likewise be scrubbed and put away.
2) There. Earthenware pots, etc. The same applies to utensils made of other materials if one does not wish to kasher them with boiling water or fire. One may scrub them and retain them until after Pesach. The Shulchan Aruch speaks of the usual case, because utensils made of other materials can be kashered and people ordinarily kasher them with boiling water or fire rather than put them away. Chok Yaakov, subsection 4.
3) There. In which chametz was used, etc. Regarding new utensils purchased in the marketplace, even earthenware utensils, one need not suspect that they were used, because people do not ordinarily use utensils sold as new. Metal utensils purchased in a non-Jew's home, however, must be kashered out of doubt with boiling water or fire, according to their use, unless one can identify them as certainly new. Issur VeHeter, rule 58, law 46; Chok Yaakov, subsection 2; Mekor Chaim, subsection 2. After kashering with boiling water or fire, they must be immersed in a mikveh even though they are old, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, siman 121, se'if 1. See there. See also siman 122, se'if 11. Nevertheless, clear glass utensils may be purchased from a non-Jew's home even if the non-Jew does not make an incidental statement that they are new and does not select one from among other utensils. Since they are transparent, it is certain that they have not yet been used. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 5.
4) There. In which chametz was used, etc. Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 75, issued a practical ruling permitting food cooked during Pesach in a clean chametz pot that had remained unused for twelve months. This applies even according to our practice of prohibiting a detrimental absorbed flavor, as stated above in siman 447, se'if 10, because after twelve months no flavor remains at all. Nevertheless, he did not have the confidence to permit cooking in it initially. Rabbi Naftali Katz agreed there in siman 76. See there. This was cited above in siman 447, subsection 10, where we also cited the opinion of Shevus Yaakov permitting after two months. See there. Responsa Panim Meiros, volume 1, siman 24, however, disagrees. See there. Radbaz, siman 739, likewise writes that twelve months of disuse was permitted only regarding wine, not other prohibitions. See there. Erech HaShulchan, subsection 6. Responsa Chaim Sha'al, volume 2, siman 38, likewise writes that Rashba in Responsa Rashba, siman 575, disagrees with Chacham Tzvi, for Rashba writes that aging is effective only for wine utensils. He reports a case in which a Jew received a gift of fine porcelain utensils and it was unknown whether the non-Jewish sender had used them. Two years had passed. He writes that they apparently could not be permitted initially, as Chacham Tzvi himself writes, and Rashba implies that they are prohibited even after the fact. Here, however, there is a compound doubt: perhaps they were never used, and even if they were used, perhaps the law follows the lenient opinion. The matter still requires resolution. These are his words. It appears that if they can be kashered three times with boiling water, this should be done. If they are utensils not ordinarily used with food that is scalding hot, one may also be lenient. Sha'arei Teshuvah, subsection 1. See Yoreh De'ah, siman 113, se'if 16; siman 121, se'if 1; and siman 122, se'if 11, regarding these laws, and analyze carefully.
5) There. Throughout the year. The same law applies if chametz was used in them during Pesach. Chok Yaakov, subsection 3; Levushei Serad; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, siman 447, subsection 3. In other words, they must be thoroughly scrubbed and put away, and they may be used after Pesach. Absorbed chametz in a utensil is not subject to the prohibitions against chametz being seen and found, as stated below in subsection 8. Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes likewise there.
6) There. Even those in which porridge is prepared, etc. Such utensils absorb much chametz, and if one cooks in them after Pesach the food might not contain sixty times the absorbed chametz. Nevertheless, they are permitted. We follow Rabbi Shimon, who maintains that chametz after Pesach is prohibited only as a penalty. The Sages imposed the penalty only when tangible chametz was retained, not chametz absorbed in a utensil. Therefore, one may even initially cook in them after Pesach. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1; Magen Avraham, subsection 1.
7) There. One thoroughly scrubs them, etc. If one did not scrub them before Pesach, one scrubs them during Pesach or after Pesach, like the law of one who failed to eliminate chametz before Pesach explained above in siman 435. See there. Chok Yaakov, subsection 4; Mekor Chaim, subsection 4; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 3. Scrubbing during Pesach applies specifically on Chol HaMoed. On Shabbos or Yom Tov it is forbidden to touch chametz even to eliminate it, as stated in siman 446. See there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, there.
8) There. And one may retain them, etc. This is unlike chametz mixed into food, which may not be retained. There, the chametz exists visibly, although it cannot be identified. Chametz absorbed in a utensil, however, is neither visible nor accessible, and therefore is not subject to the prohibitions against chametz being seen and found. Yad Ephraim. Raavan writes likewise, as does Rabbeinu Yerucham. This also appears from Ran. Magen Avraham, subsection 1; Chok Yosef, subsection 1.
9) There. And one may retain them, etc. Pesachim 30a records a dispute. Rav says the utensils must be broken and may not be retained for use after Pesach, even with a different kind of food, as a decree lest they be used with the same kind. Shmuel says they may be retained until after the prohibited time and then used with either the same kind or another kind. See there. The authorities rule like Shmuel. Although the general rule is that the law follows Rav in matters of prohibition, this case differs because Shmuel follows Rabbi Shimon. Beis Yosef; Bach; Erech HaShulchan, subsection 1.
10) There. And one puts them away during Pesach, etc. A recent practice arose of placing tin chametz utensils high up in the winter room and claiming there is no concern that they will be used because of their height. People likewise leave Pesach utensils high up throughout the year. This is a complete error. They must be guarded in a place people do not frequent, lest someone mistakenly use them during Pesach. Bach; Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, subsection 1; Olas Shabbos, subsection 1. It is possible that the custom arose because the utensils had not been used that day, and many authorities permit after-the-fact use of such utensils, as stated in siman 447, se'if 10. This is certainly so for utensils used primarily as a second vessel or with cold food, such as pitchers, where there is no concern. One who is stringent will be blessed. Magen Avraham, subsection 2. Chok Yaakov, subsection 7, and Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1, however, permit this without qualification. See there. Peri Chadash writes that it is proper to take Bach's opinion into account. Birkei Yosef, subsection 2, writes likewise. Chok Yosef, subsection 2, agrees with Magen Avraham that one who is stringent will be blessed. Mekor Chaim, subsection 5, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 4, write likewise.
11) Nevertheless, utensils used for liquor, for which a detrimental absorbed flavor does not provide leniency, as stated by Magen Avraham in siman 447, subsection 25, must be prohibited. The same applies to utensils belonging to a non-Jew that a Jew holds as collateral in a place where people commonly cook sour borscht and the like in them. Care is required because a detrimental absorbed flavor provides no leniency for these utensils, as explained. Eshel Avraham, subsection 2. See above, siman 442, subsection 65.
12) Where there is no established custom, one may at least be lenient with a second vessel. There is room to permit even earthenware utensils used as a second vessel or through pouring, if they are placed where no person can reach them. Responsa at the end of Shitas Mahari Sh, siman 13. See the full discussion there. Machazik Berachah, subsection 1.
13) There. And put away the key. This prevents one from entering there throughout Pesach. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1. It may also be explained that the key is hidden because other people, who do not know the utensils are prohibited, might open the room and use them.
14) There. Heating does not help them, etc. Heating only from the outside does not extract what the utensils absorbed. One might suggest heating them from the inside, but one will be protective of the utensils out of concern that they might crack. Pesachim 30b. See below, subsection 21.
15) There. But heating, etc. Kashering with boiling water does not help earthenware utensils, because the Torah attests that an earthenware utensil never loses its defect. The Gemara, there. Tosafos there explain why heating is effective if one is not protective of the utensil, or if one returns it to a kiln as explained below. This is not considered removing its defect, because heating it makes it like a new utensil; when earthenware utensils are manufactured, they are placed in a kiln and fired. See there.
16) There. But one merely poured boiling liquid into them. For example, bowls into which one pours from a first vessel. See below, se'if 5.
17) There. But one merely poured boiling liquid into them. This is the language of Tur. Beis Yosef explains that even if an earthenware utensil was used with hot food only as a second vessel, heating does not kasher it. He writes that Rambam in chapter 5 likewise equates an earthenware utensil used for chametz as a second vessel with one used as a first vessel. Rema in Darkei Moshe, subsection 1, however, writes that Beis Yosef's explanation does not appear correct. According to Tur, a second vessel can be kashered because it does not absorb. In the name of Hagahos Maimoniyos, however, he writes that even if an earthenware utensil was used only as a second vessel, heating does not kasher it. See there. Rema therefore comments on the Shulchan Aruch's words, which are the language of Tur, that some prohibit even a second vessel. He means that although Tur's language implies that only a utensil used by pouring from a first vessel cannot be kashered, while one used as a second vessel can be, some maintain that even a second vessel cannot be kashered by heating. Chok Yaakov, subsection 8, and Ma'amar Mordechai, subsection 3, write likewise. The practical ruling will be explained below in subsection 20.
18) There, gloss. Some prohibit even a second vessel. This means even where one did not pour directly from the pot into the utensil, but poured from the pot into a second vessel and then placed that second vessel into this utensil. Magen Avraham, subsection 3; Mekor Chaim, subsection 6. If one poured from a bowl into the pot, however, this is pouring from a second vessel and all authorities permit it. Erech HaShulchan, subsection 5.
19) There, gloss. Some prohibit even a second vessel. This proves that Rema maintains that even a second vessel causes a minute absorption. This is also evident below in se'if 5. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1. The Shulchan Aruch states there that if utensils were used as a second vessel, they are kashered in a second vessel. If they absorbed nothing at all, why would kashering be required? It must be that they absorb at least a minute amount and therefore require kashering.
20) There, gloss. Some prohibit even a second vessel. This is also the opinion of the Shulchan Aruch, as stated above in subsection 17. Peri Chadash and Chok Yaakov, subsection 8, write likewise. Magen Avraham, subsection 3, writes that by omitting the law of a second vessel, the Shulchan Aruch retracted what was written in Beis Yosef. Chok Yaakov there challenges this. Yad Aharon in Hagahos Beis Yosef likewise writes that Magen Avraham's ruling requires consideration. Nehar Shalom, subsection 2, challenges Magen Avraham's entire discussion. See there. In Yoreh De'ah, siman 105, se'if 2, the Shulchan Aruch permits after the fact food heated in a second vessel, whereas here it maintains that heating does not kasher such a utensil. Yad Aharon there suggests that the Shulchan Aruch may have wished to be stringent regarding chametz. See there. Regarding the practical law, Chok Yaakov writes there that in a case of substantial loss and loss of Yom Tov rejoicing, one may be lenient after the fact if a utensil not used that day was used as a second vessel, even if it is earthenware. See there. Yad Aharon, there; Mekor Chaim, subsection 6; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 33; Chayei Adam, rule 125, subsection 3, write likewise. For example, a spoon used primarily for eating is ordinarily inserted into a bowl after food was poured into it from a first vessel. If the spoon was not kashered and during Pesach was inserted into a first vessel, the food is permitted after the fact when the spoon had not been used that day and a substantial loss or loss of Yom Tov rejoicing is involved. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, there. Obviously, this applies only if the spoon was clean. Otherwise, chametz during Pesach prohibits even in the slightest amount, as is known.
21) There. Even if one fills them with coals, etc. Even filling them with coals, which heats them from within and extracts what they absorbed, does not help. We are concerned that one will be protective of the utensils and not heat them sufficiently because they are not ordinarily heated from within, but from without. This differs from a tile-lined oven and the oven of se'if 2, for which internal heating is effective. Since they are ordinarily used this way, one will not be protective of them and will heat them properly, as explained below.
22) There. Lest one be protective of them, etc. Bach writes that earthenware ovens and stoves whose prohibited absorption came through fire cannot be kashered even by internal heating, and this is the ruling to follow. See there. This also appears to be the opinion of Peri Chadash in se'if 2. Magen Avraham, subsection 4; Chok Yaakov, subsection 9; and Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 5, however, challenge him. They write that internal heating is effective because these utensils are ordinarily used this way, so there is no concern that one will be protective of them lest they crack. See there. Beis Hillel at the beginning of siman 461 writes likewise. Chok Yosef, subsection 4, agrees, contrary to Bach. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 8, and Chayei Adam, rule 125, subsection 2, rule likewise. See below, subsection 32, and at the beginning of siman 461.
23) There. Nevertheless, if one returned them to a kiln, etc. I was asked whether earthenware utensils that remained after a fire could be used on Pesach, since it is known that this is more intense than returning them to a kiln. I permitted them. Nevertheless, everything depends upon the circumstances. Ba'er Hetev, subsection 10.
24) There. If one returned them to a kiln, etc. Issur VeHeter, rule 58, law 49, cites a dispute and writes that our custom is therefore not to kasher them by heating at all, even in a potter's kiln. See there. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 3, cites this. Peri Chadash likewise cites the authorities who prohibit even returning them to a kiln and writes that in practice one should be stringent. See there. Rabbeinu Tam in Sefer HaYashar, siman 614, however, writes that returning them to a kiln repairs them. Rabbeinu Yerucham, path 5, section 2; She'iltos of Rav Achai, parashas Matos; Tosafos, Pesachim 30b; Rokeach, siman 484; and Rabbeinu Manoach, chapter 5, write likewise. Chukas HaPesach, subsection 3, cites these opinions. See there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 9, rules likewise. Therefore, since the matter is disputed, each community should follow its established custom.
25) There. But not in our ovens. We are concerned that one will be protective of the utensils and remove them from the oven before they become white-hot. Tur in the name of Rosh. In other words, although the utensils would become thoroughly white-hot even in our ovens if left there for a long time, one might not leave them long enough. In a kiln, however, they become white-hot quickly. Taz, subsection 2. Ma'amar Mordechai, subsection 5, explains that because a kiln is intensely heated, once the utensils do not crack immediately upon being inserted, there is no further concern for them.
26) There. But not in our ovens. See Responsa Be'eros HaMayim, Yoreh De'ah, siman 6, which writes that our large ovens are permitted. See there. Derashos Maharil, page 6a, however, writes that our ovens are ineffective, even a baker's large oven. See there. The unqualified language of the authorities likewise implies that no distinction should be made between one oven and another. Moreover, some authorities are stringent even with a kiln, as stated in the preceding subsection. We should therefore not extend the leniency.
26) If one placed an earthenware utensil in an oven and heated it until sparks flew from it, and then deliberately cooked in it during Pesach, the food is prohibited, just as when one deliberately cooks in a utensil that had not been used that day regarding other prohibitions. If it was done inadvertently, it may be permitted. Mishbetzos Zahav, subsection 2. It appears that for a poor person facing a substantial loss, one may be lenient even if it was deliberate. That person should nevertheless be rebuked for violating the enactment of the Sages and warned not to do so again. See Zivchei Tzedek on Yoreh De'ah, siman 103, subsection 23.
27) There, gloss. It is forbidden to use it even with cold food, etc. This refers to regular use, lest one come to use it with hot food. Therefore, regular use is forbidden even if one first rinsed it. Temporary use, however, is permitted after rinsing, contrary to Olas Shabbos. Regular use is forbidden even on the eve of Pesach after chametz becomes prohibited. Chok Yaakov, subsection 11; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 4; Mekor Chaim, subsection 8; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2. Regarding old earthenware utensils, however, the custom is not to use them even with cold food and even temporarily, as stated below at the end of se'if 22. See there.
