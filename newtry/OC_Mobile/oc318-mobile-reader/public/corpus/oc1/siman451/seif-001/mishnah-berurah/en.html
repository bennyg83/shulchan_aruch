(1) Pots. The same law applies to bowls whose use is through pouring from a first vessel or through a second vessel.
(2) Made of earthenware. The same applies to other utensils not made of earthenware. If one does not wish to kasher them by hagalah or libun, he may also scrub them and keep them until after Pesach. The ruling speaks of the common case, because other utensils can generally be kashered, so one normally performs hagalah or libun, as explained below.
(3) That were used, etc. New utensils purchased in the marketplace, even earthenware utensils, need not be suspected of prior use, because new utensils are not ordinarily used before sale. Metal utensils require only immersion. If, however, they are purchased from a non-Jew in his home and one does not recognize them as certainly new, metal utensils must, because of the doubt, undergo hagalah or libun according to their manner of use, in addition to immersion. Earthenware utensils must be broken because of the doubt.
(4) Throughout the year. The same applies even if chametz was used in them during Pesach. After the fact, thorough scrubbing is sufficient, and they may be retained until after Pesach, as explained above at the beginning of siman 447. Chok Yaakov and Levushei Serad state this. Peri Megadim questions whether one may then use the utensil when food was cooked in it on the seventh day of Pesach in Eretz Yisrael, or on the eighth day for us outside Eretz Yisrael, while the absorbed taste was still less than twenty-four hours old. See there.
(5) Even those in which, etc. Meaning, even though they have absorbed a great quantity of chametz, and if one cooks in them after Pesach the food will certainly not contain sixty times the absorbed chametz, it is nevertheless permitted. We rule that chametz after Pesach is permitted according to the Torah and is forbidden only because the Sages penalized one who retained it. They imposed the penalty only upon actual chametz, not upon flavor absorbed in a utensil. One may therefore cook in these utensils after Pesach even initially.
(6) He scrubs them. If he did not scrub them before Pesach, he may scrub them during Pesach or after Pesach, following the law of one who failed to eliminate chametz before Pesach, explained in siman 435.
(7) And stores them away, etc. Some arrange their chametz tin utensils high upon the wall, beyond a person’s reach, and leave them displayed throughout Pesach as decoration. They should not be protested. Although whenever the Sages prohibited an object because one might forget and use it they did not distinguish, even if it was raised ten stories, as explained in siman 275, here the utensils are not ben yomo from absorbed chametz when arranged. This is especially true of utensils used mainly with a second vessel or cold food, such as pitchers. Nevertheless, one who is stringent will be blessed.
(8) And conceal the key. Meaning, if he begins searching for the key, he will remember that it is Pesach.
(9) It is ineffective, etc. Heating only from the outside does not extract the absorbed flavor.
(10) Into which boiling liquid was poured. Such as bowls, upon which food is ordinarily poured from pots.
(11) Even in a second vessel. Meaning, even when the food was not poured directly from the pot into it, but first into another vessel, which was then placed inside this utensil. The later authorities agree that initially one should certainly be stringent in accordance with the Rama and not permit the utensil even when it is known that chametz was used in it only through a second vessel. Metal utensils therefore require hagalah, as explained below in seif 5. Earthenware utensils, for which hagalah is ineffective and heating is impractical, may not be used on Pesach. After the fact, however, if a utensil used only as a second vessel, such as a plate or spoon, was used without hagalah, one may be lenient even with earthenware where there would be substantial loss or loss of Yom Tov joy, provided it was not ben yomo and the absorbed taste was detrimental. Several authorities are lenient regarding detrimental taste in chametz.
(12) And even if one fills them with coals, etc. This is considered heating from within and extracts the absorbed flavor.
(13) Lest he spare them, etc. Such utensils are ordinarily heated only from the outside. Ovens, however, even earthenware ovens, including portable ones, are always heated from within. We therefore do not fear that one will spare them from cracking, and heating is effective. One must heat them thoroughly until they glow. See siman 461 concerning kashering our ovens.
(14) But not for our ovens. We fear that he will spare the utensil and not leave it in the oven long enough, removing it before it glows. In a kiln, however, it becomes glowing hot even in a short time.
(15) Even cold food. This applies even if the utensil was thoroughly washed and scrubbed, because one might come to use it with hot food. See Yoreh Deah, siman 121, where the same rule is stated regarding other prohibitions. One should therefore be stringent here even on Erev Pesach after midday.
(16) See Yoreh Deah, siman 121. There it is explained that occasional use is permitted after the utensil has first been washed and scrubbed. See above at the end of siman 450 and the Mishnah Berurah there.
