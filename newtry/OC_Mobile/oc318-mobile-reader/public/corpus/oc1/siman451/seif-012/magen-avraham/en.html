One should not prohibit after the fact. We do not say that a utensil necessarily carries its absorbed prohibition throughout its entire body, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, siman 121, seif 6, in Darchei Moshe. Issur VeHeter, Shaar 58, writes: the handle of a frying pan requires hagalah, though after the fact there is less concern. Certainly a wooden stirring spoon, which is ordinarily inserted entirely into the pot, requires hagalah. Even if one knows that only part was inserted, we rule that a utensil prohibited in part requires hagalah in its entirety. If part of a spoon was inserted into a prohibited food, he performed hagalah only on that part, and then inserted the spoon into a cooked dish, the dish is permitted, but the spoon must be kashered again by inserting all of it into boiling water. It appears to me that this is specifically where the part inserted into the cooked food was the part that had undergone hagalah. Even if the original prohibition spread through the entire spoon, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, siman 92, we do not say that it is expelled from the side that was not inserted, as stated in siman 94. If he inserted the side that was not kashered, however, the food is prohibited. This also appears from Yoreh De'ah, siman 92, seif 5, regarding a drop of milk that fell on a pot. Darchei Moshe's statement that the absorption does not travel through the whole utensil means that it does not necessarily emerge from the other side. In this way Beit Yosef's questions in Yoreh De'ah, siman 121, against Tur are resolved. Tur discusses a case where he wishes to use the portion he kashered, so kashering that portion is sufficient. If he wishes to use the other half, he must kasher that half as well. See there and the end of seif 26. What is written here, that we do not prohibit after the fact, means if he already used them. Initially, however, one may not use them even on Pesach where hagalah cannot now be performed. By pouring. This too resembles pouring, because the absorption travels from this side to that side.
