A mortar. Whether made of wood, stone, or metal. It was customary to pound garlic and other sharp foods in it together with bread crumbs. It is therefore comparable to a kneading trough used for sourdough or charoses, discussed below in seif 22. The Mechaber follows his own ruling there that hagalah is effective even for such a utensil.
And if it is large, etc. If it is small enough to fit into a cauldron, one may not be lenient by kashering it in this manner, because some authorities maintain that even with heated stones this is considered only pouring and not hagalah in a first vessel.
Along its entire rim. Meaning, the water must rise over the thickness of the upper rim as well.
To heat the mortar. This opinion maintains that even a kneading vessel used for sourdough cannot be kashered through hagalah, but requires libun. The authorities write that this applies specifically to chametz for Pesach. During the rest of the year, where a mortar absorbed another prohibition, the concern is only prohibited fat, and such fat is not ordinarily pounded together with sharp foods. Hagalah therefore suffices according to all opinions.
To perform libun initially; nevertheless, it is sufficient, etc. According to the strict law, hagalah alone suffices, following the first opinion, but the custom is to perform light libun, until straw burns. Where libun is impossible, such as with a wooden utensil, hagalah suffices if it is smooth and has no indentations. The later authorities write that our small mortars, in which chametz is never pounded and only saffron, cloves, or similar spices are used, and our pepper mills, may initially be kashered through hagalah even when made of metal. Hagalah is still required, because a trace of chametz may once have entered and later been absorbed through the sharp spices pounded there. Ideally, one should kasher and grind everything before Yom Tov. After the fact, if it was not kashered and spices were pounded in it on Yom Tov and added to food, the later authorities incline to permit the food, especially if the utensil was not ben yomo. We do not establish a prohibition merely from uncertainty that a chametz crumb entered the spices, or that ginger or the like was cut with a chametz knife. This applies where there is only concern for a small chametz absorption. A chopping knife known to be used to chop onions previously cut with a chametz knife and then placed with meat may render the food forbidden even after the fact. Where, as commonly practiced, a piece of wheat bread is placed with the onions and everything is chopped together, it certainly absorbs actual chametz together with the sharpness of the onions, and there is no basis for leniency. Magen Avraham cautions that ginger should be cut only with a new knife. Those who are not careful may cut sharp ginger with a meat knife and then pound it in a mortar, causing the mortar to absorb meat; afterward they pound spices there and eat them with dairy.
And afterward perform hagalah. Peeling alone is ineffective, because the absorption travels through the wall from side to side and leaves only through hagalah.
