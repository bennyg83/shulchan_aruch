A pan. The Peri Megadim analyzes the kashering of utensils for Pesach, according to their normal mode of use, with distinctions between hagalah, libbun, pouring from a kli rishon, soaking, cleaning away visible chametz, and after-the-fact cases. He also weighs the rules of absorbed taste, visible residue, sharp foods, earthenware, and whether the absorbed taste is treated as permitted before Pesach. In particular, it addresses spoons, bowls, and pans.
Said. The writer says: I have here some points for reflection, and I will detail them with the help of Heaven. It is obvious to me that by fire means without an intermediary. But through an intermediary, whether water or other liquids, oil, and the like, hagalah helps; see Magen Avraham 21. What the Shach wrote in Yoreh Deah 121:8 in the name of Rema of Pano, that the difference between a non-Jew's frying pan, whose usual use is with oil, and a Jew's, whose use is with water, is that oil burns and dries and is considered use by fire, unlike water, and we follow the majority of its use; see Pri Chadash here. Now, if a non-Jew roasted neveilah meat in a pot, it may be said that it requires libbun, for before it emits fat it absorbed from the substance, and also the fat is without an intermediary. Likewise, if they melted forbidden fat in a cauldron, it requires libbun. What the Beis Yosef wrote, that the Sages did not distinguish regarding a cauldron in which forbidden fat is melted, may be said only as an initial assumption. In truth, by a frying pan the reason is that there is oil, unlike forbidden fat without an intermediary. Also what the Shach wrote there in Yoreh Deah, that from meat to milk requires libbun when without an intermediary, requires examination whether from milk to meat is also considered without an intermediary. Although milk is a liquid, nevertheless the waters of hagalah act only when the prohibition was absorbed through an intermediary, whether water or other liquids. But if the body of the prohibition itself is water, such as water poured before idolatry, or milk for meat, hagalah may no longer act upon it. Even according to us, who hold chatichah na'asis neveilah and the water becomes the body of the prohibition, the prohibited item is no stronger than what prohibited it, and they did not impose such a D'Rabbanan stringency. According to this, a utensil in which blood was cooked, even though cooked blood is D'Rabbanan, certainly even in a D'Rabbanan matter it requires libbun, since this was without an intermediary. In the utensils of Midian, where the Torah commanded hagalah, as Rashi wrote in Parshas Matos, pots are by hagalah and spits by libbun, they did not suspect that perhaps the non-Jew certainly used yayin nesech in the pot, or wine for a nazir, as Tosafot writes in Pesachim. And in the Targum Yonasan, the wording there should be examined.
Again. I later saw that it implies that whenever it absorbed water or other liquids, even if they are the body of the prohibition, hagalah is permitted. See siman 452 regarding hagalah with other liquids. Regarding forbidden fat and grease, one may say that their use is by fire. In Yoreh Deah 121, Shach 8, only a frying pan used for milk, frying in butter and cheese, requires libbun, etc. Even Rav Ashi concludes in Avodah Zarah 76 that where it absorbed a permitted item, hagalah suffices, as the Ran writes and Pri Chadash in Yoreh Deah 33, os 4, cites him: with nosar, at first it was meat and now it is nosar, unlike chametz, whose name remains upon it. The same applies to meat and milk, whose name remains upon it and did not change, although noten taam bar noten taam helps with meat and milk, because there the reason is different, that it no longer has the strength to combine afterward; originally it was meat and now it is meat.
And one must consider. Is permitted absorption dependent on nothing? Since it absorbed through fire, the water of hagalah does not act upon it, so what difference is there between permitted and prohibited absorption? We raised a similar difficulty regarding noten taam bar noten taam, and there there is an answer. One could say that when something absorbed through fire, hagalah emits it but something remains; therefore when it absorbed a permitted item, they did not decree so much. See Yoreh Deah 99:6, Shach and Taz 15 and 23, and 122:5. Therefore with chametz, from the law it is forbidden, for even a minute amount forbids, and with a utensil nullification does not apply; see Magen Avraham 20. According to this, on Erev Pesach before night, if one used a utensil that requires libbun after only hagalah, one might say that after the fact it is permitted, since a minute amount is nullified in sixty. But this is a mistake, for certainly even after the fact it forbids if he used, after hagalah, a utensil that requires libbun and cooked in it, for it emits little by little and the reasoning of whichever way you turn does not apply, as written in Yoreh Deah, Tur there 151, and it requires study. See siman 509, seif 5, where it is clear that from meat to milk is called permitted absorption and light libbun suffices, as the Magen Avraham alluded in os 11, and Pri Chadash in Yoreh Deah 121, os 8. With Hashem's help this will be explained further in Magen Avraham.
