That are coated, etc. On every side. Although the Mechaber writes in seif 26 that glass does not absorb at all, here the glass coating is fused onto earthenware in the kiln and therefore absorbs more readily.
With molten glass. The later authorities agree that one should be stringent even with a coating of molten lead or tin, because hagalah is ineffective. When hot chametz was used in it, the chametz was absorbed through the entire thickness of the utensil, including the earthenware beneath the metal coating, and can never be expelled from there.
Their law is like that of earthenware. If their use was with hot food, hagalah is ineffective. There is no difference whether the utensil was made from green, white, or black earth, and even if it is smooth and has no cracks, it absorbs through heat. If its use was cold, however, there is a distinction. A utensil made from green earth cannot be kashered because it characteristically absorbs. One made from white or black earth may be kashered through hagalah or three-day soaking, as above in seif 21, even if it has cracks. If it has no cracks, hagalah is unnecessary and mere rinsing suffices, as explained in Yoreh Deah 135 concerning yayin nesech. [Maamar Mordechai.]
Even new ones. Although they were never used with chametz, because some craftsmen prepare the glaze using bran, so chametz is absorbed within the coating. Even a small amount of coating on the outside is insufficient to permit them. [Peri Megadim.] Drops that occasionally drip upon them from the melting of other utensils need not be considered.
Only where this is the custom. Even in those places, if it is known that the craftsman did not mix bran into the coating of this earthenware vessel, one may be lenient. For this reason, in some places a Jew stands over the craftsman during the work to ensure that he does not prepare the coating with bran.
