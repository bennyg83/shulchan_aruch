And if he filled it with coals from inside, it is permitted. This is the reading in the Gemara before us, chapter Kol Shaah, Pesachim folio 30: if he filled it with coals, it is valid. Yet this requires some study there in the Gemara, for we read there regarding the statement that heating is effective for an oven: if so, why did Rav say that pots on Pesach must be broken, let one heat them? The Gemara answers: this oven is heated from inside, while these pots are heated from outside. And if you would say, let him heat them from inside, he will have pity on them because they may crack. Therefore this buchya is heated from outside and is forbidden, but if he filled it with coals it is valid; end quote. This is difficult, because once the Gemara first concludes regarding pots that since they are heated from the outside, even if he wants to heat them from inside it is not effective because he will have pity on them lest they crack, how does it conclude that with a buchya, whose heating is from outside, nevertheless, if he fills it with coals it is valid? One must say that a buchya is different, for we are not concerned lest it crack, as the Beis Yosef wrote to answer his difficulty with the Rashba. But the Beis Yosef himself requires study, for regarding his question against the Rashba, according to his own words this must also be said in the sugya of the Gemara, which is like the wording of the Rashba. Similarly, one must say this according to the Tur in Yoreh Deah siman 121 and here. This is clear. However, Shibbolei HaLeket, klal 7, siman 58, and Sefer Tanya wrote as follows: a buchya, which is like a thin pan, is forbidden for baking on Pesach. What is the reason? Its heating is from the outside. But if he filled it with coals and heated it from inside, it is permitted, such as an earthenware pan whose way is to be heated from inside and under which cakes are baked, since its heating is from inside it is like an oven; end quote. So too the Kol Bo, folio 50b, wrote that he may have pity on them lest they crack, and it is not comparable to an earthenware pipe, which he calls the buchya of the Gemara by the term pipe, following the wording of the Rambam in chapter 5, law 22, who wrote so; see there. We wrote that if he filled it with coals and heated it, the utensil he uses is permitted, for there its heating is from inside, whereas these are heated from outside. If one heats them from inside, he does not heat them well, for he has pity on them, since they commonly crack; end quote. It is explicit from their words that even for a buchya, heating from inside is not effective unless it is the type of buchya whose way is to be heated from inside. Although the wording of the Kol Bo could perhaps be pressed and explained somewhat differently, the wording of Shibbolei HaLeket and Tanya cannot be explained except as I wrote. It appears that in this they followed the reading and text of the Rif and the Rosh in chapter Kol Shaah, where we read in the Gemara: if he filled it with coals and heated it from inside, it is valid. According to this reading, at first glance it is repetitive, for filling it with coals and heating it from inside are the same. Therefore they explain that this is what it means: if he filled it with coals, meaning in that buchya whose heating is from inside, it is valid. According to their words the sugya of the Gemara is very well resolved, and one need not press and say that a buchya is different, as the Beis Yosef wrote. I have written at length about all this, even though in terms of practical law perhaps they do not disagree, for they are not speaking of a buchya made of bricks and earth, where perhaps all agree that those made of bricks and earth are not subject to the concern that they may crack, and their dispute is only in explaining the buchya of the Gemara. Nevertheless, I have written at length to clarify the matter, because I saw that the Beis Yosef labored to press and resolve the words of the Rashba, and the later authorities after him were drawn into difficulties with his words and with the words of the Tur in Yoreh Deah. In truth there is not even a slight difficulty here, for all the matters are taken explicitly from the sugya of the Gemara. The words of the Rashba himself and the words of the Tur in Yoreh Deah require study; here in Tur Orach Chaim the view appears to be like that of the Mechaber, and he interpreted the words of the Rosh simply and did not wish to be exacting about the phrase, and heated it from inside. This is clear.
