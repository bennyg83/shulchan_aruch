In which one fries, etc. Only an oven in which chametz was baked without liquid requires libun. A frying pan does not, because we do not assume that the oil dried completely and the chametz baked without liquid. Even if it burned and adhered to the wall of the pan, some moisture still remained there. The same applies to a metal pot used for cooking, even though food sometimes sticks to its walls.
Into which oil is poured. The same applies to fat. Some wished to say that fat is not considered a liquid, but rather part of the meat itself, and its use is therefore considered direct fire. The language of the authorities, however, does not indicate this.
It is permitted through hagalah. The reason is as stated above: it is used for baking with liquid, and there is no difference between oil and water. Trays upon which cakes are baked directly, however, require libun according to the law, even if their underside is greased with fat, oil, or butter, such as pastry tins used for tarts or baked cakes. Likewise, pans sometimes used to warm bran for a sick person require libun according to the law.
And some are stringent to heat the frying pan. This is because at times the fat dries and the dough bakes directly upon the pan.
Nevertheless, it is sufficient, etc. Meaning, according to the strict law. Peri Chadash tends to rule that full libun is required according to the law; see there and Biur Halachah.
