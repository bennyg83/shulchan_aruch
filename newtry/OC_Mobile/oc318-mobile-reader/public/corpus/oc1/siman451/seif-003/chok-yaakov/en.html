Knives, one performs hagalah on them in a first vessel. This is specifically regarding Pesach knives, or from meat to milk or the reverse. But knives of prohibited foods require libun, even if they have no indentations, whenever one wishes to use them with hot food, as explained in Yoreh Deah siman 121, seif 6; see there. In some editions of the Shulchan Aruch there a printing error occurred; see Pri Chadash there. Even though nearby, in seif 4, the Mechaber ruled that spits and grates require libun even for Pesach, because it is considered that they absorbed prohibition, since the name of chametz already rests upon it, as explained in the Tur and Beis Yosef, and see Yoreh Deah siman 121, nevertheless here, regarding knives, since there is another basis for leniency, we rely on the poskim who hold that it is considered absorption of permitted matter. See Olas Shabbos, who left the Mechaber's words as difficult, but in truth this is not difficult at all. Similarly one must say regarding the Mechaber's words in this siman and in siman 452 several times, that sometimes where there is another basis for leniency we rely on this reasoning, that it is considered absorption of permitted matter. See Pri Chadash there, end of siman 121, who decided that even knives of prohibition are sufficient with hagalah even ab initio. In truth, it appears to me that the main ruling ab initio is like the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch there, and so wrote the Issur VeHeter, klal 58, law 21, in the name of Or Zarua. However, after the fact one should not forbid if one performed hagalah on it and used it with hot food.
In a first vessel, and they are permitted. The Bach and other later authorities wrote that since one must also perform hagalah on their handles, therefore if the handle is fixed with small nails, hagalah is not effective, because there are many indentations at the place of the nails. The same applies wherever the handle has two pieces, and the same applies if part of the handle is horn, or if the handle is attached to the iron with glue; one should not perform hagalah on it, because he has pity on them lest they crack. Libun also is not relevant for their handles unless the whole knife is iron. Since most knives are made in this manner, the poet was correct to state that if new ones can be made, they are preferable. The Netz argued at length to be lenient in this, and held that the handle is of no consequence at all. See Yoreh Deah siman 121, seif 7, in Hagahos Ashri, that a knife of prohibition is subjected to libun and afterward hagalah. So too wrote the Issur VeHeter, klal 58, law 21: either he removes the handle and subjects it to libun, or he subjects it to libun first and afterward performs hagalah; see there. Nevertheless, one must clean out the handle well as much as possible before performing hagalah, and so is the practice. See below, s.k. 20.
While it is still boiling. The Magen Avraham wrote: and to insert the knife into it; but he should not perform hagalah on it by means of a heated stone.
To remove all rust. Meaning that he need not sharpen it except in order to remove rust. But if he sharpens it well, in a way that removes somewhat of the iron, as explained in Yoreh Deah siman 121 in the words of the Mechaber, it is like libun; see there in the gloss.
All rust. That is what we call rust, from which a dust-like substance forms when one scrapes it. But if it has only one of the discolorations that we call spots, there is no concern. So wrote the Taz, and the same law applies regarding tin utensils, where it is common to have what are called earth spots in German.
If it has indentations. See Tosafos and the Rosh at the end of the last chapter of Avodah Zarah, and the Beis Yosef in this siman and in Yoreh Deah siman 151, where it is explained that regarding large knives there is greater concern for indentations than with ordinary small knives, which do not have indentations. They derived this from the Yerushalmi. However, the Issur VeHeter, klal 58, law 17, wrote the opposite: most of our knives, and especially the small ones, have indentations; end quote.

See Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 121 in the gloss, where it is explained that after the fact, if one performed hagalah on it or sharpened it well and used it with hot food, it does not forbid. Ab initio it requires libun, and if he cannot subject it to libun well because of the handle, he should perform hagalah afterward. After the fact, with libun alone, the food is not forbidden. If he sharpened it well with a whetstone in every place and performed hagalah afterward, this is effective even ab initio like libun, provided he can clean its indentations.
