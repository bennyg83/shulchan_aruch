The cited ruling. The Taz explains the kashering of utensils for Pesach, according to their normal mode of use, with distinctions between hagalah, libbun, pouring from a kli rishon, soaking, cleaning away visible chametz, and after-the-fact cases. He clarifies the underlying sugyos and when the stringency is required by law or only as the preferred practice. In particular, it addresses knives, including handles, cracks, rivets, and rust.
All rust. For it is written, only the gold and the silver; from here we learn that one must remove the rust. It seems to me that this refers to what we call rust, where there is actual substance in that rust, which comes from the utensil having been in a hidden place where the air did not reach. When it is scraped from there it is like earthiness. The reason is that perhaps there is actual chametz there and the rust covers it, and hagalah does not help for something that is actual substance. But sometimes there is blackness or redness in some place on the utensil, especially in cauldrons and frying pans, and it is impossible to remove it completely even after scraping, but there is no actual substance there, only appearance. He may perform hagalah on it, for otherwise you would find scarcely one in a city that could be kashered, since many have such marks that cannot be removed. And if you would say that indeed this is so, why did he conclude, therefore if there are hollows in the knife, etc.? He should have said that even without hollows, if one cannot remove the black marks he should not perform hagalah. Rather, certainly there is no concern about such appearance, since it has no substance that we should say is actual chametz. Hagalah helps after he scrapes all that is possible, though in any case he should perform good libbun on that place, as seems to me.
And it requires libbun, etc. With libbun one need not remove the rust, for the fire burns everything, as written in Yoreh Deah, siman 121.
