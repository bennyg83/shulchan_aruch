Through fire. Meaning, without water, such as a spit or grate upon which chametz-filled pastry is roasted. Although a grate is commonly greased with fat, that slight moisture does not prevent the fire from acting directly upon it, unlike the frying pan discussed below in seif 11. Since the chametz itself touches the utensil during roasting and no liquid lies between them to carry the taste into the spit or grate, the chametz taste is absorbed solely through the heat of the fire. It therefore cannot be expelled by hagalah in boiling water, but only by fire, following the rule that it releases in the same manner in which it absorbed. If one knows that chametz was not roasted upon it and only meat was roasted with salt that had not been checked for chametz, hagalah is sufficient even initially.
They require libun. A brief introduction is necessary for several places. The Gemara states that only prohibited matter absorbed into a utensil through fire requires libun. Permitted matter absorbed through fire that later becomes prohibited, such as sacrificial meat roasted on a spit and later becoming nosar, requires only hagalah. The authorities disagree concerning chametz absorbed into a utensil. Some consider this permitted absorption, since when the chametz pastry was absorbed during the year it was permitted, and therefore hagalah suffices. According to them, every utensil used through fire needs only hagalah for Pesach. Others maintain that nosar is different, because at the time of absorption it was not yet nosar at all. Chametz, however, was already chametz when absorbed and remains chametz now; only its prohibition has now taken effect. It is therefore considered prohibited absorption and requires libun. Most authorities agree with this view, and the Mechaber rules accordingly. Nevertheless, one should not automatically apply this ruling elsewhere, because when additional grounds for leniency exist, the view that chametz is considered permitted absorption may be combined with them.

From the outside. Meaning, if straw is placed against its outer surface, the straw will burn from the heat of the libun.
And the practice follows the first opinion, etc. The principal ruling follows the Mechaber, that chametz is considered prohibited absorption. Proper libun is therefore required, until sparks fly from the utensil. Even after the fact, if it was used on Pesach after only lighter libun or hagalah, the food should be forbidden. In a case of major loss or loss of Yom Tov enjoyment, however, if the utensil was not ben yomo from its chametz use, one may after the fact rely upon the authorities who consider chametz permitted absorption and accept hagalah or light libun.
But it has cracks. As explained above, libun is required at that location. Burning coals should be placed there long enough that straw burns on the outside. At that level of heat, any actual prohibited substance deep within the cracks and indentations is certainly burned away.
A trivet requires libun. This is a three-legged implement upon which a pot or pan is placed over the fire throughout the year. To use it on Pesach, it should be heated with fire, because dough may sometimes spill upon it and chametz taste may be absorbed through fire. This is only an initial stringency because of chametz. In truth, taste does not pass between two dry pots that touch one another, as explained in Yoreh Deah 92:7, and even if something spilled upon the trivet, it may be assumed to have burned away because the trivet is constantly over the fire. Light libun is therefore certainly sufficient. After the fact, even if it was used without any libun, the food is not forbidden.
