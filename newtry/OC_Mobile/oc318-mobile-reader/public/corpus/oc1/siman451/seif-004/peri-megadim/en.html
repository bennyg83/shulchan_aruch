And if. See Taz and Magen Avraham, os 4. With fired bricks, specifically libbun is required, while a brick dried in the sun has the law of earthenware and is permitted through hagalah. But if it was heated from the outside, even after the fact it is forbidden, according to its manner of use is its kashering, since it absorbs chametz on the inside even though it is always heated from the outside. So too is in the Gemara and Levush: a baker's tile is heated from the outside, and is forbidden, with a vav. With metal utensils, specifically if he filled them with coals on the inside; but if from the outside, even if their use is always that way and they became hot until sparks fly from them, it is forbidden after the fact. So too is in the Tur. See below, seif. The same applies to metal for which hagalah suffices: if he inserted it into the cauldron up to its rim, and afterward that part, but the water did not enter inside, even after the fact it is forbidden.

The Peri Megadim analyzes the kashering of utensils for Pesach, according to their normal mode of use, with distinctions between hagalah, libbun, pouring from a kli rishon, soaking, cleaning away visible chametz, and after-the-fact cases. He also weighs the rules of absorbed taste, visible residue, sharp foods, earthenware, and whether the absorbed taste is treated as permitted before Pesach. In particular, it addresses ovens, winter stoves, and hot surfaces, earthenware and its special stringencies.
