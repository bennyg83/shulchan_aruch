Taz s.k. 8. And it is difficult to me: for that which we hold in siman 447, se'if 4, is not understandable; regarding nullification, what does it matter that its name remains upon it and it is called by the name of a prohibition? Nevertheless put it on the law that it is nullified like all other prohibitions. For even when it was mixed on eve of Pesach after midday, when certainly the name of a prohibition is upon it, it is nullified; and since it was nullified and its prohibition departed, it was permitted entirely. But regarding "it absorbed permitted [food]," one may say that since its name remains upon it, the name of a prohibition is upon it. And likewise regarding nat bar nat, where the Rashba holds that it is not called nat bar nat of permitted [food], for chametz still has its name upon it. And that is all that it is not called "permitted absorption," but it is not more stringent than the absorption of other prohibitions. Also what the Taz wrote, and further "I am puzzled by a frying pan": behold the Taz himself in se'if 16 wrote that there it is by means of liquid. Also what he wrote, "and one must say that this spit is dealing with," etc.: the difficulty from siman 452 is still not resolved, for regarding nat bar nat it is called "it absorbed permitted [food]," and if so, even if one roasted chametz in kind, hagalah should suffice. And all of this requires further study.
Se'if 4 in the gloss. With light libun like this. A trivet needs libun: it implies at first glance that even after the fact it is forbidden. And it appears to me in my humble opinion that that is specifically if one used upon it in kind; but by means of a pot, such as fish and the like, it is not less than two pots; see Yoreh De'ah siman 97 and in the Taz; and so wrote Mishbetzos Zahav here. And accordingly, regarding one who lodged in a non-Jew's house, it is possible to be lenient.
