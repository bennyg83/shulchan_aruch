That has a patch. This refers to a patch on the inside of the utensil. A patch on the outside causes no problem.
The patch preceded it. Meaning, the patch was applied when the utensil was new, or the utensil underwent hagalah before the patch was applied.
It need not be removed. This is during hagalah, and applies when the patch is firmly attached so that there is no concern for actual chametz beneath it. One need be concerned only for chametz taste absorbed through the patch, and we say that just as it entered through the patch, so it leaves through the patch. If cracks surround the patch and actual chametz may lie there, libun is required, as above in seif 4, or the area must be cleaned thoroughly. If neither was done, the food is forbidden even after the fact.
It must be removed, etc. Even when firmly attached, we are concerned that a small amount of actual chametz remained on the utensil before the patch was applied, and hagalah does not remove actual substance. Some authorities also fear that absorbed prohibition entered the utensil before the patch was applied and cannot fully exit through the intervening patch, and may later enter food on Pesach.
Until it burns, etc. According to the first reason, concerning actual chametz, light libun is sufficient, until straw burns on the outside, for this certainly burns any actual chametz, as stated above regarding other cracks. According to the second reason, full libun is required, with abundant coals upon the patch until sparks fly from it, thereby burning the absorbed prohibition within the utensil. It appears that when the utensil is not ben yomo one may be lenient. This applies to a plate-like patch attached with nails, as is common on cauldrons. A patch fused by fire, as is done when a perforated tin vessel is repaired by dripping molten metal over it, is kashered by hagalah and requires no coals upon the patch even if the prohibited absorption preceded it. The heat of the molten metal burns any actual prohibition beneath it, and absorption beyond the patch leaves through hagalah. Since the patch was fused by fire, it becomes fully one with the utensil.
And afterward he performs hagalah on the entire utensil. Old first-vessel utensils newly coated with tin could, according to the strict law, be permitted without hagalah, because they are ordinarily heated from the outside until the metal melts within, and that itself is equivalent to hagalah. Since craftsmen sometimes coat them hurriedly and the coating does not reach every area because the utensil was not heated properly, one should always require hagalah. After the fact, however, if they were used without hagalah, the food is not forbidden, especially when the utensil was not ben yomo. If the craftsman who coated it was a non-Jew, it is proper to immerse it without a blessing.
