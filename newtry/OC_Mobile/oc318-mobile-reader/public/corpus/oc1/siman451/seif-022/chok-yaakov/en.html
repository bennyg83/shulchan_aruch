Than a leavening vessel. This is the utensil in which sourdough is made in order to leaven the dough. The Rav already wrote above in siman 402, seif 11, and see this siman, seif 16, that for all kneading utensils the practice is to coat them with clay or give them to a non-Jew.
And a charoses vessel. This means that sharp items are placed in it together with chametz. See the Taz, who wonders at the Rav for ruling here without qualification like the Mechaber, that hagalah suffices, while in seif 16 regarding a mortar he required specifically libun, and that was learned from the law of a leavening vessel and a charoses vessel. He answered that there it is different, for it speaks of a metal mortar, since libun is possible, whereas here it speaks of wooden and tin utensils, where libun is impossible; end quote. I have not reached the end of his thinking, for regarding the mortar above the Rav wrote explicitly that if it is wooden, it has no remedy through hagalah alone, but one must peel it with an artisan's tool and perform hagalah. In my humble opinion, there is no initial difficulty. First, it is possible that the Rama himself relied on the fact that a charoses vessel is comparable to a leavening vessel, which is a kneading utensil and requires coating with clay, as I wrote in s.k. 59. Alternatively, above it speaks where one wants to resume pounding sharp items in the mortar on Pesach in its ordinary manner, and therefore we are more stringent, whereas here he does not want to use it during Pesach in its original manner for sharp items, but only for other things. The proof is that it is similar to a leavening vessel, where he certainly is not using sourdough or chametz in it during Pesach. This is clear.
But cold. See what the Rav wrote above in seif 1 in the gloss, and what I wrote there.
And not to use even cold. Even if it is not a leavening vessel or a charoses vessel.
And a winter-house oven. The later authorities agree that after the fact the dish cooked inside it is permitted, and so it appears to me to be the main ruling, for it is no worse than two pots, which do not absorb from one another, as explained in the Taz, Yoreh Deah siman 92, s.k. 29. See my work Minchas Yaakov, klal 55, s.k. 22. Likewise, if it is a pressing circumstance and it is impossible in another way, it is treated like after the fact. Nevertheless, in any case one should clean the oven well on all sides, heat it well, and afterward coat with clay the place where the pot rests, to the thickness of a finger. But if one baked matzah inside it without an intervening barrier, one should forbid even after the fact. However, in the Levush it implies that one may permit through thorough heating even ab initio, since it was heated from inside. One need not heat the entire oven full of wood, only the place where he usually heats all year and the place where the pot rests, for as it absorbed, so it discharges. This is unlike an oven in which one bakes, where we require coals to pass over all of it, since its way is to heat all of it. The same applies if one bakes in this oven. This is clear. So too I concluded in my work Minchas Yaakov, klal 60, that this appears to me to be the main ruling, and see what I wrote above in s.k. 9. Yet who would act lightly in a matter where the later authorities agreed to be stringent? My grandfather was also stringent in the matter, as my brother-in-law the Rav wrote in Sefer Eshel Zahav. Therefore one should not be lenient if matzah was baked in it, in any case. Nevertheless, in a case of great loss and prevention of Yom Tov joy, one may permit after the fact, and in pressing circumstances even ab initio. Regarding the outside of the oven, one may permit simply by coating it with clay to the thickness of a finger.
