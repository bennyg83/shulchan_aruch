In which chametz was used cold. Meaning, dry chametz was used in them, or even something moist, provided it did not remain there for twenty-four hours and was not sharp. If it remained there for twenty-four hours, it is considered pickled in the utensil, and even other utensils require hagalah or three-day soaking, as stated above in seif 21.
Even with hot food. Since they were used only cold, they absorbed nothing, and rinsing suffices to remove what is actually adhering to the walls of the utensil.
Except for a sourdough vessel. This is a utensil in which a woman soaks sourdough before placing it into dough. At times the sourdough remains in that utensil for a long time, and because its leavening power is strong, its taste is absorbed into the utensil even while cold.
And a charoses vessel. This means a utensil into which sharp substances are placed together with chametz, even if the utensil is not earthenware.
Baked matzah. Cold matzah. The same applies to any other cold item, even if moist, provided the utensil was first rinsed. This permission applies only occasionally, not on a regular basis, as stated above in seif 1 in the gloss.
It is permitted. A cold item, even when moist, does not extract what is absorbed in the utensil unless it remains there for twenty-four hours.
It is forbidden to knead in them. The leavening potency absorbed in the utensil causes dough kneaded in it to ferment more quickly.
But through hagalah, etc., they are permitted. They may then be used either for kneading or with hot food. All this is according to the strict law. According to the Rama above in seif 17, however, the custom is not to use kneading utensils because they cannot certainly be cleaned thoroughly, and the same stringency should apply to a sourdough vessel. If it is made of metal, the Rama already wrote above in seif 16 regarding a mortar, which the authorities equated to a sourdough vessel, that the custom is initially to subject it to libun. This is all the more true of an actual sourdough vessel.
He should not use matzah in them, except cold. This is the reading of Bach and Peri Chadash. Others read, “not even cold.” The beginning of the seif, which permits cold use, does not discuss an earthenware sourdough or charoses vessel.
Not to use them. Even cold, and even other utensils that are not sourdough vessels but were used with chametz only while cold.
And a winter-house oven. This means an oven used to heat the house in winter, upon which people regularly place pies and other chametz throughout the year. Chametz taste is absorbed there through the heat of the fire when the oven is heated, and can never be fully expelled, even by firing the inside of the oven. The same applies to the tiles built into such ovens, upon which chametz dishes and pieces of bread are regularly placed.
Its law is like that of earthenware, etc. Earthenware never releases what is absorbed in its walls except through firing, where there is no concern that it will crack, as above in seif 2 regarding a small oven. Here, firing the inside, even if done properly, does not remove what was absorbed from above. It resembles the case there in seif 2, where heating from the outside does not remove what was absorbed from the opposite side.
To place anything upon the oven. If the oven is hot, it is forbidden even when the matzah is cold; likewise, if the matzah is hot, it is forbidden even when the oven is cold. If both are cold, regular use is forbidden, while occasional use is permitted after sweeping and cleaning the oven surface so that no actual chametz remains, as stated above at the end of seif 1, seif katan 16. After the fact, if matzah was placed there while the oven was hot, while the matzah was hot, or while both were hot, the underside of the matzah is forbidden to the depth of peeling. This applies when the matzah was placed directly upon the oven. If pots containing food were placed there, even when both surfaces were hot, the food is not forbidden after the fact, because absorbed prohibition does not pass from the oven wall into the wall of the pot. Initially, however, pots may not be placed there under any circumstances unless both are cold. If an intervening sheet of metal or similar object is placed beneath, or the surface is coated with clay one fingerbreadth thick, everything may be used above it. These laws also apply to the tiles built into the sides of ovens, except that there an iron barrier or clay coating only beneath does not suffice unless clay is also applied to the sides and above. The upper clay coating does not prevent permission after the fact. When the pots placed there are covered, initial use is permitted even without clay above. Regarding the inside of an oven that was not kashered, if matzah was placed there, its law is the same as upon the oven, as explained. Baking matzos there is governed by siman 461. Cooking there initially requires kashering as for baking, or coating the floor with clay one fingerbreadth thick. After the fact, however, the cooked food is not forbidden even if the oven was not kashered.
