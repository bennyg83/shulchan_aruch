Seif 2: On Chol HaMoed it is likewise forbidden, etc. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 2, who writes that one who puts on tefillin on Chol HaMoed does not violate bal tosif because he does not intend to add, meaning that he acts because of the doubt whether Chol HaMoed is obligated in tefillin. At first glance this contradicts Mordechai in the first chapter of Megillah. Mordechai asks why the Megillah is not read on the fifteenth as well because of uncertainty regarding the day, and answers, according to one explanation, that this would violate bal tosif. This shows that bal tosif applies even in a case of doubt. One may answer that this supports Radbaz's view, cited by Magen Avraham in siman 60, that D'Rabbanan mitzvos do not require intent. Accordingly, Semak and Magen Avraham mean that because he does not intend to add, and mitzvos require intent, he does not violate bal tosif. With a D'Rabbanan mitzvah, however, which does not require intent, he would violate bal tosif even without intent. Although Rosh Hashanah 28b concludes that where intent is not required to fulfill a mitzvah, intent is nevertheless required to violate bal tosif outside the proper time, that applies when he has no intent at all for the sake of a mitzvah. Here, however, he does intend to perform a mitzvah, but only because of uncertainty whether it is obligatory. One may therefore distinguish: if mitzvos require intent, such conditional intent is insufficient for bal tosif; but if mitzvos do not require intent, such conditional intent suffices, and he violates bal tosif. Yet Megillah is based upon prophetic ordinance and is treated like Torah law. It should therefore require intent. Why, then, should there be any concern for bal tosif? We must conclude that even where one acts because of doubt, there remains a concern of bal tosif. One may explain Mordechai differently. He offers another answer based upon the phrase “and these days shall not pass.” The two answers may be complementary. One might argue that because it is only doubtful whether the prohibition of “they shall not pass” is violated, the rule of doubt concerning a D'Rabbanan matter should lead to leniency. Yet with respect to the obligation to read, since one was certainly obligated and it is doubtful whether he has fulfilled his obligation, one should be stringent. If Megillah is considered like Torah law, then the prohibition of “they shall not pass” is also treated like Torah law, and doubt requires stringency. Mordechai may therefore be uncertain whether Megillah is fully comparable to Torah law. If it is, bal tosif would not apply because intent is required and he acts only because of doubt, but “they shall not pass” would require stringency. If it is not fully like Torah law, then the prohibition of “they shall not pass” would be treated leniently as D'Rabbanan, but because the mitzvah is D'Rabbanan and does not require intent, bal tosif would apply. Either way, the difficulty is resolved. In truth, however, the question can be answered more simply based upon Mordechai's own words. He writes that although outside the proper time bal tosif applies only with intent, the fifteenth is nevertheless the proper time for the residents of walled cities. Rosh Hashanah concludes that during the proper time bal tosif does not require intent, while outside the proper time it does. Accordingly, on Chol HaMoed, if it is not a time for tefillin at all, it is outside the proper time and intent is required. Since he does not intend to add, but acts only because of doubt, there is no violation. Regarding the Megillah on the fifteenth, however, since that day is the proper time for the residents of walled cities, bal tosif applies even without intent. This is satisfactory.
