There is no obligation to eat matzah. Magen Avraham cites Ran as explaining that although one is obligated to eat bread on Yom Tov, he may fulfill that obligation with egg matzah. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 58, however, writes that Semag and Or Zarua prove from here that one is not obligated to eat bread on Yom Tov. I likewise found in Tosafot, Sukkah 27a, s.v. “If he wishes,” that one is not obligated to eat bread except on the first night of Pesach and the first night of Sukkos. See Magen Avraham, siman 188, seif katan 9.
Except on the first night. Kol Bo, siman 50, writes that for this reason we recite the blessing on eating matzah only on the first nights. From then onward eating matzah is optional, and one eats it only because chametz cannot be eaten, just as one who eats the meat of a kosher animal rather than a non-kosher animal does not recite a blessing over that choice. Such eating serves only the needs of his body and satisfies his hunger, rather than constituting a mitzvah. Eating in the sukkah is different. Although it is also optional on the remaining days, sitting there is not a bodily need but the fulfillment of a mitzvah, and therefore it is proper to recite the blessing “to dwell in the sukkah.” See below, siman 639, and the words of the later authorities. Tosafot in Sukkah 27a, s.v. “He retracted,” writes that if one did not eat matzah on the first nights of Pesach, there is no makeup obligation during the remaining days of Pesach. It appears to me that one may derive from this that a person who did not eat matzah on the first nights because of some unavoidable circumstance does not afterward recite the blessing on eating matzah when he is able to eat it, since the later eating is optional and there is no makeup obligation. This is clear.
A remembrance of the breads of the thanksgiving offering. Maharil further writes that it is called lechem oni, and a poor person brings one tenth of an ephah.
And they make a distinguishing mark upon them. Rama writes in responsum 119 that one should not form letters upon them as a distinguishing mark, because breaking the matzah would constitute erasing on Yom Tov. See siman 340. Magen Avraham cites Mahari Weil as ruling that if one has many children and the required amount of dough is insufficient, one may knead two doughs, each smaller than the measure requiring challah, join them, and make three matzos from both together. Mahari Weil further writes that the second matzah should be made larger than the others, because each person must eat two portions from it, one at HaMotzi and one for afikoman. Maharil writes: It is proper to measure carefully so that the matzos do not exceed one isaron. However, if there are four or five eggs more, one need not be particular. This is contrary to what Magen Avraham cites in the name of Darkei Moshe from Maharil. See below, simanim 456 and 457, regarding the practice.
If one of them broke. In these countries the custom is to make four matzos from the dough, calling the fourth the “matzah of doubt,” so that if a mishap occurs to one of the others, they use it. Although only three should be made, corresponding to the breads of a thanksgiving offering, see Levush. It is possible that the common practice relies upon the fact that they customarily make the dough somewhat larger than one isaron. They therefore make only three matzos from the isaron and make one additional matzah from the excess. Furthermore, since it is prepared only for a possible doubt, there is no objection.
They use it as the second matzah. This applies even if the third matzah broke, so that the matzah originally designated as the third now precedes the second. Nevertheless, one should not bake another matzah merely to preserve the original order of precedence for the mitzvah. This is the ruling of the Agur. In these countries they use the extra matzah so that the order is not reversed and the second matzah is not displaced from its mitzvah. This applies specifically if the third matzah broke. If one of the first two broke, however, it appears clear to me according to the Agur’s reasoning that since the broken matzah is not entirely displaced from its mitzvah, and on the contrary its status is elevated, if the first matzah broke it should be used as the second so that it is not completely displaced from its mitzvah. This is clear. Although it is an enhancement of the mitzvah that the second matzah also be whole, as Maharshal writes in responsum 88, we nevertheless do not displace it in such a case when it broke after the fact.
