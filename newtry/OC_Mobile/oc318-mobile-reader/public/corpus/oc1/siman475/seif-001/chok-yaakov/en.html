He should wash his hands. The language of the Gemara is: Although he already washed once, since he must recite the Haggadah and Hallel, perhaps he diverted his attention and touched something. Beit Yosef writes that one should not intend with the first washing to complete the entire meal without diverting his attention, for one should not annul the enactment of the Sages, who instituted washing twice on the night of Pesach. His intent is that since the Gemara states that a second washing is required because perhaps he diverted his attention, we may not act more wisely than the decree of the Sages and say that he will not divert his attention and will wash only once. His words are correct, and this is the custom. Bach and Taz objected to him without justification in this matter.
And he should hold them in his hand. Since there are differing opinions as to over which matzah one should recite HaMotzi and over which one should recite the blessing on eating matzah, or whether both blessings should be recited over one matzah, he should hold all of them in his hand in order to satisfy all opinions. Maharil writes: He should hold the three matzos in his hand, the upper one for HaMotzi, the third for lechem mishneh, and the broken one because it is lechem oni. He should recite HaMotzi, allow the third matzah to slip from his hand, and recite the blessing on eating matzah while holding the broken piece together with the upper matzah. He should recite both blessings before breaking them. This is also the conclusion of the later authorities.
And on eating matzah. Regarding why we recite the blessing with the wording “on eating matzah” rather than “to eat matzah,” see above, siman 433.
The custom is not to dip it in salt on the first night. Maharil writes that this is because of affection for the matzah, while the Levush writes that the reason is that it is lechem oni. During the rest of the year the custom is to dip even clean bread in salt, and this is also done throughout Pesach, except on the first two nights. The Rama’s reference to the first night is not exclusive, as Maharil writes explicitly; see there.
And he should eat them together while reclining. The reason appears straightforward to me. Even according to the opinion that one recites HaMotzi over the whole matzah and the blessing on eating matzah over the broken one, or the reverse, one cannot recite a blessing and eat them one after the other. If he first recites HaMotzi and eats, how can he afterward recite the blessing on eating matzah when he has already eaten matzah? Likewise, he certainly cannot first recite the blessing on eating matzah and eat before reciting HaMotzi. He must therefore recite both blessings first. If he then eats only the piece over which HaMotzi was recited, there will be an interruption between the blessing on matzah and eating the matzah; the reverse is likewise true. He should therefore eat them together so that there is no interruption at all. Nevertheless, if he cannot eat them together, he may eat them one after the other, and this is not considered an interruption after the fact, since it relates to the eating. See above, siman 167. This appears to me to be the simple reason of the early authorities. See the later authorities, who offered other reasons; according to what I have written, this is the primary reason. See Bach and Taz, who cite Maharal of Prague as asking how they may be eaten together, since in the chapter HaIsh Mekadesh, and in Eruvin 50, it is stated that anything that cannot take effect successively cannot take effect simultaneously either. See what they answered. In my humble opinion, there is no difficulty at all, and that principle has no relevance here. There are many matters and mitzvos whose proper fulfillment is specifically simultaneous, such as matzah and maror according to Hillel, and the four species of the lulav, and many similar cases. The rule there concerns one who betrothed two sisters. If he betroths one first, her kiddushin takes effect, and the second woman whom he betroths afterward is not betrothed. Therefore, even if he betroths them simultaneously, neither is betrothed. The same applies to the other cases listed there. Here, however, the mitzvah is specifically to eat both together, not one alone and not one after the other. Their mitzvah is fulfilled together, and this is clear.
A kezayis from each. This implies that the portion for HaMotzi also requires a kezayis. Magen Avraham writes likewise. See what Taz writes regarding this.
He should eat the HaMotzi portion. See above, siman 472, seif katan 16, where I cited Mahari as ruling that the kezayis eaten for HaMotzi does not require reclining. The words of the Tur, however, imply that both portions require reclining, and this is the custom. Magen Avraham writes that initially one should at least swallow a crushed kezayis at one time. After the fact, even if he eats it little by little, he fulfills his obligation, provided he does not pause between the portions for the time required to eat a pras. The same applies to maror; even initially, if it is difficult for him to swallow it together, he may eat it in portions.
And he should immerse it entirely in the charoses. This is to neutralize the harmful substance within it. He should not recite the blessing until after dipping it, immediately before eating, for that is considered prior to performing the mitzvah. This is also evident above, siman 473, seif 6, concerning the first dipping, contrary to Maharil. Magen Avraham writes likewise there.
And he wraps it together with the maror. This is stated in Pesachim 115a, where Hillel and the Sages dispute whether, when the Beis HaMikdash stood, one wrapped matzah and maror together or ate each separately. The Gemara concludes that since the halachah was stated neither in accordance with one nor the other, we practice both ways: first we eat them separately, one after the other, and then together. Rambam, Rosh, and Tur rule accordingly. I saw in Beis Hillel, siman 98, seif katan 4, that he expresses astonishment at Rosh, Tur, and Beit Yosef for ruling there simply that prohibitions nullify one another. He writes: It is exceedingly astonishing that they overlooked what is stated in Zevachim 79 concerning Reish Lakish’s statement that prohibitions nullify one another. The Gemara says that this disputes Rabbi Elazar, who states, “Just as mitzvos do not nullify one another, so too prohibitions do not nullify one another.” Whom do we find saying that mitzvos do not nullify one another? It is Hillel, for it was taught that Hillel the Elder would wrap them together and eat them because it is stated, “They shall eat it with matzos and maror.” Since the Gemara links the rule that prohibitions do not nullify one another to Hillel’s rule that mitzvos do not nullify one another, and in the final chapter of Pesachim we rule stringently to satisfy all views, why did they rule leniently here that prohibitions nullify one another? This is the end of Beis Hillel’s question, and he labors greatly to answer it; see there. In my humble opinion, there is not even the beginning of a difficulty. According to his understanding that the two laws are fully dependent upon one another, why does the Gemara say that Reish Lakish disputes Rabbi Elazar? It should have said that he disputes Hillel, who holds that mitzvos do not nullify one another. Rather, the Gemara’s meaning is plainly this: Reish Lakish disputes Rabbi Elazar, who says that just as mitzvos do not nullify one another, prohibitions likewise do not nullify one another. Reish Lakish holds that we do not make this comparison. Although mitzvos do not nullify one another because both are directed toward the same purpose, namely remembrance of the Exodus from Egypt, prohibitions nevertheless do nullify one another. This is simple and clear, and there is no difficulty. Regarding his further question as to why they ruled contrary to Rambam, I already noted this in my work Minchas Yaakov, klal 85, seif katan 41; see there.
And so I have seen practiced. The later authorities agree that it should be dipped in charoses. Magen Avraham writes that where this is the custom, the custom should be followed.
And he says, “A remembrance of the Mikdash.” The Aruch, under the entry “zecher,” writes: “A remembrance of the Mikdash according to Hillel” means that when the Beis HaMikdash stood, Hillel would wrap matzah and maror and eat them together. Since both obligations were according to the Torah, neither nullified the other. Nowadays, however, matzah is according to the Torah, as it is written, “In the evening you shall eat matzos,” while maror is D’Rabbanan. They may therefore not be eaten together, for the D’Rabbanan obligation would nullify the Torah obligation. Consequently, one first eats each separately and afterward eats them together as a remembrance of the Mikdash according to Hillel. Since this latter eating is only a remembrance, no blessing is recited over it. On this basis, Rambam’s ruling in chapter 8 of Hilchos Chametz U’Matzah can also be reconciled and defended against Raavad’s objection. Rambam rules that when the Beis HaMikdash stood one could eat matzah and maror together or one after the other, because it made no difference whether they were eaten together, since they did not nullify one another. This is especially understandable according to his position that prohibitions do not nullify one another. Although the halachah was not stated either in accordance with Hillel or with the Sages, one who acted according to either view fulfilled the mitzvah, and it was preferable initially to eat them together, as the wording of the baraisa also implies. Nowadays, however, because maror is D’Rabbanan and would nullify the Torah obligation of matzah, we must practice both ways. This is clear. Responsa Shaar Ephraim, simanim 53 and 54, discusses Rambam’s view at length. According to what I have written, the matter is clear and correct.
According to Hillel. Our text reads: “This is what Hillel did when the Beis HaMikdash stood: he would wrap matzah and maror and eat them,” etc. Taz writes that one should say, “He would wrap the Pesach offering, matzah, and maror,” as Rashi explains in the Gemara that this is what Hillel did. Indeed, Rashi explains it this way, and so write Rosh, Tur, and Tosafot in Pesachim 120a, s.v. “At the end.” Rambam, however, in chapter 8 of Hilchos Chametz U’Matzah, writes that even when the Beis HaMikdash stood they did not wrap the Pesach offering together with the matzah and maror; rather, they ate the Pesach offering afterward. His view is more reasonable, in accordance with the wording of the verse in Behaaloscha, “They shall eat it with matzos and maror,” meaning that they should eat the Pesach offering upon the matzah and maror. The Gemara likewise implies that only matzah and maror were eaten together. Even according to the authorities who hold that the Pesach offering was wrapped with them when the Beis HaMikdash stood, our text is correct. Since we say, “This is what he did,” and we now have no Pesach offering, it should not be mentioned. Otherwise, how could one state something untrue when he has no Pesach offering? It could also lead to error: by mentioning the Pesach offering and saying, “This is what he did,” one might come to eat roasted meat together with it, appearing to eat sacred meat outside its proper place. The ancient text should therefore not be altered. In the Sephardic text cited in Pesach Meubin, nothing is mentioned other than, “According to Hillel, who would wrap them and eat them together.”
He should not interrupt with speech. Shelah writes that one should not speak at all until after eating the afikoman, and that when reciting the blessing on matzah one should also intend for the afikoman. This is an excessive stringency contrary to the implication of the poskim, and it is virtually impossible to observe throughout the entire meal. The blessing certainly applies to the entire meal. What harm is there if he speaks, since throughout the year we speak during the meal even though the initial blessing remains effective until the meal is completed? The instruction not to speak before korech is because one opinion maintains that the principal mitzvah is specifically the wrapping together, and according to that opinion a blessing should be recited over it. One should therefore not speak until after korech, in order to satisfy all opinions. See Beit Yosef.
Until he eats the korech. If he transgressed and spoke, the later authorities agree that he need not repeat the blessing. He should merely take care initially not to speak.
