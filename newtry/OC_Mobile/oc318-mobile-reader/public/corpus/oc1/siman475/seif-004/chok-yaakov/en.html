Without intention. Magen Avraham writes that although mitzvos require intention, as stated in siman 60, eating is different because he has in fact eaten and derived benefit. Tosafot in the final chapter of Pesachim 115a, s.v. “Maskeif,” writes likewise. See Beit Yosef, who cites Ran and Maggid Mishneh. This requires some consideration, because the implication of the discussion in Rosh Hashanah 28a concerning Rava’s statement, “This teaches,” appears not to distinguish in this manner. See Lechem Mishneh, chapter 2 of Hilchos Shofar. The Gemara and poskim indicate that the same applies to maror: if one ate it without intention, he fulfilled his obligation. Bach writes that even according to the authorities who require intention for matzah, maror is D’Rabbanan and therefore, according to all opinions, does not require intention. This reasoning is also mentioned by Tosafot in Rosh Hashanah 28a, s.v. “Rava said,” and Magen Avraham writes similarly in siman 60, seif katan 3, in the name of later responsa. Accordingly, it is possible that regarding maror, even if he did not know that it was maror, or thought that the day was an ordinary weekday, he nevertheless fulfilled his obligation. This appears to me to be the primary ruling.
He did not fulfill his obligation. The implication of the poskim is that on the second night as well he did not fulfill his obligation. Although the second day of Yom Tov is D’Rabbanan, we do not treat it lightly. See above, siman 496. Magen Avraham asks from siman 204, seif 8, where Rama rules that if one was compelled to eat or drink, he does not recite a blessing even though his palate derives pleasure, while here the Mechaber rules that if one was compelled and ate matzah, he fulfilled his obligation.
