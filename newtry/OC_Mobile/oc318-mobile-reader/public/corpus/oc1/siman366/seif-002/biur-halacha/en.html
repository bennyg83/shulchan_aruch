Or a camp. See Mishnah Berurah. All this is in peacetime, but when Israel would go out to war, even an optional war, they are exempt from eruv chatzerot, even on their return from war, as the poskim write.
But a caravan. See the Maggid Mishneh, from whom it sounds that the difference between a camp and a caravan is that a camp means they encamp for many days, while a caravan means they encamp only one day, such as for Shabbat. Some commentators copied this. But this is not compelled by the Rambam's language, and from where is it known? On the contrary, reason suggests that even if a caravan camps for several days, as happens among desert travelers who must wait several days for some reason, they likewise need no eruv, because the Rambam's reason applies: those tents are not fixed for them. I would add that perhaps even a camp that remains several days, if they are still traveling, is like a caravan, since their tents are not currently fixed but only for rest or another reason. In my humble opinion the clearest explanation is as I wrote in Mishnah Berurah: "camp" means what is called a lager, not soldiers traveling from place to place, but soldiers encamped in one place, not in houses but in the field, as armies encamp in summer in the field and set up tents. The law teaches that even such temporary tents are included as houses and are subject to the decree of carrying from domain to domain. This applies only where they encamp with some permanence. But where people are traveling, there is no distinction between camp and caravan, nor between one day and two; as long as they do not establish themselves for a long period, they were certainly not included in the decree. Know that the basic law of caravan is unclear, and most early poskim disagree with it; see Rashba, Ritva in the name of R. Moshe HaKohen, Or Zarua, and R. Yonatan. Rashi's view in Kol Gagot also seems so, for regarding three karpeifot he writes "such as where they made an eruv," even though the case concerns a caravan; evidently a caravan is not exempt from the basic eruv requirement.
Since we have come to this, the Mechaber's view is puzzling. Here he rules plainly like the Rambam, while in siman 360:3 he copies Rashi's language about a caravan that made an eruv, the opposite of the Rambam's view. Perhaps this is what the Gra of Liadi meant when he wrote on the law of caravan "see 363"; it is a scribal error and should say "360:3," intending the difficulty I raised. One could distinguish: the Rambam exempts a caravan only where the entire caravan was surrounded from outside by one partition, though each person set up tents inside. In that case they are exempt because they are all mixed together; we ignore their inner division by tents because those tents are not fixed, and it is as though there were no tents and they were dwelling in one courtyard or karpef. But in siman 360:3, each made his own separate partition, so they cannot be considered mixed together; on the contrary, they are divided, each to himself, with openings between them, no better than two houses open to one another that require an eruv. This is indeed suggested by the Rambam's language, copied by the Mechaber: "because they are all mixed together." Still, when we examine carefully, Rashi does not agree to the caravan law even in the case of siman 366, for regarding the four exemptions of a camp, one of which is eruv chatzerot, Rashi also explains that each surrounded himself with partitions and an opening, just as on 93, and only because they are going to war were they exempt; otherwise they would be obligated. Or Zarua similarly concludes that the exemption of a camp from eruv chatzerot speaks where they made partitions, but a caravan is different. I found in Shibbolei HaLeket two explanations of "a camp is exempt from eruv chatzerot": first Rashi's explanation, and then R. Shimon ben HaYatom's explanation like the Rambam's case, where one partition surrounds all and they are divided inside by tents.
Houses on a ship. See what I wrote in Mishnah Berurah in the name of Magen Avraham and Eliyah Rabbah. At first glance, from where is this derived? Since many people ultimately dwell on the ship, there is reason to decree lest one carry from his house to the ship, analogous to carrying from a private domain to a public domain, as with people living in houses. Peri Megadim already noted this. Their words were apparently taken from Shibbolei HaLeket siman 111, from which the Rama copied his law here. There R. Binyamin writes that since we rule that houses on a ship require an eruv, their law is like a courtyard: if there are two Jews divided into two houses, they must make an eruv, but if they are not divided into two houses, they need not. In truth, there is no proof from there. He discusses that point because the ship belongs to a non-Jew, as he concludes; and with a non-Jew, one Jew is not forbidden, nor even two Jews if they are in one house, as below siman 382:1. Therefore he required two houses. But it is not necessarily because eruv always requires two houses on a ship; rather, whenever there are domains of many people they prohibit, unless there are no houses at all, for the Sages did not decree in a bare courtyard. Perhaps the reason is that we do not decree more on a ship than in a courtyard, and a courtyard is called such only when at least two houses open to it; therefore they decreed on a ship only in that way.
And if the ship has no partitions. Magen Avraham writes that the Gemara there says regarding the "boats of Meishan," according to Rashi, that these were small boats wide above and narrow below like a knife edge, and nevertheless they are private domains if they have ten-tefach partitions, because we say gud achit mechitzta, following Abaye's conclusion there. The Agudah writes that if it is not four tefachim wide within three tefachim of the bottom, it is like the sea; one may carry in it only four amot and may carry from it to the sea. I do not understand the Agudah's reason. Machatzit HaShekel explains that he follows Tosafot, who challenge Rashi and hold that gud achit does not apply here, but this is not clear, because according to Tosafot too the law may be the same for another reason: since it can be filled in, it is considered a private domain. It seems clearer that the Agudah's words derive from Or Zarua, who rules like Rav Huna, but this too is difficult from the Gemara's conclusion. Perhaps he follows Rashi's teachers' explanation that the Gemara's "is it comparable?" rejects Abaye, though Rashi forcefully rejects this, and Rabbenu Chananel explains like Rashi. In truth, Rashi's words should not be rejected from halakhah: the Arukh cites both explanations of the boats of Meishan, Rashi's and Rabbenu Chananel's; Meiri cites only Rashi and rules unlike Rav Huna; and the Ran and Ritva explain like Rashi. Tosafot explain the boats differently, as boats with walls and floors made of boards with gaps between them, water entering so people sit as if in water; nevertheless they are private domains, even if there are three-tefach gaps between boards, because we view the ship's walls as bent around the whole ship and the boards as connected. It appears both explanations are correct for law.
