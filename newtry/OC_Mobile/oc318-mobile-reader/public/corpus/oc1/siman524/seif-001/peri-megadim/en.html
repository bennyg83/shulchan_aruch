One may not. See Taz. I record here a noteworthy textual point, although it is not directly relevant. In Tehillim 104, the word describing the wild animals quenching their thirst is printed in some editions with the wrong form of the letter shin. The correct reading uses a right shin. Targum, Radak, Minchas Shai, and the grammatical works discuss the word describing the branches or leaves and whether its alef or yud is vocalized and which letter belongs to the root. Rabbi Shmuel ben Melech, in his grammatical work on Tehillim, explains the alef as quiescent and the yud as part of the verbal root, supported by the Aramaic form. The concordance appears to treat the alef as a root letter, while the Aramaic in Daniel uses a final hei. Several variant readings can therefore be accounted for, though the matter remains difficult. Since I possess only elementary knowledge of this discipline, I will not elaborate further.
The prohibition against riding on Yom Tov is a decree lest one cut a branch in order to guide the animal. See simanim 339 and 305, seif 18. In my work Maggid Mishneh I discussed the expressions “rider” and “leader.” The same concern applies to riding in a wagon when another person guides the animal. Rashi to Chullin 79a explains the case of one riding in a wagon. Bava Metzia 10b may indicate that sitting in a wagon is exempt by Torah law but forbidden D’Rabbanan. If so, the language of Scripture concerning riding requires explanation. It may be that “rider and leader” are certainly liable because they actively cause the animal to proceed. Thus the verse concerning bringing up the Ark may mean that those guiding it are treated as carrying it, which explains the punishment of Uzzah. See there and consider the matter.
The prohibition against performing kiddushin on Yom Tov should be compared with Taz and with siman 339, Magen Avraham, where Rambam’s view is discussed. What is written there regarding acquisition on Shabbos should also be consulted.
