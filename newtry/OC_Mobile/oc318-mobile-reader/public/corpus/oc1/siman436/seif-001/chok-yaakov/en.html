One who sails. Because I have seen much confusion among early and later authorities, I must discuss the sources in the Bavli and Yerushalmi at length. Pesachim 6 states in the name of Rav: One who sails or departs with a caravan more than thirty days before Pesach need not remove chametz. Within thirty days he must remove it. Rava qualifies that the exemption before thirty days applies only where he does not intend to return. If he intends to return, even from Rosh Hashanah he must remove it. The Yerushalmi states: One who departs or sails more than thirty days beforehand need not inspect, but within thirty days he must inspect. This applies where he intends to return. If he does not intend to return, even before thirty days he must inspect. In a doubtful case this is the rule, but with definite chametz, even from Rosh Hashanah. Rava says that even one who intends to return must inspect, lest he change his mind and not return. Ran explains Rashi to mean that “does not intend to return” means during Pesach. If he intends to return during Pesach, even before thirty days he must remove it so that he does not find chametz in his house upon returning. On this reading, the Bavli need not disagree with the Yerushalmi, which may mean that if he intends to return before Pesach he need not inspect before thirty days, but if he will not return until Pesach he must inspect. Others explain that “does not intend to return” means he will not return at all until after Pesach. If he intends to return either during Pesach or before Pesach, he must remove it lest he return on Erev Pesach at twilight and have no time. Rambam and Semag follow this view, though Ran writes that it does not appear consistent with the Yerushalmi. Ran further explains the Yerushalmi’s distinction between doubt and certainty to mean that the exemption before thirty days applies only where no known chametz is present. If known chametz exists, it must be removed even from Rosh Hashanah. Ran says this does not appear consistent with the Bavli, which says “need not remove,” rather than “need not inspect,” implying that even known chametz need not be removed before thirty days. It is treated like chametz under a collapsed structure, for which nullification suffices. At first glance, according to Ran’s own interpretation of the Yerushalmi, one could easily reconcile it with the second interpretation followed by Rambam. The Bavli’s rule requiring removal when he intends to return could refer to definite chametz, matching the Yerushalmi’s statement that definite chametz must be removed even from Rosh Hashanah. The Yerushalmi’s exemption where he intends to return before Pesach could refer to doubtful chametz. If he will not return before Pesach but only during Pesach, inspection would be required even in doubt. The two Talmuds would then not disagree. Perhaps Ran was compelled by the wording of Rambam, Semag, Semak, and Kol Bo, who speak only of inspection, implying doubtful chametz, yet still require inspection before thirty days whenever he intends to return before Pesach. This shows that they disregard the Yerushalmi’s distinction. The alternative opinion in seif 2 follows the Yerushalmi according to Rashi, as Ran explains. My brother-in-law in Eliyah Zuta questions Ran and Beit Yosef because the Yerushalmi concludes in Rava’s name that even one who intends to return must inspect. One may answer that since Rava requires inspection in every case, even before thirty days, his is an isolated view and need not be followed. Were I not afraid to depart from Ran, I would explain the Yerushalmi differently. Its phrase “in a doubtful case, but with certainty even from Rosh Hashanah” may refer not to the first distinction, but to the duration of absence. The Yerushalmi first says that one who will not return before Pesach, but only during Pesach, must inspect even before thirty days, meaning a relatively short period before thirty days. It then adds that this applies to doubtful chametz. Where definite chametz is present, he must remove it even a very long time beforehand, from Rosh Hashanah. According to this reading, if he intends to return before Pesach, even known chametz need not be removed before thirty days. If he will return only after Pesach, even within thirty days he need not inspect, including known chametz. If he will return during Pesach, doubtful and definite chametz differ. This interpretation, however, does not follow the Bavli as understood by Rambam and Semag, and the halachah follows the Bavli. According to Ran’s reading of the Yerushalmi, if he intends to return before Pesach, doubtful chametz need not be inspected before thirty days, but definite chametz must be removed even from Rosh Hashanah. If he will return only during Pesach, inspection and removal are required in all cases even from Rosh Hashanah. According to my alternative reading, if he intends to return before Pesach, neither definite nor doubtful chametz requires removal before thirty days. If he will return only after Pesach, even within thirty days no inspection is needed, even for definite chametz. If he intends to return during Pesach, certainty and doubt differ. This is not accepted because it conflicts with the Bavli according to Rambam and Semag. According to Rashi, there is no contradiction between the two Talmuds. The Bavli’s requirement where he intends to return during Pesach, even from Rosh Hashanah, concerns definite chametz, as implied by the term “remove.” If he does not intend to return during Pesach, even definite chametz need not be inspected before thirty days, as in the Yerushalmi. This framework clarifies every clause of the siman.
Someone who will inspect. If he leaves someone to inspect, he need not inspect himself, because a person’s agent is equivalent to himself. The later authorities agree. My brother-in-law in Eliyah Zuta correctly challenges Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah, who distinguished improperly. This is especially clear according to the ruling above, siman 434, that nullification may also be performed through an agent.
He must inspect. Taz writes that he need not nullify at this time. He may wait until Pesach arrives and nullify then. This may also be Rama’s intent in the next gloss, “and when Pesach arrives he should nullify it,” which may apply to this clause as well. Magen Avraham disagrees. Since the obligation of inspection has taken effect, he must also nullify in accordance with the Sages’ enactment, and should not rely upon remembering to nullify later. In my opinion Taz is correct. At the proper time of inspection where he is staying, there is no concern that he will forget. Moreover, according to the custom stated by Rama at the end of siman 437, every person must inspect the pockets and sleeves of his garments. Since he must inspect then, he will also remember nullification.
Before thirty days he need not inspect. Bach and Magen Avraham write that known chametz must nevertheless be removed before departure, based upon the Yerushalmi. This is incorrect according to every interpretation explained above when he does not intend to return until after Pesach. The same is certainly true according to Levush.
He must inspect. Even from Rosh Hashanah, and even for doubtful chametz. This follows Rambam and those who agree with him in their reading of the Bavli, unlike the implication of the Yerushalmi.
We are concerned that he may return on Erev Pesach at twilight. Taz limits this to one traveling far away who schedules his return only one or two days before Pesach. If he schedules it much earlier, Taz says we need not fear a delay of such length. Magen Avraham rules the opposite, that even when he intends to return much earlier, one who sails or travels with a caravan must inspect. He cites Hagahos Maimoniyos, which distinguishes ordinary travel from sailing or caravan travel, because the latter are liable to delay. Magen Avraham misunderstood the phrase “a long time” in that source. It refers to departing a long time before Pesach, not planning to return a long time before Pesach. Hagahos Semak makes this explicit. Nevertheless, the practical ruling of Magen Avraham appears correct. The Bavli and poskim state without distinction that one who sails or travels with a caravan and intends to return must inspect. We should not invent a distinction based upon speculation concerning the scheduled return. Such travelers are inherently subject to delay. Perhaps this was Magen Avraham’s true intent.
And he will have no time. Beit Yosef, citing Hagahos Semak, and the later authorities write that even if he nullified, he must inspect before leaving, lest he return at twilight, encounter chametz, and have no time to remove it. My brother-in-law in Eliyah Zuta rules that if he intends to return before Pesach, one may rely upon the alternative opinion in seif 2 when he already nullified.
Where there is fermented wheat. Taz challenges Rama, claiming that Rashba spoke only of doubtful chametz and that known chametz is forbidden. He also questions Rama’s distinction involving thirty days. After examining Rashba’s responsum, however, Rama is entirely correct. Rashba was asked about pits filled with wheat, with some kernels in the floors and walls possibly split from moisture. The questioner asked whether nullification alone suffices, like making a house into a storehouse before thirty days when he does not intend to clear it during Pesach. Rashba answered that whether the split kernels are merely suspected or are definitely present, nullification suffices, like the storehouse case. His words “either way” explicitly include known chametz. He distinguishes only when the storehouse is cleared after Pesach. If known chametz was present, perhaps it must then be removed, because the person appears to have nullified it while intending to reacquire it after Pesach. With unknown chametz, nullification may suffice and it may remain permitted, as with chametz under a collapsed structure, because there is no apparent evasion. Still, this case may be more stringent because he intended from the outset to clear the storehouse after Pesach. Rama follows this responsum. He rules that after Pesach known chametz is prohibited for benefit. Where the chametz was not known, Rashba remained uncertain, and Rama therefore permits selling the entire mixture together. Taz’s objection is therefore unfounded. Magen Avraham’s distinctions between initial and after-the-fact cases were driven by following Bach’s incorrect premise that known chametz always requires removal even before thirty days. That premise was addressed above. See also siman 433 concerning a collapsed structure.
It is forbidden for benefit. Rosh Yosef is uncertain about one who departed more than thirty days before Pesach and was therefore exempt from removal, then returned after Pesach and found chametz that he had nullified. Perhaps it is prohibited as chametz that remained over Pesach even where the failure was inadvertent or unavoidable. Alternatively, since he was not home, nullified it, and violated neither Torah nor Rabbinic law because no inspection or removal was required, perhaps it remains permitted even according to Tur below, siman 448. I discussed this question in Toras HaShelamim, responsum 6. See also simanim 433 and 448.
