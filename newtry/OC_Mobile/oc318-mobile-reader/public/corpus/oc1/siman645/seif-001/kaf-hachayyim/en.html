1) [Seif 1] A lulav whose leaves have separated, etc. The manner of a lulav is that when it emerges from the palm tree it emerges entirely as one shaft like an arrow. After it grows somewhat, it opens slightly, and the more it grows, the more its leaves open. If it remained on the tree until it opened and its leaves separated, it is valid even if he did not bind them. If, however, it remained on the tree until it opened greatly and hardened like wood, it is invalid. Tur.
2) Ibid. A lulav whose leaves have separated, etc. As long as its leaves are attached and close to one another, it is the choicest manner of performing the mitzvah, because its leaves must be bound together and not separated, as is derived from the word kapos, and this is its beauty. Levush, seif 2. If one took palm branches while they were still on the tree, before their leaves had separated at all, it appears that this is not considered a lulav without leaves. Derishah. So too ruled Chayei Adam, klal 149, sec. 8.
3) Ibid. A lulav whose leaves have separated, etc. This means that they are like the branches of a rod, which stand apart but can be bound together. Magen Avraham, s.k. 1. At first glance, his meaning is that they are like the branches of a rod, widely separated and far from the spine, yet they rise together with the spine at a distance and do not hang downward. Such a lulav is valid, provided that they can be bound together. If the leaves hardened in this manner, however, it is invalid, as the Rama wrote in seif 2. This is what the Mechaber means by saying, 'even though he did not bind them,' namely that in such a case they must at least be fit to be bound. But where they hang downward like the leaves of palm branches, even if they can be bound, it is an abnormal change, is not beautiful, and is invalid. Eshel Avraham, sec. 1.
4) Ibid. And they did not become detached, etc. This means that they were not uprooted, as occurs with palm branches whose leaves have been uprooted. Taz, s.k. 1. That is to say, although they separated, nevertheless the place where they are attached did not harden, and they can still be bound; this is called not uprooted. Levushei Serad on Taz, s.k. 2. This is also compelled by the words of Taz, s.k. 2. What he wrote, that where one can bind them it is valid, means that after binding, the leaves will lie properly upon the spine and be bound together, for otherwise even palm branches can be bound. So too is implied by what was written in the preceding paragraph.
5) Ibid. Even if he did not bind it. Nevertheless, it is certainly a mitzvah to bind it ab initio. Bach; Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahos Beit Yosef, sec. 1.
6) Ibid., Rama's gloss: Nevertheless, it is the choicest manner of performing the mitzvah, etc. For the Shulchan Aruch's statement that a lulav whose leaves separated is valid implies that it is valid specifically after the fact. Biur HaGra. So too wrote Taz, s.k. 1.
7) Ibid., Rama's gloss: Nevertheless, it is the choicest manner of performing the mitzvah, etc. Whenever its leaves do not hang downward, but merely incline somewhat to the sides, there is not even a deficiency in the choicest manner of performing the mitzvah. Taz, s.k. 2. However, Maamar Mordechai, sec. 1, Nehar Shalom, sec. 1, and Bikkurei Yaakov questioned the aforementioned words of Taz and wrote that the statement that it is the choicest manner to take a lulav whose leaves are not separated means that one should not take a lulav whose leaves are separated and incline to the sides, even though they do not hang downward; see there. So too wrote Bigdei Yesha. The Bach likewise wrote that there is certainly greater beauty when its leaves are not separated at all, and this is the choicest manner of performing the mitzvah.
8) Lulav has the numerical value of 36 and 32, meaning the 36 righteous individuals in every generation and corresponding to the 36 tractates. The number 32 alludes to the Torah, which begins with the letter beis and concludes with the letter lamed. The numerical value of lulav is also chaim, life, which alludes to the Torah. Mahariv. In the Derashos Maharil there is an allusion that one who purchases a beautiful esrog together with a beautiful lulav merits life in this world and the World to Come. In Sefer HaChinuch, Parashas Emor, it is written that the esrog resembles the heart, which is the dwelling place of the intellect, alluding that one should serve his Creator with his intellect. The lulav resembles the spine, alluding that one should subjugate his entire body to the service of the Blessed One. The hadas resembles the eyes, alluding that one should not stray after his eyes on the day of the rejoicing of his heart. The aravah resembles the lips, alluding that one should place a restraint upon his mouth, direct his words, and fear Hashem even at a time of joy. Eliyah Rabbah, sec. 1. It appears that this is alluded to in the final letters of esrog, lulav, hadas, and aravah, whose numerical value is sod, secret, indicating that they contain a secret. See the sacred work Yismach Yisrael, discourses on the Torah, Parashas Emor, where I wrote additional allusions regarding this; take them from there.
