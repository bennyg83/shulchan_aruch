And some explain. See Turei Zahav. The Beis Yosef brought Tosafos, Bava Kamma 96a, s.v. 'the tiyomes split.' Leaves are called hotzin in their natural formation, each folded into two, with its front separated and its back joined as if it were one; a chufya is when one leaf separated into two at the back; see there. Tosafos there brought the explanations of the Geonim, Rashi, and the Maggid Mishneh. All speak of those lulavim that end above in two leaves, meaning that at the top of the spine two leaves emerge, each folded and double. According to the Geonim, those two upper leaves must be connected at their backs, while at their fronts they are separated, as two upper leaves. See the Beis Yosef, who wrote that sometimes, opposite their fronts, a red thing is also found between them, joining them as if the two leaves actually appear as one. The back of the leaves is on the back of the spine and the fronts of the leaves are separated like the other leaves; the two upper leaves must be connected at their backs, and see siman 651. Whenever the tiyoma, meaning those two leaves, split from one another at their fronts, even though their backs remain fully stuck together, it is invalid. About this Tosafos wrote that so appears from Baal Halachos Gedolos. The Rosh in Sukkah 32 explained that Baal Halachos Gedolos means that 'the tiyomes split' refers to the back of the leaves, namely the two upper leaves; therefore he speaks of the back, while at their fronts they are separated like other leaves, and there is a red-looking substance between them at the front that joins their backs together. This invalidation is when the majority of the length of their backs split; see the Rosh and Tur. About this Tosafos wrote that in their country, one valid lulav in five hundred was not found where the two leaves were joined at their backs. It is found and common for there to be two leaves at the top of the spine, a common minority, but for their backs to be separated; only one in five hundred would be valid, and it is almost never found. About this they wrote that if from the beginning of their formation they were born with their backs separated, one can say it is valid, as the Rosh concluded. Afterward they brought Rashi's explanation. According to him there is a leniency: as long as the spine above did not split so much that the two leaves at the head of the spine appear divided from each other, it is valid. Rabbeinu Yitzchak's explanation in the name of 'some explain' is somewhat more stringent than Rashi: whenever a little of the spine split so that the two leaves are not adjacent to each other, it is invalid; but as long as they are truly adjacent, even if their backs are not stuck together, it is valid. According to the Geonim, however, whenever the majority of the upper leaves are not actually attached at their backs as one body, they are invalid. About Rabbeinu Yitzchak's explanation in the name of 'some explain,' Tosafos wrote that although there is a small difficulty, that the question should have been asked simply about a split tiyoma of the two upper leaves, for most lulavim do not grow this way, since most have only one leaf at the top of the spine, nevertheless they are comfortable saying that the question was about the minority that is found. This is unlike the explanation of the Geonim, where it is almost never found that the majority of those two leaves are attached at their backs. This is how the matter appears to one who examines the Beis Yosef, and the words of the Turei Zahav are somewhat resolved. The Turei Zahav explains the view of the Geonim this way: initially, two leaves are connected at their backs, just as the other leaves are folded and connected at their backs. So too these two upper neighboring leaves, which are adjacent to each other, with no spine separating them except that at their root a little of the spine separates, must be connected at their backs; when separated, there are four leaves there. Nevertheless, they do not emerge from one root, but one above the other like the other lulav leaves, and it is called tiyoma because their backs are attached. The explanation in the name of 'some say' is that they are not attached at their backs; they are called tiyoma because they emerged from one root in the spine, from one actual place, and if a little of the spine split at their root, it is invalid. About this they also asked that such a thing is not really found, even slightly, from one root; see there. As to practical halachah, the Mechaber and Rema did not bring the view of the Geonim or the 'some say' at all, but only the explanation of the Rif and Rambam, namely the back of the majority of the leaves of the lulav, and the explanation of the middle leaf, where there is only one leaf at the top, one leaf folded in two like the other leaves, in the name of Terumas HaDeshen in the name of Rashi. That means that whenever the majority of the upper leaf separated at the back, one should invalidate it. For the choicest mitzvah, it should not be separated at all; but such a thing is not actually found, that the minority of the tiyoma leaf above is not separated. I was uncertain about a lulav that has two leaves above and is split at the back more than half, and a lulav that has one leaf in such a case split in the majority: which is preferable without a berachah? Likewise, when they are split in the minority, with a berachah, which is preferable? With Hashem's help, in Magen Avraham letter 4 and in the Turei Zahav below I will explain further.
