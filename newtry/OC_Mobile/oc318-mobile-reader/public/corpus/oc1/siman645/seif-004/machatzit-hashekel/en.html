Seif katan 4, "The upper leaf." This refers to the normal central leaf, etc. Bach writes that the same law applies if 2 leaves emerge upward from the spine. Each of these 2 leaves is itself doubled along its back like all lulav leaves, but the 2 leaves are not attached to one another and merely lie adjacent. Each of them then has the law of the middle leaf, and if even 1 of them splits the lulav is invalid. See there. Taz, seif katan 4, comments on Rama's statement that initially the custom is to take a lulav whose upper leaf has not split at all. Taz explains that this stringency concerns the central leaf and that even a small split is avoided initially. Nevertheless, where the upper leaf has only begun to separate at its top while most of it remains joined, the strict law depends upon the dispute among the authorities concerning how much separation constitutes nechlakah hatyomet. Bach's rule that 2 upper leaves can each receive the status of the central leaf means that the relevant criterion is not merely which single leaf happens to stand at the physical center, but which leaf or leaves form the upper termination of the lulav's spine. Therefore, where the spine terminates in 2 parallel leaves, damage to either may affect the lulav as damage to the tiyomet. The later authorities discuss cases in which the 2 upper leaves are not equal in height. Where 1 clearly extends above the other and constitutes the actual termination of the spine, there is stronger reason to treat that leaf alone as the upper central leaf. Where they are approximately equal and both emerge from the top, Bach's stringency applies to each. Accordingly one should inspect the upper leaves carefully before taking the lulav for the mitzvah.
