They were split apart. See Be'er Heitev. See She'eilas Yaavetz, part 1, siman 71. He wrote that not only when it actually broke, meaning most of its width was torn and severed, is it certainly invalid, but even if only a minority was torn and severed, or even if it is still whole and entirely intact but bent so much and folded in the middle that half hangs downward and rests on the other half, it is also invalid. This is like what we invalidate in an animal if the brain was softened so much that it cannot stand, where even if the membrane of the cord and the spine remain intact, it is a tereifah, etc. It is obvious that it is not hadar, for it is worse than its spine drying, and the break itself usually comes from dryness. Therefore one should invalidate it even on the other days, except in pressing circumstances, when invalid species are taken. The remedy the Magen Avraham wrote, to bind it so that it will not fall downward, is ineffective; if you compare it to a tereifah, it should also be compared in this respect, that it has no remedy. See there. In my humble opinion, if it is intact and merely bent downward, which he wrote to invalidate and compared to softened brain, this is not compelling. There, because of damage to the brain inside it, we judge that it will ultimately snap, and it should not be compared to there except perhaps with an esrog that liquefied, about which the Talmud asks; and even there, the Rif and other poskim take the question leniently. With a lulav, although where it broke one should be stringent for the reasons mentioned above, if it merely bent because of the softness of the spine's branch, one should bind it there and it is valid, unless another valid one can easily be found, in which case it is proper to be among those who beautify the mitzvah and perform it in the choicest way.
