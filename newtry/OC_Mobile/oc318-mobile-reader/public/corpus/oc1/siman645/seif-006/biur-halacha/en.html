The majority of the upper leaves. The Bayis Chadash, Turei Zahav, and Gra explained this, and something like this is implied by the Levush: it means the majority of the number of upper leaves that rise above the spine, which is called the head of the lulav. It is proven from the Turei Zahav and Gra that according to this view, the middle leaves have no special status in this matter. The Rema, in the name of the Maggid Mishneh, disagrees with it. The measure of cutting was not mentioned in the Shulchan Aruch. From the Levush it implies that the measure is any amount, and his words derive from the Rosh, who wrote that one must say that 'its top was cut off' speaks of the top of most of the leaves, and the top of each leaf. However, from the Ran, who explains the reason for any amount regarding the middle leaf, because it is similar to the nose of an esrog, where any amount invalidates, it implies that for the other leaves, according to the first view where this reason does not apply, any amount does not invalidate. The Maamar Mordechai also questions him; see there. The Nahar Shalom and Maamar Mordechai explain that the Shulchan Aruch's expression 'the upper leaves' means the two upper middle leaves in which the lulav ends. What it wrote, that most were cut, means that we do not require them to be cut entirely, unlike the view of the Baal HaIttur brought in the Tur, but it is enough that most of them were cut. Still, even the Mechaber agrees that if above there are three leaves in which the lulav ends, the middle leaf among them is primary, and if its top was cut it is invalid, as he wrote in Beis Yosef. This is the case about which the Rema speaks, and they do not disagree at all. The Pri Megadim wanted to explain further that 'most of the leaves' means that if the lulav ends above in three leaves and most of them, meaning two, were cut, it is invalid, and that what the Rema wrote, 'if the upper middle leaf was cut,' refers to a case where the lulav ends in one leaf, and the Mechaber also agrees to this. But one who examines the Darchei Moshe will see that when the Ran and Maggid Mishneh depict the upper middle leaf, they mean a case where there are three leaves in which the lulav ends, and the middle leaf among them is primary; if it is cut, it is invalid. This law is copied here in the name of the Maggid Mishneh and Ran. If so, it is explicitly proven, even according to him, that the Maggid Mishneh and Ran disagree with the first view. According to the first view, it is specifically if two of them were cut; according to them, even the middle one alone invalidates.
Invalid. Know that specifically regarding a lulav they agreed in practice that 'its top was cut off' applies even to the leaves alone. But regarding myrtle and willow, it is not called 'its top was cut off' until the wood itself is cut; so wrote the poskim. I did not find the measure of the cutting stated explicitly, but it is reasonable that its measure is any amount, as the Ran wrote regarding a lulav whose top was cut off.
But if no other is available, one recites a berachah over it. The Magen Avraham wrote about this that it is imprecise, for the Mordechai refers only to myrtle, as below in siman 646, se'if 10, but not to lulav. However, several Acharonim defended the words of the Rema, including Eliyah Rabbah, Bigdei Yesha, Maamar Mordechai, and Shaarei Ephraim. When no other is available, he relied on several poskim who are lenient regarding the middle leaf, holding that 'its top was cut off' means specifically the majority of the leaves, like the Mechaber's first view. And even if we understand that it refers to the middle leaf, there is the view of the Ra'avad, that it is only if it was also cut from the spine, or the view of the Baal HaIttur, that the cut must reach the spine. Added to this are the poskim who are lenient about invalid species in pressing circumstances. Although regarding a berachah several are stringent, here, where in any event there are many views to be lenient, one should not be stringent regarding the berachah.
