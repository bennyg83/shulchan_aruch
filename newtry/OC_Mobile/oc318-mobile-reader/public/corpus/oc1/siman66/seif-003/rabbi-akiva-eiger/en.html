Now. In a responsum I wrote that it may be said that the law accords with Magen Avraham. Tosafot's words may follow Rashi's approach that 'out of fear' means that one fears the other person may kill him, making it a matter of danger to life. Therefore, answering Kaddish and Kedushah cannot be compared to interruption out of fear, and Rabbi Meir prohibits it. According to what we rule in Shulchan Aruch, however, 'out of fear' refers to one's father or teacher, whom one is obligated to fear. All the more so, an interruption for the honor of the Divine Presence is classified as one made out of fear. Accordingly, as a matter of law one may interrupt even in the middle of a section and recite the blessing over thunder. Although this resembles initiating a greeting, it is nevertheless like initiating one out of fear. Magen Avraham precisely indicates this in his wording: 'If one interrupts to initiate a greeting out of respect for flesh and blood'; meaning a distinguished person whom he must fear. Regarding the substance of Magen Avraham's ruling, however, Eliyah Rabbah writes that it is not compelling. See Tevuot Shor in his novellae to Berakhot, who concludes as the primary ruling that one should not recite the blessing.
Seif 3, in the gloss: "Some say that the Amen to which one responds." This refers to interrupting in the middle of a section. Between the sections, however, the Tur in siman 61 implies that one interrupts to answer every Amen. I have likewise seen that Peri Megadim writes this in Eshel Avraham, siman 51, seif katan 3.
