1) [Se'if 1] Between sections, one may inquire, etc. Tosafos at the beginning of the second chapter of Berachos, folio 13a, s.v. "and in the middle," writes that wherever speaking is forbidden, it is forbidden even in Lashon HaKodesh. Magen Avraham at the beginning of the siman; Pri Chadash, paragraph 1; Soles Belulah, paragraph 2; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1. See what we wrote above in siman 51, paragraph 6. Even interrupting with a single word is forbidden; the rule does not apply only to more than twenty-four words. Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, beginning of the siman.
2) There: One may inquire after another's welfare, etc. This applies even in a foreign language. Kesef Mishneh, end of chapter 2 of Hilchos Krias Shema, citing Rabbeinu Yonah; Magen Avraham at the beginning of the siman; Pri Chadash, paragraph 1; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Siddur Beis Oved, laws of Birchos Krias Shema, paragraph 9.
3) There: He may inquire after the welfare of an honored person, etc. Rabbeinu Yehudah HaChasid writes that only a greeting of peace is permitted, nothing more. Every permission to inquire or respond concerns a new encounter in which failure to greet will cause hatred. In a synagogue, where we do not ordinarily exchange greetings, Heaven forbid that one should inquire or respond (not even with words of Torah) whether between the sections, within their blessings, or during Pesukei DeZimrah. So I found in Hagahos Mordechai printed in Krakow. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos on the Tur; Olas Tamid, paragraph 3; Magen Avraham there; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2; Soles Belulah, paragraph 2; Shaliach Tzibbur, folio 97b. See above, siman 51, paragraph 28. Eliyah Rabbah there implies that when one is not engaged in the blessings or Pesukei DeZimrah, greetings may be exchanged in the synagogue. This is also implied by Tashbetz, siman 246: Maharam would greet every person before prayer, even in the synagogue. Machatzis HaShekel, paragraph 1, cites this, as do Soles Belulah there and Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 1. Nevertheless, when possible one should be stringent not to greet anyone in a synagogue. See below, siman 151, paragraph 8, for what we wrote in the name of the Arizal, and above at the end of siman 1. Consider this carefully.
4) There: One may inquire after the welfare of an honored person, etc. Sefer HaChinuch by the Raah, Parashas Va'eschanan, writes that one should not interrupt even between sections for a person whom we have never seen become upset with his fellow over a missing greeting. Magen Avraham there; Eliyah Rabbah there; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1; Siddur Beis Oved, laws of Pesukei DeZimrah, paragraph 7. Nowadays people are not particular about greetings, especially when they recognize that a person is occupied with Krias Shema and prayer. One should therefore make no interruption, either in the middle or between sections, for any person (even his father or rav) and even if he is not praying in the synagogue.
5) There: And he may return a greeting, etc. Rashba implies that one may initially greet a person reciting Krias Shema even though he knows that the person will have to respond. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav there. Nevertheless, one should be stringent, for why cause his fellow to interrupt Krias Shema?
6) There: In the middle, one may inquire after another's welfare, etc. One who wears Rabbeinu Tam tefillin and recites Krias Shema without its blessings should not interrupt needlessly, but may inquire out of honor even in the middle of a section. Responsa Panim Meiros, volume 1, siman 27; Rabbi Akiva Eiger; Pischei Olam, paragraph 4.
7) There: In the middle, one may inquire after another's welfare, etc. Ritva in Yoma 19 writes that to separate a person from a prohibition that cannot later be repaired, one may signal and even interrupt. This is no less than interrupting because of fear or honor, for the honor of Heaven takes precedence. Erekh HaShulchan, addenda to siman 63; Kisei Eliyahu, siman 63, paragraph 3; Kaf HaChaim, siman 14, paragraph 31; Pischei Olam, paragraph 1.
8) There: One whom he fears, such as his father or rav, etc. Chazal state at the beginning of the chapter Hayah Korei that in the middle one may inquire because of fear and respond because of honor, while between sections one may inquire because of honor and return a greeting to any person. The poskim dispute the definitions. Rashi explains "because of honor" as an honored person whom it is proper to greet first, and "because of fear" as one whom he fears may kill him. Rambam, Hilchos Krias Shema, chapter 2, halachos 15-16, similarly defines honor as one's father, rav, or a person greater in wisdom, and fear as a king or violent person. The Rosh, however, defines fear as fear of one's father or rav, since it is written, "Every person shall fear his mother and father," and the Mishnah says, "Fear of your rav should be like fear of Heaven." Tur cites him and writes that the Geonim explain likewise. Rashba agrees, as Beis Yosef records, and in Responsa Rashba, siman 321, he cites Rabbi Yitzchak ibn Ghayas. Abudraham and Agur likewise agree, as Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1, records. Raavad and Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Yesh Nochalin also agree, as Divrei Menachem, Hagahos Beis Yosef, paragraph 1, writes. Maran rules accordingly here in Shulchan Aruch, as do Levush, Pri Chadash, paragraph 1; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1; Chayei Adam, rule 20, paragraph 6; and Siddur Beis Oved, laws of Birchos Krias Shema, paragraph 9. Taz, paragraph 8, and Erekh HaShulchan, paragraph 1, also appear to agree, since they resolve Bach's objections. This is unlike Bach, who writes that the Rosh and Rashba stand alone. The later authorities disagree, however, with Maran's inclusion of someone greater in wisdom among those feared; they classify him under honor. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2; Pri Chadash, Eliyah Rabbah, Shulchan Aruch HaRav, and Chayei Adam there. One should be stringent accordingly. A Torah scholar preeminent in his generation is nevertheless included under fear. Magen Avraham, Eliyah Rabbah, Shulchan Aruch HaRav, and Chayei Adam there. See siman 472, se'if 5, and what we wrote above, paragraph 4.
9) There: Or someone greater than him in wisdom. If the person reciting is greater than the other, he does not interrupt for him any more than for any ordinary person, even though the other is a Torah scholar. Magen Avraham and Shulchan Aruch HaRav there. See what we wrote above, paragraph 4.
10) There: And certainly a king, etc. This applies even to a Jewish king, for it is written, "You shall surely place a king over yourself," teaching that his fear must be upon you. Only concerning prayer does the Mishnah say that one does not respond even if a Jewish leader greets him. During Krias Shema one may even initiate a greeting because of fear, and certainly may return the king's greeting. Bach; Ateres Zekenim, paragraph 1; Olas Tamid, paragraph 2. See Yafeh LaLev, paragraph 1.
