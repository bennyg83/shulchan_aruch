Such as his father or his rebbe. The Mishnah states simply, "out of fear." Rashi explains that this means fear of a person who may kill him. The Rosh and Rashba objected that it is obvious that nothing stands before danger to life, and they answered that it refers to fear of one who is greater than him, as Shulchan Aruch writes here. This is difficult, for the Gemara in the chapter Ein Omdin, page 32b, states explicitly regarding the Amidah that Rav Yosef said: They taught this only regarding Jewish kings, but for non-Jewish kings one interrupts. One could likewise object there that this is obvious. It may be answered that there the concern is not that the king will immediately kill him. On the contrary, an ordinary king does not kill immediately, but first questions him according to law and asks, "Why did you do this?" The person can then explain himself, and the king will certainly be appeased when he sees that he did not act in rebellion against him, but rather for the honor of Hashem. One might therefore have thought that he should not interrupt. The Gemara teaches that he may not rely on this. This situation also contains fear, and the principal novelty is that he has permission to interrupt if he wishes. This also resolves the difficulty concerning the incident there involving the pious man and the governor. The governor challenged him, "Is it not written, 'Take utmost care of your lives'?" What did the pious man answer him? Even were one to say that the pious man relied on the merit of his prayer, what answer did he give to the challenge? According to what I have written, it is resolved. This is why the pious man said, "Wait until I appease you with words." At first glance this was unnecessary, for he could immediately have said, "Had you been standing before a flesh-and-blood king, etc." Rather, through this he hinted at the answer: You will necessarily wait for me and will not kill me immediately, because that is not the way of authority. You will first ask me why I acted this way, and I will then appease you with words. It follows that I did not violate "Take utmost care, etc." Although Rambam mentions "a king or a violent person," and the Rosh nevertheless objects to his ruling, Rambam refers to a king who is comparable to the violent person mentioned afterward. This is evident from the phrase "out of fear," which implies a situation already known to involve danger to life. This is unlike the present case. If, according to Rashi's explanation, "fear" meant that an oppressor or enemy had come to kill him and he must save himself, so that he must interrupt, this would be obvious. They therefore explained that it refers to fear of a distinguished person. In this manner it appears to me that Rashi's explanation can also be reconciled. He did not mean that an enemy had come to kill him, but rather a case like that in Ein Omdin, where a king approached and the person feared that the king might kill him afterward because he did not interrupt and might not accept his explanation. Regarding the Rosh's explanation, I find difficulty from what we find concerning Yaakov. When Yosef came to him, Yaakov did not interrupt Krias Shema, as Rashi writes in his commentary on the Torah. Yet elsewhere we find that Yaakov accorded Yosef honor because of the monarchy, as it is written, "Yisrael strengthened himself," and Rashi explains: From here we learn that honor is accorded to royalty. Why, then, did Yaakov not interrupt Shema for him? It may be answered that he was engaged in the first verse, during which one may not interrupt, as Shulchan Aruch writes here.
