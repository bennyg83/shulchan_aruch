[6] Blessed are You, Hashem, Who sanctifies, etc. This implies that the conclusion contains no mention of kingship, but only “Blessed are You, Hashem,” and not “our God, King of the universe.” Lechem Chamudos, folio 61, likewise expresses surprise at the statement that the blessing concludes with kingship, for where have we ever found kingship in the conclusion of a blessing? Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah challenges Lechem Chamudos on the grounds that we have not seen this, etc. I found, however, that Kesef Mishneh, chapter 2 of Hilchos Berachos, writes concerning Rambam’s ruling that the Rosh Chodesh formula has no conclusion: “It appears from the words of Rabbeinu that the other formulas conclude with Hashem’s Name and kingship. This is the view of most commentators, and it is also implied by the fact that he was uncertain only about Rosh Chodesh, which shows that regarding the others it was obvious to him that they have a conclusion.” His words are astonishing, for I examined all the early and later poskim and found none who mention kingship in the conclusion other than Rabbeinu Yonah. In my humble opinion, however, after examining Rif, Semag, and Kol Bo, who give the formula, “Blessed is He Who gave, etc. Blessed are You, Hashem, Who sanctifies Shabbos,” and likewise certain versions of Rambam, it appears that because they did not mention Hashem’s Name in the opening but only in the conclusion, Kesef Mishneh understood that they hold that Hashem’s Name is not mentioned at all in the opening, but only in the conclusion. He therefore understood that the conclusion likewise includes kingship, since the blessing did not begin with it. Perhaps this is also Rabbeinu Yonah’s view, since he explains the words of Rif. Examine this carefully; it still requires study. In any event, all the other poskim mention Hashem’s Name and kingship in the opening, and Hashem’s Name but not kingship in the conclusion, in accordance with Levush and Shulchan Aruch, and this is the principal ruling. Incidentally, I will explain Kesef Mishneh’s words and remove the great difficulty raised by Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah. Even if the conclusion does not contain both Hashem’s Name and kingship, the doubt concerning whether the Rosh Chodesh formula has a conclusion still remains; see his lengthy discussion. In my humble opinion, two further questions must first be raised against Kesef Mishneh. First, the uncertainty concerning Rosh Chodesh is explicit in Berachos 49a. Second, Rambam himself has no uncertainty, for he rules in accordance with the anonymous view that it has no conclusion. It therefore appears that Kesef Mishneh means as follows. If the conclusion did not contain Hashem’s Name and kingship, then even though the Gemara is uncertain, we would rule that it should be recited because of the doubt. If, however, it contains Hashem’s Name and kingship, the uncertainty is resolved stringently by refraining from reciting Hashem’s Name in vain, as implied by Levush at the end of siman 8. Thus, when he says that the matter was uncertain regarding Rosh Chodesh, he means that because the ruling resulting from the doubt is not to conclude, it necessarily follows that every such conclusion contains Hashem’s Name and kingship. This appears correct; examine it carefully. Maharshal writes in the name of the Rosh that if one does not know this blessing, he returns to the beginning of Bircas HaMazon, but if he does not know the Rosh Chodesh blessing, he does not return. Taz writes that if he knows the opening and conclusion, even if he does not know the remainder of the text properly, he recites it and need not repeat Bircas HaMazon. This demonstrates that whenever one errs in Bircas HaMazon, he returns to the beginning of Bircas HaMazon, except when he errs in the fourth blessing, in which case he returns only to HaTov VeHaMeitiv. Magen Avraham writes likewise. See above, siman 183, seif katan 14.
[7] To Israel, etc. Lechem Chamudos writes: “The Tur has the wording ‘to Israel,’ and the later authorities followed him. The Gemara, Rif, and Rambam, however, have ‘to His people Israel.’” It appears to me that one should indeed say “to His people Israel,” for this is the version I found in Knesses HaGedolah, Semag, Semak, Raavan, Rosh, Agudah, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Tzeidah LaDerech, and Rabbi David Abudraham. I also found that Agur cites this wording in the name of the Tur itself, and it is likewise written in Kitzur Piskei HaRosh, composed by the Tur. Accordingly, there must be a scribal error in the editions of Tur before us, and the word “His people” was omitted, or the Tur relied upon the Shabbos ruling that he wrote immediately beforehand.
[8] And Yom Tov for gladness, etc. Lechem Chamudos and Malbushei Yom Tov write that Shulchan Aruch and Levush omit the words “to His people Israel” here, whereas Rosh and Tur include them. I found support for Shulchan Aruch and Levush in Kol Bo, folio 43, and Rabbi David Abudraham. Nevertheless, Tosafos on page 43, Semag, and Beit Yosef have the wording: “Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe, Who gave Shabbosos for rest and Yamim Tovim to His people Israel as a sign and covenant and for rejoicing,” etc. This is more correct, since “to His people Israel” then applies to both Shabbos and Yom Tov. Baal Halachos Gedolos, folio 13, also has this wording. Lechem Chamudos further comments on the Rosh’s wording, “for rejoicing, for gladness, and for remembrance”: “I have already written that some omit ‘for remembrance.’” His words are difficult, for the poskim who say this write it only regarding Yom Tov alone, not when Shabbos and Yom Tov coincide. One must say that he holds there is no reason to distinguish between Yom Tov alone and Shabbos together with Yom Tov. Bach, however, gives a reason: since Shabbos commemorates Creation and Yom Tov commemorates the Exodus from Egypt, we add “for remembrance,” which refers to both Shabbos and Yom Tov. See there. I also examined all the poskim and found no one who disagrees when Shabbos and Yom Tov coincide. On the contrary, Tur and Kol Bo, who disagree regarding Yom Tov alone, nevertheless write that when Shabbos and Yom Tov coincide one says “for remembrance.” Knesses HaGedolah writes that on Rosh Hashanah the conclusion is “Who sanctifies Israel and the Day of Remembrance.” Magen Avraham, however, writes that one does not conclude, just as on Rosh Chodesh, because some hold that it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah, and therefore one who fasts then need not observe another fast for having fasted on a festival, as stated in siman 597. It appears to me, however, that one who eats on Rosh Hashanah is obligated according to all authorities to eat bread, and for him it is like any other Yom Tov. Moreover, even regarding Rosh Chodesh some hold that one concludes. Since many poskim forbid fasting on Rosh Hashanah, one may certainly rely upon them. It therefore appears that if he began HaTov VeHaMeitiv, he must return to the beginning, as on any other Yom Tov. A sick person who ate on Yom Kippur and forgot Yaaleh VeYavo, however, should say: “Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe, Who gave holy days to Israel, this Day of Atonement,” and should not conclude the blessing, for it is like Rosh Chodesh and failure to mention it does not require repetition.

[Levush] Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe, who gave Rosh Chodesh, etc. The Shulchan Aruch writes that one should say only, 'Blessed is He who gave Rosh Chodesh, etc.' This implies that Hashem's Name and kingship are not mentioned. Shiyarei Knesset HaGedolah is puzzled by the reason and agrees with the Levush; see there. I say that if the Shulchan Aruch intended this, the reason is that Rosh Chodesh is more lenient because one is not obligated to eat bread then. We therefore rely on the Raavad cited by the Tur, who holds that these blessings are recited without Hashem's Name and kingship because they are not fixed obligations. According to Rashi, based on my explanation above of the Rif, Semag, and Kol Bo, the openings of all such blessings lack Hashem's Name and kingship. Nevertheless, I searched and found that the Agudah, Rokeach, Semak, Rabbenu Yerucham, Shibolei HaLeket, Abudarham, Tzeidah LaDerech, Shiltei Gibborim, and Sefer Tanya all write like the Levush. They are sufficiently authoritative to rely upon.
