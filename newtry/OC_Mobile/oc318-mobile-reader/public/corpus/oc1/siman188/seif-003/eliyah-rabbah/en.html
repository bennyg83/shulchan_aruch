[3] One should not mention, etc. Rama writes: “Some say that even when saying Yaaleh VeYavo, one should not conclude ‘King, gracious and merciful,’ but should omit the word ‘King.’ This is a sound view, though I have not seen the custom followed.” Levush apparently omitted this because he left the Jewish custom in place, for a Jewish custom has the force of Torah. This is unlike some people whom I have recently seen changing our custom. Taz and Magen Avraham also write that the word should be recited because it is an independent phrase. It further appears to me that there is another reason why “King” is not said in Boneh Yerushalayim: it is a blessing adjoining another blessing. Since “King” is nevertheless mentioned in the Amidah, which is likewise composed of adjoining blessings, they did not wish to alter the wording in Bircas HaMazon, lest people err and omit it in the Amidah as well, thinking that it is omitted because the blessing adjoins another blessing. Tosafos on page 49 clearly does not accept the reason that earthly kingship should not be equated with heavenly kingship, and the same is evident from the Rashba cited by Beit Yosef. Rashba asks that “King” is mentioned many times in adjoining blessings, which shows that these references are not considered a formal mention of kingship. In my humble opinion, one may answer that Boneh Yerushalayim is different. Since heavenly kingship must be mentioned there after earthly kingship has been mentioned, people might mistakenly think that this constitutes a complete mention of kingship and would then mention kingship in other adjoining blessings as well. Yaaleh VeYavo, however, is infrequent, so they will not err. Moreover, they know it is not considered a formal mention of kingship because it is also recited in an adjoining blessing of the Amidah.

[5] Levush: In the middle, etc. Magen Avraham writes that if one said it in another blessing, the Gemara implies that even after the fact he has not fulfilled his obligation, since the halachah follows the Sages and not the first Tanna. This requires study, for Rif and Rosh in the chapter Sheloshah SheAchlu cite only the words of the first Tanna, as does Rambam in chapter 2 of Hilchos Berachos. Moreover, the first Tanna represents an intermediate view, for according to Rabbi Eliezer one may say it in another blessing even initially. On this basis, it appears to me that one can resolve the apparent contradiction between the Rosh at the end of chapter 2 of Niddah and his words in chapter 7 of Bava Metzia. See there and examine carefully. I later found that Baal Halachos Gedolos, in the chapter Sheloshah SheAchlu, explicitly rules in accordance with the Sages.
