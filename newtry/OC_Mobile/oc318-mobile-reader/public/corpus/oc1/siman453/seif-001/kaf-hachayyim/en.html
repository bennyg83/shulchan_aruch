1) [Se'if 1] These are the substances with which one fulfills the obligation, etc. This refers to the obligation of matzah on the first night, when one is required to eat matzah, as it is written, 'In the evening you shall eat matzos.' Rashi, Pesachim 35a. See below, siman 475, se'if 7, and our words there, with the help of Heaven.
2) There. With wheat, barley, etc. The verse states, 'You shall not eat chametz with it; for seven days you shall eat matzos with it.' One fulfills the obligation of matzah with substances that can become chametz. This excludes rice and millet, which do not become chametz but instead spoil. The Gemara, there. Rashi explains that rice and millet do not produce chametz; if one attempts to ferment them, they spoil. Levush writes likewise. See below, subsection 7.
3) There. With wheat, barley, spelt, etc. Spelt is a type of wheat. Oats, whose ear resembles the tail of a fox according to Rashi on the Mishnah there, and rye are types of barley. The Gemara, there.
4) There. With wheat, barley, etc. Wheat is listed first because it precedes barley in the verse, 'A land of wheat and barley,' and so forth. Barley is listed before spelt, although spelt is a type of wheat, because barley is one of the seven species and therefore precedes spelt, as stated above in siman 168, se'if 4. Spelt precedes oats and rye because spelt is a type of wheat. In the Mishnah there, however, the version places rye before oats. We likewise wrote above in siman 168, subsection 19, that rye precedes oats. See there. See the following subsection.
5) There, gloss. The custom is initially to use wheat. Wheat is the choicest grain and takes precedence for the mitzvah. Levush. If one has no wheat, Chok Yaakov, subsection 2, writes that according to all opinions one should choose the species one prefers, in order to fulfill the mitzvah of eating matzah with greater appetite. See there. Chemed Moshe, subsection 1, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2, write likewise. If all the species are equally preferred, one should follow the order stated in the preceding subsection.
6) There. But not with rice. This is what is called rice or millet. The grain called buckwheat is included as well. Responsa Maharam of Lublin, siman 78; Chok Yaakov, subsection 3; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2. I have heard that what is called Turkish wheat in these regions, namely the cities of Ashkenaz, is not wheat at all but is considered a type of kitniyos. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1; Eliyah Rabbah, there; Eshel Avraham, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 3.
7) There. They also do not become chametz. Even if one kneads rice flour or a similar flour with boiling water and covers it with cloths until it swells like dough that became chametz, it remains permitted for consumption. It is not chametz but spoilage. Rambam, chapter 5, halachah 1; Kol Bo, siman 48; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1. This also follows from Tur and the Shulchan Aruch, which write that these substances do not become chametz, implying that they cannot become chametz under any circumstances. It likewise follows from Rashi and Levush cited above in subsection 2, and from Rabbeinu Yerucham, path 5, section 3, who writes that one may prepare every type of cooked food from them. See there. Raavad's objection there to Rambam, that this matter is not clear and not everyone agrees, refers to halachah 2, concerning dough kneaded with fruit juice, as Maggid Mishneh explains there while reproducing the objection in halachah 2. Yad Aharon in Hagahos HaTur writes likewise. This also appears from Maran in Kesef Mishneh, who does not address Raavad's objection because he relies upon Maggid Mishneh. It is also true that not all authorities agree regarding dough kneaded with fruit juice, as stated in Tur and Beis Yosef, siman 462. See there. See Chok Yaakov, subsection 4.
8) There. One may prepare a cooked dish from them. A person who fears Heaven should not eat rice, since its prohibition has become widespread in many communities. Rabbi Mahari Zayin in the manuscript Responsa Sha'arei Yeshuah, sha'ar 6, siman 4. Many upright people have adopted this practice. Birkei Yosef, subsection 1. See below, subsection 10.
9) There, gloss. Some prohibit them. Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 5, writes in the name of Sefer Mitzvos Katan that some customarily refrain from eating kitniyos such as beans and lentils on Pesach. Rabbi Shmuel of Evreux did likewise. Rabbi Yechiel and other great authorities, however, treated them as permitted. It is very difficult to permit them once others have adopted a prohibitive custom. The custom was apparently not adopted because of leavening, for people could not have erred about this; even schoolchildren know that the Gemara explicitly states that only the five grain species can become chametz. Rather, they were prohibited because grain and kitniyos are both cooked as porridge, so one might be confused with the other. In some places bread is also made from kitniyos, creating a risk of confusion with grain porridge. Vegetables, however, do not resemble grain at all and will not be confused with it. Grain is also sometimes mixed into kitniyos and cannot be perfectly sorted out. Mustard should similarly be prohibited on Pesach because it is gathered into a pile. Although it was permitted in earlier generations, in these generations one should be stringent, because people are not as knowledgeable about prohibitions and permissions as the earlier generations, in accordance with the explanation of Rav Saadiah Gaon. These are his words. Mordechai, chapter Kol Sha'ah, likewise writes all this in the name of Sefer Mitzvos Katan. Beis Yosef cites it and writes that no one takes these concerns into account except Ashkenazim. Darkei Moshe, subsection 2, likewise writes, 'We Ashkenazim have customarily been stringent.' Rema similarly writes here in the gloss to the Shulchan Aruch that the custom in Ashkenaz is to be stringent and must not be changed. Levush and Bach write likewise. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 3, writes that in these regions the custom is not to eat during Pesach cooked dishes made from rice, millet, beans, lentils, sesame, or other types of kitniyos, even on the last day of Yom Tov. Chayei Adam, rule 127, subsection 1, likewise writes that dispersed Ashkenazic communities everywhere, including the countries of Poland whose residents are also regarded as Ashkenazim, adopted the prohibition. They prohibit even cooking whole kitniyos, buckwheat, rice, or millet, because of the possibility of error. See there. See also the gloss below in siman 464, which states that the custom in Ashkenaz is not to eat mustard at all during Pesach. Levush, Bach, and the other later authorities write likewise.
