With wheat. I have heard that what is called in these countries Turkish wheat is not included in wheat at all, but is considered among the types of kitniyos.
And the practice is initially to take wheat. At first glance it would seem that if one has no wheat he should take them according to the order in which they are taught in the Mishnah, and similarly the Tur wrote below in siman 473 regarding maror. But in truth there is no proof from there at all, for the tanna lists them according to the order of the verse and what comes earlier in the verse. There is also some proof to this from the language of the Maharil, who wrote: The Maharil expounded that the essence of the mitzvah is to take wheat, for no tanna ever uses any wording other than wheat for Pesach. He did not say that it is because the tanna listed wheat first. Rather, it is certainly that the tanna merely listed the species in the order in which they are written in the verse. See above in the Tur, siman 208, that spelt is included in the species of wheat, and in siman 211, that whatever comes earlier in the verse takes precedence for a blessing. Nevertheless, it appears to me that here, according to all opinions, the species that is most beloved to him takes precedence, so that he may better fulfill the mitzvah of eating matzah with appetite. Perhaps for this reason as well the tanna everywhere says wheat for Pesach, because presumably it is beloved to most people.
With rice and millet. This is what is called rice or millet, and likewise buckwheat, which is called heiden, as in the responsa of Maharam Lublin, siman 78.
They do not come to leavening. The Rambam wrote in chapter 5 of Hilchos Chametz, halacha 1: There is no prohibition of chametz on Pesach even if one kneaded rice flour and it swelled like dough that became chametz; it is permitted to eat. In the Venice edition of the Rambam, the gloss of the Raavad is recorded there: There is no prohibition. The Raavad says: This is not a simple matter, and not everyone agrees with him. Granted, it does not become complete chametz and one is not liable to kareis for its leavening, but it is nevertheless chametz nuksheh and prohibited. This requires study, for the words of the Raavad are explicitly against the conclusion of the Gemara and all the poskim. See Pesachim 114. From the Maggid Mishneh there it appears clear that he equated the Raavad's view with what the Rambam wrote there in halacha 2 regarding fruit juice. But in truth, according to how the Raavad's glosses are recorded there, and according to the implication of the wording, it seems that he is referring to types of kitniyos, and this requires study.
And there are those who prohibit. The reason is explained in the name of the poskim: they prohibited it because grain is made as cooked dishes and kitniyos are made as cooked dishes, so there is a decree lest one confuse one with the other. Also, sometimes they make bread from them, and one may come to confuse it with porridge. Also, sometimes grain is mixed into them and it is impossible to clarify it well. In Terumas HaDeshen, siman 113, he wrote that they prohibited it because they are things gathered into piles, and one may come to confuse them with grain. According to this, rice should have been permitted, because it is not something gathered into piles and is not comparable to kitniyos in this regard, as is stated in the Gemara in Nedarim 55 regarding one who vows from dagan. See there. But from the implication of the poskim, one should not distinguish between rice and types of kitniyos. See the Bach, who writes that reason would give greater basis to be stringent with rice according to Rabbi Yochanan ben Nuri, as stated in the chapter Arvei Pesachim. Yet no one is concerned for that. The agreement of the poskim is that all of this is merely a stringency; however, where the prohibition is practiced, it is practiced, and one should not change. So too wrote the Taz.
If they fell into the cooked dish. From the language of the Rav it implies that even if there is not sixty times against them it is still permitted, even to eat, and so is the implication of the later authorities. However, in Terumas HaDeshen, at the end of siman 113, it implies that only a minimal admixture should not be prohibited, but if there is less than sixty it should be prohibited to eat. The primary view, it appears to me, is like the later authorities, since in any case it is only a stringency and a distancing measure, as written in seif katan 5. Nevertheless, at least a majority is required, for otherwise it is not called a mixture at all, since it is as though one is eating the dish from the kitniyos themselves.
And they do not prohibit if they fell into the cooked dish. Meaning, even though they first soak the seeds before making oil from them, so that it is likely that they became chametz, nevertheless it is permitted. So wrote Terumas HaDeshen there. This is the intent of the Rav here, who repeated the matter. See the Magen Avraham, who wrote in the name of the Darchei Moshe that it is permitted to light with these oils even if they were crushed in a mortar in which barley is soaked, and they do not prohibit after the fact if they fell into the cooked dish, since they were already nullified before Pesach. It is implied from Maharil that it is permitted to eat olive oil. See above, siman 447, seif 5 in the gloss, seif katan 27, where I wrote how to conduct oneself on Pesach with candlesticks used all year. See there.
And likewise it is permitted to keep types of kitniyos in the house. This is somewhat difficult, for what novelty does this teach? Even the five species of grain may be kept in the house. One cannot say that it comes to teach that even if they were soaked and immersed in water it is still permitted to keep them, for this too is already heard from the fact that he permitted them even for benefit and for lighting from the oils, even though they were first soaked, as written in seif katan 7. Also, the main novelty is missing from the text. Perhaps it was necessary to teach that it is permitted to keep them in the house and we do not fear that he may err and come to a stumbling block by cooking and eating them, since they are not prohibited by the law itself. With the five species of grain, where we do not fear that he will cook them, this is because they are complete chametz and people separate themselves from them. Also, it is not the normal way to cook and eat them in that manner, except for kinds of groats called gropen, and these in any case are forbidden to keep, as the Magen Avraham wrote, because they moisten them with water before crushing. From what I heard, immediately after they are moistened with water they crush them, and the bran and flour dust scatter from them; thus it is complete chametz, and if Pesach passed over it, they are forbidden. As for what my brother-in-law the Rav wrote in Soles Belulah in the name of what he heard, that his grandfather the gaon permitted selling them to a non-Jew, I already discussed this matter with him, and he said that he too investigated and concluded that it is complete chametz, and that the listener heard and erred.
And anise and coriander are not types of kitniyos, and it is permitted to eat them on Pesach. The Taz wrote that nevertheless they require checking to ensure that none of the five species is among them. So is the practice when they put them into the beverage called licorice. It is proper that each head of household himself check and not rely on the checking of women and minors. See my work Minchas Yaakov, klal 46, seif katan 32. Also with cumin, called kimel, the practice is to permit it, though some are somewhat stringent regarding these species. See the responsa of Maharil, siman 25, who wrote two opinions regarding cumin. The Taz wrote to distinguish between one kind of cumin and another, as follows: caraway cumin is forbidden; field cumin is permitted. In my humble opinion, one should not be more stringent, for kitniyos itself is only a mere stringency, and let us not add to it. See below, siman 464, regarding mustard.
