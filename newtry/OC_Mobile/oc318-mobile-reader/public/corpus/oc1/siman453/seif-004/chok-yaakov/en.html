Matzah for the mitzvah, it is good to guard them. This implies that specifically matzah for the mitzvah requires guarding in order to eat on the first nights, whereas other matzos require no guarding at all, and it is permitted to take flour from the market even not in pressing circumstances. See above at the beginning of siman 567. Even guarding from kneading and baking is not required, as explained in the Gemara and poskim, that one may fill his stomach from their doughs, provided that he eats an olive's volume of matzah. According to Rabbeinu Chananel, and this is the agreement of the poskim, this refers specifically to dough kneaded before a Jew with water that rested overnight, and the Jew saw that no leaven was mixed into it, but it was not guarded for the sake of matzah. In such a case, even if the baking was also done by a non-Jew while a Jew stood over him, it is also permitted, only it is not guarded matzah with which to discharge the obligation of the first night, as explained below in siman 460. See Beit Yosef, siman 454. The reason he used the term their doughs and did not say matzah, which would imply that it was baked, is to teach the novelty of the latter clause: even though the baking was done by a Jew, nevertheless guarding for the baking alone is not called guarding, and one must eat an olive's volume of matzah for the mitzvah at the end. Alternatively, he used the term doughs because matzah is prohibited because of the decree against their bread, whereas dough is permitted, as the Pri Chadash wrote in Yoreh Deah at the beginning of siman 112, where he challenged the Ran there. See Tosafot at the beginning of Chullin regarding the chametz of transgressors. But certainly, even the baking of other matzos does not require guarding, and likewise the kneading. This may also be proven from the sugya at the beginning of Chullin regarding the matzos of a Kusi. See there. Only a Jew must be present at the kneading and baking so that chametz is not mixed into it. All this is simple and clear, unlike the Olas Shabbos, who was not precise. However, all this is according to the law. In practice, Jews are holy, and they have the custom to make guarding for all matzos from the time of grinding, or at least from the time of kneading. Therefore the poskim wrote that all our matzos have the presumption of being guarded, as explained below in siman 477.
Flour from the market. This implies specifically flour, but clean semolina is forbidden to take, for it is complete chametz, as written in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 119, because they soak it and are not careful with their soaking. This is cited in Beit Yosef, siman 442. However, the language of the Kol Bo, page 51b, says that one may take semolinas from the market. More difficult at first glance is how they ruled simply that in pressing circumstances one may take flour from the market because we do not presume a prohibition, when it is explicit in the tenth chapter of Machshirin, mishnah 2, and in the Rambam, chapter 7 of Tum'as Ochlin, that all flour and semolinas from the market are presumed to have been soaked. One must say that this is the meaning: we do not presume that they soaked it in a way that made it chametz, for according to the law of the Gemara it is permitted to soak, only the Geonim decreed and prohibited it, and in pressing circumstances they did not decree. Similarly wrote the Taz. However, in the responsa of the Tzemach Tzedek, siman 32, he is stringent in this matter. He wrote that when it is certainly known that some of them always wash the wheat, which is worse than soaking, then even if most sellers do not wash, it is forbidden because a fixed source is treated as half and half, and even keeping it is forbidden. From bakers one should not buy at all, for they certainly wash. He concluded: However, my practice is not to prohibit keeping the flour, and also not to prohibit eating it on Pesach in pressing circumstances, because it is not certainly known that some flour sellers always wash, so the fixed source is not identifiable in its place. But from bakers one should not buy, for they certainly wash, though grain flour may be bought from them. So too the Magen Avraham wrote in the name of one great authority, that in our country, where they regularly wash the wheat and leave it in water, it is forbidden to take flour from the market, for it is exactly like grain that sank in a river. So ruled all the sages of Prague, and once a certain decisor erred and ruled to take flour from the market, and he decreed a fast upon himself for his error. In my humble opinion, it appears that one should distinguish: specifically from those millers in the city or those who sell flour on a fixed basis one should not buy, and certainly not from bakers, who have clean semolina. But it is permitted to buy from villagers, who do not have clean semolina, as is explicit in the chapter Kol Sha'ah, Pesachim 40a: What was the reason at first? Because they had not soaked. And if they did soak, we do not presume that they soaked in a way that became chametz. Also, the reason of the Tzemach Tzedek based on a fixed source does not apply to them, for they are all mobile each day; these go and others come, and all have separated from their fixed place. The matter is also visible to the eye, for their semolina is not so clean. I heard that in the days of my grandfather, the gaon Maharshal of blessed memory, there was once a shortage of flour for Pesach and he permitted buying from the market. Possibly this too was in the manner I wrote. One should not be so stringent against the implication of the early and later poskim, especially for matzos that are not for the mitzvah. See above, seif katan 15. Maharil wrote that the flour made by the peddlers and called kraft meal is complete chametz.
From the market. The Taz wrote: In a place where they grind wheat in mills in which malt is ground, it is forbidden even in pressing circumstances, because it is complete chametz. In my humble opinion, in pressing circumstances even such a case should be permitted, for even if some of the malt remained and became mixed in, it certainly became mixed and nullified before Pesach. Certainly where it is not definitely known that malt had been ground beforehand, we do not presume a prohibition in pressing circumstances, as all the poskim wrote. Nevertheless, the proper practice in pressing circumstances is to bake everything before Pesach, as I wrote in seif katan 13. The Kol Bo, the Taniya, and Shibbolei HaLeket wrote: One need not be concerned lest crumbs of chametz became mixed into the flour, since the practice is to sift the flour with a sieve, and if there is chametz, the sieve catches it. From this one may learn that if crumbs of chametz became mixed into flour, there is a remedy by sifting it. However, one cannot bring proof from pressing circumstances and a mere doubt to a case where it certainly became mixed in. Nevertheless, at least it should be permitted for benefit, to keep it until after Pesach.
