Matzas mitzvah. This implies that specifically matzas mitzvah needs guarding in order to eat on the first nights; which is not so for other matzos, which need no guarding at all, and it is permitted to take flour from the market even without pressing need; and even guarding of the kneading and baking we do not require, as is explained in the Gemara and in the poskim. However, all this is according to the strict law; but Israel are holy, and they practiced to make a guarding for all the matzos from the time of milling, and at least from the time of kneading.
Water. If the non-Jew from whom he took the wheat said that he washed them, if he is believed regarding him, they are forbidden; see Magen Avraham siman 467 s.k. 3, and see there in Chok Yaakov.
The market. For we do not presume a prohibition, lest it touched water; and it appears that even in a place where they practice soaking the wheat, we say perhaps they did not soak it in a way that it became chametz (Taz). However, in Tzemach Tzedek siman 32 he was stringent in this matter, and wrote that when it is known clearly that some of them always wash the wheat, which is worse than soaking, even though most sellers do not wash, it is forbidden because of kevu'a, which is like half and half; see Tzemach Tzedek there. (And if the seller brings it to a Jew's house, it is permitted, for whatever separates [from a mixture is presumed from the majority]; and see Peri Chadash.) And so wrote the Magen Avraham in the name of a certain great authority, that in our country, where they are accustomed to wash the wheat and leave it soaking in water, it is forbidden to take flour from the market, for it is literally like grain that was submerged, as is written in siman 467 se'if 2; and so ruled all the sages of Prague; see there. And Chok Yaakov wrote that it appears one should distinguish: specifically from those millers in the city, or those who sell flour regularly, one should not buy, and all the more so one should not buy from the bakers; but it is permitted to buy from villagers who bring flour every day from the villages; see there the reason. And I heard that also in the days of my maternal grandfather, the Gaon Maharash of blessed memory, there was once a pressing need for Pesach flour, and he permitted buying from the market; and it is possible that it was also in the manner I wrote; and one should not be stringent, especially regarding matzah that is not of the mitzvah, as I wrote in s.k. 8. And the Taz wrote that in a place where they grind the wheat in mills that grind maltzin (malt), even under pressing circumstances it is forbidden, for it is absolute chametz. And Chok Yaakov wrote upon him: in my humble opinion, under pressing circumstances even in such a case there is room to permit, for even if some remnant of the maltzin remained and became mixed, it certainly became mixed and was nullified before Pesach. Nevertheless the correct practice under pressing circumstances is to bake everything before Pesach. And Chok Yaakov brings a proof from when crumbs of bread fell into flour, that sifting helps for it; see there.
