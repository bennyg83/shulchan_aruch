See Taz: it is implied that he holds that what the Rav wrote, that there should be sixty, also refers to the eating of mice; and one must mill at least before Pesach, for if not so, dry with dry again awakens on Pesach, as in siman 447 se'if 4; and not like Magen Avraham letters 5-6, who brought one reason that it is hard and a little saliva does not leaven; and God willing there it will be explained. And what he wrote, not like the Hagahos Semak, who holds that flour with flour is called dry with dry, and only after baking does it become wet with wet, even for a leniency; and therefore regarding grain that has sprouted, which is chametz, there must be sixty against it, and the milling and baking before Pesach, so that it become wet with wet, and then it does not again awaken on Pesach; for to bake on Pesach is forbidden, since it is called dry and awakens, as in siman 444 se'if 4 in the gloss. But we rule like the Semag, that flour with flour is called wet with wet; and whenever one milled before Pesach, it is permitted to bake during Pesach, for it was already nullified as wet with wet at the milling, and it does not again awaken. And so wrote Terumat HaDeshen; and a proof from Tosafos Pesachim 44a s.v. "they challenged him," who brought the words of Terumat HaDeshen briefly: namely, wheat flour with barley is wet with wet; and therefore in that which is not its species, sixty; and even so we say "I say," and he challenges him, etc. And if it were dry, he would not challenge, for dry with dry even in that which is not its species is nullified in a majority; see Shach Yoreh De'ah 109 letter 10. And what he wrote "from itself it is implied," meaning: if so, that flour with flour is wet, then from itself, regarding its own species at least rabbinically one needs sixty for wet with its own species as a stringency; and the same law applies for a leniency, that whatever was milled before Pesach does not awaken during Pesach. (According to this, dry in that which is not its species depends on the dispute: according to the Semak, nullified; and according to the Semag, rabbinic; and we rule like the Semag.) And what he wrote from siman 466, and he distinguished: if it was mixed before milling, then the milling makes them one body and it is called wet; which is not so if it was mixed after the milling, for then it is not called wet, only dry; and if so, on Pesach it is forbidden to bake, for dry awakens on Pesach. And the Mechaber in siman 466 is stringent even not to bake before Pesach, as a decree on account of during Pesach. But from Tosafos Pesachim 44a s.v. "they challenged him" it is proven that even if flour mixed with flour after the milling, it is called wet; and so is the law. And that of siman 467 the Magen Avraham explained there in letter 9, that crumbling is called dry. And what he wrote on the Levush, that there it is implied that he holds like the Semak that flour is called dry, and sprouted [grain] one must sort so that there not be from the sprouted one-sixtieth, and bake before Pesach, for if not so it awakens. However, in the gloss there he answers his words, and they need explanation. And behold it is implied that he holds that flour is called dry; and so it is somewhat implied in siman 447 se'if 2, where he wrote "wet with wet that was blended well," or even flour with flour that was mixed very well; but regarding what is visible and recognizable, bread into wine, perhaps crumbs remained and give taste on Pesach; see there, where he changed somewhat from the language of the Rav there. And see what I wrote in letter 11; and what he wrote in Prey there: if so, he holds that flour is called dry; only that whenever it is not recognizable at all and no new taste is added now, it is permitted; therefore whenever there is sixty before Pesach, baking is permitted on Pesach, for in baking, even though it becomes wet, there is no new taste, since there was already sixty beforehand; which is not so regarding a visible crumb in wine with a minute amount during Pesach, for when it is recognizable it is significant, and like heating a chicken with wheat on Pesach. And the explanation of his words inside is thus: sprouted [grain] needs sorting, for without sorting it is possible that there is not sixty, only a majority; and if so, even though dry, one in two is nullified, and it is permitted afterward to cook them together, for it is completely permitted, and as is written in Yoreh De'ah regarding 109 according to the Rosh, and the Beis Yosef there permits in such a case. And regarding chametz that was mixed into it in a time of permit, one can say that even if there is not sixty it is permitted; regarding this he said that it is not so: for certainly if there is not sixty against the sprouted, even though one may keep it on Pesach or eat of the flour, for even dry does not awaken when it is not recognizable at all, nevertheless when he bakes on Pesach, a new taste is added by the baking, since there is not sixty; and even though it is its own species and the taste is not sensed, nevertheless it again awakens in such a case, and it is forbidden even after the fact. Therefore one must sort, and also sort well; for if not so, he would need to bake before Pesach as stated; and even if there is not sixty, since it was permitted in a time of permit, one can say that even without sixty it is permitted, for it was already baked in a time of permit; which is not so if he sorts so that there not be [a lack of] sixty: then he need not bake before Pesach, for it does not awaken even when dry in such a case, since it is not recognizable at all, and no new taste is added by baking, since there is already sixty, and it is impossible at all for it to come to giving taste even in that which is not its species; and therefore it does not again awaken. And that is what he wrote: that nullification in a majority is not better than nullification in sixty, for this again awakens; and understand this. In sum: it is implied from the Levush there that flour is called dry, and whenever there is sixty it is permitted to bake even during Pesach. And the Taz did not have this gloss before him, and therefore he wrote that according to the Levush one must bake before Pesach.
And see Olas Shabbos in siman 447 se'if 4, for in my view his intent is that wet with wet that was mixed with sixty before Pesach is permitted to heat in a kli rishon during Pesach; and so it is implied in Terumat HaDeshen 114: since the name of a mixture was upon it before Pesach, even though a new taste is given on Pesach, it does not again awaken; and that is flour with flour, which is called wet, namely that it was mixed and blended well; and nevertheless now, when it is thus, there is no taste of one in the other. And if so, when he bakes on Pesach, a new taste is given; even so it does not awaken, since the name of a mixture was upon it before Pesach; which is not so regarding dry, which is not included well, and each stands on its own: even though there is sixty, it is forbidden to bake them on Pesach, since a new taste now on Pesach awakens and resurfaces. And correspondingly, without cooking it is permitted to eat them thus on Pesach; and it is possible that a majority suffices, as one who looks in his words will see; which is not so regarding wet with wet. Why then is beer in wine permitted to heat on Pesach? For it does not awaken any new taste at all, since in the cold it gives taste as in heating. Even flour with flour, where a new taste is given by baking: even so, since it was mixed well before Pesach, baking on Pesach is permitted. And this will be the explanation of what is written in Terumat HaDeshen 114: flour is called wet, and baking on Pesach is permitted; which is not so if it is called dry, for the baking must be before Pesach, for a new taste is given; which is not so regarding chametz bread in matzah, one in two: it is permitted to eat cold on Pesach. See what I wrote in siman 447 in Magen Avraham letter 11.
Again I saw in Responsa Maharil siman 97, and his words need explanation. And the content of his intent: barley beer that was mixed into wine with sixty before Pesach is forbidden on Pesach, for it again awakens; and a proof from germir, regarding which the Semak wrote that what was milled before Pesach is forbidden on Pesach when they bake on Pesach. And many wondered at the Semak, that it does not again awaken; and I reconcile it like dry in that which is not its species according to Rabbeinu Baruch, with sixty: since if he cooks it gives taste, we require nullification in dry as in its end when it will be cooked; and likewise chametz needs nullification as in its end when it will be cooked, and behold it is in its substance; and likewise beer; end quote. You see that Maharil holds that wet also again awakens (and we do not rule so); and if so, this requires further study: what is his force against the Semak? And at first glance the deed contradicts: for if so, what does baking before Pesach help regarding germir, for even if you say it is truly wet, and even for a leniency, wet also again awakens. However, one can say that this is the meaning: certainly whenever he bakes before Pesach and it remains thus, it does not again awaken, for no new taste is renewed on Pesach; which is not so when he bakes on Pesach, for a new taste is renewed on Pesach and it again awakens. And Maharil brings a proof that beer in wine, even cold, gives taste on Pesach, and since it is in its substance; which is not so regarding wheat in a chicken, which is not in its substance, and it is permitted to heat. And regarding this he wrote as Rabbeinu Baruch wrote in Sefer HaTerumah regarding dry in that which is not its species, etc.; the same applies to chametz: and if he did not bake before Pesach, it is forbidden to keep it on Pesach, for it needs nullification as when it will be cooked; and even though if so, cooking is rabbinic; and see Shach Yoreh De'ah 119 regarding chametz, that they were stringent like a Torah prohibition, and they fit well, as Olas Shabbos wrote. And if so, even though we do not rule like Maharil regarding wet, regarding dry we do rule, as Olas Shabbos wrote. And according to this there is no proof from the Semak whether flour is called dry, as stated. And see what I wrote here in se'if 3: one who is careful like the Semak to bake before Pesach, and on Pesach they cook the matzos: there is no reason; and Magen Avraham in siman 458 letter 1 wrote that these are wondrous words; and according to what I wrote they have a place from Maharil, as I have written. And the general rule of the matter: even if he bakes before Pesach, what of it? If it is called wet, [that is] for a stringency that there need be sixty, but not for a leniency. And therefore, either way: if flour is wet, one need not bake before Pesach, for the name of a mixture is upon it, as is written in siman 447 se'if 4; and if they hold that it is dry, and whenever he bakes on Pesach a new taste is given and it awakens, then even if he bakes before Pesach let us say that the law of dry applies: for what of it that it became one lump? Nevertheless it is not comparable to wine and beer; and according to this there is no name of a mixture upon it. And granted that it is not forbidden to eat cold on Pesach according to the view of Olas Shabbos, nevertheless in cooking the taste is renewed, and it is not comparable to a beverage; and therefore he wrote that there is no reason for their words. And in truth, according to the one who permits even during Pesach, all the more so cooking; and Magen Avraham according to his view in siman 447 letter 11; and God willing in Magen Avraham there, and here letter 6, it will be explained further.
