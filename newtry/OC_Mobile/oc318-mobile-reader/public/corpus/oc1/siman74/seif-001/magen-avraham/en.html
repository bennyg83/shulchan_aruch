Beit Yosef writes in siman 206 in the name of the Rosh, chapter HaRoeh: The Jerusalem Talmud states that Rabbi Yirmeyah asked, 'If one was sitting inside a wooden cabinet, may he treat it like a garment, extend his head outside through its window, and recite a blessing?' The uncertainty is whether it is prohibited or permitted when one's heart sees the ervah. Above we ruled that it is prohibited. These are the Rosh's words. I am astonished by how this great luminary explained the passage. If that were the uncertainty, why did the Jerusalem Talmud ask about a cabinet, an uncommon case? It should have asked about one wearing a tallit or robe. It is clear that the passage in Sukkah 10b escaped his attention. There it is stated: 'One who was sleeping in a canopy extends his head outside the canopy and recites; if it was ten handbreadths high, it is prohibited.' Rashi explains that a tent is not considered a separation, just as when one stands in a house and extends his head through the window, his head is assigned the status of the majority of his body. If the canopy is not ten handbreadths high, it is like extending one's head outside a robe. It is therefore clear that the Jerusalem Talmud's uncertainty is whether a wooden cabinet is considered a tent or is instead considered a vessel and thus like a robe or tallit. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes in Netiv 13, part 2: 'If one was sitting inside a wooden cabinet and made it like a garment, he may extend his head through the window and recite a blessing; but if his heart sees the ervah, it is prohibited. So it is stated in the Jerusalem Talmud.' He thus resolves the uncertainty leniently. It is difficult, however, why the halakhic authorities omit the passage in Sukkah. It therefore appears that they maintain that both the Jerusalem Talmud and the Gemara in Sukkah hold that it is permitted when one's heart sees the ervah. According to us, however, who hold that it is prohibited, either way it remains prohibited: Even if the tent is considered a separation, his heart still sees the ervah. If he also extends his heart through the window, then his head and the majority of his body are outside, and it is as though his entire body were outside. It is possible that Rabbeinu Yerucham establishes the case as one in which his heart is covered by a garment, so his heart does not see the ervah. To create a separation between his head and the ervah, however, he must extend his head through the window; in a house, such an arrangement is not considered a separation. It appears to me that the same applies if there is excrement in the house and he extends his head through the window: He may not recite, because his head is assigned the status of the majority of his body. If he inserts his head into a room containing excrement, he likewise may not recite, because the excrement is at least in front of him. Another person's ervah. This means that there is no separation between his heart and the other person's ervah.
