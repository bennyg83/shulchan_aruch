The Beit Yosef wrote, etc. “He was uncertain, etc.” These are the words of the Rosh explaining Rabbi Yirmeyah's question. The Rosh maintains that it is obvious that a tent is considered an interruption. The question arises only because he puts out merely his head while his heart remains inside the cabinet. His heart therefore still sees the ervah. The Rosh consequently concludes, “Above we ruled that it is forbidden,” meaning that in the chapter Mi Shemeiso the Rosh ruled that one's heart seeing the ervah is forbidden. The question is therefore resolved for us.
We associate his head with the greater part of his body, so that he is considered entirely inside, and his eyes are considered to see the ervah.
If it is not ten handbreadths high, etc. Outside the window. This applies specifically to a canopy, which is not fixed. A house, however, is forbidden even when it is not ten handbreadths high, because it is fixed. In every case we associate his head with the greater part of his body. This is stated there in the Gemara in Sukkah.
Whether a wooden cabinet is considered a tent, etc. In our Talmud the two cases are explained: if it is ten handbreadths high, it is not considered an interruption, while if it is not ten handbreadths high, it certainly is an interruption. The Yerushalmi is uncertain, but it is unclear whether it discusses a cabinet ten handbreadths high while a lower one is certainly effective, or a cabinet lower than ten while a taller one is certainly ineffective, or whether the Yerushalmi is uncertain in every case, both when it is ten handbreadths high and when it is not. A cabinet may perhaps be more fixed than a canopy, as will be stated below. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes: “If his heart sees the ervah, it is forbidden. This is also stated in the Yerushalmi.” Rabbeinu Yerucham must therefore have explained the Yerushalmi as the Magen Avraham does, and he ruled leniently on its unresolved question, as the Magen Avraham writes. His words are then well understood. If he had explained the Yerushalmi like the Rosh, as questioning whether one's heart seeing ervah is forbidden, what would he mean by concluding, “This is also stated in the Yerushalmi”? If he referred to his statement that one's heart seeing ervah is forbidden, treating the question stringently, he would not need proof from the Yerushalmi's uncertainty, because this is already a dispute among Tannaim in our Talmud in the chapter Mi Shemeiso. If he referred to the first clause, that one puts his head outside the window, meaning where his heart does not see the ervah, as the Magen Avraham explains afterward, that permission is not established in the Yerushalmi. On the contrary, perhaps that is precisely the Yerushalmi's uncertainty, as the Magen Avraham indeed understands it. We must therefore say that Rabbeinu Yerucham also explained the Yerushalmi as the Magen Avraham does. Why, then, did Rabbeinu Yerucham need to cite the Yerushalmi rather than the passage in Sukkah cited by the Magen Avraham, where the Gemara plainly permits it? One may answer that the latter version there does not state the law of a canopy concerning Krias Shema at all. It discusses only one who sleeps beneath a canopy in a sukkah, whether he is considered to be sleeping within the sukkah and the canopy is not an interruption. Since the latter version is primary, the law concerning Krias Shema is not established there, and Rabbeinu Yerucham therefore cites the Yerushalmi. Alternatively, Rabbeinu Yerucham may hold that a wooden cabinet is more fixed than a canopy. The Yerushalmi discusses a cabinet less than ten handbreadths high and does not disagree with our Talmud. Although a canopy less than ten handbreadths high is an interruption, it remained uncertain whether a cabinet, being more fixed, is not an interruption. Rabbeinu Yerucham therefore states the greater novelty, that even a wooden cabinet is an interruption, and all the more so a canopy. The law of a cabinet is not stated in Sukkah. His failure to distinguish between a cabinet lower than ten and one ten handbreadths high may be because an ordinary cabinet is lower than ten. Alternatively, Rabbeinu Yerucham permits it in every case, because he considers the latter version in Sukkah primary, so our Talmud never establishes the law of a canopy concerning Krias Shema. Since the Yerushalmi is simply uncertain about a cabinet, it implies both one ten handbreadths high and one lower than ten. He ruled the unresolved question leniently, and for this reason it is considered an interruption and is permitted in every case.
If he also puts his heart outside, etc., he is considered entirely outside. Meaning, in such a case it is forbidden.
It is possible that Rabbi Yirmeyah establishes it, etc. The authorities who omitted this law evidently considered it forced to establish the case in this manner. Had the case involved one who covered his heart, the Yerushalmi should have stated so explicitly. Rather, those authorities understood that the Yerushalmi permits one's heart seeing ervah. Tosafos in Sukkah likewise state explicitly that the permission to put one's head outside a canopy and recite Krias Shema necessarily assumes that one's heart seeing ervah is permitted. They plainly considered it forced to establish the case as one who covered his heart. Since we rule that one's heart seeing ervah is forbidden, these authorities omitted the law. Although they could still have recorded that if he covers his heart, the canopy is an interruption when he puts out his head, this is no objection. The authorities are not required to record every novel law inferred through analysis when it is not stated explicitly in the Gemara. This has likewise been written many times regarding the Rambam. Rabbeinu Yerucham, however, according to the Magen Avraham, establishes both our Talmud and the Yerushalmi in precisely such a case, and therefore records the law.
But in a house, etc. This means even when it is not ten handbreadths high.
