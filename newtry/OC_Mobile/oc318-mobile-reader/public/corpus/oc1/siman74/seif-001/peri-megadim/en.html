Regarding what the Mechaber writes in seif 1, that it is forbidden when one's heart sees ervah, the Rama adds that the same applies to another person's ervah. Beit Yosef does not identify the source. Bach writes that it follows from siman 75, where even seeing another person's ervah is forbidden. Yet the eyes seeing ervah is a Torah prohibition, while the heart seeing it is Rabbinic, as we shall explain with Hashem's help in letter 1, so there is no proof. Ateres Zekeinim derives it from siman 73, where otherwise why would both people have to turn their faces away? See there. I do not understand this proof. If so, it should suffice for one of them to turn away and recite. Moreover, each necessarily places an interruption with the tallis at his own heart so that his heart should not see his own ervah, and then his heart would likewise not see the other person's ervah.
So that. See Taz. You will require here one introduction, as follows.
Know that the law divides into five categories: first, his eyes see his own ervah; second, his heart sees his own ervah; third, the ervah is exposed although neither his eyes nor heart sees it; fourth, his eyes see another person's ervah; fifth, his heart sees another person's ervah. When his eyes see his own ervah or that of a non-Jew, the prohibition is D'Oraisa from the verse, “No nakedness shall be seen among you.” This appears from Berachos 25 and Shabbos 150a, where the Gemara treats the verse as necessary for this law, indicating a complete exposition. It also appears from the unqualified rulings of all the authorities and from Chiddushei HaRashba to Berachos. Thus, one who sees ervah while reciting Krias Shema, studying Torah, or uttering any sacred matter violates a Torah prohibition. The positive commandment, “Your camp shall be holy,” however, concerns excrement and not ervah. Therefore, regarding ervah it suffices to turn away, as in siman 75 seif 6, whereas regarding excrement the Rashba and Rosh disagree whether “shall not be seen” also applies to excrement or only to ervah. In Shabbos 150, when the Gemara answers that the verse is needed for Rabbi Yehudah's teaching, it could equally have answered that it is needed for excrement in a different domain, as in siman 79 seif 2, where thinking is permitted. Accordingly, one who recites Krias Shema while seeing ervah violates a Torah prohibition, and the same applies to every sacred matter. Thinking is permitted, as is evident in Shabbos 150 and Rashi there. See Eliyah Rabbah 75:7, Taz and Shach to Yoreh Deah siman 1, seif katan 19 and 37, and Peri Chadash there, who permit thinking Torah in the presence of ervah. Listening under the principle that one who listens is as one who responds is forbidden near ervah, because it is equivalent to speech. The translator's phrase there, “transgression of the word,” means a transgression through speech, namely reciting Krias Shema in an improper place. In Sefer HaMaggid we explained at length that this is also akin to speaking evil and that his sin is grave, like one who brings words of Torah into a place of impurity, Heaven forbid.
It should be examined whether one who recited Krias Shema while his eyes saw ervah is disqualified from testimony. He is certainly not disqualified by Torah law, because the prohibition carries no lashes. Speech is considered an action only when it produces an effect, unlike here where no action results. One might argue that he fulfills the positive commandment of Krias Shema and that this constitutes an action, but this is mistaken. Something is called an action only when an actual effect is produced, as with muzzling an animal by voice, which prevents it from eating, or substituting one consecrated animal for another. See chapter 11 of Hilchos Sechirus and chapter 1 of Hilchos Temurah. Moreover, the premise is itself incorrect, because he does not fulfill Krias Shema, as in siman 83 seif 5. Even Peri Chadash in siman 71 seif 7 would agree here, as explained. The question remains whether he is Rabbinically disqualified. Perhaps Rabbinic disqualification applies only to an offense analogous to one punishable by Torah lashes, for which Rabbinic lashes are imposed, as appears to one who studies chapters 9 and 10 of Hilchos Edus, where the Rambam writes, “If it was an offense for which one is liable by Rabbinic law, etc.” Here, however, since it is only speech, perhaps Rabbinic lashes are not imposed, for a Rabbinic law should not be more stringent than a Torah law, and one is not lashed for a prohibition without an action. Nevertheless, in Shoshanas HaAmakim, in the discussion of Rabbinic lashes, we explained that even prohibitions and positive commands involving no action at all incur Rabbinic lashes, as Peri Chadash writes in Orach Chaim 496:1. His proof is from the Rambam at the end of Hilchos Na'arah Besulah regarding one who defames his wife. See there. This will be explained with Hashem's help in its place, and this is not the place to elaborate. The same implication appears in Even HaEzer 21:1 concerning listening to a woman's voice, in any event.
Accordingly, if his eyes see his own ervah or another person's ervah, he violates a Torah prohibition and receives Rabbinic lashes. If his heart sees his own or another person's ervah, the prohibition appears to be Rabbinic, because no Scriptural source is found for it. All this applies when he wears a shirt and the ervah is covered. If the ervah is exposed, however, even though his heart is covered and his eyes do not see it because he closes them, according to the Mechaber in siman 75 seif 6 and Taz there seif katan 2, or because he puts his head outside a window, as the Magen Avraham writes here, it is nevertheless forbidden to recite or study Torah. Taz and Shach discuss this at length in Yoreh Deah siman 200, and Shach rejects Bach's view that exposed ervah entails no prohibition except that the heart sees it. The truth is as Shach says. All the more so according to the Mechaber in siman 75, who holds that closing one's eyes helps. Otherwise, one standing naked could simply close his eyes and interrupt with his arms as the Mechaber writes here in seif 3. As for the reason for this law, Derishah there implies that it is Rabbinic, because it is not respectful to the Shechinah to study Torah while the ervah is exposed. Taz here and there, and Shach there, imply that it is D'Oraisa, based on the Rashba's comments to Berachos 24 and 25: the verse says, “No nakedness shall be seen,” not, “You shall not see,” meaning that it must not be visible to others. We discussed this at length in our novellae. Certainly, when another person stands opposite him it is forbidden by Torah law. If no other person is present, it would be Rabbinic because of respect for the Shechinah according to Perishah. Yet Rashi on the Torah explains “shall not be seen among you” to mean by the Holy One, blessed is He. See Re'em. It appears that there is a textual error and the wording should be otherwise, for if the verse had said “you shall not see” in the second person, it could be explained differently. See Sifsei Chachamim. In any event, Rashi explicitly teaches that exposed ervah is forbidden by Torah law even when neither his eyes nor his heart sees it.
It follows that when his eyes see his own ervah, or the ervah is exposed, it is forbidden by Torah law even if no other person is present. In a case of doubt, it is forbidden, and he must repeat Krias Shema because of the Torah-level doubt. Yet if we rule like Rava in Temurah 4 that when the Torah forbids an act, performance does not take effect, the Torah-level doubt concerning Krias Shema may require repeating it with its blessings, as in siman 67, according to Magen Avraham there, though not according to Perishah, because a presumption does not change the law. Understand what I wrote there. If, however, the obligation to repeat is only Rabbinic, one may say that a Rabbinic doubt is treated leniently. There is a distinction between one's eyes seeing ervah and exposed ervah. Covering by water does not help for sight, just as ervah behind glass remains visible, whereas water does cover exposed ervah, as Taz, Shach, and Magen Avraham explain in Yoreh Deah siman 200 and here. In truth, I do not know the reason for the distinction. Since “shall not be seen” applies both to sight and exposure, why should one differ from the other? In our novellae we raised this difficulty. I have now seen that Peri Chadash also sensed it, and Shach in seif katan 14 notes it without resolving it. According to Perishah, who holds that exposed ervah is only Rabbinically forbidden, the matter is understandable, because water may serve as a covering. Rashi's commentary on the Torah, however, does not appear so, as we wrote. See also Sema to Choshen Mishpat siman 8 and understand. See further Magen Avraham seif katan 5.
When his heart sees ervah, the prohibition is Rabbinic. We may therefore say that whenever his heart is outside the water the Sages were lenient. The baraisa in Berachos 25b, “In clear water he may sit up to his neck and recite,” supports the rule that it suffices for the heart to be outside the water. This is a novelty, and for that reason the first opinion disagrees, but even that opinion may concede this point. It is no difficulty that ervah behind glass remains forbidden. His eyes seeing it is a Torah prohibition, while the heart seeing it is Rabbinic. Whenever the ervah is covered by water, and similarly by glass, it is permitted even if his heart sees it. See Peri Chadash. According to what we have written, this is well understood. For his eyes, covering by water does not help, as Magen Avraham writes, even if he closes his eyes. From here the Mechaber in siman 75 seif 6 derived his ruling. There is no distinction between another person's ervah and his own. See Magen Avraham seif katan 5. His eyes seeing another person's ervah is D'Oraisa, while his heart seeing it is Rabbinic. There is a distinction: regarding his own ervah, according to the opinion in siman 75 that closing his eyes does not help, turning away cannot readily be envisioned, as Taz writes there in letter 2. Regarding another person's ervah, all agree that turning away is effective. His heart seeing another person's ervah is also forbidden. Indeed, we find that the heart seeing another person's ervah can be more stringent. Regarding his own ervah, an interruption with his arms helps; regarding another person's ervah, his heart ultimately still sees it. Covering his heart with his hand likewise does not help for another person's ervah, because one part of the body does not cover another, as will be explained below with Hashem's help.
