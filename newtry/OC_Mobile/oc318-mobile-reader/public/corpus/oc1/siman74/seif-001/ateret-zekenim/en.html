In the gloss, and similarly: if his heart sees his companion's nakedness, even though his heart does not see his own nakedness—for example when he is covered by a garment—it is forbidden. Thus wrote the Beit Yosef, and he quoted our teacher's wording and did not write on it "a teacher may point to a place"; it appears that he too was uncertain from which source he drew this ruling. It is difficult to know from where our teacher learned this law. It seems to me that our teacher learned it thus from the question in the chapter "One whose dead..." as explained in the Beit Yosef's words in siman 73—whether it is permitted to recite by turning one's face away—and R. Yehuda says: even with his wife with him, he must at least turn his face away, as Rashi wrote. Now, if the reason were solely on account of arousal from contact with nakedness alone, why would he need to turn his face away? Is it not sufficient to distance himself greatly from his companion so that he does not touch his flesh? Rather, certainly they required him to turn his face away not only because of this concern alone, but also so that his heart should not see his companion's nakedness—and correcting this is impossible except by interrupting with a tallit or by turning his face away; see below, siman 51.
