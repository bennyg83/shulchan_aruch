One of the seven species takes precedence, etc. Even if it is only half a fruit and the other item is whole. If both are of the seven species, or neither is of the seven species, the whole item takes precedence. Nevertheless, if one is preferred and the other is whole, the whole item is superior, according to Magen Avraham. See above, siman 168.
And if their blessings are not the same, etc. This view is that of the Rosh and Rabbenu Yonah. The opinion that the preferred item takes precedence is cited by the Tur and Beit Yosef in the name of Semak. In Eliyah Zuta I expressed surprise, because Semak siman 151 implies that a species of the seven takes precedence even when the blessings differ. I have now found in Piskei Recanati siman 78 that he explicitly states this in the name of Semak. After examination it appears to me that the Tur did not interpret Semak precisely. Semak's phrase "whether their blessings are equal or not equal" refers specifically to his rule that if one item is of the seven species, it takes precedence even when it is not preferred. This applies even when their blessings differ, as the glosses to Semak there explain. The Tur mistakenly understood the phrase as referring to the rule that the preferred item takes precedence. I am even more surprised by Bach, Magen Avraham, and the later authorities, who write that Baal Halachot Gedolot, the "some say" in seif 3 who holds Borei Pri HaEtz precedes Borei Pri HaAdamah, disagrees with Semak. They therefore rule that HaEtz has no precedence and call Baal Halachot Gedolot an isolated opinion. This is astonishing, because Semak there explicitly rules like Baal Halachot Gedolot that HaEtz takes precedence, and Recanati writes so in his name. Moreover, Semag siman 27, Mordechai and Agudah in chapter Keitzad Mevarchin, Rokeach siman 329, Shibbolei HaLeket, Tanya, Sefer Yereim, Tashbetz siman 322, Piskei Recanati, and Kaftor VaFerach all rule like Baal Halachot Gedolot. The opposite is therefore true: the Rosh and Rabbenu Yonah are the isolated opinions against all these early and later authorities. The Rosh rejects Baal Halachot Gedolot because he understands the Gemara's statement, "he blesses over this and then blesses over that," as teaching only that one blessing does not exempt the other, not precedence, and he argues that the language does not support that reading. Yet Tosafot, Berachot 41, explains that Baal Halachot Gedolot also reads it regarding precedence, but rules according to the view that the dispute applies even when the blessings differ. Agudah states this explicitly. This also appears to me to be Rambam's meaning, unlike Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh. Beit Yosef cites Mordechai and Rabbenu Peretz that even when the blessings differ one blesses first over the seven species, and he strains to explain the Gemara according to them. In my humble opinion, the Gemara is plainly explained as I have explained Baal Halachot Gedolot. Beit Yosef's claim that this is against all the commentators is surprising, for all the authorities I cited prove or explicitly state otherwise. This also appears from Agudah. In practical law, since according to the first opinion one may choose whichever he wishes, there is certainly no prohibition in blessing first over the seven species. Likewise, where neither is of the seven species, Borei Pri HaEtz precedes Borei Pri HaAdamah, and we do not follow preference. See below, seif katan 7.
[Levush] He may give precedence, etc. Bach asks that the Tur says wheat precedes an olive because earlier mention in the verse gives precedence. All the more so here, where a radish is not of the seven species at all. Bach labors to reconcile this. Taz, Magen Avraham, and Shelah, page 89, distinguish that the advantage of earlier mention in the verse is stronger than the advantage of belonging to the seven species when the blessings differ. This is surprising, because Chiddushei Rashba, page 33b, Kol Bo, page 20, and Rabbenu Yonah in chapter Keitzad Mevarchin clearly indicate that the status of the seven species always takes precedence. It appears to me that their difficulty arose because they did not penetrate the Tur's intent, as I explained above. According to my father's view, where the blessings are equal, perhaps the case concerns even chewing raw wheat. That is, according to the Rosh himself, who holds that one may choose whichever blessing to recite first, it cannot concern chewed wheat, because wheat would then precede the olive. But according to his view that HaEtz and HaAdamah are equal, the case can concern chewed wheat, with the explanation that when blessings differ a species of the seven takes precedence. Clear proof is that the abridged rulings of the Rosh say that between wheat and an olive, the olive takes precedence. One should not ask why the olive takes precedence if the Rosh holds one may choose. It appears to me that here he takes Baal Halachot Gedolot into account, because the wheat mentioned in the verse does not refer to raw chewed wheat at all, since one does not recite Birkat HaMazon or Al HaMichyah over it. Thus it lacks that special importance and is inferior even to other items not of the seven species.
If their blessings are the same, etc. This is difficult, because he already wrote that if neither is of the seven species he gives precedence to the preferred item. One may answer that this teaches that even according to the "some say" who, when the blessings differ, give precedence to the preferred item even against a species of the seven, when the blessings are the same they agree that the preferred item takes precedence only if neither is of the seven species. Another distinction is possible, but strained.
[Levush] What is preferred to him now, etc. Sefer HaChinuch in Parashat Eikev writes likewise.
[Levush] Even though the species, etc. Therefore one who has liquor and a preserve made from tree fruit or ground produce may not bless first over the liquor even if the liquor is preferred to him. Rather, he first blesses over the preserve. If the preserve itself receives Shehakol, however, he blesses over the liquor and thereby exempts the preserve. So writes Shelah there, concluding that this is unlike the masses who always bless first over liquor. In my humble opinion, perhaps one can defend their practice: the preserve is neither good nor useful unless one drinks the liquor first, making it somewhat comparable to one who does not intend to eat both items in seif 5. Beit Yosef similarly explains Rif's ruling that the wine comes only to remove the sweetness of the fruits. Here too, the preserve comes to remove the taste of the liquor. See siman 272 seif 9, where kiddush is made over beer even though the bread is brought out first; there too the necessity of kiddush determines the order.
