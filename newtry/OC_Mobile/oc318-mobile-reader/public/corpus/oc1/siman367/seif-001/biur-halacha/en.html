But courtyard residents — see Mishna Berurah that we explained that 'without knowledge' means without his knowledge, and this is the explanation of the commentators; and the source of these words is in Tur. And in truth this law is very puzzling, as Bach, Taz, and other commentators wondered: why did he write 'courtyard residents' for one courtyard, or when accustomed to participate — because it is a benefit for him and certainly it is agreeable to him, as Magen Avraham wrote seif gimel; so too we should permit in these cases also for courtyard residents, for certainly he would agree to this. And in truth it would appear because of the great difficulty that Tur and Mechaber did not intend at all to innovate that courtyard residents are worse than members of the household, for there is no source for this at all; and that which he wrote 'but courtyard residents,' etc., does not refer to what he appended regarding the law of members of his household, but to the beginning of the siman — regarding the law that his wife makes an eruv even by force and without knowledge; and regarding courtyard residents he means 'by force' of the homeowner, and the essential law comes to teach us that by force of the homeowner entirely there is no way to permit other people; and such are the words of Rosh, from whom this entire siman flows, see there, who wrote that courtyard residents entering and taking by force is also at least with the wife's knowledge; but against everyone's will there is no reason to make an eruv, see there. And if we say thus, it is fine that Tur's words are exactly like Rosh's words, and likewise in Biur HaGra seif zayin he explains thus in Mechaber's words [see there that he did not bring proof except regarding by force]; except that according to this it is not well settled what Tur and Mechaber conclude — 'without the knowledge of one member of the household' — for it implies from this that with the knowledge of one member of the household it is permitted even by force of the homeowner; and apparently we do not find this in Rosh, who permits only with the wife's knowledge and not with the knowledge of other members of the household; requires study. And for halacha, there are views on this among the Acharonim: Taz and Magen Avraham in siman 368 imply that courtyard residents cannot take bread for eruv without the homeowner's knowledge, even if the house opens to one courtyard; and Bach and Maharam Lublin imply that it is permitted when open to one courtyard; and likewise Beit Me'ir and Nehar Shalom in siman 368 side with this for halacha; and see in Biur Halacha below that we also sided that where he was accustomed to participate until now with them, one can rely on them to be lenient to take without his knowledge.

And some say that his wife, etc. — this is Rambam's view; and Mechaber brought it only in the name of 'some say,' implying that for the essential law he holds like the first view; and because according to this view one must corrupt the textual reading 'accustomed — no, not accustomed — yes,' as written in Kesef Mishneh and Beit Yosef. And in truth there is no need at all to corrupt the books, and this view fits fine also according to our reading, as Magen Avraham wrote, and likewise Eliyah Rabbah and Gra in his Biur, and likewise it is explicit as their words in Ra'avan's explanation, which also follows Rambam's view, see there. And behold according to their words, both statements fit their plain meaning: the first statement — his wife makes an eruv without his knowledge — and we mean only without his knowledge alone, and the plainness of the language is when he is not accustomed to participate; and the second statement that he mentioned — 'accustomed' — there courtyard residents enter and take by force; and therefore Rif mentioned only the two statements, because he follows Ra'avan his teacher's view — that his wife when not accustomed is permitted only without knowledge, per the plain language of Shmuel, and not by force; and if so, since the sugya of Shulchan Aruch is explained well like Rambam, and also Ra'avan and Rif hold thus [and for them the sugya is settled better — for according to Rosh the last statement of Shmuel deals with a house open to two alleyways and he does not forbid them — what relevance is there to entering by force? What do they care if he does not give a share in the eruv? He would be forbidden to take utensils out of the house, not they] — it is possible to be stringent according to this view not to make an eruv by force of the homeowner even with the wife's knowledge when he is not accustomed to participate with them; however in Eliyah Rabbah I saw that he wrote to rule leniently like the first view; requires study. But even if we do not wish to rule like this view to be stringent when the husband protests, in any case one can rely to rule like them to be lenient regarding when he is accustomed to make an eruv with them — that courtyard residents enter and take from him by force and we do not require the wife's knowledge at all; for in truth the first view that requires the wife's knowledge also here is difficult, for this is not the meaning of Shulchan Aruch at all; and likewise Rashba in Avodat HaKodesh — even though he also ruled like Rosh's view that his wife makes an eruv without knowledge, meaning even by force of the husband — nevertheless here he wrote explicitly that courtyard residents enter and take by force even without the wife's knowledge [except that according to his view there, who explains 'accustomed' means accustomed to forbid and not accustomed to participate, he was challenged in his chiddushim: if so, why did Shmuel say his wife makes an eruv, etc. — behold even without the wife's knowledge it is permitted, for courtyard residents enter, etc., see there, it remains requires study]; however according to Rosh's view, who says 'accustomed to participate' — and this is the main elevation — and likewise Rif, Rambam, and Ri explain in his chiddushim — if so, everything comes out well: when accustomed to participate, we do not require even the wife's knowledge]; except it appears that according to Rambam and his supporters, courtyard residents do not take by force when accustomed, but specifically when he forbids them, as we wrote in Mishna Berurah; and likewise it is implied in Ra'avan's explanation, see there. The conclusion from all this is that for halacha it is reasonable that when accustomed and he forbids, courtyard residents take even by force and we do not require the wife's knowledge at all; and in any case, without his knowledge one can certainly rely to be lenient, for many Acharonim are lenient even according to Tur's view, as we wrote above.

One must return and acquire — the wording is somewhat garbled, for behold it deals with the bread placed in its place; if so, what is 'return and acquire'? It is not reasonable to say he must take the bread from its place and return and give in partnership, for money is worse to return than if he had a partner in wine that relies on leaves because of eruv (see siman 366 seif 11); and here too, why can he not rely on it? And one should not explain that it deals with until now he gave bread on behalf of all and acquired for them, and therefore after his retraction if he wishes to participate anew he must acquire for them through someone that they again have a share in his bread — one cannot say it deals thus, for if so, what does the Rama gloss conclude: 'but if accustomed to make an eruv, it is an eruv by force' — for in such a case it is reasonable that 'accustomed' does not help that he should always be obligated to give a share to all in his bread; and therefore it deals with when he gives only his own share; and it is difficult as above. And it appears the intent is not that he must acquire and give anew, but that he must say with his mouth that he returns and participates with them and acquires for all that they have a share in his bread; and because we say 'one who is particular about his eruv — it is not an eruv,' and there is none more particular than he who retracted; and therefore he must say he is not particular about his bread and acquires for all that they have a share in it.
