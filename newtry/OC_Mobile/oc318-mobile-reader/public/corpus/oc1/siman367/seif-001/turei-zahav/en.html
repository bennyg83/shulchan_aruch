A man's wife. We will write the general rule that emerges for us in this siman, with God's help. It says on daf 81: that guard — meaning a non-Jew who guards weapons of the city that was in the neighborhood of Rav Yehuda bar Oshaya — they said to him: rent us your domain; he did not rent to them. He came before R' Yehuda; he said: thus said Shmuel — a man's wife makes an eruv for him without his knowledge. They challenged: without knowledge of their husbands their eruv is not an eruv. No difficulty: this where he forbade them — Rashi explained on alleyway residents — we do not require his knowledge; and the mishnah is where he did not forbid, such as a courtyard between two alleyways accustomed with this one and not with that one. Here too it is reasonable; for if so Shmuel contradicts Shmuel — perhaps that which Shmuel said 'not accustomed' means without protest, as he said anonymously; and 'they take by force' implies he stands and protests, as Rashi explained that he intends to forbid — unlike that guard, where certainly whoever does not wish to rent truly protests. This is the law of his wife. Members of the household have power to give bread in the house as long as they do not know his opinion; but if he revealed he does not want — this is like protesting and a member of the household has no power for this; and even anonymously it helps only in that courtyard open to the alleyway specifically — when there is only one courtyard, or there are two and he is accustomed in one and not the other — then he has power when accustomed; but for courtyard residents Tur wrote they cannot take his bread from his house for eruv without knowledge of any member of the household, even accustomed with them and he forbids them. Question on this: behold in siman 368 Tur wrote that when open only to this courtyard, they make eruv without knowledge — implying even courtyard residents have power on this; and this refers to making eruv from the homeowner's [bread]; if so, what advantage has a member of the household over a courtyard resident? And Maharach distinguished between them: a member of the household helps even when he does not want, only he did not protest explicitly; and a courtyard resident — specifically when they do not know his opinion at all it helps; and we already proved one should not distinguish this from the Talmud, but all who do not want have the law of explicit protest; and it appears to me that siman 368 does not deal with courtyard residents having power to take bread from the homeowner without his knowledge, but the member of the household gives him the bread and the courtyard resident makes eruv with the courtyard's bread; and this is why Tur was precise and wrote 'but courtyard residents cannot take his bread for eruv,' etc. — meaning the taking they cannot do; and now the wording 'but' that he wrote is fine — for apparently he should have said 'and courtyard residents,' behold there is nothing here and its opposite, but it is another matter; and according to what we said it is fine: it refers to members of the household, and this is what he means: members of the household can make eruv without knowledge and take bread from the homeowner; but courtyard residents, although they have power to make eruv without knowledge, nevertheless taking the bread requires knowledge of a member of the household; and Tur in siman 368 comes only to teach the law of eruv and relied on what he wrote here regarding taking bread; and all that Maharach and Beit Yosef wrote that members of the household and courtyard residents are equal to make eruv without knowledge in one courtyard is not true; and I say it is absolutely true, only in taking bread there is a distinction, as we wrote; and similar is the view of 'some say' here — Rambam — who holds beit din compels to give and they make eruv; as we wrote, it is correct.
