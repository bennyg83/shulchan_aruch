[5] Even to take it, etc. Even taking it without cutting is prohibited because it is muktzeh, as explicitly stated in siman 519. If he cuts it, Rabbenu Yerucham, netiv 4 part 1, writes that by hand he violates a rabbinic prohibition, while with a utensil such as a knife it is biblically prohibited, whether on Shabbos or Yom Tov, and whether he cuts it to pick his teeth or to open a door. Rosh, Beitzah chapter 4, likewise makes this clear. Accordingly, Rashi's statement on 33a, "he cuts and smooths it with a knife," does not mean smoothing is a separate act. Cutting with a knife itself has the status of smoothing, as Maggid Mishneh, chapter 11, also implies. Taz in siman 519 understood that cutting with a knife is exempt, but he did not examine the cited authorities carefully.
[6] To cut, etc. Specifically by hand, but not with a knife (Yam Shel Shlomo there). Taz in siman 519 argues at length that since even cutting with a knife to make a utensil creates liability only in a certain case, cutting merely for fragrance should be initially permitted. According to what I explained above, cutting with a knife to make a utensil is liable, which supports the opposite conclusion. His question why the Gemara on 33a did not answer that the baraita saying one should not cut concerns a knife is no difficulty, because ordinary "cutting" there means by hand, as Rosh and the other authorities show. Maharshal also notes that the Gemara compares it to crushing with one's fingers, which is by hand, so it cannot be established as using a utensil. Taz further argues that the Gemara compares cutting for fragrance to breaking a barrel, which uses a utensil. This is no proof. The questioner challenges from breaking a barrel because no decree was imposed there. The answer is that the barrel is a mustaki, where even intentionally making a utensil would not create biblical liability, so breaking it with a utensil was not prohibited by decree. Cutting a hard twig for fragrance, however, carries concern that one might cut with a knife to make a utensil, which would be liable, and therefore even cutting for fragrance was prohibited. His proof from Rosh that animal fodder may be cut even with a knife is likewise misunderstood. With edible animal fodder there is no underlying liability at all, so the only possible decree would be lest one cut a nonfood twig. Rosh properly proves from the permitted breaking of a mustaki that we do not make such cross-item decrees. Therefore one should prohibit like Maharshal. Taz concludes that permission is obvious, but his proofs have been rejected. All this concerns hard twigs. With soft ones, even Maharshal certainly agrees that cutting with a knife is permitted. See siman 321, seif 1, where use of a utensil is prohibited in another context, and the cases can be distinguished.
