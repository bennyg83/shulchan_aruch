(Se'if Katan 1) We begin early. Tur writes, etc. This is Beis Yosef's language. Know that Rabbi Yehudah and the Rabbis disagree about the time for Minchah. The Rabbis hold that its time extends until evening, meaning until tzeis hakochavim, whereas Rabbi Yehudah holds that its time extends until plag haminchah, one and one-quarter hours before tzeis hakochavim. The time for Maariv consequently begins when the time for Minchah ends. According to the Rabbis, Maariv begins at tzeis hakochavim; according to Rabbi Yehudah, it begins one and one-quarter hours earlier. Berachos 27a asks which view is the halachah. It argues that since Rav prayed the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos - that is, he prayed the Friday-night Maariv from plag haminchah onward - we may infer that the halachah follows Rabbi Yehudah. It responds: On the contrary, since Rav Huna and the Rabbis did not pray until evening, we may infer that they follow the Rabbis. It concludes that since the halachah was stated neither in accordance with one master nor the other, one who acts in accordance with either has acted properly. Shulchan Aruch therefore writes here that 'we begin early,' etc., earlier than on weekdays. This means that even those who follow the Rabbis during the week and pray Maariv specifically at tzeis hakochavim should pray early on Erev Shabbos because of the mitzvah of adding to Shabbos. Beis Yosef then cites Tur in the name of Ritz Gias, who distinguishes based upon the availability of wine, etc. That is, even if by praying during the day from plag haminchah onward he would have wine for Kiddush or Havdalah, while waiting to pray until tzeis hakochavim would leave him without wine - for example, he must travel before night to a place where wine is unavailable, as discussed below in siman 293 - he nevertheless should not pray until night.
For bread can be used. This resolves the matter regarding Kiddush, which may be recited over bread. Regarding Havdalah, although it may not be recited over bread, Tur concludes in siman 293: 'Havdalah can be recited tomorrow,' as stated in siman 259:6.
Since Rambam and Rosh, etc. They hold that even one who follows the Rabbis nevertheless prays early on Erev Shabbos, and they disagree with Ritz Gias.
This requires examination, for the Gemara says, etc. Rambam and Rosh are difficult to understand: according to them, what proof is there that Rav follows Rabbi Yehudah from the fact that he prayed the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos? Even if he follows the Rabbis, he may pray early on Erev Shabbos.
They all hold like Rabbi Yehudah. This means that all the Amoraim whom the Talmud reports as having prayed the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos, whether they prayed early on Erev Shabbos or prayed the post-Shabbos prayer while it was still Shabbos, hold like Rabbi Yehudah. This implies that according to the Rabbis it is forbidden to pray early even on Erev Shabbos.
His words therefore contradict one another - meaning Beis Yosef's words. Apart from the aforementioned difficulty from the Talmud, his own words contradict one another: in Beis Yosef he writes that Rambam agrees with Rosh, unlike Ritz Gias; in Kesef Mishneh he writes that Rambam agrees with Ritz Gias, that Shabbos and weekdays are alike and one should not pray earlier on Erev Shabbos than during the week.
Magen Avraham writes: But Talmidei Rabbeinu Yonah implies that on Erev Shabbos, etc., it must be for a mitzvah purpose. That is, regarding Rav's act of praying the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos, it explains that he prayed early because of the mitzvah of adding to Shabbos. Subsequently, concerning Rabbi Yoshiyah's act of praying the post-Shabbos prayer while it was still Shabbos, it writes in Rav Hai Gaon's name that since the public follows the Rabbis, one should not pray Maariv before sunset unless necessary for a mitzvah; see there. Since it does not say this concerning Rav's act of praying the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos, it implies that no separate mitzvah purpose is needed: the mitzvah of adding to Shabbos itself permits him to pray early, although the public follows the Rabbis.
I afterward found that Behag writes, etc. This means that he found a great authority upon whom those who disagree with Ritz Gias may rely: Behag writes in accordance with their position.
'Rabbi Yirmeyah prayed the weekday prayer behind him.' The three words 'the weekday prayer' do not appear in our Talmudic text. Rashi explains the statement that Rav did not interrupt Rabbi Yirmeyah's prayer to mean that upon completing his prayer Rav did not step backward, so as not to interpose between Rabbi Yirmeyah and the wall, since one may not pass in front of people praying. This is also explicit in the Gemara there. According to Behag's approach, he must have had a different text throughout that passage; see there.
According to him, one must say, etc. Behag's own words are difficult for the reason stated above: how does the Talmud infer from Rav's praying the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos that he holds like Rabbi Yehudah? From where does this follow? This is the aforementioned difficulty.
Initially, etc. This means that initially it was assumed that the halachah was definitively either like the Rabbis or like Rabbi Yehudah. If Rav held like the Rabbis, it would be improper to pray outside the appointed time merely because of adding to Shabbos. According to the conclusion, however, that one who follows either view has acted properly and the halachah is not definitively like either, even one who follows the Rabbis during the week may pray early on Erev Shabbos because of adding to Shabbos. His praying early cannot be described as praying before the proper time, since he could choose to act this way at any time; it is a valid time.
It appears to me to provide another sound reason - meaning that besides the mitzvah of adding to Shabbos described above, there is another reason. For, etc.: Maariv corresponds to the limbs and fats of the afternoon Tamid, since the prayers were instituted to correspond to the daily offerings. Shabbos Maariv therefore corresponds to the limbs and fats of Friday's afternoon Tamid. It is consequently improper to pray Maariv at night, when it would no longer have been permissible to burn Friday's limbs and fats. Tosefes Shabbos challenges this: If it is improper to pray at a time when the offering replaced by the prayer could not be brought, what is gained by praying early while it is still day? Once the congregation has said Barchu, it has accepted Shabbos, as stated above at the end of siman 261. How may one then pray Maariv, which corresponds to the limbs and fats? In my humble opinion, speech itself involves no Shabbos desecration. The reason not to pray Maariv on Friday night is only a symbolic remembrance that the limbs and fats were not burned then. A remembrance concerning the essential period of Shabbos suffices, even if there is no such remembrance during the added period. Moreover, in the period added to Shabbos it would have been permissible to burn the limbs and fats. The essential addition, which is required by Torah law, has no prescribed measure, and slightly more than the smallest amount suffices. Any further period voluntarily added is prohibited only rabbinically, and in the Temple it would have been permitted in order to perform the mitzvah of burning the limbs and fats at its proper time. I wrote similarly above in siman 253, Magen Avraham 26. I have now found support in Talmidei Rabbeinu Yonah, chapter Tefillas HaShachar, concerning Rav's praying the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos: 'Rabbi Yitzchak of Orleans would say that although the slightest amount suffices for adding to Shabbos, if one adds much more, it is nevertheless included in the addition to Shabbos and he is bound by it. Although one could say that this is only rabbinic, he may nevertheless perform no labor, just as during the slightest addition,' etc. Thus, Rabbi Yitzchak of Orleans is not certain about the matter and allows the possibility that the period one adds beyond the minimum is forbidden only rabbinically. One may similarly say that the poskim who require praying Maariv early on Erev Shabbos for Magen Avraham's reason - that it corresponds to the limbs and fats - also hold that the voluntarily added period is only rabbinic. The matter is therefore resolved.
A similar idea appears in Beis Yosef, siman 236, etc. It explains why the verse VeHu Rachum is not said before Maariv on Shabbos as it is during the week. We recite VeHu Rachum because Maariv corresponds to the limbs and fats, which atoned for Israel's sins. Thus the Sages said that a person never slept in Jerusalem with a sin in his hand: the morning Tamid atoned, etc. We therefore beseech Hashem that our lips in Maariv should substitute for bulls, and that 'He, the Merciful One, will atone,' etc. On Friday night, when the limbs and fats were not burned, we do not say VeHu Rachum. This is the substance of his words.
Therefore, one must take care, etc. Since even those who follow the Rabbis during the week pray early on Erev Shabbos while it is still day, after plag haminchah, relying upon Rabbi Yehudah, they must pray Minchah before plag haminchah. If they also pray Minchah after plag haminchah, Minchah and Maariv contradict one another. Mordechai agrees that on a single day one may not follow mutually contradictory practices.
All this, etc. - to pray Maariv at night. This means throughout the week. Those who also pray weekday Maariv after plag haminchah do not require the preceding rationale. It is nevertheless obvious that they too may not pray Minchah after plag haminchah.
Olas Shabbos writes, etc. He did not examine the Gemara. According to his position, how does the Gemara infer from Rav's praying the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos that the halachah follows Rabbi Yehudah? The Rabbis would agree on Erev Shabbos. Rather, the Rabbis certainly disagree even on Erev Shabbos. The poskim made a distinction for our practical observance because the Talmud concludes that one who acts like either master has acted properly; they therefore write that one should pray early on Erev Shabbos.
Also, what he writes, etc. - the disagreement between Terumas HaDeshen and Levush, etc. Olas Shabbos challenges his own approach. If the Rabbis agree that one may pray early on Erev Shabbos because of adding to Shabbos, the difference between them and Rabbi Yehudah is minimal. The measure one may add is explained above in siman 261 by Shulchan Aruch as three and one-quarter mil, which is one and one-quarter hours less one twentieth of an hour, as Magen Avraham writes there in paragraph 10. The Rabbis would therefore permit prayer then, while Rabbi Yehudah permits it from plag haminchah, exactly one and one-quarter hours before night. They differ by only one twentieth of an hour, and it is forced to say that they disagree over so little. Indeed, there is no difference at all, since Rama there writes that one may add from plag haminchah, and Shulchan Aruch itself writes this in siman 263. Olas Shabbos therefore writes that their principal disagreement concerns weekdays. He further writes that even on Erev Shabbos there is a large difference between them because Terumas HaDeshen and Levush disagree about the endpoints used to calculate the day. Terumas HaDeshen calculates the day from alos hashachar until tzeis hakochavim; Levush calculates it from sunrise until sunset. Rabbi Yehudah, he argues, follows Levush and calculates from sunrise until sunset. In the chapter Mi SheHayah Tamei, the authorities disagree over the time from alos hashachar to sunrise and from sunset to tzeis hakochavim. Rabbi Yehudah holds that each interval is four mil. Since Rabbi Yehudah permits Maariv from plag haminchah, one and one-quarter hours before night, and 'night' is calculated from sunset, one may begin one and one-quarter hours before sunset. Sunset to tzeis hakochavim is another one and one-quarter hours according to Terumas HaDeshen's measure of a mil as one third of an hour less one thirtieth of an hour. Four mil from sunset to tzeis hakochavim therefore equal one and one-quarter hours less one twentieth of an hour. By way of illustration with equal hours of sixty minutes: one third of an hour less one thirtieth is eighteen minutes; four times eighteen is seventy-two minutes, or one hour and twelve minutes. This is three minutes less than one and one-quarter hours, which is seventy-five minutes; three minutes are one twentieth of sixty minutes. From plag haminchah through tzeis hakochavim the total is consequently two and one-half hours less one twentieth of an hour. This is Olas Shabbos's position; I have explained his calculation. He does not explain the Rabbis' position. He must hold that they follow Terumas HaDeshen and calculate the day from alos hashachar to tzeis hakochavim. If the Rabbis also followed Levush and calculated from sunrise to sunset, then, since in his view they agree on Erev Shabbos that prayer may begin at the time of tosefes Shabbos, close to plag haminchah, their difference from Rabbi Yehudah would remain minimal. Rather, the Rabbis follow Terumas HaDeshen, so daylight continues until the end of sunset, meaning tzeis hakochavim. According to them, plag haminchah begins one and one-quarter hours before tzeis hakochavim - close to the aforementioned time of tosefes Shabbos. Rabbi Yehudah and the Rabbis would thus differ by one and one-quarter hours less one twentieth of an hour.
Magen Avraham's statement in his name that according to Levush plag haminchah occurs two and one-half hours before tzeis hakochavim is imprecise, for it is less by one twentieth of an hour, as explained above. Olas Shabbos states this explicitly; see there.
For the Rabbis did not mention, etc. How can one formulate a dispute between Rabbi Yehudah and the Rabbis concerning when plag haminchah begins? The Rabbis never mention plag haminchah. How can we invent a disagreement between them and Rabbi Yehudah about plag haminchah?
He was imprecise, for if we calculate, etc. - two fifths of an hour. Terumas HaDeshen holds that a mil is one third of an hour less one thirtieth of an hour; this is the correct text. Rambam, however, writes in his Commentary on the Mishnah that a mil is two fifths of an hour. Maadanei Melech, Berachos 1, explains their positions. Terumas HaDeshen calculates the day from alos hashachar until tzeis hakochavim, and therefore a mil is one third of an hour less one thirtieth. The chapter Mi SheHayah Tamei states that on an equinoctial day, whose daylight is twelve hours, an average person walks ten parsaos. A parsah is four mil, so he walks forty mil in a day. If a mil were a full third of an hour, three thirds making an hour, forty mil would total thirteen and one-third hours. Subtract one thirtieth of an hour from each mil. The forty thirtieths removed total one and one-third hours: thirty thirtieths make an hour and the remaining ten make one third. In equal sixty-minute hours, one thirtieth is two minutes; thirty times two is sixty minutes and ten times two is twenty minutes, one third of an hour. Rambam, however, agrees with Levush that the day is calculated from sunrise until sunset and with the opinion that the walk from alos hashachar to sunrise is five mil and from sunset to tzeis hakochavim another five. Since walking ten parsaos means from alos hashachar to tzeis hakochavim, only thirty mil remain within the day from sunrise until sunset; five mil are walked before the day begins and five after it ends. Dividing the twelve-hour day into thirty parts yields two fifths of an hour per mil. Thirty mil are sixty fifths of an hour, or twelve hours, since twelve times five is sixty. Yet I am perplexed and do not know what practical difference exists between Terumas HaDeshen and Rambam beyond terminology, since hours are always seasonal hours; Rambam's day is no shorter or longer than Terumas HaDeshen's. In any event, if a mil is two fifths of an hour and, according to Olas Shabbos, sunset to tzeis hakochavim is four mil, the interval is eight fifths of an hour, the measure Magen Avraham states. Using equal hours for illustration: one fifth of an hour is twelve minutes, so two fifths are twenty-four minutes. Four mil are eight fifths. Five fifths form one hour, leaving three fifths, or thirty-six minutes - one-half hour plus six minutes. Those six minutes are one tenth of a sixty-minute hour. Thus the four mil equal one and one-half hours plus one tenth of an hour. Add the one and one-quarter hours from plag haminchah to sunset, and the total is two and three-quarter hours plus one tenth. The calculation is nevertheless imprecise. If a mil is two fifths of an hour, one must follow the opinion that alos hashachar to sunrise is five mil and sunset to tzeis hakochavim is five mil, as explained. If each interval were four mil, a mil would not be two fifths but three eighths of an hour - one quarter plus half a quarter. Subtracting four mil before sunrise and four after sunset, a total of eight, from the forty mil leaves thirty-two mil in the day. At three eighths of an hour per mil, thirty-two mil equal twelve hours: thirty-two quarters equal eight hours, and thirty-two half-quarters equal another four, totaling twelve. Since Magen Avraham assigns two fifths of an hour per mil, he necessarily follows the view that there are five mil from alos hashachar to sunrise and likewise from sunset to tzeis hakochavim. Yet he then writes, 'It therefore totals two and three-quarter hours plus one tenth.' That result assumes four mil from sunset to tzeis hakochavim. Chok Yaakov also raises this point. Furthermore, how can Rabbi Yehudah - whose position we are following - be assigned a mil of two fifths of an hour, which depends on there being five mil from alos hashachar to sunrise and from sunset to tzeis hakochavim? Rabbi Yehudah states explicitly in Pesachim 93b that each interval is four mil; see there. Rather, since the only poskim who assign a duration to a mil are Terumas HaDeshen, who gives one third of an hour less one thirtieth, and Rambam, who gives two fifths, while no posek explicitly gives three eighths of an hour, Magen Avraham did not wish to introduce a new measure absent from the poskim. The true calculation is nevertheless that if each twilight interval is four mil, a mil must be three eighths of an hour. Since Olas Shabbos's words are refuted either way, Magen Avraham uses the two-fifths measure explicitly found in Rambam. He nevertheless records a total of two and three-quarter hours plus one tenth according to Olas Shabbos's own assumption and Rabbi Yehudah's explicit position in Mi SheHayah Tamei that the interval is four mil. Since Olas Shabbos's words are rejected in any event, this is why Magen Avraham presents the measure in that manner.
Also, what he writes concerning the practical difference for an individual who erred, etc. This means that he prayed Maariv during the day on a cloudy Erev Shabbos, thinking it was already tzeis hakochavim, after which the clouds dispersed. Above, siman 263 states that an individual must pray again. Olas Shabbos explains that this applies only according to the Rabbis, who hold that the primary time for Maariv is tzeis hakochavim but permit one to pray early from plag haminchah on Erev Shabbos because of adding to Shabbos. Since his acceptance of the addition was mistaken, he must pray again. According to Rabbi Yehudah, however, the primary time for Maariv begins at plag haminchah. Even if he mistakenly prayed from plag haminchah onward, he need not pray again. This is difficult, because Berachos 27b's ruling that an individual who erred must pray again follows Rav: the discussion there proceeds according to Rav, while immediately beforehand the Gemara says that Rav holds like Rabbi Yehudah because he prayed the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos. Nevertheless, it states that an individual who erred must pray again.
Bach writes that one may accept, etc. Bach comments on the statement that one may pray early because of adding to Shabbos: Why must one pray early? He can accept the addition to Shabbos without prayer. He answers that it is preferable for all Israel to accept the addition to Shabbos together. Maggid Mishneh writes, 'Although he recited Kiddush while it was still day,' etc. This appears in Hilchos Shabbos 29:11. Regarding the requirement that Kiddush be recited at the place of the meal, see siman 273 for how he should proceed.
And in Mordechai, etc.: He nevertheless fulfills the obligation, etc. Mordechai was troubled by Berachos 20b, which asks whether women's obligation in Birchas HaMazon is from the Torah or rabbinic. The practical difference is whether they can discharge men's Birchas HaMazon obligation: if their obligation is from the Torah, they can; if it is rabbinic, they cannot, because one with a rabbinic obligation cannot discharge one with a Torah obligation. Here, according to the view that adding to Shabbos is rabbinic, how can a person at that time discharge the Torah obligation of Kiddush that takes effect after dark? Mordechai answers that because he himself will later become obligated by Torah law, he may discharge the obligation even now, while his obligation is rabbinic. Women, by contrast, according to that side of the question, never become obligated by Torah law.
Shelah writes, etc. The three Shabbos meals apply on Shabbos itself, for the three occurrences of 'today' in the verse from which the obligation of three Shabbos meals is derived refer to the day of Shabbos itself, not to its added period.
Tosafos and Rosh, however, imply, etc. This refers to what Magen Avraham cites in the next paragraph: they hold that although Rav prayed the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos, he recited Krias Shema at night. Regarding his eating before Krias Shema, Magen Avraham writes that he began permissibly, meaning at least one-half hour before tzeis hakochavim. If one were required to eat specifically a kezayis at night, then even if he began one-half hour beforehand, he knew that his meal would necessarily continue beyond tzeis hakochavim. Such a case is not considered beginning permissibly, as Magen Avraham writes above in siman 263:29; see there.
