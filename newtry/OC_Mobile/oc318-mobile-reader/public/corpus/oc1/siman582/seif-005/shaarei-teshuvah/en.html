See Pri Chadash: if on a weekday he said "a remembrance of teruah," he has not fulfilled his obligation, and even on Shabbat, if he said "a day of teruah," he has not fulfilled his obligation, since a fixed, ordained court in Eretz Yisrael would blow, or alternatively, etc. See below siman 591 regarding the blessing "Who hears the sound of teruah," etc.
(*) (In Shulchan Aruch, seif 6:) He says in the prayer, etc. If he erred and did not mention the theme of the festival, he is brought back, as written in Beit Yosef in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah. Although the evening prayer is optional, the prayer of Shabbat and Yom Tov is obligatory; so wrote Moed Katan. Machazik Berachah wrote that this is not so, but in any event nowadays all evening prayers have been made obligatory; see there and siman 265. He further wrote regarding one who recited the evening prayer properly and concluded "Who sanctifies Israel and the seasons": although Rosh Hashanah is included among the seasons, nevertheless he did not intend Rosh Hashanah and merely followed the accustomed formula of the festivals, just as on Yom Tov if one concluded "Who sanctifies Shabbat" he has not fulfilled his obligation, even though Yom Tov is called Shabbat. Moreover, this person did not know from the outset that Rosh Hashanah is included among the seasons, etc.; see there. See Machazik Berachah, siman 268, who brought in the name of Maamar Mordechai that if Yom Tov or Rosh Hashanah falls on Shabbat and he forgot in the evening prayer and did not mention Yom Tov, he repeats and cannot rely on hearing the chazzan's blessing, since Yom Tov is not mentioned in it. Machazik Berachah wrote that he fulfills his obligation with the blessing resembling the seven, but in Shulchan Aruch, p. 306, he disagreed with Machazik Berachah, comparing it to eating fruit and mezonot, etc., and that comparison does not work; rather he does not fulfill his obligation with the blessing resembling the seven. So it appears to my humble mind. See there in Shaarei Teshuvah, that if it falls on Motzaei Shabbat and he forgot to say Vatodi'enu, he need not repeat, since he will later make havdalah over a cup. See Dagul Merevavah, that the Mechaber omitted the word "with love" according to his own view in siman 87, which follows the Rambam's version; but according to our custom, in which we say it on every Yom Tov, also on Rosh Hashanah, one should say it, unlike Eliyah Rabbah; see there. He wrote in Shulchan Aruch HaRav that if one forgot to say "Reign over all the earth" in Shacharit, he has fulfilled his obligation, and even if he did not say "Our God and God of our fathers, reign over all," but after Yaaleh VeYavo concluded "Blessed are You, Lord, Who sanctifies Israel and the day of remembrance," he fulfilled his obligation. But in Tefillat Mussaf, which is Malchuyot, perhaps he must conclude "King over all the earth," similar to Zichronot and Shofarot. Yet according to what the Abudarham and Kol Bo wrote, that in the verses of Zichronot and Shofarot one says near the conclusion the final Torah verse because in Malchuyot he does not conclude with the matter of kingship but with the sanctity of the day, etc., this implies that the word "King" was not fixed as an absolute requirement; otherwise one would have needed to say a verse of kingship near the conclusion so that the conclusion would be thematically adjacent to it; see there. If Rosh Hashanah falls on Shabbat and he erred in the conclusion and did not mention Shabbat, Kenesset HaGedolah in siman 487 holds that he fulfills his obligation since he mentioned it in the middle of the blessing, while Pri Chadash disagrees, as do several great authorities; see above siman 487 in Baer Heitev, seif katan 5. In Birkei Yosef, Machazik Berachah also agreed with Pri Chadash and wrote that this also appears from Ritva, Perek 2 of Beitzah; see there. See there, where he discussed at length that our version "Reign over the entire world" is proper, as the Taz wrote, and Machazik Berachah brings proof from several statements in the Talmud.
