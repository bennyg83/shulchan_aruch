Nevertheless, after the fact, etc. See Peri Megadim, who cites Bach that one should be stringent when other tefillin are available. Regarding the batim, however, it appears obvious that one may rely upon the lenient view if a Jew subsequently blackens them for the sake of the mitzvah, as Magen Avraham writes. Even without this there are many doubts in the matter, as Dagul Merevavah and Levushei Serad explain.
But the straps. Sefer HaTerumah gives the reason: since the essential requirement that the straps be black is a halachah transmitted to Moshe at Sinai, blackening must likewise be performed for the sake of the mitzvah, just as spinning tzitzis and dyeing techeiles must be for the sake of the mitzvah. Accordingly, it would appear that the actual making of the strap, meaning cutting it from the leather, must likewise be performed for the sake of the mitzvah. I found that Emek Halachah writes this explicitly.
Even after the fact, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote, “And all the more so, etc.” See below, siman 39, seif 2. All matters included in making the body of the tefillin are treated like writing the tefillin; see Magen Avraham there. It is therefore clear that they are certainly no less stringent than blackening the straps, which must be performed for their sake. I saw in Magen HaElef that Rambam necessarily disagrees with Sefer HaTerumah's rule. Rambam writes in chapter 3 of Hilchos Tefillin: “Tefillin may be made only by a Jew. Therefore, if a non-Jew covered or sewed them, they are invalid.” If Rambam required all of this to be done for the sake of the mitzvah, the invalidity would already follow because it was not done for that purpose. Rambam maintains that wherever intent for the sake of the mitzvah is required, a non-Jew cannot accomplish it even while a Jew supervises him. According to Rabbi Akiva Eiger's answer in his novellae to siman 39 upon a similar question of Magen Avraham, this proof disappears. This is especially so according to Peri Megadim's suggestion in siman 32, seif katan 32, that writing for the sake of the mitzvah may itself be only D'Rabbanan, with several practical differences. See Peri Megadim's introduction to the laws of Krias Shema. In any event, one should not be lenient in practice against Rama's ruling. Perhaps scribes are not careful to explicitly think “for the sake of the mitzvah” during all these acts because, once the leather was already processed for the sake of the mitzvah, its ordinary use is presumed to be for that purpose. But this too is difficult. Concerning attaching tzitzis to a garment, siman 14 teaches that if a Jew attached them without intent, one should not recite a blessing, even though the strings were already spun for the sake of tzitzis. We do not say that their ordinary use is presumed to be for the mitzvah. The same is true concerning writing sacred texts. Even after the fact, writing without explicit intent is ineffective, as is clear from siman 32, seif 19. What seif 8 says, that it is good to verbalize the intent, means that after the fact thought suffices, not that no intent at all is needed. Perhaps, since Sefer HaTerumah himself was uncertain about this law, as Beit Yosef explains, whether it may be derived from tzitzis, it is sufficient to be stringent after the fact only when a non-Jew performed the act or when a Jew explicitly intended not for the sake of the mitzvah, but not when a Jew acted without explicit intent. The matter requires study. Although we cannot clearly invalidate after the fact on this basis, initially one must certainly perform all construction of the batim and shin, their stitching, the making of the straps, and their blackening explicitly for the sake of the mitzvah. Know that concerning what we wrote about permitting a minor to blacken the straps when an adult supervises, although Peri Megadim initially wished to be stringent in his introduction to siman 32, in his actual discussion here, Mishbetzos Zahav, seif katan 5, and in siman 39, Eshel Avraham, seif katan 6, he retracted and permitted it. With Hashem's help, I found this explicit in responsa Maharam of Lublin, siman 68: blackening the straps is not included in the primary construction. Regarding our statement that a non-Jew may blacken them when a Jew supervises, there is room for hesitation. See above, siman 11, where Dagul Merevavah questions Shulchan Aruch's permission, according to Rosh, for a non-Jew to spin under supervision. The same concern would apply here. Nevertheless, it appears to me that one should not be stringent after the fact, since Sefer HaTerumah himself was not entirely certain of this law.
Invalid. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote that the later authorities reject Magen Avraham's proposed remedy. Nevertheless, it appears to me that if a Jew blackened the straps the first time without thinking explicitly for the sake of the mitzvah, but also without contrary intent, we may possibly rely upon Magen Avraham and have him blacken them a second time explicitly for the sake of the mitzvah. This is a type of double doubt. At first glance I thought of another remedy that would work according to all opinions even if a non-Jew blackened them initially. Their entire objection to Magen Avraham is that this would be ink placed over ink. One could first paint over the black with red, green, white, or a similar color, and that color would nullify the black, as Gittin 19a says that red dye over ink constitutes erasure. He could then paint black over the new color for the sake of the mitzvah. Ink over red is considered writing according to all opinions. Although the Gemara there says, “Is it because we compare that we should act upon it?” this case is different, because it would again be a form of double doubt, since Sefer HaTerumah himself was not certain of the law, as explained above. However, the matter still requires study. Perhaps only writing ink over written red nullifies the lower writing, because black writing is superior according to all opinions. In the case of black paint over red paint, perhaps the lower color remains primary and one who does this on Shabbos would not be liable for coloring. In our case as well, the upper black color might not be significant enough to nullify fully the color that was previously beneath it, leaving only the name “black” upon it. This resembles the discussion there concerning red over ink. The matter requires study.
