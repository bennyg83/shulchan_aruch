The leather of the straps, etc., from kosher animals. See Mishnah Berurah. Peri Megadim writes in his introduction that he does not know whether this requirement is according to the Torah or D'Rabbanan. A practical difference concerns whether one may be lenient where it is uncertain whether the leather came from a kosher or nonkosher animal. He further writes that where there is a doubt whether the leather was processed for the sake of the mitzvah, it appears one may be lenient because that requirement is D'Rabbanan and a doubt concerning a D'Rabbanan matter is treated leniently. See there also regarding the leather covering of the tefillin of the arm. See Rambam, chapter 3 of Hilchos Tefillin, halachah 15, who treats the covering and the strap alike. See also above, siman 32, Taz, seif katan 27, and Peri Megadim there.
And it must be processed for its sake. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote that if the leather was not processed for the sake of the mitzvah, subsequently blackening it for the sake of the mitzvah does not help, unlike Be'er Eisek. I saw in Damesek Eliezer, seif katan 11, that he wishes to say that the Gra agrees with Be'er Eisek. In truth, it appears clear to me that this is not so. The Gra's intent is that since we see that the Torah requires the leather to be fully prepared, even to the extent of blackening it, then all the more so the essential preparation of the leather itself, namely its processing, must be done properly. This is not a formal a fortiori argument from a received law, but merely a clarification of the matter. This is exceedingly precise in the Gra's wording, for he then writes, “This is what Magen Avraham wrote: ‘It appears to me, etc.’” His intent is to answer Peri Megadim's question upon Magen Avraham. According to Damesek Eliezer's interpretation, Magen Avraham's wording “processing and blackening” would be very difficult, because blackening alone would suffice. I learned the reasoning I have written from Sefer HaTerumah, cited by Beit Yosef regarding blackening for the sake of the mitzvah. He writes that blackening is like the other finishing processes of the leather that take place after processing; see there. Know further that in my humble opinion, according to Rambam, who maintains that processing for the sake of the mitzvah is only D'Rabbanan, even if he also agrees with Sefer HaTerumah that blackening must be for the sake of the mitzvah, that requirement too would be only D'Rabbanan. Sefer HaTerumah's reason is that since the essential requirement of blackening is a halachah transmitted to Moshe at Sinai, the blackening must consequently be done for the sake of the mitzvah, as Beit Yosef explains. Yet the essential requirement of processing is likewise a halachah transmitted to Moshe at Sinai, as Peri Megadim explains at the beginning of siman 32, and nevertheless processing for the sake of the mitzvah is only D'Rabbanan according to Rambam, as Peri Megadim discusses at length there. This appears correct.
Straps made from either type of leather, etc., are valid. It requires study whether parchment processed for writing sacred texts may be used to make a strap. Peri Megadim, introduction to siman 32.
A halachah transmitted to Moshe at Sinai, etc. This requirement is indispensable even after the fact. Rambam, chapter 3, halachah 1. He had to explain this because some received laws are only requirements for the preferred fulfillment of a mitzvah, as in Shabbos 79b; see there.
The straps must be black. This applies both to the tefillin of the head and to those of the arm. Rambam there. I am uncertain whether according to the Torah it is sufficient for each strap to be black only for its minimum required length, while the remainder is merely for preferred fulfillment and beauty, or whether, since the entire strap is one connected object, all of it must be black. There is some proof for the latter from Menachos 35a. The Gemara cites the baraisa that permits straps of green and other colors and does not answer that the permission applies to the portion beyond the required length. Nor would the Gemara then have needed to ask whether the color was on the inner side. This proof can be rejected, because the Gemara may assume that the baraisa would not permit even D'Rabbanan coloring one side in two different colors, because it appears mottled. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 7. The matter requires study. I also do not know whether the received law of blackness applies to the segment of strap inserted through the ma'abarta. Initially there is no question that it should be blackened, because the bayis may turn over from that point, as mentioned in the Gemara. After the fact, however, perhaps it is comparable to the inner side. See Rambam, chapter 3 of Hilchos Tefillin, halachah 14. For the degree of blackness, see Baruch She'amar, who writes that the strap should be blackened once, then when it dries somewhat it should be blackened a second time, and then a third, until the leather is fully black like a raven. It appears to me that this is only for preferred fulfillment. According to strict law, once the color is properly called black, it suffices. Accordingly, even if its appearance resembles dark blue, which is called “blue” in the vernacular, it is valid. A proof is from Niddah 19a concerning the colors of blood. If it is black like ink it is impure, while if it is lighter, even black like blue, it is pure, as Rashi explains. This shows that dark blue is included within black. The same appears in Chullin 47b concerning a lung, and Rambam, chapter 7 of Hilchos Shechitah, halachah 19, who says “black like blue.” Thus a blue appearance is included in the category of black. Nevertheless, initially there is certainly a mitzvah to blacken it fully, as Baruch She'amar writes. See what I wrote above in siman 32, seif 3, concerning the blackness of ink, where actual black is required. Know that what we wrote there resolves a question I saw raised by a great authority: why did Rambam not formulate the received law simply as requiring writing with something black? According to our explanation, this is clear. A general statement of “black” would have included a color merely dark blue. Examine this carefully.
On the outside. See Mishnah Berurah. This is demonstrated by Rashi on Menachos 35b, s.v. “and their beauty.” If the requirement were merely to color black whatever side one intends to wear outward, and this were the Gemara's meaning when it answers “here, on the outside,” how did Rashi know that the smooth side is specifically called the beautiful side? Rashi should have explained simply that whichever side is smooth and attractive should be worn outward, and consequently that side must be colored black because of the received law. Tur likewise states the rule as I explained. I saw that Peri Megadim writes that if a non-Jew blackened the strap and no other strap is available, one may blacken the reverse side. Although we have a received tradition that the straps must be black, it need not specifically be the hair side. His intent is that the strap should be reversed and the newly blackened side worn outward. Derech HaChayim explains his words this way. According to this, the Gemara's answer, “here, on the outside,” depends only upon which side is worn outward. The Gemara's phrase “their beauty should face outward” means that black and smooth facing outward is only the preferred form of the mitzvah. He cites Rashi, s.v. “and their beauty,” as proof. In truth, Rashi appears at first glance to imply the opposite. Even if our inference is rejected, there is certainly no proof from Rashi for Peri Megadim's view. Rambam, chapter 3 of Hilchos Tefillin, halachah 14, does imply somewhat like Peri Megadim. Nevertheless, initially he too certainly agrees with the preferred practice, as he himself writes. Know that according to Peri Megadim, a serious stringency may sometimes result. If at the place where the knot encircles the head the strap rests with its black side downward, he has not fulfilled the mitzvah and the blessing would be in vain. It appears that this matter is disputed. See above, siman 27, Eliyah Rabbah, who cites this in the name of Hagahos Mordechai. In my humble opinion Beit Yosef disagrees and maintains that the only violation is of “their beauty should face outward.” This is why even if it later turned over it is still forbidden only because of appearance, as Eliyah Rabbah explains there. Examine this carefully.
