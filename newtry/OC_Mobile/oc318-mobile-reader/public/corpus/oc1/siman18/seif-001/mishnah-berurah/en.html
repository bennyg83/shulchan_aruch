1) According to Rambam, etc. Nevertheless, one may go out into the public domain on Friday night wearing a tallit with its tzitzit. They are not considered a burden, since they are an adornment and accessory of the garment.
2) To be lenient. This applies specifically regarding the blessing. It is prohibited, however, to wear a night garment during the day without tzitzit, or a daytime garment at night, because of the uncertainty. Nevertheless, it appears that a prayer leader who wears a tallit at night need not inspect the tzitzit, for in such a case we may certainly rely upon their previously established valid status.
3) Also for the day. This means that it is designated for the day as well, even if it is also designated for the night; all the more so if it is designated for the day alone.
4) The evening prayer. On a public fast, when people wear a tallit for Minchah, one should remove the tallit upon reaching Barechu. Since nowadays the tallit is worn only for the mitzvah of tzitzit, leaving it on would make it appear that one holds that nighttime is a time for the mitzvah of tzitzit. If the prayer leader standing at the lectern is not wearing an outer garment, however, so that removing the tallit would be disrespectful to the congregation, it is obvious that he should not remove it.
5) And on Yom Kippur night. Anyone who leads the prayers must wrap himself in a tallit. Lechem Chamudot writes that even one who recites Mourner's Kaddish at the lectern should wrap himself in a tallit out of respect for the congregation, but he does not recite a blessing over it.
6) He should wrap himself. This is in order to resemble the angels, who are wrapped in white. They should therefore not remove the tallit at night until after Maariv on Motzei Yom Kippur.
7) While it is still daytime. If he delayed wrapping himself until night, he should no longer recite a blessing, for perhaps the halakhah follows Rambam. Magen Avraham's words in seif katan 4 imply that even during bein hashmashot one may recite the blessing until the stars emerge. His reasoning is that the Torah does not use the word 'day' regarding tzitzit, but says, 'You shall see it,' implying any time when we are able to see. Baal Derech HaChaim rules likewise. I saw, however, that Mateh Ephraim is stringent about this. If one is uncertain whether bein hashmashot has already arrived, it appears that he may certainly recite the blessing. Apart from this consideration, Tosafot and the Rosh maintain that a daytime garment is obligated in tzitzit at night, and the Gra in his commentary likewise inclines toward that approach; see there.
