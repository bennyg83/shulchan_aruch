Seif katan 5. The Hagahot Mordechai there imply that the reason is, etc. Meaning, designating a place is legally effective because until then the non-Jew has not yet acquired the article. The reference should read: see what is written in siman 246, seif 3, that we rule accordingly. Although there, with respect to a courtyard, a rented place is properly called the renter's courtyard, that was said only to reconcile the view cited in Choshen Mishpat siman 260. The Hagahot Ashri there refer to siman 313 and to the Shach, who in fact argues that even for purposes of acquisition a rented place is not considered the renter's courtyard, following the Rambam. Although the Shach explains the rule that one rents the place of an article either because another person's intent transfers ownership or because the article was placed there with the renter's knowledge, those explanations may apply only to a Jew. A non-Jew does not acquire through the law of courtyard, as I wrote concerning the Magen Avraham in siman 246, seif katan 10. Meshichah or actual possession in his own courtyard is required. Since rental does not confer ownership of the premises, the place is not considered the non-Jew's house and he does not acquire the article until he removes it to his house or to an alleyway. The proof from siman 442 is similar: mere designation does not make the place the non-Jew's house. This nevertheless requires study. Why should lifting not effect acquisition for a non-Jew? Lifting is equivalent to meshichah, as the Sema writes in siman 490, seif katan 3, since anything that comes into a person's hand is treated as though it were in his house.
