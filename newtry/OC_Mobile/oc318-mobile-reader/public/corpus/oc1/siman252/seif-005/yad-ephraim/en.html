Seif katan 14. It is prohibited even for the needs of Shabbat, etc. Although the Beit Yosef says this concerning an article completed on Yom Tov, prohibiting even sending it to the owner's house for the needs of the festival, whereas here the article was completed before Shabbat and the Rama nevertheless prohibits taking it from the craftsman's house while permitting it if the craftsman sends it, the conclusion remains that it is prohibited even for Shabbat needs. The Beit Yosef interprets the Mordechai as discussing a festival need. The Rama, however, interprets the Mordechai to prohibit taking utensils from a craftsman's house even when they were completed before Shabbat. It follows that according to the Rama it is prohibited even for Shabbat needs. The Magen Avraham then writes that perhaps it is prohibited even through a non-Jew. The reason is that if we permit him to bring it, we must fear that he will give work to the craftsman on Shabbat or Yom Tov to repair. This is unlike the Levush, who bases the prohibition on appearance and would therefore permit bringing it through a non-Jew. That is incorrect. The reference to the Beit Yosef and Mordechai means that the Beit Yosef writes that we do not fear appearance, because people will assume it was completed yesterday. One might reject this by saying that the Beit Yosef writes so only according to the Ran. Yet the same conclusion follows from the distinction between the non-Jew sending it to the Jew's house, which is done through a non-Jew, and the prohibition against taking it from the craftsman's house, even through a non-Jew. The reason is therefore as stated above. This explains the Magen Avraham's ruling at the end of siman 515 that where the non-Jew is a shopkeeper rather than a craftsman, it may be permitted, since there is no concern that the Jew will commission forbidden labor. Although the Nimukei Yosef in siman 534 says that people may think the Jew gave it for repair during the festival, there the prohibition applies only when the article is not needed for the festival. We do not fear that permitting collection will cause him to commission work during the festival, because if there is no need he is unlikely to do so, and if there is a genuine festival need the work may be permitted. On Shabbat and Yom Tov, however, permitting collection from the craftsman creates a concern that when he needs the article he will give it for repair on Shabbat, and that remains forbidden even for Shabbat needs.
