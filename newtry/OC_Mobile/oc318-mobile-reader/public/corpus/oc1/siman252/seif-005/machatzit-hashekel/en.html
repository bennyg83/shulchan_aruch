Seif katan 5: To be stringent, etc. The non-Jew has not yet acquired it. The issue depends on the dispute among the authorities whether a non-Jew acquires through meshichah or through payment. The Mordechai may hold that a non-Jew acquires specifically through meshichah and not through money. Since the article has not yet been removed from the Jew's premises, even a Jew would not acquire it, because meshichah performed in the seller's domain does not effect acquisition, as explained in Choshen Mishpat. If, however, a place was designated for the non-Jew, that place is treated as his courtyard and his courtyard acquires for him. I nevertheless do not understand this, since acquisition by a courtyard is derived from agency, and we rule that there is no agency for a non-Jew. I have already discussed this in the laws of Pesach, siman 448; see there.
And there is no concern of carrying out. Meaning, there is no concern that people will suspect the Jew of having instructed the non-Jew to carry it out.
For we rule that if a poor person standing in the public domain extends his hand inside on Shabbat while holding a utensil and places it into the hand of the householder standing in the private domain, the poor person is liable, because he performed both the uprooting in the public domain and the placement in the private domain. The householder, however, is exempt and, with respect to the labor of carrying, it is permitted even initially, as taught at the beginning of tractate Shabbat. This is so despite the appearance that the householder may have instructed the poor person to bring it in. Evidently we do not ordinarily entertain such a suspicion.
This applies only to chametz. The Mordechai writes there that with regard to chametz Scripture specially decreed that rental effects acquisition, as stated in the first chapter of Pesachim, 6a, from the verse 'it shall not be found.' See there, and see what I wrote in siman 246. This is the correct text.
And also chametz, etc. Although we rule concerning chametz that rental effects acquisition, and according to Rabbeinu Tam a Jew who accepts responsibility for a non-Jew's chametz does not violate bal yera'eh when the chametz remains in the non-Jew's house, the same being true of a genuine rental, we do not follow Rabbeinu Tam's further view that chametz belonging to a non-Jew may remain in a Jew's house merely because a place was designated for it even when the Jew accepted responsibility. Only an actual rental suffices; see the Magen Avraham at the beginning of siman 440. The ruling in siman 347 likewise rejects the Mordechai's proof that there is no concern of appearance that people will say the Jew instructed the non-Jew to carry it out. The Mordechai relied on the Mishnah's ruling that when the poor person extends his hand inside, the householder is exempt and permitted. The Magen Avraham rejects this independently: the Rosh asks why the householder should be entirely permitted, since there is a prohibition of placing a stumbling block before the blind. Even where that prohibition does not technically apply, it is no less serious than a minor eating forbidden food, whom beit din must restrain. The Rosh therefore explains that the householder did commit a prohibition; 'exempt and permitted' means only that he committed no prohibited act of carrying. The same response defeats the Mordechai's proof. We may indeed be concerned that observers will say the Jew instructed the non-Jew, and in the case of the poor person the householder may likewise violate a prohibition because of appearance, although he is permitted with respect to the intrinsic labor of transferring domains.
Accordingly, one cannot rely on the lenient position. The Mordechai's phrase 'one should be stringent' implies that the matter is fundamentally permitted and leaves room for leniency. According to the Magen Avraham, however, there is an actual legal prohibition, and leniency is available only in a walled city where the non-Jew lives within the city, as the Magen Avraham states in seif katan 4.
