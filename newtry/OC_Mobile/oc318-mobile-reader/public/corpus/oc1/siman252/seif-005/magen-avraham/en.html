And one loads. In this case even Beit Shammai concedes, as Tosafot write, s.v. “Let the master say,” because it proceeds automatically to a greater degree than all the cases listed in the mishnah. The Olat Shabbat was not precise.
And the winepress while it is still day. The reason he repeated “while it is still day,” although this appears obvious, is to teach that it must be loaded sufficiently early in the day for the grapes to be crushed by the beam before Shabbos. Consequently, even if one later squeezes them by hand, there is no liability to bring a sin-offering, and the liquids that flow from them are permitted. In truth, one may load it close to nightfall, but the liquids that flow from them are prohibited, because we decree lest one squeeze them.
Unripe grapes and ears of grain. We must say that these are more commonly intended for their liquid than other fruits, for we rule that one may initially squeeze other fruits, as stated in siman 320 (Tosafot). This requires study, because even if one squeezes them there is no liability to bring a sin-offering; by Torah law one is liable only for pressing olives and grapes, as stated in Shabbos 145a and ruled by Rambam in chapter 8. Accordingly, the liquids that flow from them should be permitted even if they were not crushed beforehand, as Tosafot themselves write subsequently that wherever there is no liability to bring a sin-offering, the liquid is permitted. A further difficulty is why the Tur omitted garlic, which is mentioned in the Gemara, and listed unripe grapes and ears of grain; in his abridgment as well he did not list garlic. This requires study. It appears to me that he understood the Gemara's reference to garlic to mean that if it was not crushed before Shabbos, one may not finish crushing it by hand on Shabbos, as stated at the end of siman 321. The liquid that flows from it, however, is permitted even when it was not crushed, as explained above. Unripe grapes are grapes, and ears of grain are stalks. It is known that squeezing is a derivative of threshing; separating kernels from stalks therefore also entails a Torah prohibition. Consequently, if they were not crushed beforehand, the liquid that flows from them is prohibited. This appears clearly correct.
Because it produces sound. This degrades the dignity of Shabbos. If the mill belongs to a non-Jew, it is obviously permitted to place grain into it before Shabbos. If a Jew gives grain to a non-Jew before Shabbos, it is permitted even if the non-Jew grinds it on Shabbos, as stated in seif 2, provided the Jew does not stand beside him, for then he appears to be his agent, as stated at the end of siman 307. If circumstances require it, such as shortly before Pesach, the Jew may guard the mill so that the grain not become leavened, because this is a rabbinic prohibition compounded with another rabbinic prohibition. Even if the Jew places the grain into the mill on Shabbos, he is not liable to bring a sin-offering unless he grinds with a hand mill. This is also implied by Tosafot on Shabbos 18a, s.v. “Let the master say,” who explain Beit Shammai's reason as a decree. One might ask why the Gemara does not likewise explain the mill by way of a decree. It must be that even if he placed it there on Shabbos, he would not be liable to bring a sin-offering. Semag and Semak state this explicitly. See, however, Tosafot at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah. Moreover, from what is written in siman 316 regarding a trap in the name of Tosafot, it appears that one is liable to bring a sin-offering for an action that proceeds automatically, and Tosafot in Menachot 56b likewise rule so, comparable to attaching bread in an oven and its subsequently baking. This is also implied in chapter 8 of Rambam. Accordingly, instructing a non-Jew is a rabbinic prohibition compounded with another rabbinic prohibition, which is permitted for the sake of a mitzvah, as stated in siman 307, seif 5. All the more so is it permitted where he gives no instruction at all and merely sits and guards it. See the end of siman 307.
