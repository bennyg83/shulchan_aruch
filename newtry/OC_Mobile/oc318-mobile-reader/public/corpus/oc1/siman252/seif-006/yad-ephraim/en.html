Seif katan 15. All agree that one may not bring, etc. The Beit Yosef understands the rule 'one may not bring' as referring to an article made during Chol HaMoed or Yom Tov. According to the Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri, it is prohibited on Shabbat and Yom Tov even when needed, for they understand that this is the case addressed by 'one may not bring'; on Chol HaMoed, however, it is permitted for the festival's needs. According to the Ran, even on Shabbat and Yom Tov it is permitted when needed, while without need it is prohibited because of exertion. When needed, one may wear it even if it was completed on Shabbat, as the Mechaber rules here. The Beit Yosef challenges the Hagahot Ashri: in any event, should it not be forbidden because 'one may not bring'? He was unwilling to answer that Rabbenu Yom Tov and Rabbenu Eliyahu follow the Ran and therefore distinguish shoes. The Magen Avraham objects that the Beit Yosef appears to understand the Ran and Hagahot Ashri as disputing the rule 'one may not bring.' This is not so. The Ran certainly agrees that one may not bring; their dispute concerns the law after it was brought, corresponding to the two views recorded by the Mechaber and Rama. The Beit Yosef assumed that 'one may not bring' also prohibits benefit after the fact and therefore had to explain the Ran's case as one of necessity. The Magen Avraham rejects this: although bringing is prohibited, if it was brought the Jew may use it. The same applies where the craftsman sent it to his house; see the Bach. Accordingly, the rule 'one may not bring' raises no difficulty here, because this seif addresses the after-the-fact case. Even the Hagahot Ashri agree that where the non-Jew made the item for sale, if he brought it, it is permitted, and if it was made on Shabbat in a city whose majority is non-Jewish it is permitted. This is the correct text.
