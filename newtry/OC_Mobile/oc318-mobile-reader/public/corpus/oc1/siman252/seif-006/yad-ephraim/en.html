Seif katan 15: "All agree that one may not bring," etc. Meaning, the Beit Yosef understands that the ruling "one may not bring" speaks where it was made on Chol HaMoed or Yom Tov. According to the Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri it is forbidden on Shabbat and Yom Tov even for a need, for they understand that this is the case of "one may not bring"; only on Chol HaMoed, for the need of the festival, is it permitted. According to the Ran, even on Shabbat and Yom Tov it is permitted for a need, but not for a need it is forbidden because of exertion, and for a need it is permitted to wear even if it was completed on Shabbat, as the Mechaber wrote here. The Beit Yosef objected to Hagahot Ashri: nevertheless it should be forbidden because "one may not bring," etc. He was not satisfied to answer that R. Yom Tov and R. Eliyah hold like the Ran and therefore hold shoes are different. The Magen Avraham objected that it is implied from the Beit Yosef that there is a dispute between the Ran and Hagahot Ashri regarding "one may not bring," but this is not so: certainly the Ran too agrees one may not bring; only if it was brought do the Ran and Hagahot Ashri dispute, corresponding to the two opinions written by the Mechaber and Rama. The Beit Yosef thought that "one may not bring" means even after the fact it is forbidden to benefit from it, and therefore he had to distinguish according to the Ran that it speaks of a need. The Magen Avraham holds this is not so: although it is forbidden to bring, if it was brought it is permitted, etc. The same applies if it was sent to his house; see Bach. According to this there is no difficulty from "one may not bring," for here it speaks after the fact, where it was brought. Even Hagahot Ashri agrees in this case that if it was made for sale it is permitted, even if made on Shabbat in a city whose majority is non-Jewish, etc.; so it must read.
