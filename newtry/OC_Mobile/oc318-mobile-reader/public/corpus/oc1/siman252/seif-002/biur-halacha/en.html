To give his garments, etc. See the Mishnah Berurah, where we wrote that the same applies to all forms of labor. See the Beit Yosef, who concludes that there is no distinction between a non-Jew completing work on an existing utensil and his making the entire utensil on Shabbos, such as where the Jew gave him wood and he made a utensil, or yarn and he made a garment. Nor is there a distinction between the wood or yarn belonging to the Jew and the Jew asking him to make everything from his own materials; see there.
If he fixed a price, etc. See the Mishnah Berurah regarding what we wrote that nowadays garments given to a launderer are different. This is stated by the Magen Avraham and Eliyah Rabbah, but the Magen Avraham expressed himself very briefly and did not explain whether he meant that no stipulation at all was made, following the Hagahot Mordechai, because such work is not done gratis. If so, why did he make this dependent on present-day practice, and why did he conclude, “Must one fix the price with him every time?” If we explain that on the first occasion he fixed the price and the novelty is that this suffices thereafter, as the Tosefet Shabbos explains, it still does not explain why he made the ruling dependent on present-day practice. It therefore appears that he means that the Jew promised to pay him but did not specify how much. As to the Nishmat Adam’s objection that in siman 247 we rule that an express fixed price is unnecessary, it appears that there we permit an unspecified amount only where the Jew promised to pay the proper wage or to reach an agreed settlement with him. This is also evident from the responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 247. Therefore the non-Jew relies upon receiving full payment, and the case is treated as one in which a price was fixed. But if the Jew merely said that he would pay him, without more, this is not treated as a fixed-price arrangement, for the non-Jew does not know how much he will receive and perhaps will receive only a small amount. The Magen Avraham therefore wrote that nowadays, when the customary rate for laundering garments is known, even a general promise to pay suffices, for the non-Jew will not assume that he will receive only a token amount. It is then equivalent to having fixed the price once, upon which one may certainly rely thereafter. His reference to the Hagahot Mordechai may mean that with laundering, whose rate is universally known, even no express stipulation may suffice, because the non-Jew relies on receiving the established rate. This differs from tanning hides, for which there is no fixed known rate. Practical application nevertheless requires study. Although the Chayei Adam rules like the Hagahot Mordechai that any work universally known to be paid is treated as fixed-price work, it is very difficult to rely on this in practice. All the early authorities explicitly require a fixed price even for tanning hides and laundering garments, although such work is certainly not done without pay. The reason is as stated above: since the non-Jew does not know how much he will receive, he may expect only a small amount and therefore works on the Jew’s behalf, appearing as his agent. The Magen Avraham was lenient only with laundering in his time, because its rate was known.
Provided that he does not say, etc. This is prohibited even if he gives it to him several days beforehand, as stated in the Mishnah Berurah in siman 247.
It is prohibited on Friday. The Rama follows his position in siman 247:1, where he permits giving work without a fixed price on Thursday. Although there the Rama is stringent, when there is no need, in accordance with the Mechaber, here he is lenient for the reason explained there. Eliyah Rabbah and the other later authorities likewise imply that one may be lenient on Wednesday and Thursday.
If he sees him doing it, etc. See the Mishnah Berurah regarding the case where he stipulated payment. This ruling is straightforward according to all opinions, for it falls within fixed-price work. Some later authorities are even more lenient: whenever the non-Jew works for pay, although no payment arrangement was made at all, if the item was originally given in a permissible manner, such as several days before Shabbos or on Friday with enough time to finish before Shabbos, the Jew need not protest when he sees him working on Shabbos. Since he is in any event working for pay, we say that he is occupied with his own work.
For the benefit he will receive. If the non-Jew himself began by saying that he would do the Jew a favor, then according to the Rama above in siman 247, who treats this as a fixed-price arrangement, and as the Darchei Moshe appears to imply here, there is seemingly no reason to be stringent. According to the Mechaber, however, there is plainly no distinction. In his view, whenever the work is gratis it may initially be entrusted to the non-Jew because it is done in return for goodwill, yet if the Jew sees him working on Shabbos he must protest. The same therefore applies here. The authorities’ failure to distinguish likewise implies this, and Rabbi Akiva Eiger explicitly rules stringently.
