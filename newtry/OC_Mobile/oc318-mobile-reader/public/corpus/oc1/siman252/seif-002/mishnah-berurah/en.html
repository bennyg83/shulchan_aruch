(12) To give his garments, etc. - The same applies to other forms of labor. Since he fixed a price for the work and does not insist that it be done immediately, when the non-Jew works on Shabbos he acts for his own benefit, in order to finish the job sooner.
(13) Close to nightfall - The non-Jew must also remove them from the entrance of the Jew's house before nightfall [Rambam].
(14) If he fixed a price, etc. - This means a set sum rather than employment by the day. See siman 247, seif 2: if he stipulated that he would pay him and later settle on the amount, even though he did not specify how much, it is treated as a fixed-price arrangement. Nowadays, garments given to a launderer are ordinarily regarded as fixed-price work because the rate is known, and the accepted practice is therefore to give them to him even close to nightfall on Friday.
(15) In return for a favor - The non-Jew agreed to do the work without payment, and we assume that he does so in return for some earlier benefit that the Jew provided him. He therefore acts for his own benefit.
(16) To do it on Shabbos - Similarly, if the Jew tells the launderer, 'See, I need them immediately after Shabbos,' such as because he wishes to travel then, it is forbidden even when a price was fixed. Since the job cannot be completed unless he works on Shabbos, this is considered as though the Jew expressly instructed him to launder them on Shabbos, and the non-Jew becomes his agent [later authorities].
(17) In his own house - Meaning, not in the Jew's house, for there it appears that he works as the Jew's agent. The Chayei Adam writes that work in the Jew's house is forbidden even when it is the universal local practice to contract out that task and no one would suspect that the non-Jew is a day laborer, because people may still say that the Jew instructed him to work on Shabbos.
(18) If he did not fix a price - Meaning, he made no wage agreement with the non-Jew, although the non-Jew expects payment. It is forbidden, and we do not say that the non-Jew acts for his own benefit, because the Jew did not expressly promise him payment.
(19) On Friday - When the Jew gives it to him on Friday, it appears as though he gave it on condition that it be done on Shabbos. When a price was fixed, however, the non-Jew acts for his own benefit and it does not appear that the Jew instructed him to work then. The poskim write that when enough time remains to finish the work before nightfall, it is permitted even on Friday and even without a fixed price according to all opinions. The Rama specifically mentions Friday because on Wednesday or Thursday, when the delivery is well before Shabbos, it is permitted even if the work cannot be completed before Shabbos, provided the Jew did not instruct him to work on Shabbos.
(20) Some disagree, etc. - They maintain that this is treated like a case in which no price was fixed, so that the non-Jew acts on the Jew's behalf, and it is forbidden on Friday. See Darkei Moshe, who agrees with the dissenting opinion, and what I wrote above in siman 247, Mishnah Berurah seif katan 16.
(21) Without payment - Unless the non-Jew himself initiates and tells the Jew that he will do it free of charge. In that case he certainly intends repayment for a benefit previously received, and the law is like fixed-price work [above, siman 247, seif 4 in the gloss].
(22) If he sees him doing it, etc. - Although the Mechaber permitted giving the work to him before nightfall even in return for a favor, without concern for his working on Shabbos, even when the Jew knows he will do so, because he did not instruct him, nevertheless, if the Jew comes to his house and finds him working, it appears that he acts with the Jew's approval, since he receives no wage. The Jew must therefore tell him not to work. If, however, they originally agreed that he would receive payment, even without specifying a fixed amount, the Jew need not protest, because the non-Jew is occupied with his own paid work.
(23) Several days - Meaning, everyone agrees that it was permitted to give the work to the non-Jew in this case, as explained above; nevertheless, the Jew must protest when he sees him working. Know also that if the non-Jew is the Jew's weekday day laborer and now wishes to perform the Jew's work privately in his own house, the Jew must go and protest once he learns of it, even though the work is not being done in his presence. The Magen Avraham writes that if a Jew orders shoes from a non-Jew and sees him making them on Shabbos, he need not protest even if no price was fixed, because the Jew's name has not yet become attached to those shoes. The non-Jew could sell them to another and make different shoes for the Jew; thus he is not presently working on the Jew's property.
