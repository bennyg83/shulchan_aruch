Seif katan 2: Because of cooking, implying that if it is fully cooked it is permitted, as stated in siman 318. The Tosefet Shabbat objects that this is difficult, since in siman 318 the Mechaber explicitly permits stirring a pot only when its contents are fully cooked, whereas wool in a dye-vat remains forbidden even after the dye has fully cooked. The Rosh gives two explanations: dyes require prolonged cooking, or stirring further drives the color into the wool. The Tosefet Shabbat therefore understands the Tur and Mechaber to have adopted the first explanation and to prohibit stirring even after complete cooking, and he also questions why the Magen Avraham in siman 318 attributes the prohibition to dyeing when the Mechaber here states cooking. In my humble opinion, however, the Magen Avraham's words can be sustained. The Beit Yosef explains that the Tur, when ruling that food remains subject to cooking until fully cooked even after reaching the stage of ma'achal ben Derusai, did not rely on the Rosh's unsupported distinction. The Rosh had said that before ma'achal ben Derusai one who advances cooking is liable, but once it has reached that stage liability arises only if it cooled and was reheated. He then offered two strained answers to the Gemara's question concerning stirring a dye-vat: either dyes always require further cooking, or stirring constitutes dyeing. Since the Tur rejected those strained answers and ruled that ordinary food also remains subject to cooking until fully cooked, he evidently saw no essential distinction between food and dyes. Accordingly, the Mechaber, who follows the Tur in siman 318, may likewise hold that once the dye is fully cooked there is no further concern of cooking, and therefore the Magen Avraham correctly inferred here that the decree lest one stir applies only before complete cooking. When the Magen Avraham adds that according to Tosafot and the Rosh it nevertheless remains prohibited because of dyeing, he refers to their distinct approach, under which stirring may deepen the color even after cooking is complete. He chose the language of dyeing because Tosafot state that reason explicitly, whereas the cooking explanation appears only in the Rosh. The Mechaber's later ruling in siman 318, that one may not stir wool in the vat even after it is fully cooked, can still be reconciled. The Beit Yosef argues that with respect to cooking there is no middle category: if the act constitutes cooking, there is Torah liability; if it does not, why should it be prohibited? That reasoning does not necessarily apply to dyeing. Once the wool is fully dyed, further stirring may no longer incur a sin-offering for dyeing, yet it can remain rabbinically prohibited because it improves and repairs the wool. This resembles the Rambam's ruling, cited by the Magen Avraham in siman 340, that writing ink over red pigment is liable, while passing ink over existing ink is exempt but prohibited, because it brightens and improves the letters. Likewise, the Magen Avraham explains in siman 328 that placing a cloth over a bleeding wound is prohibited even though the resulting color is destructive; an already red cloth is certainly prohibited because the blood intensifies its color and thereby repairs it. Thus all the rulings are consistent. Here, where the vessel is not sealed and the only concern is a decree lest one stir, the case must concern dye not yet fully cooked. Once it is fully cooked there is no Torah prohibition of cooking, and any additional improvement of the color is only rabbinic; prohibiting the unsealed vessel on account of a possible rabbinic act would be a decree upon a decree. In siman 318, however, actually stirring the wool remains rabbinically prohibited even after complete cooking because it improves the dyeing. This also explains why the Magen Avraham there gives the reason of dyeing rather than cooking.
