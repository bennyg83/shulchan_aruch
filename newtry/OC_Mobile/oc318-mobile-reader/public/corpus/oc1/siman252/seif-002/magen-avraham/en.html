If he fixed a price. It appears to me that nowadays, when utensils or garments are given to a launderer, it is ordinarily regarded as a fixed-price arrangement, because the rate is known. Is he expected to negotiate a price with him each time? The widespread custom is therefore to give them to him on Friday close to nightfall. See Beit Yosef in the name of the Hagahot Mordechai.
For the sake of goodwill. If, however, he expects payment and no price was stipulated with him, it is prohibited, because he acts for the Jew, as stated in siman 247.
It is prohibited on Friday. This applies unless there is enough time to complete it while it is still day. This implies that on Wednesday or Thursday it is permitted even when the work cannot be completed before Shabbos. See siman 247, where the Beit Yosef holds that it is prohibited even on Wednesday and Thursday. Although the Rama there is inclined to be stringent when it is not needed, that ruling concerns sending a letter: when the Jew dispatches the messenger, it is as though he instructed him to travel every day, even on Shabbos. Here, however, the non-Jew receives articles into his own house to perform contracted work, and the Jew is indifferent as to when he performs it. Nevertheless, if it can be completed before Shabbos, all agree that it is permitted, even according to Beit Shammai. This excludes a mission, since the messenger may not find the person at home and may have to follow him.
If he was doing it, etc. If he performs the work for payment, even when no price was fixed, the Jew need not protest (Beit Yosef and Bach). This requires study, for paid work without a fixed price is worse than gratuitous work, since one may not initially give it to him. It therefore appears to me that the Rokeach is correct: only where a price was fixed is there no need to protest. The Yerushalmi likewise concerns a fixed-price arrangement, for without a fixed price it is prohibited to give it to him at all, and all the more so must one protest. See what I wrote at the end of siman 276, seif 4, and in siman 334, seif 25. This applies specifically where one gives garments to a launderer. If, however, he ordered a non-Jew to make shoes for him and sees him working on Shabbos, he need not protest, because the Jew's name has not yet become attached to those shoes; if the non-Jew wishes, he may give those shoes to someone else and make another pair for the Jew. The Hagahot Mordechai, chapter one, states: We say that the non-Jew acts on his own behalf only when he performs labor with his own property. To kindle a fire with a Jew's wood, however, he must obtain permission from the owner of the wood. If the Jew sees and remains silent, the non-Jew acts on his behalf, and the Jew must protest.
