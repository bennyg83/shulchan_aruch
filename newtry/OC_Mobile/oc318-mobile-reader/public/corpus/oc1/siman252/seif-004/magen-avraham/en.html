To wear the article on Shabbos. This is unlike lighting a lamp at the beginning of siman 276, because there the non-Jew acts for the Jew (Ran and Shiltei HaGibborim), meaning that he lights it on Shabbos so that the Jew may use it immediately. Here, by contrast, he acts merely to complete his contracted work.
For the time required to perform it. This is so that the Jew not benefit from labor performed on Shabbos (Bach). Accordingly, on the two festival days observed in the Diaspora, if it was made on the first day, it is permitted on the second after the time required to perform it has elapsed. There is no distinction between the person for whom it was made and others. See what I wrote in siman 515.
If there is reason to attribute it, etc. This is because some authorities permit it in every circumstance. It appears to me that if the shoe was already completed before Shabbos and the non-Jew merely repaired and polished it on Shabbos, it is permitted, since it could have been worn even without that work, as stated in siman 253, seif 1, gloss 4.
To take articles. Even when needed for Shabbos, it is prohibited (Beit Yosef). It is possible that it is prohibited even to have a non-Jew bring it, as appears to me, contrary to the Olat Shabbat, who wrote that it is prohibited only because of appearance. That is incorrect; see Beit Yosef and Mordechai.
Who makes shoes. The Beit Yosef writes: The Hagahot Ashri, chapter three of Beitzah, states that shoes made by a non-Jew on a festival are not inherently muktzeh. A Jew came and took them from the shop. Rabbeinu Yom Tov prohibited them because of muktzeh, while Rabbeinu Eliyahu permitted them, since their completion depends upon human action, they could be completed on the festival, and they were made generally for sale to anyone. This requires study, for they should be prohibited because one may not bring articles from a craftsman's house, as stated below. Perhaps shoes are different, because people do not ordinarily give the craftsman materials to fashion them; rather, he makes them from his own materials and sells them. Therefore, even if he made them on the festival, it is of no concern, because he labored for himself. Based on this, the Rama wrote this gloss. Yet anyone who examines the Beit Yosef will see that his words are surprising. All agree that one may not bring articles from a craftsman's house, as discussed in siman 534. If the non-Jew brought them, according to the Ran one may benefit from them, whereas according to the Hagahot Ashri it is prohibited because he made them for a Jew. If, however, a non-Jew makes goods for general sale and brings them, one may benefit from them, since he did not make them for the Jew. If he made them on Shabbos in a city whose population is mostly Jewish, they are prohibited, as stated in siman 515, seif 6. Initially, however, it is prohibited to go and take them from him. Perhaps only from a non-Jewish shopkeeper who is not a craftsman at all would it be permitted. This appears correct. See Bach.
