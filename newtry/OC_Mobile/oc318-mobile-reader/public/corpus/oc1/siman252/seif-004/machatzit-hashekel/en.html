Seif katan 4: One should be stringent, for in any event an observer assumes, etc. The Magen Avraham gives this reason according to the Rama, but the Mordechai himself gives another reason for stringency, as stated in seif katan 5. The leniency for lending applies when there is a need, the city is walled, and the non-Jew lives within the city, so no one can say that the Jew instructed him to carry into a prohibited domain, since no prohibition of carrying applies there. Although one might still suspect that people will say the Jew sold it to him on Shabbat, as the Rambam explains, in a case of need we rely on the Beit Yosef, who did not entertain that suspicion and was concerned only that people might think the Jew instructed him to carry it out. According to the Beit Yosef, therefore, it is permitted in a walled city. A sale, however, should be treated stringently even in a case of need, because perhaps even the Beit Yosef agrees that people may say the Jew sold it on Shabbat. With a loan, observers suspect only what is in fact true, namely that the article is on loan. With a sale, even if people initially assume it was lent, when they later investigate and learn that it was sold they may conclude that their original assumption was mistaken and suspect that the sale itself occurred on Shabbat.
See siman 244, Magen Avraham seif katan 5, where he writes, 'Perhaps in the case of a violent non-Jew, etc.' The Taz is even more lenient, permitting it in the case of a violent non-Jew even when no place was designated for him.
