And one who stirs. See Taz in siman 318, seif katan 22 and 23. Either the wool requires extensive cooking, which appears to be the Mechaber's view here and will be explained, with God's help, below regarding Magen Avraham, seif katan 2, or the prohibition is dyeing. See the Rosh, first chapter of Shabbos 18b, who cites Rashi's explanation that one who stirs is cooking, and in chapter Kirah, 40b, siman 11, cites two explanations: stirring constitutes cooking, for although there is generally no cooking after cooking, dye materials always require further cooking; alternatively, stirring constitutes dyeing, because by stirring he causes the dyes to enter the wool. This is what the Taz means in siman 318, seif katan 22, when he says that wool requires much cooking. More precisely, it is the dye materials that require much cooking, and with them there is always cooking after cooking. A practical difference is one who stirs a vat of dyes that contains no wool; this will be explained, with God's help, in siman 318 and here regarding Magen Avraham, seif katan 2. The author says: because people act hastily, I saw fit to write here several particulars on this subject. Rashi on Shabbos 18b, s.v. "stirs," writes, "and one who cooks," meaning that according to the Rosh one is liable for cooking. The Bach, however, infers from the word "and in a cooked dish" that in ordinary food stirring constitutes cooking, while here the concern is dyeing, as Tosafos write, s.v. "lest," and as Maharshal explains. The phrase can also mean that so long as the first vessel is at yad soledes bo, stirring causes cooking, but in a second vessel or where the temperature is below yad soledes bo even stirring cannot cause cooking, in accordance with the Rosh. According to the Bach, it appears that wherever there is no cooking there is likewise no dyeing; below, however, I will explain, with God's help, that even in a second vessel and the like stirring may cause the dye to enter the wool, as I wrote. See Yoreh De'ah, siman 69, Shach seif katan 37 and 38, that vinegar and brine may cook even in a second vessel. Accordingly, those who steep meat, onions, and sharp vinegar and pour them into a second vessel may not stir so long as it is yad soledes bo if the food is not fully cooked. If it is fully cooked, it is permitted, as stated in siman 318, seif 18; where it is not yad soledes bo, it is proper to be lenient there and here. According to the Rama in siman 318, the same applies in this manner even when fully cooked in a second vessel.
Rambam, chapter 3, halacha 11 of Hilchos Shabbos, writes: it is forbidden to insert a ladle into a pot while it is on the fire, because by doing so one stirs it, and stirring is among the requirements of cooking; he is therefore considered as cooking on Shabbos. In chapter 9 he likewise writes that one who stirs cooks; and in chapter 21, halacha 13, he permits finishing the crushing of groats after the pot has been removed from the fire. See Maggid Mishneh, Kesef Mishneh, and Lechem Mishneh to chapter 3. After carefully considering their words, the Lechem Mishneh understood the Maggid Mishneh to mean that merely inserting the ladle does not itself constitute stirring; rather, it was prohibited lest one stir and turn the contents. Therefore, when the food is fully cooked, although placing it on the fire entails a rabbinic prohibition, as will be explained below with God's help, merely inserting the ladle is permitted; actual stirring, however, is rabbinically prohibited. If the food is not fully cooked, stirring incurs a sin-offering; therefore even inserting the ladle is prohibited lest he stir. Rambam wrote this law here rather than in chapter 9, where the melacha of cooking is discussed, because he learned it from the case of wool placed into the dye vat, where the concern is lest one stir, as Maggid Mishneh writes. Although there the concern is lest stirring constitute dyeing, nevertheless the cases are analogous: just as there we prohibit because he might stir, so too here, because it is the normal manner to turn the ladle, removing or inserting it is prohibited. All of this, however, applies while the pot is on the fire. If it has been removed, even if it is a first vessel and yad soledes bo, stirring does not cause cooking according to this understanding; therefore inserting a ladle is permitted. This is why Tosafos on Shabbos 18b, s.v. "lest," explain the concern as dyeing rather than cooking: the Baraisa in Beitzah need not be limited to a pot on the fire, and in a removed pot stirring does not cause cooking even in a first vessel. This also explains the ruling in chapter 21 concerning groats: once the pot is removed from the fire, one may turn them, although stirring a fully cooked food on the fire is at least rabbinically prohibited according to Rambam, because in a removed pot stirring has no effect at all. Kesef Mishneh, however, understood that everyone agrees that even in a removed first vessel that is yad soledes bo, stirring can cause cooking. When Rambam writes "on the fire," that is not essential. Tosafos explained the concern as dyeing rather than cooking because they were troubled by the question: presumably the dyes were already cooked, as is the way of dyers, and only afterward is the wool placed into the vat; Beis Hillel permits doing so before Shabbos even though the color has not yet been absorbed. If so, there is no cooking after cooking in the dyes. Therefore Tosafos wrote that the prohibition is dyeing, corresponding to the Rosh's second explanation in chapter Kirah, daf 40b, siman 11. Rambam also writes that the Ran learned the rule from the principle that stirring cooks; this is what Ramach, cited by Kesef Mishneh, writes. The difficulty is then the ruling in chapter 21, halacha 13, permitting one to finish crushing the groats after removing the pot from the fire. If actual stirring of fully cooked food is rabbinically prohibited, why is it permitted after removal? It is true that in chapter 9, halacha 3, Rambam writes that one who cooks on the fire something already fully cooked is exempt, implying that it remains forbidden; distinctions concerning whether it cooled are explained elsewhere. Still, Kesef Mishneh's simple reading is difficult. With God's help, the Mechaber's ruling will be explained in siman 253 regarding Magen Avraham, seif katan 22 and 32, and in siman 318, seif 4 and 15.
I will add one more point. In the glosses to chapter 3, seif katan 6, he distinguishes concerning Sefer HaTerumah's view of something fully cooked. Some explain that Rabbeinu Chananel is discussing fully cooked food and that inserting the ladle itself constitutes actual stirring, which is prohibited while the pot is on the fire even when fully cooked, either by Torah law because stirring produces further cooking although there is ordinarily no cooking after cooking, or at least rabbinically. On this reading, Rambam's phrase "it is considered as cooking" is precise as a comparison. See Magen Avraham, siman 318, seif katan 42. The practical ruling will be explained there, with God's help.
It remains for us to clarify that there are three possible reasons for the prohibition of stirring a dye vat that has been removed from the fire. First, cooking, as Rashi explains and as the Rosh writes in chapter Kirah 40b, siman 11, and as the Bach explains in siman 318: dye materials always require cooking. According to this, the prohibition applies specifically in a first vessel that is yad soledes bo. Second, the Rosh's second explanation, dyeing: when one stirs, the dyes enter the wool. Each explanation apparently has a leniency and a stringency. According to the first, there is a leniency in a second vessel or where the temperature is not yad soledes bo; the stringency is that even if the wool has already taken the color, stirring remains forbidden because the dyes always require further cooking. According to the second, there is a leniency when the color was already absorbed before Shabbos, for there is no further dyeing; but there may be a stringency that even in a second vessel stirring can cause dyeing. I learned a third reason from the Ran on Shabbos: it is the normal practice of dyers to stir so that the wool not become scorched. This is also implied by the Yerushalmi, and appears to be part of the melacha of dyeing, because this is itself a normal act of the dyeing process. Accordingly, even where the color was already absorbed, stirring is prohibited; but in a second vessel, where there is no concern of scorching, it may be permitted even before the color was absorbed. Thus each explanation has its own leniency and stringency. This will be explained, with God's help, regarding Magen Avraham, seif katan 2.
From where do the Sages know that even in a first vessel removed from the fire, stirring has an effect? This appears from the Ran and from the concern "lest he stir it," even where the vessel is removed. The fact that a first vessel cooks after removal is learned from the Mishnah concerning a saucepan or pot that one removed boiling on Shabbos. According to the explanation that the concern is dyeing, however, there is no proof. One can answer that it is logical even without a separate source: a first vessel cooks even after being removed, and stirring itself is an act of cooking, so why should removal make a difference? Yet the Ran is difficult, because he first writes "from here we learn" and afterward gives the reason of the melacha of dyeing; if so, there is no proof. The Yerushalmi also requires study. The Gaon who comments on the Yerushalmi already addressed what the Yerushalmi derives there; see there. The Yerushalmi itself is likewise difficult for me.
