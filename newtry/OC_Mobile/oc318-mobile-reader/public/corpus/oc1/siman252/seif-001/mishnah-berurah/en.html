(1) To soak ink, etc. - On Shabbos this is forbidden because of kneading, since he mixes the ink and water together; some say it is forbidden because of dyeing. Nevertheless, since he begins at a permitted time on Friday and the process is completed of itself on Shabbos, it is permitted. The same applies to all the cases listed here: although performing them on Shabbos would involve a Torah prohibition, beginning them on Friday is permitted.
(2) Flax in an oven - Even when its cover is not sealed with clay, we do not fear that he may uncover it and stir the coals, because exposure to air harms the flax and he therefore will not uncover it.
(3) Wool into the dye-vat - The wool absorbs the dye.
(4) It is forbidden lest he stir the coals - Even when sufficient time remains for the wool to absorb the color before nightfall, it is forbidden, lest he stir the coals so that it will absorb the dye more thoroughly.
(5) Lest, etc., and one who stirs, etc. - Although the Mechaber equated stirring a dye-vat with stirring a cooking pot, they are not entirely alike. In a cooking pot, once the food is fully cooked there is no further prohibition of stirring, whereas stirring a dye-vat is forbidden in every circumstance, as explained below in siman 318, seif 18. Therefore, one may not place wool into a boiling dye-vat close to nightfall when it is not sealed, even if enough time remains for the wool to absorb the dye before nightfall, lest he stir it after dark.
(6) He is liable for cooking - Stirring a boiling pot hastens the cooking process.
(7) Traps for animals, etc. - If one sets them on Shabbos and an animal enters while he is spreading the trap, he is liable for trapping.
(8) Provided that he leaves, etc. - Meaning, if there is not enough time for the non-Jew to leave, or if the non-Jew stipulates that he will take the item from him on Shabbos, even the sale is forbidden.
(9) While it is still day - So that it should not appear that the Jew instructed him to carry it outside. There is also another reason: people should not suspect that the Jew sold it to him on Shabbos or gave it to him as security. For this reason it is forbidden even in a walled city or a place with an eruv. The later authorities likewise agree that, regarding both a sale and the rental of an object, one must certainly be stringent even in a walled city where the non-Jew resides, and require him to remove it from the Jew's house before nightfall.
(10) If he designated, etc. - Their reasoning is that it is regarded as though the object were already resting in the non-Jew's house.
(11) One should be stringent - So as not to accustom the non-Jew to this practice. The later authorities agree that, as a matter of law, one should not be lenient, for in siman 440 we rule even concerning chametz that designating a place for the non-Jew does not help. Moreover, an observer may think that the Jew instructed him to remove it. One should therefore be lenient only in a case of loss or great need. If the non-Jew is violent or coercive, it is permitted even without designating a place for him, because the Sages did not enact their decree in such a case. The Olat Tamid writes that if the Jew rents the room to the non-Jew for a perutah for the duration of Shabbos, there is no need for stringency; see there. The Magen Avraham and other later authorities, however, write that even rental of the room cannot be relied upon for leniency.
