[7] In expectation of a benefit, etc. If he performs the work for payment, then even where no price was fixed, one need not protest. Beit Yosef and Bach. This requires study, for paid work without a fixed price is worse than unpaid work, since one may not give it to him in the first place. One must therefore say that Rokeach and the Yerushalmi discuss a case where the price was fixed. These are the words of Magen Avraham. I find this surprising, for Beit Yosef too must necessarily be understood in this manner, as he writes: “This is what it means, etc., for payment and with a fixed price.” It is possible that this is also the view of Shulchan Aruch, which relies on its earlier statement that work done in expectation of a benefit has the status of a fixed-price arrangement. It also appears to me that Bach’s reference to payment without a fixed price means that one stipulated that he would pay him but did not specify the amount, as above in siman 247, seif 3, for this is no worse than unpaid work. But where there was no stipulation at all, one must protest. Examine carefully. This applies specifically where one gave his garments to a launderer. But if one instructed a non-Jew to make shoes for him and sees him making them on Shabbos, he need not protest, for the Jew’s ownership has not yet become attached to them, and the non-Jew could sell them if he wished.
