(1) A Jew and a non-Jew, etc. The fully permissible arrangement is where a Jew and non-Jew jointly own a business or undertaking and stipulate before entering the partnership: “You work on Shabbos and take whatever your labor produces, whether much or little, and I will work one weekday in exchange and take whatever my labor produces.” At the division, the non-Jew receives the Shabbos earnings and the Jew receives the weekday earnings, whether much or little. If they made no initial stipulation and only at the time of division the Jew says, “Take the amount corresponding to your Shabbos labor and I will take the weekday earnings,” this is certainly prohibited. If they made no initial stipulation but later divided the profits equally without specification, the Gemara leaves the question unresolved. Rambam rules stringently and the Rosh leniently. The Mechaber follows Rambam, while Rama relies on the Rosh in a case of substantial loss. If they stipulated before entering the partnership and later agreed to divide equally, this is permitted even according to Rambam, as stated in seif 2. These are the basic rules of the opening seifim; the details and reasons follow. The reason an initial stipulation is required regarding a field or oven is that otherwise, when the partnership was formed, the work rested upon both of them together. If the Jew later tells the non-Jew, “You alone work on Shabbos and take all its profits, while I alone will work one weekday and take that day's profits,” it is as though he actively appoints the non-Jew as his worker, saying, “Work for me on Shabbos and I will work for you during the week.” The non-Jew is then truly his agent. According to this, if the non-Jewish partner performs no labor himself, but other non-Jews come to bake in the jointly owned oven and pay a fee, the Jew may receive from his partner the amount due according to his ownership share even without an initial stipulation. He must not, however, take payment specifically for Shabbos; he should receive it without specification together with the earnings of other days, so it is not direct Shabbos wages. There is no concern of appearance that the work is being performed for the Jew, since it is known that the non-Jew has an ownership share.
(2) If they stipulated initially. Since they stipulated at the outset, the Jew was never obligated to work on Shabbos, and the non-Jew is not his agent.
(3) That the Shabbos earnings, etc. Regarding a field, the Jew must say: “Labor to plow and sow on Shabbos and take at harvest time the amount corresponding to your Shabbos labor, while I or my agent will labor on a weekday and take the amount corresponding to my day.”
(4) Whether little, etc. Since they do not calculate the two days against one another so as to divide equally, they are not partners on Shabbos and on that weekday, but only during the other days. This excludes a stipulation that they will ultimately calculate and divide both days' earnings equally, which is prohibited, for that shows that they remain partners on Shabbos as well.
(5) Or much. If at the time of division they calculate and say, “You received more and I less; let us divide the excess so that we receive equal amounts,” Magen Avraham rules like Raavad, who is stringent because it becomes evident retroactively that the original stipulation was merely an evasion. Others are lenient because they did stipulate initially. Eliyah Rabbah discusses this at length and remains unresolved.
(6) It is permitted. If the profit earned by each on his respective day is unknown, as in a store, they may initially divide equally.
(7) If they did not stipulate initially. The Mechaber prohibits without qualification where no initial stipulation was made, even if the Jew did not tell the non-Jew, “You work on Shabbos and I during the week,” and even if at the time of division he made no mention of Shabbos but simply wished to divide equally. It is nevertheless prohibited because they did not stipulate before becoming partners.
(8) And they divided without specification. At the time of division the Jew did not mention Shabbos by saying, “You take the amount corresponding to your Shabbos labor and I will take the amount corresponding to my weekday labor,” but they divided equally without specification. The case also assumes that the Jew did not initially tell the non-Jew, “You work on Shabbos and I will work a corresponding weekday,” but the non-Jew worked on his own initiative. Otherwise all authorities prohibit it, because the Jew thereby revealed that the non-Jew was his Shabbos agent and that he labored for him during the week, since the work originally rested upon both. This entire seif concerns a case where the Jew and non-Jew jointly own the oven or bathhouse. If the non-Jew owns no share in the oven itself and merely receives a portion of the profits for baking and firing it, some hold that even an initial stipulation, “Take your portion on Shabbos and I one weekday,” is ineffective. Since the oven itself belongs to the Jew, it resembles renting the oven for Shabbos in exchange for the non-Jew firing it on a weekday, and the Jew thereby receives direct Shabbos wages. Magen Avraham concludes that this stringent view should be taken into account. This applies only where the payment is not absorbed with other days. If the Jew tells the baker, “Take two or three days,” it is permitted when this was stipulated at the start of the partnership. Some later authorities hold that where the non-Jew has no ownership share, an initial stipulation is unnecessary so long as the payment is absorbed with other days.
(9) On his day. Since the Jew works every weekday, the non-Jew is considered his agent to work every Shabbos, and an initial stipulation is therefore required. The later authorities write that the same applies where a non-Jew gives money to a Jew to trade with during the week and the non-Jew trades with it on Shabbos. The Jew may not receive a share of the profit generated on Shabbos unless he stipulated initially that Shabbos would not be his responsibility at all. Otherwise, since the work rests upon the Jew and the non-Jew performs his task, it is prohibited.
(10) He acts for his own benefit. He works for his own share, like a tenant farmer, which is permitted by law, as stated at the beginning of siman 243. Since he receives a share of the produce, he acts for himself and the Jew's share improves incidentally. There, however, an oven is prohibited because of appearance, lest people think he is the Jew's employee. Here there is no such concern, because everyone knows that the non-Jew owns a share.
(11) The Jew does not benefit, etc. His benefit is merely incidental. The non-Jew does not perform the work for the Jew's benefit so as to become his agent, because the Jew has no obligation at all to work on Shabbos. During the week both heat the bathhouse, but on Shabbos the non-Jew cannot compel the Jew to work with him. He therefore certainly acts for his own benefit.
(12) Absorbed with other days. Otherwise it appears to be direct Shabbos wages.
