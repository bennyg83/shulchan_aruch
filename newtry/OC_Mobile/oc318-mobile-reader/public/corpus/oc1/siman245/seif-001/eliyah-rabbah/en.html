[1] [Levush] As though he appoints, etc. Accordingly, if they themselves do nothing, but the non-Jew heats the oven and bakes in it, and they receive rent, then although the Jew may not receive the rent because it is Shabbos wages, he may nevertheless divide it without specification by way of inclusion. We are not concerned that people will say the non-Jew works for the Jew, since the non-Jew owns a share in it, for otherwise even a prior stipulation should be forbidden. This is the language of Magen Avraham. It appears to me that his words, “even the oven,” are a scribal error and should read, “even a stipulation would be forbidden.” Where the oven itself belongs solely to the Jew, but the non-Jewish baker who heats it takes a certain number of days and the Jew takes a certain number of days, Magen Avraham agrees that it is forbidden even if they stipulated, for this resembles renting out an oven on Shabbos on condition that the non-Jew heat it for him during the week, and receiving Shabbos wages is like giving him money as payment. However, one may say to the baker, “You take two or three days and I will take two or three days,” since this constitutes Shabbos wages received through inclusion. In this manner Rabbeinu Tam permitted a prior stipulation, contrary to Beit Yosef, who writes that Tosafos retracted their words. In my humble opinion, if the case involves inclusion, why is a prior stipulation needed? Even after the partnership begins it should be effective, since all the work is imposed upon the hired non-Jew, and this resembles the case of renting discussed by Levush at the beginning of this siman. This is also implied by Magen Avraham himself in seif katan 6. If so, Bach correctly wrote that Tosafos retracted, for they required a prior stipulation. Nevertheless, as a matter of practical halachah one may perhaps be stringent, since this appears to be the view of Rosh and Rabbeinu Yerucham.
[2] If little, etc. It is forbidden, however, to calculate with him and say, “You took more on Shabbos and I took less; let us divide the remainder,” as wrote Raavad, Beit Yosef in the name of the Geonim, and Bach. See seif 2. This is the language of Magen Avraham. It appears that he explains the Shulchan Aruch in seif 2, which states that afterward the non-Jew agreed to divide equally, as referring to a division made without specification, without mentioning that the Shabbos proceeds were greater. In such a case, Raavad may agree that it is permitted even if the Shabbos proceeds exceeded those of the weekday, since they divided equally without mentioning that fact. Yet this is difficult, for Beit Yosef disagrees with Raavad. It appears that Beit Yosef disagrees only with the plain implication of Raavad, namely, that it is forbidden even if they did not mention it. This resolves Beit Yosef’s apparent contradiction, for he also cites the responsum of the Geonim without qualification. Beit Yosef agrees that if the Jew explicitly says, “Make up for me what was greater on Shabbos,” it is evident that their original stipulation was merely an evasion. It now appears that Beit Yosef’s citation of Mahari Abuhav, that where they stipulated it is permitted in every case, means both where they calculate and say how many Shabbos days the non-Jew took and how many weekdays the Jew took, whether many or few, and where they divide equally without mentioning that the Shabbos proceeds were greater. Bach understood Mahari Abuhav to disagree with the responsum of the Geonim, but in my humble opinion there is no dispute. Taz, however, explains the Shulchan Aruch to permit even an explicit mention, since they stipulated initially. He proves this from Rambam’s failure to explain that the Gemara’s phrase, “if they came to an accounting,” refers back to a case where they stipulated, and he therefore understands it as addressing the prohibited case. This is no proof, for Bach already answered that without a stipulation it is forbidden even if the Jew does not say, “Make up for me.” Nevertheless, as a matter of practical halachah one may perhaps be lenient, for Bach’s entire foundation rests on the responsum of the Geonim, and that responsum may be explained as referring to a case where they made no stipulation. The end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah also implies that where they stipulated, it is permitted in every case. The reason is that once they stipulated initially, any later adjustment resembles a gift. This requires study. Nachalas Tzvi discusses at length how Raavad answers Tosafos’ question at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah, but his words are strained. He also did not consult the source of the law in Ran, who brings Raavad’s words and explains how Tosafos’ question is resolved. See there.
[3] [Levush] And they came to an accounting, etc. Meaning, even if they wish to calculate without specification, without mentioning Shabbos, and even if the Jew wishes to take a weekday in exchange, whether much or little, without saying that it is in exchange for Shabbos, it is forbidden because they did not stipulate initially. In seif 3, which states regarding this, “Some say that after the fact it is permitted,” the permission applies only when they divided without specification. If they mention the Shabbos proceeds, all agree that it is forbidden where there was no prior stipulation. All this is clear from the Gemara and the poskim. Olas Tamid writes: “One may wonder about those who prohibit a division made without specification, for the question remained unresolved, and in a D’Rabbanan doubt we rule leniently.” He overlooked the words of Ran at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah, who explains that according to the first version it was obvious to the Gemara that an unspecified division is forbidden. Since the first version is the anonymous formulation of the Gemara, whereas the second version is reported in the name of Rav Geviha of Bei Kesil, we follow the first version, as is the practice of the Geonim in every such case.
[4] And if they wish, etc. Taz disagrees with this, maintaining that the permission applies specifically to a mint or tax farm, which is a major enterprise and a person is anxious about it. The proof he brings from Rivash, that one may not take one weekday in exchange for Shabbos unless the payment is included with other days, may be rejected. There, the non-Jew appears to be the Jew’s agent, whereas here the Jew rents it to him for all Shabbosos. See Magen Avraham and Responsa Rabbi Yom Tov Tzahalon, siman 76.
[5] [Levush] In the manner explained in siman 243, etc. This is an error, for that matter is not mentioned there, but only at the end of siman 244. Indeed, Rama writes only at the end, “in the manner explained at the end of siman 244.” There is no doubt that this also refers back to the beginning of the gloss, concerning renting one’s share to the non-Jew for Shabbosos.
[6] [Levush] Some say that after the fact it is permitted, etc. This does not resemble the ruling at the end of siman 243, where some say that if one violated the law and rented it out, the proceeds are forbidden. Here the Jew did nothing, for the non-Jew acts on account of his own partnership. The words of Olas Tamid on this point are unclear and plainly contain a scribal error. His statement that “above he permitted hiring the worker explicitly” appears to refer to the end of siman 244, and his answer, that there it is contracted work because the worker receives a fixed amount when the revenue is collected, proves this. Magen Avraham understood him to refer to what was mentioned earlier in siman 243 and therefore challenged him, but the challenge is unnecessary. See there; I have abbreviated.
