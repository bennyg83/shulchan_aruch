Regarding seif katan 6: “Nevertheless, it appears like Shabbos wages. Ran.” His intention is that according to Ran’s reasoning, the earlier incident, where the oven belonged exclusively to the Jew, should likewise have been permitted. The work was not incumbent upon the Jew, for he was free not to operate the oven, so the non-Jew was acting for his own benefit and laboring with his own property. Magen Avraham therefore answers that this is indeed so: if the non-Jew received half the profit each day, it would be permitted, since the Jew was not required to work and only the baker was involved. It appears that he means that the baker works for himself and acts for his own benefit, like a sharecropper. Even without an initial stipulation it would be permitted, provided that the Jew receives his share through havla’ah, meaning that he does not take the Shabbos earnings separately but together with the earnings of the other days, because otherwise it appears like Shabbos wages, as Ran writes. This differs from the earlier case, where if the non-Jew did not heat the oven on Shabbos, he would not heat it for the Jew during the week either. That case resembles leasing the oven. One cannot say there that the non-Jew is like a sharecropper, because he keeps all the Shabbos earnings for himself and during the week heats the oven for the Jew. It is therefore inappropriate to say that on Shabbos he labors for himself, since in exchange for doing so he heats the oven for the Jew during the week and receives no share then. Rather, the Jew allows him to use the Jew’s oven in return for his heating it on Shabbos. It is therefore like leasing the oven. Consequently, if they made no stipulation, the Jew may not take the money even through havla’ah with the earnings of the other days. Havla’ah alone does not help. They must specifically stipulate, “You take two or three days,” etc., for then it is a lease through havla’ah and is permitted, as stated at the beginning of siman 246. It is forbidden, however, to say, “You take Shabbos,” etc., because the Jew cannot transfer permanent ownership to him. It is therefore like leasing the oven for Shabbos, as explained above in the name of Rabbeinu Elchanan. Thus, they must specifically stipulate, “You take two or three days,” as explained above at the end of seif katan 6. Some authorities wrote that the text here contains a scribal error. Eliyah Rabbah cites a manuscript pamphlet that Magen Avraham sent him during his lifetime, before the work was printed, which had a different formulation. In my humble opinion, however, the text before us is correct. Magen Avraham later revised his words as they appear here and retracted what he had written in the pamphlet sent to Eliyah Rabbah, where he compared the earlier case to Ran’s ruling. In truth, they are not comparable. The earlier case is permitted only when the lease was arranged from the outset through havla’ah, by saying, “You take two or three days.” In Ran’s case, by contrast, the non-Jew works without specification and only afterward divides the money together with the earnings of the other days. That kind of havla’ah does not suffice in the earlier case. Since the non-Jew receives no share for heating the oven for the Jew during the week, but does so only because he is permitted to use the oven on Shabbos, the arrangement resembles leasing the oven. The Jew’s weekday benefit is effectively the payment he receives. They must therefore stipulate, “You take two or three days,” etc., which creates a lease through havla’ah. Whether the stipulation must be made specifically at the outset has already been explained above in seif katan 3.
