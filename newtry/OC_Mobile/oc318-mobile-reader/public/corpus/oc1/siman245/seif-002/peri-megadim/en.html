Where. See Taz. This subject is profound, and since the paths of wisdom are hidden from me, I must elaborate somewhat. Avodah Zarah 22a teaches: If a Jew and a non-Jew accepted a field in partnership, the Jew may not say, “You take Shabbos and I will take a weekday.” If they stipulated initially, it is permitted. If they came to an accounting, it is forbidden. Rashi explains the final clause as referring to a case where they did not stipulate, for two reasons. First, the non-Jew may have borne more of the burden. Raavad, as cited by Ran at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah, explains that the final clause refers back to a case where they stipulated, and “an accounting” means an accounting of the produce, where the non-Jew says, “Make up for me during the week what you received in excess,” and all the more so if the Jew says the reverse to the non-Jew. According to Rashi, Tosafos’ inference there is readily understood: the Gemara implies that an unspecified division is permitted. Tosafos conclude that it is permitted in every manner, meaning not only where they stipulated and later calculated the days, mentioning Shabbos and weekdays, but even where they later calculated the produce and one says, “Make up for me.” According to Raavad, Ran answers that an unspecified division is a greater novelty. Rambam, Hilchos Shabbos, chapter 6, halachah 17, rules that if they stipulated initially it is permitted whether one receives much or little, and if they did not stipulate it is forbidden. He omits the case of coming to an accounting. Maggid Mishneh explains that Rambam follows Rashi: where they did not stipulate, the unresolved question is ruled stringently, so even an unspecified division is forbidden, and certainly an accounting of the days is forbidden. Mahari Abuhav was uncertain whether Rambam agrees with Raavad that even after a prior stipulation an accounting of the produce is forbidden, or whether he permits it. He proves that Rambam permits even an accounting of the produce, for otherwise why did he omit the case of an accounting? He should have explained the Baraisa according to the first opinion, which is more plausible because the clause follows immediately after the prior stipulation. One cannot answer that Rambam rejected this because of Tosafos’ and Ran’s question, for the Gemara itself could have explained the Baraisa like Raavad, thereby avoiding contradiction between its beginning and end and resolving that an unspecified division is forbidden. Since it did not do so, the Gemara evidently regarded it as obvious that where they stipulated initially, it is permitted whether one receives much or little, even if they later calculate the produce. Shulchan Aruch rules accordingly here following Rambam. This excludes Bach’s view that Rambam agrees with Raavad. The word printed as demah li should read malei li, “make up for me.”

In Yoreh Deah 294:13, concerning a Jew and non-Jew who are partners in orlah fruit, Shulchan Aruch rules that if they did not stipulate it is forbidden, and if they stipulated it is permitted, provided that they do not calculate how much fruit the non-Jew consumed during the orlah years so that the Jew will consume an equivalent amount later. These are Rambam’s words in Hilchos Maachalos Asuros, chapter 10, halachah 14, and they appear to follow Raavad. Shulchan Aruch would then contradict himself, ruling stringently regarding orlah even after a prior stipulation, while ruling leniently here regarding Shabbos. The phrase “coming to an accounting of the produce” is the same according to both versions of the Gemara. Shach there, seif katan 27, reconciles Rambam’s rulings concerning Shabbos and forbidden foods, but how will he reconcile the words of Shulchan Aruch? In my humble opinion, the matter may be explained according to Lechem Mishneh there. Rambam’s condition that they not come to an accounting was stated based on logic specifically regarding orlah, which is more severe than Shabbos because one benefits from exchanged prohibited produce. Rava would not have erred by permitting orlah without a prior stipulation and then been embarrassed. See there. Accordingly, Shulchan Aruch’s rulings are reconciled as I have written. It is difficult, however, to say that here the parties do not mention Shabbos, whereas there they explicitly mention it and say, “Make up for me,” for if so, Shulchan Aruch should have taught here that such an explicit accounting is forbidden. This is Taz’s position here.
However. Rambam’s wording in Hilchos Maachalos Asuros, chapter 10, halachah 14, is: “If they stipulated that the orlah years belong to the non-Jew and the permitted years to the Jew,” meaning whether many or few, “it is permitted. If they did not stipulate, it is forbidden, provided that they do not come to an accounting and calculate how much fruit the non-Jew consumed during the orlah years so that the Jew will consume an equivalent amount. If they stipulated in this manner, it is forbidden as an exchange.” Bach in Yoreh Deah appears to explain Rambam’s condition as applying to the initial stipulation, meaning that if they stipulated from the outset to calculate the produce and later divided the years, it is forbidden, but if they initially stipulated merely that each would receive certain years and only afterward calculated the produce, it is permitted, since the non-Jew voluntarily gives the excess as a gift. According to this, Rambam’s rulings do not contradict one another. In Hilchos Shabbos he writes that whether much or little, a prior stipulation suffices; only an initial stipulation concerning the produce is forbidden. Bach’s own rulings, however, appear contradictory, for in Orach Chayim he writes that Rambam agrees with Raavad, while here he writes that Rambam permits any case where they initially stipulated that one receives certain years. Bach therefore objects to Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh Deah for omitting the words “if they stipulated in this manner.” Furthermore, Ran writes the condition only after discussing a case where they did not stipulate, so no one should mistakenly think that an initial general stipulation followed by an accounting of the produce is forbidden. Shulchan Aruch, however, altered the order and wrote, “If they did not stipulate, it is forbidden; if they stipulated, it is permitted, provided,” etc., while omitting the phrase “if they stipulated in this manner.” Taz there, seif katan 20, therefore writes that Shulchan Aruch understands Rambam’s wording as teaching a greater novelty: even where there was some initial stipulation, an arrangement explicitly tied to the produce is forbidden, and certainly an initial general stipulation followed by an accounting of the produce is forbidden as an evasion. According to Shulchan Aruch, this stringency is based on logic specifically regarding orlah, which is more severe, whereas regarding Shabbos every case is permitted after a prior stipulation, even a later accounting of the produce, as Lechem Mishneh explains. Taz, however, interprets Rambam stringently even regarding Shabbos: if the initial stipulation concerned the produce, it is forbidden, which is a greater novelty, and certainly a general stipulation followed by an accounting of the produce is forbidden. This disagrees with Shulchan Aruch here in Orach Chayim, seif 2. If one were to follow Bach and permit a later accounting after a general stipulation, why did Rambam not teach this regarding Shabbos? According to Taz there is some difficulty in Shulchan Aruch’s ruling here. Shach there, seif katan 27, understands Rambam to prohibit an initial stipulation of this sort even if they later divide by years. The wording “how much he consumed” is somewhat difficult according to this. Taz’s statement there, seif katan 19, “as regarding Shabbos,” means that if they initially stipulated concerning the produce, it is forbidden even if they later divided by years, in agreement with Shach. Thus Taz’s words are firm and correct. Tosefes Shabbos, letter 2, claims that Taz contradicts his words in Yoreh Deah, but I am astonished that he wrote such harsh words against Taz, whose mastery of the entire Talmud was clear. Nor do I know how he inferred from Shach that an initial general stipulation followed by an accounting of the produce is permitted. This is what appears to me here.
