[2] If little, etc. It is forbidden, however, to calculate with him and say, “You took more on Shabbos and I took less; let us divide the remainder,” as wrote Raavad, Beit Yosef in the name of the Geonim, and Bach. See seif 2. This is the language of Magen Avraham. It appears that he explains the Shulchan Aruch in seif 2, which states that afterward the non-Jew agreed to divide equally, as referring to a division made without specification, without mentioning that the Shabbos proceeds were greater. In such a case, Raavad may agree that it is permitted even if the Shabbos proceeds exceeded those of the weekday, since they divided equally without mentioning that fact. Yet this is difficult, for Beit Yosef disagrees with Raavad. It appears that Beit Yosef disagrees only with the plain implication of Raavad, namely, that it is forbidden even if they did not mention it. This resolves Beit Yosef’s apparent contradiction, for he also cites the responsum of the Geonim without qualification. Beit Yosef agrees that if the Jew explicitly says, “Make up for me what was greater on Shabbos,” it is evident that their original stipulation was merely an evasion. It now appears that Beit Yosef’s citation of Mahari Abuhav, that where they stipulated it is permitted in every case, means both where they calculate and say how many Shabbos days the non-Jew took and how many weekdays the Jew took, whether many or few, and where they divide equally without mentioning that the Shabbos proceeds were greater. Bach understood Mahari Abuhav to disagree with the responsum of the Geonim, but in my humble opinion there is no dispute. Taz, however, explains the Shulchan Aruch to permit even an explicit mention, since they stipulated initially. He proves this from Rambam’s failure to explain that the Gemara’s phrase, “if they came to an accounting,” refers back to a case where they stipulated, and he therefore understands it as addressing the prohibited case. This is no proof, for Bach already answered that without a stipulation it is forbidden even if the Jew does not say, “Make up for me.” Nevertheless, as a matter of practical halachah one may perhaps be lenient, for Bach’s entire foundation rests on the responsum of the Geonim, and that responsum may be explained as referring to a case where they made no stipulation. The end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah also implies that where they stipulated, it is permitted in every case. The reason is that once they stipulated initially, any later adjustment resembles a gift. This requires study. Nachalas Tzvi discusses at length how Raavad answers Tosafos’ question at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah, but his words are strained. He also did not consult the source of the law in Ran, who brings Raavad’s words and explains how Tosafos’ question is resolved. See there.
