[Levush] And its taste is vinegar, etc. Bach explains that this is only where it also visibly appears to have soured, meaning that a film has formed, called kahn in German, producing a white surface. If no white film appears, even if it tastes like vinegar, one does not recite Shehakol. Taz at the beginning of this siman challenges him, since wine with such a film remains valid even for kiddush, as stated in siman 272 regarding wine at the mouth of a barrel. But Taz apparently did not see Bach in siman 272 seif katan 6, where Bach himself resolves this: there the wine retains the smell and taste of wine, and only the wine at the mouth of the barrel contains particles; here the wine itself is poor and tastes like vinegar, and because of its deterioration a white film develops.
And its taste is wine, etc. Even if a film has formed on the wine, called kahn in German, and a white coating appears on it. For kiddush, however, Bach rules that it is prohibited. This requires study, because what then is the Gemara's question in Bava Batra 97, "to exclude what"? Perhaps it comes to exclude precisely this case.
People refrain, etc. This concerns wine that has not become complete vinegar to the point that drinking it is harmful, but merely has a vinegar taste such that people refrain from drinking it. Otherwise no blessing at all is recited, not even Shehakol. This is simple. From here it follows that Or Chadash is incorrect in writing that wine which soured, and likewise all beverages, once people refrain from them, are governed by siman 204 seif 4. With other beverages whose blessing is Shehakol, that blessing remains until drinking them is harmful. Perhaps he means beverages that ordinarily receive Borei Pri HaGefen, as discussed above in siman 202, in which case one would recite only Shehakol. Or Chadash further writes that vinegar which is poured on the ground and does not bubble is not considered vinegar and receives a blessing, citing Bach siman 205 and claiming that this is explicit in Yoreh Deah siman 123. This is astonishing. Bach does not say this there, and Yoreh Deah proves the opposite: there the reason is that we are not expert in determining when it is vinegar, so regarding stam yeinam we are stringent until it bubbles. For blessings, if we are uncertain, we should instead be lenient and not bless. Moreover, it is explained there that during the three days before it bubbles it is certainly complete vinegar, so how could one bless when it may bubble within three days? This is why the Levush and poskim here give the criterion that once it harms him it is certainly vinegar. Magen Avraham likewise was not precise on this point.
