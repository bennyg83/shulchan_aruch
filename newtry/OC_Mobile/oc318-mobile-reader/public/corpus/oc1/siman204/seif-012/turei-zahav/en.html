Rambam first wrote, however, “but if he mixed it in to impart flavor,” which appears out of sequence. It seems that Rambam arranged his words this way so that we would understand what he means by “to impart flavor.” At first glance, one might think that whenever an ingredient imparts any flavor, even if one merely adds a small quantity of spices to a cooked dish, the blessing should follow the spices. This is incorrect, because in that case the spices are secondary and the cooked dish is the principal food. Rather, Rambam discusses a case in which the flavoring ingredient is itself part of the food, such as when wheat starch is placed in honey not merely to bind it, but because one wishes to eat the resulting food. He therefore says that it is added “to impart flavor to the mixture,” meaning that through combining the two ingredients, the eater obtains a pleasant taste from eating both. This is precisely the homlita discussed in the Gemara, over which one recites the blessing appropriate to the spices. Rambam apparently agrees with the Tur’s colleagues that whenever something is placed into honey and becomes food through the combination of both ingredients, we follow the principal component intended for eating. Thus, if wheat starch is placed into honey so that the wheat-starch food will be eaten after acquiring flavor from the honey, it is comparable to the roses discussed above, and one recites the blessing over the wheat starch. Rambam therefore placed first the rule concerning a mixture made to impart flavor, so that his intent would be understood from what he subsequently writes concerning wheat starch used merely as a binder. We thereby know that the second category concerns wheat starch added not as a binder, but as food created through the mixture.
