There, seif 9. If one ate forbidden food or drank a forbidden beverage because of danger, etc. Note: The early authorities express two views on this matter; see Taz and Magen Avraham. In my humble opinion, proof may be brought from the Gemara that he must recite both a first and an after-blessing. Shevuos 26b records that Ravina asked Rava: If one swore regarding a loaf and then became endangered because of it, what is the law? The Gemara asks: If he is endangered, let the master permit it to him. See Rashi there. This is difficult. Perhaps Ravina was asking whether a blessing must be recited. Regarding other prohibitions, since they are merely overridden for a sick person and not entirely permitted, perhaps one may not recite a first or after-blessing over them. Regarding an oath, however, Rashi writes at the beginning of the third chapter of Berachos that the concept of an overridden prohibition applies only where the permissibility is not stated explicitly within the prohibition itself. Where the Torah explicitly states the permissibility in that very context, it is not merely overridden but entirely permitted, because that prohibition was never stated regarding such a case. Here too, since the verse says “a person” in connection with an oath, excluding one who is compelled, the prohibition of an oath was never stated at all regarding coercion. It is therefore not merely overridden but entirely permitted. Ravina’s question may therefore have been whether danger that arose only afterward is also included in the Torah’s exclusion of one who is compelled, so that the matter is fully permitted and requires a blessing, or whether the Torah excluded only one whose heart compelled him at the time of the oath, whereas a danger arising later is merely like other prohibitions overridden because of danger and would not require a blessing. Why, then, does the Gemara ask, “Let the master permit it to him”? It must be that one who eats a prohibited item because of danger recites a blessing in every case, so there is no practical distinction regarding blessings, and the Gemara’s question is proper. One should not answer that Ravina’s question concerned whether he may eat without annulment, since if the oath is fully permitted he need not seek annulment, whereas if it is only overridden he must do so. This is difficult even according to Rashi, who explains the question as whether he requires a korban, depending on whether he would refrain upon learning the law. Perhaps even in danger he is considered one who would refrain, since he could seek annulment. Yet this is no difficulty. The danger itself cannot serve as an opening for annulment because it is a new circumstance. If he has another opening or regrets the oath, then wherever annulment is possible there is no doubt that he must annul it. Even if prohibitions are fully permitted for a sick person, that applies only where danger exists. Where the prohibition can be avoided through a permitted means, it is considered as though there is no danger. For this reason one feeds the lesser prohibition first, and certainly where a fully permitted alternative exists one may not feed him a prohibition at all. Since annulment is possible, the case is regarded as lacking danger and annulment is unquestionably required. Thus our question remains: perhaps the practical distinction concerns blessings. It must therefore be that a blessing is required over all forbidden foods eaten because of danger. Examine carefully. According to the Shach in Yoreh Deah, siman 238, however, who holds that a dangerously ill person requires no annulment at all, this proof falls away. Nevertheless, the Shach concludes there that one should annul the oath, and this appears correct, for prohibitions are merely overridden for a sick person, not fully permitted. Therefore he certainly should annul it. This is clear; examine carefully.
