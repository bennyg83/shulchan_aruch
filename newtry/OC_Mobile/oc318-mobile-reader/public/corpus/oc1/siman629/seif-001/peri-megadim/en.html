First One must investigate which s'chach is invalid by Torah law and which is invalid rabbinically. The practical difference is for what the Magen Avraham wrote in letter 22: if he has no other s'chach, he may cover with something invalid because of a decree. This is proven in the Gemara, namely Sukkah 14b, in a time of danger, and as written in the Chiddushei HaRan there, from which we learn that in pressing circumstances one even recites a blessing on a rabbinically invalid item, for we do not decree in such a case. The same may apply to a lulav invalid rabbinically. See siman 649:6: all invalid species are taken in pressing circumstances, but no blessing is recited. This seemingly implies that whether the invalidity is Torah-level or rabbinic, one takes it but does not bless; with God's help this will be explained there. While I was entangled in this analysis, I looked at the Magen Avraham in siman 17, regarding why a dry etrog differs from a lulav, etc. One can say that this is explicit in the Torah, the fruit of a beautiful tree, while the other is by comparison; or perhaps on a rabbinically invalid item one takes and blesses in pressing circumstances, as the Terumat HaDeshen wrote, but for something invalid by Torah law, what would it help? See there, and with God's help it will be explained further. Still, one can say that specifically regarding a decree enacted for this mitzvah, such as boards, a missing etrog, a removed pitam, and the like, in pressing circumstances we do not decree and one even blesses over rabbinic invalidities. But where the Sages said generally that something is impure rabbinically, and as a result one may not use it as s'chach, perhaps in pressing circumstances they upheld their words, since they did not make this decree specifically in this mitzvah. It is like a seventh-day impure person on Shabbos, where we do not sprinkle, and similar cases. In the general introduction we wrote about this. If so, in any event in such a case one does not bless. Now we must explain, regarding s'chach, which invalidities are Torah-level and which are rabbinic, which are because of a decree in sukkah, and which are because the Sages prohibited them elsewhere. I will answer and say:
Behold In invalid s'chach itself there are three Torah-level invalidities: attached material, material susceptible to tumah, and material that does not grow from the ground. Attached material has no rabbinic invalidity, for something detached and later attached, even if he nullified it there, appears to be valid; see Beit Yosef siman 626 in the name of the Terumat HaDeshen, and here se'if 8 in the Levush and Magen Avraham 11. Susceptibility to tumah can be Torah-level or rabbinic. If it is rabbinic, one can say it is a general decree, and therefore it is automatically invalid for sukkah; in any event one would not bless in pressing circumstances. Something susceptible to tumah is certainly invalid by Torah law even if it has not yet actually become impure. However, there is one investigation that is exceedingly difficult for me. The Tosafot Yom Tov discusses it at length in Kelim 24:4 and at the end of chapter 17 of Kelim. One who studies the Rambam in Kelim 1:10, 4:1, and 23:1 sees that anything susceptible to midras tumah is susceptible to other tumot only rabbinically, like flat wooden implements; see there well. The kal vachomer of small flasks, see Bava Kamma 25b, Shabbos 66a and 84b in Tosafot there, Niddah 49a, and Sukkah 20a, may be understood as not accepted as law except rabbinically. To say that all thirteen hermeneutical principles are called divrei sofrim in the Rambam's language, see the Maggid Mishneh and the Rambam in Hilchot Ishut chapter 1 regarding money betrothal, etc.; yet there is a difference between divrei sofrim and rabbinic law. Now according to the Rambam's view, one can say that anything that becomes impure through nega'im is not invalid for s'chach by Torah law, for we do not rule like Sumchus in Shabbos 24, but only rabbinically, because its form changed; see Taz 6 and the Rambam there. In Sukkah 12b, Tosafot s.v. be'anitzei pishtan and the Pnei Yehoshua there say that the Sages disagree with Sumchus, for nega'im tumah does not apply to foods. If so, one can say similarly that midras tumah does not apply to foods. A lump of sourdough that he designated for sitting served in the manner of wood; if so, although it is invalid because it is fit for midras and he designated it for that, this may not be a Torah-level invalidity for s'chach but only rabbinic, since according to the Rambam it receives tumah rabbinically by contact with a corpse, sheretz, or carcass. In pressing circumstances, perhaps one may use this as s'chach. With this one can learn an answer to what is written in Mahari Weil, cited by the Magen Avraham 11 and the Taz 2: perhaps something fit for midras is not invalid by Torah law but only rabbinically, and he holds that with rabbinic tumah we require specifically that it already became impure. However, we rule that even something susceptible to rabbinic tumah is invalid, as in Taz 5. I will explain the remaining matters each in its place, with God's help.
And know Regarding growing from the ground, there is Torah-level invalidity with earth, metals, and the like. We require something that grows, not merely something classified as produce of the ground, such as stone and inanimate matter, which are also called produce of the ground; animals too are called produce of the ground. However, there is rabbinic invalidity, such as flax that was beaten and combed, whose form changed. Perhaps with wood one can say that where its form changed it is invalid only rabbinically. See Yoreh De'ah regarding covering the blood; that topic is not relevant here.
