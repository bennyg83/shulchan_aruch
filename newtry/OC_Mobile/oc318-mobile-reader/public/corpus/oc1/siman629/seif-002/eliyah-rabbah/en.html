The Magen Avraham in their name writes that even if the iron was later removed, they remain invalid. It is written in Mahari Weil that he saw a piece of wood on a sukkah whose end was encircled with iron, and he ordered it removed because it was a fragment of a vessel and perhaps it had become impure. This requires examination. See siman 676, se'if katan. Incidentally I will explain the Gemara in Sukkah 12: Rav said, if he roofed it with male arrows it is valid; with female arrows it is invalid. The Gemara asks: male arrows are valid? That is obvious. It answers: one might have thought we should decree against male arrows because of female arrows; therefore it teaches us otherwise. The master said: with female arrows it is invalid. The Gemara asks: that is obvious. It answers: one might have thought that a receptacle made to be filled is not called a receptacle; therefore it teaches us otherwise. This is difficult: why does it still need to say that female arrows are invalid? From the first clause, that male arrows are valid, which was difficult because it is obvious, we see that one might have thought to decree against male arrows because of female arrows. But if female arrows themselves are valid, how could such a decree apply? It appears to me that one might have thought to decree because of a case where an iron arrowhead is inserted into the handle. Only now that it taught that female arrows are invalid is it preferable for the Gemara to say the decree is because of female arrows. It also appears to me, by first raising another difficulty: why does it need to say that male arrows are valid? We could infer this from the latter clause that female arrows are invalid; but male arrows are valid, since it did not teach the greater novelty that even male arrows are invalid. The reverse is also difficult: why does it need to say that female arrows are invalid? We could infer this from the first clause that male arrows are valid, and it did not teach the greater novelty that female arrows are valid, so they must be invalid. Therefore it appears to me that if we inferred the invalidity only from implication, one could say that it is invalid only rabbinically, as it says, a decree against male arrows, etc. But when it teaches explicitly, invalid, this implies by Torah law, as stated at the beginning of Sukkah. If so, one difficulty is resolved: from female arrows are invalid, I might have said that the same applies to male arrows; and the reason it did not teach the novelty that male arrows are invalid is because their prohibition is only rabbinic, by decree because of female arrows. This also resolves the second question: if it had taught only that male arrows are valid, I might have said that the same applies to female arrows, for a receptacle made to be filled is not called a receptacle; only one might have thought that they are rabbinically forbidden because of a decree lest one use a receptacle not made to be filled. Therefore it could not teach that female arrows are valid, because rabbinically they are forbidden; so it taught that female arrows are invalid, meaning by Torah law, because it is called a receptacle. One should not ask the question with which we began: why do I need female arrows are invalid, since it is learned from the question that male arrows are obviously valid, except that one might have thought to decree because of female arrows; if so, necessarily female arrows are forbidden by Torah law, for if they were only by rabbinic decree it would be a decree upon a decree. For if it also had not taught male arrows are valid, I might have said that a receptacle made to be filled is not called a receptacle even rabbinically, for from where would we say to decree? But now that it taught male arrows are valid and did not teach the greater novelty that female arrows are valid, we infer that female arrows are forbidden rabbinically. Consider this carefully.
