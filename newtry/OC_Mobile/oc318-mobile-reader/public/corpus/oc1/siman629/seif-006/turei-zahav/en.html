That is, most of the people of the city, etc. It appears to me that the Rav pressed himself unnecessarily to explain it this way. One must say that there is a place where there is no clarified custom in this matter; otherwise the Talmud should have answered the question it raises, namely, that the matter itself is difficult, by saying that the Tanna is speaking according to custom: if he made it for lying, meaning that the custom is so, and likewise in the latter clause, if he made it for s'chach, meaning that the custom is so. Rather, one must say that the textual inferences would still contradict one another, namely in a place where there is no clarified custom: from the first clause it implies that without specification it is for s'chach, and from the latter clause the opposite. If so, what forced the Rema to explain the Mishnah as referring to custom, which is not implied by the wording, he made it, instead of explaining it according to its plain meaning, that it speaks where there is no clarified custom, and the distinction is between making it explicitly or without specification? But where there is a custom, the law certainly follows the custom; that is what the Rosh discusses. The Mishnah and Gemara, however, do not discuss that. This is simple in my humble opinion.
In a place where they are accustomed to set mats. Meaning, even large mats that are not meant for lying upon nevertheless may not be used as s'chach, because of a decree lest one sit under the ceiling of the house. He wrote there further that in places where they are accustomed to roof with thin boards, it is forbidden to use them as s'chach for this reason. This implies that in a place where they are not accustomed to roof with natis, it is permitted to use them as s'chach for the sukkah. However, below in this siman it will be explained in the name of the Semak that it is forbidden.
