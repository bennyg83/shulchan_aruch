(14) "Which is not suitable for lying upon." The wording is not exact. If it were wholly unsuitable for lying upon, why would we say that by default it is intended for lying? Rather, it means that it is not especially suitable for lying, as Tur states explicitly. (15) "If it is small." Meaning large enough for a person to lie upon. (16) "By default it is intended for lying and can become tamei," etc. Since its ordinary purpose is lying upon, it is fit to become tamei when a zav lies upon it. Once it is fit to receive tumah, it is already disqualified for use as sechach, because anything fit to receive tumah may not be used for sechach. (17) "Meaning that the majority," etc. If he made it explicitly for sechach, this is effective even in a place where there is no established local custom. Rama comes only to exclude the opposite case: if the local custom is to make such mats for lying upon, it does not help that this particular person made or purchased his mat explicitly for sechach, because other people do not know that and may come to use ordinary such mats for sechach. Sometimes local custom also creates leniency. If he made the mat without specifying its purpose, or we do not know whether he made it for sechach or lying upon, and most residents of the city make such mats for sechach, we assume that he too made it for sechach. (18) "Unless he made it for lying, meaning," etc. This does not mean that if the local custom is for sechach, his own intention to use it for lying is ineffective. That is certainly false. Once he intended it for lying, the laws of tumah apply to it and it may not be used for sechach. The point is rather that not only an explicit act of making it for lying creates the problem; a local custom to use such mats for lying is likewise treated as though he himself made it for lying. If the local custom is unknown, everyone agrees that it may be used for sechach so long as we do not know that it was made for lying. In these countries all such mats are made for lying, so even if a person made one for sechach it should not be used. (19) "In a manner that it is fit to hold." Since it is fit to serve as a receptacle, it has the status of a utensil and is capable of receiving tumah, and therefore may not be used for sechach. This applies where nothing was specified. In that case, a raised rim indicates that it was made to hold things and it consequently receives tumah. If, however, he expressly intended it for sechach, then according to some authorities it does not receive tumah even if it has a rim, because that receptacle-like form is insignificant when the object was not made for receiving. Other later authorities hold that once it has a rim it receives tumah in every case and may not be used for sechach. (20) "Even if its rim was removed." It resembles other broken utensils, which the Sages prohibited for sechach, as explained above in seif 2. (21) "They may not be used for sechach." Meaning even if they were made specifically for sechach and therefore do not receive tumah, the Sages decreed against using them lest a person sit under the roof of a house made from such mats. Mats fixed as part of a house roof are invalid by Torah law because they were installed for dwelling, not for shade.
