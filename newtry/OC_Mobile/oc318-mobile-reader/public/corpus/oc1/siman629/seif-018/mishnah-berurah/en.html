(45) That are four tefachim wide. Such boards resemble a house roof, and there is concern that one may sit beneath the roof of the house in which he lives all year. That roof is certainly invalid, because the Torah requires a sukkah, not a year-round house.
(46) If he turned them on their sides. Meaning, he did not place their broad surfaces across the sukkah but laid them upon their narrow sides.
(47) That are not four tefachim wide. This is not precise. The same applies even if they are less than three tefachim wide. Since they bear the status of invalid sechach, they are like metal spits, which are invalid however they are turned.
(48) Which resemble utensils. Although they are fit for sitting and can also be used by placing objects upon them, they do not contract tumah because they were not designated for sitting or any other use, but remain intended for construction or sale. The decree concerning a house roof also does not apply, because houses are not ordinarily roofed with short boards less than four tefachim wide.
(49) The custom is not to, etc. The concern is that one may place the boards so closely that rain cannot enter. Some Rishonim write that nowadays such boards are strictly forbidden, because homes are now roofed with boards less than four tefachim wide and the decree concerning a house roof therefore applies. Nevertheless, one should also be concerned for the first reason. Accordingly, even narrow laths, which are not subject to the roof decree because houses are not roofed with them, should not be arranged so tightly that rain cannot enter, and this is all the more true of shingles.
(50) At all. The poskim write that in pressing circumstances, when no other sechach is available, one may use boards even four tefachim wide. The same applies to anything the Sages prohibited only because of a decree. Regarding the blessing, however, the poskim disagree. See below, siman 649, seif 6. In his novellae here, Rashba inclines to the view that one may recite the blessing.
