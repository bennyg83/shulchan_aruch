If he spread a sheet over it because of the sun - See the Taz, who wrote that there are three distinctions in this matter. If the sun has not yet reached inside the sukkah but is only close to it, then even according to Rabbeinu Tam, who is the some say opinion cited nearby, it is invalid. If the sun has reached through the holes in the s'chach to the person's body, then even according to the first opinion it is initially permitted, for it is visible to all that he does so because of the sun. They disagree where there is some indication, namely where the sun enters the sukkah but not onto the person's body: according to the first opinion it invalidates, while according to Rabbeinu Tam it does not invalidate; however, initially one should not do it. This is what the Mechaber concluded at the end of his words according to the some say opinion: nevertheless, etc., unless it is evident to all that he intends, etc. Meaning, there must be a complete indication, such as where the sun has reached the person's body, as above. These are his words. I did not copy this in the Mishnah Berurah as practical law, because several later authorities disagree with him, including the Eliyah Rabbah, Bigdei Yesha, Ma'amar Mordechai, and Nahar Shalom. Their view is that Rashi forbids in every case, even if the sun reached the person's body, since in any event the sheet protects and covers him and is called covering with something susceptible to tumah. According to Rabbeinu Tam, it is permitted even if the sun merely came near the sukkah, provided the sheet does not assist in the sukkah's validity, for example where the s'chach was thick enough that even if the leaves settle, its sun will not be greater than its shade.
And some say, etc. - This is the view of Rabbeinu Tam, brought in Tosafot. His view is that since he first made the sukkah properly according to its law, it is not invalidated by spreading a sheet over it afterwards, even if the sheet too had more shade than sun. This is how the Beit Meir and other later authorities explain Rabbeinu Tam's words. However, the sheet must not be very low beneath the s'chach, as we explain below. See the Bach, who ruled stringently like the first opinion, and so it also implies in the Magen Avraham. However, the Eliyah Rabbah's view is that the main law follows Rabbeinu Tam, because the Rosh, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Mordechai, and Agudah agreed with him. The Eliyah Rabbah's words in se'if katan 18 refer to the Levush, who mentioned Rabbeinu Tam's view as the first reasoning and the view of other poskim as a some say; so too the Bikkurei Yaakov wrote. Because of this, he ruled that if rains are dripping from the s'chach onto the table, one should spread a sheet and may even recite the blessing to sit in the sukkah. These are his words. In truth, he could have counted three additional poskim whose words appear to rule like Rabbeinu Tam: the responsum attributed to the Ramban that is cited in the Beit Yosef, which is the Rashba's responsum in siman 196, the Or Zarua, and the Semag. But we should not therefore adopt leniency in practice as he did, for there are many very early authorities opposing them who stand in Rashi's approach, which is the first opinion brought by the Mechaber: Rabbeinu Chananel in our sugya explains because of falling leaves as Rashi did, that the leaves should not fall into the food. It is known from the Rosh that the Mishnah certainly speaks where its shade is greater than its sun, for otherwise it would be invalid even if done for beautification. The responsum of the Geonim can perhaps be reconciled from the Rosh's question: when they said that the Mishnah speaks where its sun is greater than its shade, this means that if he does not cover it with the sheet, its sun would remain greater than its shade because the s'chach was sparse; so too it implies from the Mordechai. Afterwards I found in the Semag as we said. One who studies the words of the Ritz Giat will see that he too explains like Rashi, and so it also implies from the Itur. See the Bach, who wrote that the Rambam's view is also so, since he copied the Mishnah's wording without qualification, implying that he explains like Rashi. Rabbeinu Manoach had already preceded him in this in his commentary on the Rambam, where he also wrote this in the Rambam's view. See there; his own view too is that the explanation of Rabbeinu Shlomo, meaning Rashi, is reasonable, and that the fact that the sukkah was made first does not help at all. So too the Riag agreed. These are his words. So too is the view of the Ran, the Ritva in his novellae, and the gloss of the Semak; all took the main law according to the plain meaning of the Mishnah and Rashi's view. The result of our words is that Rabbeinu Chananel, Rashi, the Riag, the Rambam, the Itur, Rabbeinu Manoach, the Ran, the Ritva, the gloss of the Semak, and so it also appears in the approach of Rivav, all take the law like Rashi and like the Mechaber's plain first opinion. See the Biur HaGra, who also wrote that the Mechaber in se'if 1 ruled plainly like the first opinion, where the Mechaber wrote: whether the tree preceded or the sukkah preceded; and also from what he wrote at the end of se'if 1: in all this there is no difference between where the tree preceded and where the s'chach preceded. Here too, since the sheet has more shade than sun, why should we validate when he spread it to protect from the sun, or beneath it because of falling leaves? Although the Bach somewhat reconciled this by saying that it resembles something spread to beautify, unlike a tree, the Gra considered this forced, and so too the Beit Meir; see there. Therefore if rains are dripping onto the table, one should act according to the Magen Avraham's view, as we wrote in the Mishnah Berurah.
Or beneath it, so that the leaves not soak - Meaning that it was near and attached to the s'chach, and thereby prevents the leaves from being able to fall, as the Tosafot Yom Tov explains.
Provided that it is within four tefachim of the s'chach - It is obvious that the Shulchan Aruch refers also to protecting from the sun, and so it is proven in the Tur. I saw in the glosses to the Mishnah of Rabbi Akiva Eiger, on that which is brought in the Bartenura, the second explanation, which is the some say view brought here, that specifically there should not be ten tefachim between the s'chach and the sheet, for otherwise it is invalid because of a sukkah beneath a sukkah. His words require examination from the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, who explicitly wrote a measure of four tefachim. Perhaps he said his words only according to Rabbeinu Tam himself, who holds that since the sukkah made properly came first, the sheet no longer has power to invalidate it, similar to Rabbeinu Tam's validation of a sukkah that preceded the tree, where the tree has no power to invalidate it. Therefore he wrote that at least it should not be ten tefachim distant. But the Tur and Shulchan Aruch copied Rabbeinu Tam's approach because several poskim challenged Rashi, saying there is no reason to distinguish between spreading it to beautify and spreading it to protect from the sun and leaves; see Mordechai, Or Zarua, Tosafot, the Rosh, and Semag. Meaning, this is like spreading it for beautification. Therefore its law is exactly like beautification, where we require that it not be four tefachim away from it, as above in siman 627; see there.
Unless it is evident to all that he intends to protect - From this wording it implies that even beneath the s'chach one should be stringent because of observers, for it refers back to the case above where he spread the sheet beneath it because of falling leaves. However, the Rokeach's view, brought below in the Magen Avraham in siman 640, se'if katan 8, is that beneath the s'chach, which is inside the sukkah itself, there is no concern for appearances; see there. In practical law there is not so much difference, since in any case one should be stringent in every case, because according to the basic law the halacha follows the first opinion.
Or that it is soaked in water - See the Magen Avraham, who wrote: nevertheless, at the time of eating it is forbidden, citing the Ran and Beit Yosef. This requires examination, for the words of the Shulchan Aruch are according to the some say view, namely Rabbeinu Tam's approach, and according to him it is certainly permitted by law even at the time of eating, for he holds that anything that does not assist the sukkah's validity does not invalidate it. What the Beit Yosef copied from the Ran is also no proof, for the Ran was speaking according to Rashi, who holds that because of falling leaves is a Torah prohibition. The Ran is uncertain whether one may spread the sheet to dry it, where it is only for the garment's need. Perhaps one should be stringent, since only beautification, which is a sukkah need, is permitted, unlike this, which is the garment's need and is not subordinate to the sukkah. Or perhaps only when he spreads it to protect himself from the sun or leaves does it have the name of s'chach and is called covering with something susceptible to tumah; but when he spreads it to dry it, it does not have the name of s'chach and is permitted. This reasoning is proven in Rashi, s.v. al gabbei kinof; see there. The Ran concluded that in practice one should be stringent. But according to Rabbeinu Tam the Ran would not have been uncertain at all, for it is certainly permitted. So too it is proven in the Darchei Moshe, who wrote regarding this uncertainty of the Ran that from the Tur it implies that it is permitted; and the Shulchan Aruch copied the Tur's wording. In the Biur HaGra it is also proven that according to Rabbeinu Tam it is permitted. Afterwards I found that the Nahar Shalom also wrote that according to Rabbeinu Tam it is certainly permitted, and he remained with a difficulty against the Magen Avraham. I then saw something new in the words of the Peri Megadim, who wrote that even according to Rabbeinu Tam there is room for uncertainty, perhaps for the garment's need it is forbidden. But this is not so, for according to Rabbeinu Tam anything that does not assist the sukkah's validity does not invalidate it. Perhaps, in my humble opinion, one can somewhat reconcile the Magen Avraham's words: he may hold like the Bach's view, that the Tur, and likewise the Shulchan Aruch that copied his words, does not accept in law Rabbeinu Tam's reasoning that anything that does not assist the sukkah's validity does not invalidate the sukkah because the sukkah came first. Rather, only in this case of protecting from the sun and leaves is its law like beautification. According to this, the Ran's uncertainty applies well even to this approach, just as to Rashi: perhaps for the garment's need it is worse than protecting, for there the idea of beautification applies, so that he can sit in the sukkah and the sheet is subordinate to the sukkah, unlike here where it is only for the garment's need and perhaps it is forbidden. Therefore he wrote that it is permitted only not at the time of eating. One can also say that the Magen Avraham stated his words according to the main law, where we rule stringently like the first opinion; according to this it may not be permitted at the time of eating. This is somewhat forced. However we explain the Magen Avraham's intent, in practical law, since several later authorities agreed that one should be stringent like the first opinion, one certainly should not be lenient to spread a sheet to dry beneath the s'chach, even within four tefachim, unless it is not at the time of eating.
