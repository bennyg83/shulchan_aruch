(8) On a wet goose. The Tur speaks of bran first soaked in water. The Mechaber rules even when the bran itself is dry and only the goose is wet from water. This is a greater novelty, since even the small amount of moisture on the bird causes the bran to become chametz, and the goose is forbidden even if it is later washed only with fruit juice and not with water.
(9) That the goose is forbidden. Its skin contains many small holes in which the bran remains. Even rubbing and washing it afterward does not help.
(10) And this is the custom regarding the prohibition of chametz, etc. Regarding other prohibitions, the reasoning based on the small holes does not forbid the food unless the meat was hot, cooked, or roasted, so that it was softened and the prohibited substance penetrated deeply. Regarding chametz, however, which forbids even in the smallest amount, we are concerned even with raw meat that some chametz may remain in the holes of the goose. The acharonim write that even removing the entire skin does not permit it, because the bran penetrates through the skin into the depth of the meat.
(11) Because it forbids in any amount. Since the primary reason is that chametz forbids in any amount, on erev Pesach from the sixth hour until Pesach, when the prohibition is still nullified in sixty, the goose may be permitted after thorough rubbing and washing with water, provided it is eaten before Pesach. We are concerned that some visible chametz may remain and become significant again on Pesach. Some are lenient, in a case of major loss, even to eat it during Pesach.
(12) Not to singe, etc., over wheat or grain that became chametz. Meaning, grain upon which water fell after it was harvested, causing it to become chametz. The acharonim agree that this is merely a l'chatchilah precaution. According to the basic law, even if wheat was present there, we do not presume it had become chametz. Even if it was known to be chametz, the bird is not forbidden, since it does not touch the grain that supports it from above while the flame rises from below. If an empty ear of grain is found dissolved in a cooked dish, we do not suspect that a chametz kernel was present in the ear and dissolved into the food.
