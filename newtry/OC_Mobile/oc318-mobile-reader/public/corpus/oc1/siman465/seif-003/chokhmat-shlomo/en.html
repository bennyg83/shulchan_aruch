Se'if 3. In the gloss: And one should be careful not to singe the poultry with straw, lest there be wheat or chametz grain there. Note: See the Taz and Magen Avraham, who wrote that even if grain is found in it, we do not fear that there was more there. For I was asked by the rav of the holy community of Chorostkov regarding a case in which the upper husk of an oat stalk was found in the water, and they checked and there was no kernel in it: what is its law? And this is what I answered that rav regarding the question. Not only according to what the asking rav wrote, that the question was that the oat stalk was found on the cover over the water, on the piece with which they filter the water: it is obvious that even if it had been an actual oat stalk and split, it would likewise have been permitted, for we do not presume a prohibition from place to place, and one may attribute that it was in the jug with which they draw water to pour into the barrels, and it was there only briefly, and this water was not in the cistern from which they draw; and if so there is no prohibition at all. Rather, even according to what his honor wrote, that the question was that the husk was in the water, there is likewise no prohibition, since it was only a doubt of soaking, and if so it is a double doubt: perhaps there was no kernel in it at all; and even if you say there was, perhaps it did not remain overnight. And therefore, even regarding the Be'er Heitev that the later authorities brought, who is stringent when a hollow bone is found in a cooked dish out of concern that perhaps there was marrow in it, nevertheless in this he concedes, for here it is a double doubt, and this is only a doubt of a mere bit that is rabbinic, and it is not similar to the case of the Be'er Heitev, where there was a doubt according to the Torah. And aside from all this and that, it appears that even if this husk without a kernel is found in a cooked dish, it is likewise permitted, for this is a great principle in our hands: we are not to presume a greater defect than what has come before us. And not only according to the view of Minchas Yaakov, who argues with the Be'er Heitev regarding the law of a hollow bone, that obviously here too it is permitted; rather even according to the Be'er Heitev it appears that specifically regarding a hollow bone, since it is common in a bone that there be marrow in it, and the way of things is that the marrow dissolves within the pot, and the senses will testify to this: if one puts a bone full of marrow into the cooked dish, afterward he will take it out clean; therefore, since it is common, it does not enter the category of doubt at all. But here, although the way of things is that there be a kernel like the husk, nevertheless it is not the way for the kernel to be removed from the husk unless they took it by hand; and if it happens at times that [the kernel] falls from it by itself, the kernel is something uncommon and is not [common] in any event. And therefore, regarding anything that is not common, we are not to presume a defect from a doubt, and especially regarding a mere bit that is rabbinic. Therefore the matter is permitted without doubt, and so I have ruled several times to permit even in a cooked dish. And it is fine, and delve into it.
