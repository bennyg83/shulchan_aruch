One may not knead, etc. See the Taz, whose view is that only kneading and baking are not helped by a Jew standing over the worker, but for grinding, all agree it helps if a Jew stands and watches that no concern of chimutz occurs. Eliyah Rabbah, however, follows the Bach: grinding depends on the dispute cited in siman 453:4. If guarding lishmah is required from grinding, then this too cannot be done by a deaf-mute, fool, minor, or non-Jew, even with a Jew standing over them. If guarding lishmah is only from kneading onward, grinding may be done by a non-Jew or minor. Mateh Yehudah agrees. The Mechaber mentions kneading and baking because those are certain: all agree they are forbidden, for even one who permits taking flour from the market in pressing circumstances agrees that from kneading onward guarding lishmah is specifically required and it may not be done by a non-Jew or minor. So too appears from the Gra, that the Mechaber chose a case forbidden by all. Mateh Yehudah concludes that scrupulous people making matzah shemurah grind wheat only through a Jew with a hand mill, and likewise with a water mill, not through a non-Jew's animal-powered mill even with a Jew standing over it; so it is proper to practice and institute. Nevertheless, today's common custom is plainly like the Taz, to be lenient with grinding by a non-Jew who places the wheat into the hopper, even for matzah shemurah, provided a Jew stands and watches that no concern of chimutz arises. Chok Yaakov, Halakhah Berurah, and the Gra of Liadi copy his words as law, and so is implied in Rokeach siman 272. Still, one who can have all preparation of his matzot done by Jews is praiseworthy. Peri Megadim also questions the Taz somewhat and concludes that if possible, those careful with matzah shemurah should have Jews harvest lishmah and perform all acts, grinding and the like. Whether the requirement to guard specifically for the sake of matzah in kneading and baking, and according to some poskim also grinding, is Torah or rabbinic law is disputed. From the Bach it sounds rabbinic, and Chok Yaakov writes that it is only an asmachta on the verse "you shall guard the matzot." Peri Chadash agrees that it is Torah law.
Chok Yaakov brings proof from Rambam, chapter 5 of Hilkhot Chametz U-Matzah, halakhah 8, but Peri Megadim writes that there is no proof from there, because the Rambam wrote so only regarding grain that had not split, where by Torah law there is no prohibition because it is not chimutz, and Chazal forbade because complete guarding is needed. Responsa Rashba simanim 26 and 596, and Levush siman 454, also imply it is Torah law. In truth Peri Megadim is correct, as is proven from Rambam chapter 6 halakhah 9, where he writes that one does not fulfill with todah loaves because "you shall guard the matzot" is written, and he does not mention that this is rabbinic. It is still clearer in his commentary to the Mishnah, Pesachim chapter 2, where he writes that although it is complete matzah, one does not fulfill on Pesach because the Almighty said "you shall guard the matzot," and tradition explains: matzah guarded for the sake of matzah excludes this, which is guarded for a sacrifice. This proves it is a full derashah requiring guarding for matzah. Rava's requirement of extra guarding from harvest onward is only rabbinic and lechatchilah, as many Rishonim agree, including Ritva and Semag, but from kneading until after baking it is certainly Torah law and essential even bediavad. I later found that Beit Meir also challenges Beit Yosef and Chok Yaakov and agrees it is clearly Torah law, and notes that Peri Chadash already sensed this against the Bach. It may be that even the Bach does not hold it rabbinic; he wrote only regarding the elder who thought not to eat on the first night if a non-Jew made the matzah in his presence, that the Bach considered that concern rabbinic and not worth canceling the Torah positive command entirely. Chok Yosef likewise challenges Chok Yaakov with these proofs and writes that the main law follows Peri Chadash, that guarding lishmah is Torah law, and the Rambam's view is necessarily so; the Bach agrees, except that regarding whether a Jew standing over helps, he holds one should not cancel the mitzvah for that. Therefore one should be careful not to rely lechatchilah for mitzvah matzah on Rava's presumption that one who has reached the years of majority presumably brought signs, unless they are older in years, as Peri Megadim writes. He further writes that this lishmah should apparently be spoken explicitly, while bediavad thought suffices, for kneading and baking of matzah, because the default is lishmah; for grinding and harvesting this requires study. It seems from his words that if the Jew did not think during kneading and baking for the sake of matzah, even bediavad he does not fulfill. Rashi also implies this on Pesachim 38 regarding todah loaves, explaining that every guarding must be intended for mitzvah matzah. The default is unlike kodashim, where the sacrifice was originally consecrated for an olah or shelamim and therefore the service is presumed to remain for that purpose. Here, however, Ritva writes that if an adult Jew kneads the dough, since all he does for Pesach is done for that purpose, it is as if he explicitly said it is for the mitzvah; this applies specifically at kneading, whose default is so, but harvesting is not ordinarily for that purpose and requires explicit remembrance. A non-Jew, who has no share in the mitzvah, and likewise a deaf-mute, fool, or minor, does not act for the mitzvah even during kneading. Thus an adult Jew's default is lishmah. This is somewhat surprising compared to writing a Torah scroll, where divine names written without explicit lishmah are invalid; this requires study.
Mitzvah matzah. See what I wrote in Mishnah Berurah. See Rambam chapter 5 of Hilkhot Chametz U-Matzah, halakhah 9, where it is evident that in his view every matzah eaten on Pesach requires guarding. See Maggid Mishneh there, who brings support. Lechatchilah one should guard all the matzot, but the essential requirement is only for the kezayit eaten on the night of Pesach. The doughs of non-Jews mentioned in the Gemara, that a person may fill his stomach from them provided he eats a kezayit of matzah at the end, refers to the basic law or a place where one has only one kezayit, as in siman 482. Chayei Adam writes that the Gra was very particular about guarding all matzot eaten throughout Pesach, apparently following Maggid Mishneh. Know further that the basic law of non-Jewish doughs was not brought by the poskim. Beit Yosef at the end of siman 454 writes that their reason is that they hold like Rabbenu Chananel, that the case is where the non-Jew kneaded before a Jew, and the Jew saw that no chametz was mixed in and that it did not become chametz during kneading; only it was not guarded for matzah. If so, there is no new law. Levush, Peri Chadash, and Chemed Moshe agree. Eliyah Rabbah, however, writes that many commentators follow Rashi, that whenever it has no cracks or whitening, we do not suspect even if the Jew did not see the non-Jew knead it, and he writes that this is the view of the Rosh, Tur, Rabbenu Yerucham, Shibbolei HaLeket, Manhig, and Tzedah LaDerekh, and that one may rely on this in very pressing circumstances. But he apparently did not have the other Rishonim before him: Ramban, Ritva, and Maharam Chalavah all reject Rashi's view with several proofs and agree with Rabbenu Chananel. The Rif and Rambam omitted the law certainly because they explain like Rabbenu Chananel. It is difficult to say, as Eliyah Rabbah suggests, that they omitted it because it is obvious when there are no cracks or whitening; on the contrary, Rambam's plain language implies that guarding is needed even for the other days' matzot, as written above. Therefore one should not be lenient in this at all.
And one may not bake, etc. As is explained in the Gemara, from the time of baking, etc.; thus regarding baking all agree a Jew is required. See Beit Yosef on this, and the Bach disagrees with him. Magen Avraham writes similarly in the name of Mahariv, and all the poskim state plainly like the Mechaber, because even placing it into the oven requires guarding that it not remain opposite the oven mouth; a non-Jew is not concerned for this.
And a minor. See Mishnah Berurah, where we wrote about the mistake of allowing a minor to put water into the flour. An even greater error has spread among some people: they buy guarded flour for their matzot and nevertheless allow minors to put water into the flour. They are greatly mistaken, thinking that since the flour is from wheat guarded from harvest, they have fulfilled all requirements. This is a great error, for in every case the main issue is kneading and baking. Therefore all actions, placing water into flour, kneading, arranging, and some say also perforating, and baking, must be done by adults, and all explicitly for the sake of mitzvah matzah. They need not say it for every matzah; each person should say that whatever he does today in this matzah matter is all for the sake of the mitzvah of matzah. I remember in my youth that the custom in Israel was that while baking matzot, the owner of the matzot repeatedly told the workers to remember that it was mitzvah matzah. In our day this custom has weakened because many meticulous people take matzah shemurah for the seder nights, and matzah shemurah is baked with all kinds of care, since usually they take it from the city rabbi or other Torah scholars; therefore people are not careful with ordinary matzot. But in truth our gain has become our loss, because most people ultimately do not take matzah shemurah and fulfill with ordinary matzah. If minors are involved in kneading, arranging, and the like, they do not fulfill the mitzvah of matzah. Therefore the earlier custom was better, when the owner answered and said "mitzvah matzah" before everyone, for that is like standing over them and at least fulfills according to the second opinion.
