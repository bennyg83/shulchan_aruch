1) [Se'if 1] Mitzvah matzah may not be kneaded, etc. The verse states, 'You shall guard the matzos,' requiring guarding for the sake of matzah. Tur; Levush. Regarding whether guarding for the sake of matzah is required under Torah or rabbinic law, Bach implies that the requirement is rabbinic. Peri Chadash writes that it is required under Torah law. This also appears from Responsa Rashba, simanim 26 and 593, and Levush, siman 454, se'if 4. See there. Mishbetzos Zahav, subsection 1. See there, where he writes that no proof can be brought from Rambam, chapter 5, halachah 8, cited by Chok Yaakov, subsection 2. See there. Chok Yosef, subsection 1, writes likewise. See there. See above, siman 454, subsection 42.
2) There. Mitzvah matzah may not be kneaded, etc. Other matzos do not require guarding. Nevertheless, the Jewish people are holy and customarily guard all matzos. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1. See above, siman 453, subsection 60, and below, subsection 9.
3) There. Mitzvah matzah may not be kneaded, etc. Even if others stand over the workers and warn them to act for the sake of the mitzvah, one does not fulfill the obligation with it. Responsa Rashba, siman 26, cited by Beis Yosef; Maggid Mishneh, chapter 5, halachah 5; Levush; Olas Shabbos, subsection 1; Taz, subsection 1. Rav Hai Gaon, however, writes that matzah baked by a non-Jew in a Jew's presence under proper supervision may be eaten by a Jew. Tur cites this. Peri Chadash writes that Rav Hai permits this specifically regarding a non-Jew, not a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor. Nehar Shalom, however, writes that a non-Jew was mentioned as the greater novelty, and such Jewish persons are certainly included. See there. Beis Yosef likewise writes in the name of Orchos Chaim that Rash, Rif, and Ramban maintain that there is no concern about who kneads or bakes. See there. Derashos Maharil similarly writes that if an adult stands over the worker, it is acceptable. Only a non-Jew is prohibited even when a Jew stands over the work, because a non-Jew acts with personal intent. See there. Mateh Moshe, siman 583, cites this. Regarding the practical ruling, Peri Chadash writes that we have only the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch: if kneading or baking was performed by a non-Jew, deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, one does not fulfill the obligation even after the fact, even when a Jew stood over the worker and issued instructions. This follows Responsa Rashba, siman 26, cited by Beis Yosef. See there. Shulchan Gavoah, subsection 1, cites this and writes that it is the primary ruling. Mateh Yehudah writes likewise. In pressing circumstances, however, such as when one is elderly, ill, or held in non-Jewish custody, Bach writes that one may rely upon Rav Hai Gaon and those who agree with him, and fulfill the obligation with matzah kneaded and baked by a non-Jew in a Jew's presence. Taz, there, and Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2, write likewise. Magen Avraham at the beginning of this siman likewise writes that when no alternative is possible, a non-Jew may perform the work while a Jew stands over it and tells the worker to act for the sake of Pesach. It is preferable for the Jew to complete part of the work. Nevertheless, it is better to use a minor than a non-Jew. Chok Yosef, subsection 1; Eshel Avraham, at the beginning of this siman; Erech HaShulchan, subsection 1; Chemed Moshe, subsection 1; Chayei Adam, rule 128, subsection 31, write likewise. Chok Yaakov, subsection 2, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1, write that it is better to use a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor than a non-Jew. It is possible that Magen Avraham agrees, and his reference to a minor was not exclusive. Machatzis HaShekel writes that a deaf person and a minor are preferable to a mentally incompetent person, and a deaf person is preferable to a minor. See there.
3) Know that even according to the lenient authorities who permit the work when a competent Jew stands over the worker, it is insufficient to say once before the work begins that the worker should intend to prepare mitzvah matzah. The worker must be repeatedly warned throughout the process not to divert attention from this intent. Mishkenos Yaakov, siman 21.
4) Nevertheless, a Jew who deliberately rejects observance out of defiance is worse than a non-Jew, because such a person acts with personal intent, and one does not fulfill the obligation at all. If the person violates for convenience rather than defiance, one should not eat matzah that person prepared outside a Jew's presence, because the work requires exceptional care and the person will not exert the necessary effort. Eshel Avraham, there.
5) If a non-Jew, deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor kneaded while no Jew stood directly over the work but a Jew observed from a distance, then according to Bach, who holds that guarding is rabbinic, one should eat it on the first night. It appears that one should eat it without a blessing. Even where such persons kneaded while a Jew stood over them, whether a blessing should be recited requires consideration. Mishbetzos Zahav, subsection 1. It appears that this applies only when the Jew did not assist at all. If the Jew assisted somewhat and told the worker to act for the sake of the mitzvah, it is obvious that the blessing may be recited. See above, siman 11, se'if 2, and our words there, with the help of Heaven. Analyze carefully.
6) A person arrived on the eve of Pesach in a non-Jewish village and found that a non-Jew had already placed flour in a kneading trough. The Jew quickly poured in the water for the sake of matzah, while the non-Jew mixed and kneaded it. Immediately after kneading, the Jew separated a piece of dough, formed it into a small loaf, and baked it over coals. The Jew had no other matzah except one olive-sized quantity of guarded matzah from the previous year, carried on the journey as a protective object. If there is absolutely no way to make other matzah in such pressing circumstances, the Jew should recite Kiddush, recite HaMotzi and the blessing over eating matzah upon this loaf, and after eating it consume the olive-sized quantity from the previous year as the afikoman. Michtam LeDavid, siman 20; Birkei Yosef, siman 482, subsection 5; Ikarei HaDat, siman 19, subsection 2; Pis'chei Teshuvah. It appears that this applies specifically in a place where wheat is not soaked. Otherwise, the matzah is prohibited for consumption, as stated above in siman 453, subsection 87. It also assumes that the kneading trough was clean, as stated above in siman 451, subsection 194. See there. See above, siman 453, subsection 14.
7) There. Mitzvah matzah may not be kneaded, etc. This appears to discuss wheat milled by a Jew. If it was milled by a non-Jew, deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, then even if a competent Jew kneaded and baked it, one does not fulfill the obligation, as Rosh writes in the name of She'iltos and Tur cites above in siman 453. Bach. This implies that even a Jew standing over the milling does not help, just as with kneading and baking. Taz, subsection 1, however, writes that only kneading and baking may not be performed by a non-Jew, because they require guarding for the sake of matzah and a non-Jew acts with personal intent even when a Jew stands over the work and warns the worker to intend the mitzvah, as Beis Yosef writes in the name of Responsa Rashba. Milling wheat, however, may be performed by a non-Jew if a Jew stands over it. He writes that this is the custom in all places without water mills, where non-Jews mill by hand while a Jew stands over them, and there is no concern. See there. Chok Yaakov, subsection 2; Beis Hillel, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, siman 453, subsection 16, write likewise. Mateh Yehudah, however, writes that the Shulchan Aruch's implication that guarding for the sake of matzah is required only from kneading onward, not during milling or sifting, follows its own position in siman 453 permitting flour purchased in the marketplace. According to Rif, who requires guarding from harvesting, one likewise does not fulfill the obligation if a non-Jew milled it. He writes that meticulous people preparing guarded matzah were observed milling the wheat at home by Jewish workers using a hand mill, not at a non-Jew's animal-powered mill even with a Jew standing over it. He writes that this is their custom and is how people should act and instruct others. See there. Machazik Berachah, subsection 3, however, writes that most communities customarily use mitzvah matzah guarded from harvesting, yet the milling is performed by a non-Jew. He explains that the milling is powered by animals or the like, while the non-Jew merely places wheat into the apparatus. Throughout the milling, the Jew stands there and intends it for the mitzvah. The Jew is the principal participant, the non-Jew's intent is nullified, and we disregard it because the non-Jew is not personally performing the milling. With a hand mill operated directly by a non-Jew, however, he writes that one should be stringent and not have a non-Jew mill this way, because the milling is produced by the direct force of an unqualified person. See the full discussion there. Nowadays, however, we have neither heard nor seen anyone mill at a non-Jew's mill, because Jewish-owned mills are plentiful. If some remote place lacks a Jewish mill, one may rely upon the permitting authorities because this is a pressing circumstance. The millstones, all milling implements, and the milling area must first be picked clean and cleaned, as stated above in siman 453, se'if 8, and our words there, with the help of Heaven. If possible, the Jew should personally place the wheat into the mill and declare that it is for the sake of mitzvah matzah.
