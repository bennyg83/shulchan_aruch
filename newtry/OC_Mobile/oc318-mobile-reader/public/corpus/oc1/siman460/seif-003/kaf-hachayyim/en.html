3) There. Mitzvah matzah may not be kneaded, etc. Even if others stand over the workers and warn them to act for the sake of the mitzvah, one does not fulfill the obligation with it. Responsa Rashba, siman 26, cited by Beis Yosef; Maggid Mishneh, chapter 5, halachah 5; Levush; Olas Shabbos, subsection 1; Taz, subsection 1. Rav Hai Gaon, however, writes that matzah baked by a non-Jew in a Jew's presence under proper supervision may be eaten by a Jew. Tur cites this. Peri Chadash writes that Rav Hai permits this specifically regarding a non-Jew, not a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor. Nehar Shalom, however, writes that a non-Jew was mentioned as the greater novelty, and such Jewish persons are certainly included. See there. Beis Yosef likewise writes in the name of Orchos Chaim that Rash, Rif, and Ramban maintain that there is no concern about who kneads or bakes. See there. Derashos Maharil similarly writes that if an adult stands over the worker, it is acceptable. Only a non-Jew is prohibited even when a Jew stands over the work, because a non-Jew acts with personal intent. See there. Mateh Moshe, siman 583, cites this. Regarding the practical ruling, Peri Chadash writes that we have only the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch: if kneading or baking was performed by a non-Jew, deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, one does not fulfill the obligation even after the fact, even when a Jew stood over the worker and issued instructions. This follows Responsa Rashba, siman 26, cited by Beis Yosef. See there. Shulchan Gavoah, subsection 1, cites this and writes that it is the primary ruling. Mateh Yehudah writes likewise. In pressing circumstances, however, such as when one is elderly, ill, or held in non-Jewish custody, Bach writes that one may rely upon Rav Hai Gaon and those who agree with him, and fulfill the obligation with matzah kneaded and baked by a non-Jew in a Jew's presence. Taz, there, and Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2, write likewise. Magen Avraham at the beginning of this siman likewise writes that when no alternative is possible, a non-Jew may perform the work while a Jew stands over it and tells the worker to act for the sake of Pesach. It is preferable for the Jew to complete part of the work. Nevertheless, it is better to use a minor than a non-Jew. Chok Yosef, subsection 1; Eshel Avraham, at the beginning of this siman; Erech HaShulchan, subsection 1; Chemed Moshe, subsection 1; Chayei Adam, rule 128, subsection 31, write likewise. Chok Yaakov, subsection 2, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1, write that it is better to use a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor than a non-Jew. It is possible that Magen Avraham agrees, and his reference to a minor was not exclusive. Machatzis HaShekel writes that a deaf person and a minor are preferable to a mentally incompetent person, and a deaf person is preferable to a minor. See there.
3) Know that even according to the lenient authorities who permit the work when a competent Jew stands over the worker, it is insufficient to say once before the work begins that the worker should intend to prepare mitzvah matzah. The worker must be repeatedly warned throughout the process not to divert attention from this intent. Mishkenos Yaakov, siman 21.
