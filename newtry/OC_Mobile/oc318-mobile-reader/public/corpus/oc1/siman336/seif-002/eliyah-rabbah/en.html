[4] Tree roots, etc. The same applies to cabbage stalks, as stated by Yam Shel Shlomo and the Magen Avraham.
[5] It is permitted to walk, etc. The Taz limits the permission to walking because one's hands are distant, while actual use is prohibited. I have already written that this is incorrect. Walking is actually the greater novelty because something is more likely to be detached incidentally while walking, especially barefoot when it may be caught between the toes. Even so it is permitted.
[6] That one waters, etc. One is liable because of sowing. If one eats in another person's garden, some permit liquid to spill there because it is an inevitable consequence from which he derives no benefit. Sefer Zichronot, page 51, limits this to a non-Jew's garden. The Gemara does not imply that, for it speaks of another person's land, as the Magen Avraham notes. In my humble opinion, the Gemara discusses a case where it is known that he does not want the benefit, but ordinarily we assume a person is pleased even by improvement to his friend's property. So too it appears from Sefer Yereim, siman 102. One should not be lenient even in a non-Jew's garden, because the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 320 writes that most authorities hold there remains a rabbinic prohibition even when the result is unwanted. The carrying law of a garden will be explained in siman 358.
