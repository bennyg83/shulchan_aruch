[1] [Levush] Even if there are no, etc. This requires study. The Beit Yosef rules this because the Rif and Rambam hold so. But according to the Bach's explanation that the Rif permits a dry tree, the primary ruling should follow the Rosh and Tur, who permit when there are no branches. The Taz's question that this would make the Rif identical to the Rosh is imprecise. There is a major distinction: according to the Rosh, permission exists only when there are no branches, whereas according to the Rif even with branches and leaves it is permitted if dry, because an unintended consequence is permitted. The Perishah likewise explains the Rif as the Bach does and asks against the Beit Yosef that in Perek Mashilin the Rif gives the reason as lest one climb and detach something, implying that where that concern does not apply, such as a dry tree, it is permitted. If even a dry tree were prohibited, that should have been cited explicitly. The plain sense of Eruvin 100 and 78b also implies leniency. See siman 372, seif katan 20. The Maggid Mishneh's explanation that Rav found an unlearned population and imposed extra stringency is strained, since in many places such local stringency does not become the general halachah. Tosafot and the Agudah there say explicitly that the prohibition applies only where branches may break off. Tzeidah LaDerech rules likewise, and so too it appears from Yam Shel Shlomo in Perek Mashilin.
[2] And some say, etc. This is the primary ruling, as stated by Nachalat Tzvi.
[3] Soft like vegetables, etc. The Bach argues at length against the Rama. He says that according to Ravina in Eruvin 34, who explains the decree as lest one cut, the prohibition applies even to a soft reed, and Rav Nachman's incident disagrees with Ravina; he understands the Tur in siman 409 accordingly. The Taz likewise argues from that Gemara and adds that the Shulchan Aruch prohibits even vegetables and grasses because it says one may not use anything attached to the ground, following the Rambam. This is unconvincing. Rav Nachman's incident appears to accord with everyone. Otherwise the positions would be reversed: for Ravina soft growth would be worse than hard, while for Rav Nachman soft would be permitted and hard prohibited. Rather, there is no dispute. Ravina's concern lest one cut refers to a hard reed, though relative to a tree it is called soft. He agrees that a reed truly soft like a vegetable is permitted, as Torat Chaim implies. Moreover, the Rambam and Bartenura explain the concern with reed and thistle because detached reeds are often stuck into the ground and look attached, so one may cut an actually attached reed thinking it detached. This concern does not apply to genuinely soft growth, grasses, or vegetables. The Taz's astonishment at the Rama is itself surprising, because Shulchan Aruch siman 312, seif 6, explicitly permits like the Rama. Thus the statement of the Shulchan Aruch and Rambam that one may not use anything attached to the ground at all refers to the tree mentioned first, meaning even a dry tree without branches or leaves. This also resolves the Levush's wording. Otherwise there was no need to state the law of a tree separately. So too it appears from seif 10 regarding myrtle. The Taz's statement there that one does not take it in hand is not implied by the halachic authorities. I also found the Riaz in Shiltei Giborim, page 197, and Avodat HaKodesh of the Rashba, page 35, ruling like the Rama, and the Beit Yosef in siman 312 cites Rabbenu Yerucham similarly. Therefore one may rely on the Rama and Levush without hesitation. The Rashba concludes that reeds destined to harden are considered like trees even while soft and are prohibited, whereas marsh reeds that never harden are permitted like grasses whether small or large.
