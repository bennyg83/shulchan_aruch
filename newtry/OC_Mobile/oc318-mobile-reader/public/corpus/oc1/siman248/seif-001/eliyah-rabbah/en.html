[1] Fewer than three days, etc. Meaning that it is forbidden on Wednesday; see seif 4. This is the Magen Avraham's view. Bach and Olas Tamid, however, rule that it is permitted on Wednesday, for this appears from the Rif, Rambam, and Rosh, who explain the prohibition as due to impairment of Shabbos enjoyment, and likewise from the Perishah, who explains that it resembles swimming. So far their words. This would indeed appear primary, but I found that Raavan, siman 60, cites the Yerushalmi: One may not embark on Friday, Thursday, or Wednesday. Moreover, the explanation based on swimming necessarily requires, in my humble opinion, that departure more than three days beforehand is permitted because it belongs to the period following the preceding Shabbos, and one is not required to refrain because of the coming Shabbos, as the Beit Yosef writes according to the Ran's explanation and as stated in seif 4. Bach, seif katan 4, writes similarly according to the view that the reason is techumin. If so, Wednesday is certainly forbidden. The same follows from the Beit Yosef's explanation that the enactment serves as a recognizable safeguard lest one make a swimmer's barrel. Although that explanation is strained, they presumably required the safeguard to be outside the days immediately preceding Shabbos. Furthermore, even the Rif and Rosh may forbid Wednesday, because on Wednesday as well one lacks Shabbos enjoyment. The Rosh ultimately concludes that the Yerushalmi disagrees with our Babylonian Gemara, and the Rivash, siman 101, writes similarly in the name of the Raavad. See there. I likewise found this in Rokeach, siman 197.
[2] A journey of one day, etc. Meaning with a favorable wind. Even if no favorable wind is presently blowing, it is permitted. Likewise, for a journey of two days it is permitted to depart on Thursday, as the Beit Yosef writes.
[3] Levush: And for the sake of a mitzvah, etc. It appears to me that it is permitted even when the ship is below ten handbreadths. Although the Rosh forbids this because of techumin, we rule that before the final three days departure is permitted for a discretionary purpose, following Maharik and not the Rosh; correspondingly, for a mitzvah it is permitted even on Friday. Moreover, Bach writes that even the Rosh permits it in this case, and his remarks in seif katan 4 likewise imply that he rules leniently. This is further demonstrated by Tosafot and Sefer HaTerumos, who explain the prohibition as due to swimming, yet nevertheless permit departure for a mitzvah and do not say that the Sages maintained their prohibition even in the face of a positive commandment. The same appears from Maharshal's commentary to Semag. It also follows from the permission in seif 4 to depart for a mitzvah on Friday even though the Jew himself may ultimately perform a prohibited act, as stated by the Rivash. All this is contrary to Olas Tamid, who rules categorically at the end of this siman that it is forbidden when he will come to desecrate Shabbos or pass beyond the techum, and claims that this is also the Rama's view. His statement that Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah likewise rules so is inaccurate, for that work says so specifically according to the Radbaz. Regarding one who joins a caravan to ascend to Eretz Yisrael, since we rule permissively, the same should apply here. This will be explained further below in seif katan 12, also contrary to the Magen Avraham at the end of this siman.
[4] Levush: Even on Friday, etc. The Magen Avraham writes that on Shabbos itself it is forbidden because he cannot stipulate with the non-Jew that he rest; see siman 613. His intent is based on his question below in seif katan 8: the Tur appears to contradict himself, because he permits swimming on Yom Kippur for a mitzvah, while here he requires stipulating that the non-Jew rest because of the concern of swimming. He answers that here the non-Jew performs labor for the Jew. So far his words. This is incorrect, for Tosafot, Sefer HaTerumos, Semag, and the Tur clearly imply that the case applies even when the non-Jew performs no labor. I am also astonished by his question, because he did not examine the Tur carefully: the Tur rules like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that one need not stipulate that they rest. Even apart from this, one may say that the Tur means only that initially he should stipulate; if that is impossible, it is permitted without a stipulation, as explained in seif katan 5. Furthermore, I found in Shibbolei HaLeket, page 14, that he straightforwardly interprets the Gemara as permitting travel for a mitzvah even on Shabbos. This resolves the Beit Yosef's difficulty at the beginning of siman 339 concerning the Agur; see there. The Beit Yosef likewise implies this, for after citing the Ramban that people customarily embarked on Friday and on Shabbos, he writes that one may defend this practice because all their purposes are considered mitzvah needs. Thus, it is permitted even on Shabbos. The end of the Beit Yosef in this siman, however, somewhat implies otherwise; see there and examine carefully. One cannot challenge this from siman 613, seif 7, where the Shulchan Aruch writes that some forbid crossing in a small boat, based on Knesses HaGedolah, for one may answer that they were more stringent on Yom Kippur, unlike the Beit Yosef there. I also find the Shulchan Aruch there difficult: the Tur's language, “to cross by swimming,” implies actual swimming, whereas traveling in a boat is certainly not actual swimming but only resembles it, as Sefer HaTerumos, siman 225, and Mordechai, first chapter of Shabbos, write. Rambam and those who follow him do not accept this concern at all, as one who examines their words will see. Nevertheless, regarding a ship on which labor is performed, if most of its passengers are Jews, the Rivash, siman 101, rules that it is forbidden on Shabbos even when non-Jews perform the labor. This too requires study: why should it differ from a mitzvah war discussed at the end of this siman? The Ran implies that they are equivalent. The same prohibition applies on Yom Tov, even on the second day of Yom Tov and even in private.
