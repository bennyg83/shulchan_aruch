Even. See Taz. His intent may be to explain Rama’s statement, “even if it became mixed before Pesach,” in accordance with Maharil. Wine into which mustard was placed to preserve its sweetness may not be drunk on Pesach, although it may be retained until after Pesach. But if it fell into a cooked dish that cannot be kept until after Pesach, it does not prohibit the mixture. See siman 453 and Chok Yaakov here, who explains that because the mustard preserves the wine it may be considered as though it remains distinct. Rama’s statement that mustard without flour may be retained applies equally to mustard with flour but without water. Possibly, according to those who are concerned for Rashi’s view in siman 462, it should not be retained, and it is preferable to sell it to a non Jew. Nevertheless, it appears that one need not make an extraordinary effort to sell it, since in a pressing situation it is permitted even to knead with such a mixture and eat it. What Rama cites from Rokeach, that mustard should not be ground in a chametz bowl, is because its sharpness together with pressure causes the vessel to emit absorbed flavor and to absorb in return. The leniencies of a vessel that is not ben yomo and of secondary transferred flavor do not apply. See siman 447. Our wheat flour, when it has been moistened before grinding, is chametz and must be sold even without any later addition of water. If “mixed before Pesach” means an actual mixture that can simply be kept until after Pesach, this is only a ruling for the initial situation. One should not infer that a mixture of less than sixty is permitted for eating on Pesach, since it may be that the mustard remains recognizable or functions as a preservative. Yet because the authorities did not explicitly require sixty, Chok Yaakov in siman 453 and here understands that after the fact it may be permitted even in less than sixty. This will be explained further in Magen Avraham, seif katan 1.
