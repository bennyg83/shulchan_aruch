Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Mordechai, etc. “It is possible that anything that cleans suffices; see the end of siman 4.” Meaning, wherever washing the hands permits holding the Sefer Torah, any cleansing substance likewise suffices, just as it does for prayer, as explained at the end of siman 4.
“If so, all the more so the poles.” Meaning, since everything attached to a sacred scroll imparts impurity to the hands like the scroll itself, all the more so the poles, which are attached to the scroll and are more essential to it than its case, must impart impurity to the hands.
The reason they impart impurity to the hands. Meaning, the reason a sacred scroll imparts impurity to the hands is the statement of Rabbi Parnach that one who holds a Sefer Torah bare is buried bare, meaning without reward for the mitzvah in which he was engaged while holding it bare. To prevent people from this prohibition, the Sages decreed that hands touching the scroll become impure. Since people were extremely careful regarding ritual purity, as stated in Yoma, they would consequently avoid holding it bare. Since the poles also impart impurity to the hands, it must be that they too may not be held bare, as Bach writes. Otherwise, why would the Sages decree impurity upon touching the poles?
Seif katan 1: Sacred writings, etc. So write Tosafos, Shabbos 14a.
For they impart impurity to the hands. As stated in the Mishnah, Yadayim chapter 3, and ruled by Rambam, Hilchos Avos HaTumah chapter 9. Since they impart impurity, it must be forbidden to hold them bare. Otherwise, why was impurity decreed upon them?
However, in Chagigah, etc. “The statement of Rabbi Parnach does not apply,” meaning his ruling that one may not hold a Sefer Torah bare, as explained above.
This is what Tosafos asked there, s.v. detnan, etc. Tosafos writes: “Rabbeinu Elchanan found it difficult: what proof did Rabbi Yehoshua bring from sacred writings?” The context is the Mishnah in Yadayim chapter 3. Rabbi Yehoshua holds that anything that invalidates terumah, namely a second degree of impurity that creates a third degree in terumah, imparts impurity to hands so that they become second degree. Likewise, one ordinary hand, which is treated as second degree, imparts impurity to the other hand if it touches a hand that had already been washed and was pure. The Sages disagree and hold that a second degree does not create another second degree. Rabbi Yehoshua proves that a second degree can create another second degree from sacred writings, which are second degree and nevertheless impart impurity to hands. Tosafos objects that sacred writings are different because of Rabbi Parnach’s statement that one who holds a Sefer Torah bare loses the reward for the mitzvah. Ri answers that Rabbi Yehoshua’s proof concerns a case of a sacred writing where Rabbi Parnach’s rule does not apply, such as tefillin straps together with the tefillin. One must then explain why impurity was decreed upon tefillin and their straps. Perhaps this follows Rambam’s explanation of the Mishnah there. Shabbos explains that initially they would place terumah beside sacred writings, reasoning that terumah is sacred and the scroll is sacred, so they may be placed together. Mice then came because of the terumah and damaged the scrolls. The Sages therefore decreed that sacred writings render terumah impure, preventing people from placing them together. Rambam writes that to sustain this decree they further decreed that sacred writings impart impurity to the hands, for without the latter decree the former would not endure. According to Rambam, the Gemara’s additional reason based on Rabbi Parnach must mean that neither reason alone was sufficient to decree impurity upon the hands. Rambam’s reason alone, that otherwise the decree concerning terumah would not endure, and Rabbi Parnach’s reason alone were each weak; only together did they justify the decree. Tosafos may likewise hold this view. Once the Sages decreed that a Sefer Torah imparts impurity to the hands because both reasons apply, they extended the decree to tefillin straps together with the tefillin, even though Rabbi Parnach’s reason does not apply there, because Rambam’s reason still applies. Although Rambam’s reason alone would not have been sufficient to initiate the decree, once it had already been enacted for a Sefer Torah, they extended it to tefillin. Alternatively, Tosafos may hold, without Rambam’s reason, that impurity was decreed upon tefillin straps because the Sages did not distinguish among sacred writings. According to either explanation, however, there would be no proof that sacred writings may not be held bare. Their imparting impurity might be like tefillin. Tosafos in Chagigah would then disagree with Tosafos in Shabbos, who prove that sacred writings may not be held bare. This cannot be, because why were Tosafos in Chagigah forced to interpret Rabbi Yehoshua’s proof from sacred writings as referring to tefillin straps together with the tefillin? They could have said that it refers to ordinary sacred writings, to which Rabbi Parnach’s rule likewise does not apply. It must be that Tosafos in Chagigah also hold that sacred writings may not be held bare, and their proof must likewise be that sacred writings impart impurity to the hands, as Tosafos write in Shabbos. This remains difficult: perhaps sacred writings may indeed be held bare, and their impurity is like that of tefillin. One must answer with some difficulty that the principle that the Sages did not distinguish can extend the decree to tefillin and their straps only if all other sacred writings are forbidden to be held bare and impart impurity by their essential law. Tefillin can then be included because they may be confused with other sacred writings. But if sacred writings themselves were permitted to be held bare, one could not explain that they impart impurity merely because the Sages did not distinguish them from a Sefer Torah, since sacred writings are not readily confused with a Sefer Torah. Once sacred writings are forbidden to be held bare and impart impurity by their essential law, however, the decree may also include tefillin and straps, since tefillin may be confused with sacred writings.
Rambam writes without qualification in Hilchos Avos HaTumah, chapter 9: “Not only words of Torah, but sacred writings, even Shir HaShirim and Koheles, impart impurity to the hands.” Since Rambam did not exclude Megillas Esther, it appears that we do not rule like Shmuel in Megillah 7a, who says that Megillas Esther does not impart impurity to the hands because it was not given to be written. This is a dispute among Tannaim there, and Rambam did not rule like Shmuel.
