[2] Among those, etc. “And they are accustomed to purchase it,” etc. This means that nowadays it is customary to purchase the privilege. In the time of the Gemara, it belonged to the greatest person without payment even if another offered money. Magen Avraham understood the statement as referring to the time of the Gemara and was therefore forced into a strained explanation. This is not precise. The customs of purchasing the privileges of taking out and returning the Sefer Torah are based on “In a multitude of people is the King’s glory.” Maharil. Knesses HaGedolah, siman 153, cites Mahari Bruna regarding two people who jointly purchased mitzvos and stipulated that whenever one was absent from the synagogue, the other would take his privilege in his place. If one died, the condition is void. It appears that the privilege passes to his heirs. Similarly, Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes here that when one purchased a privilege and died, even where there is a holder of deficiencies, meaning the local practice of purchasing the right to fill vacancies, the privilege belongs to the heirs. Where there is an established custom, however, everything follows the custom. He concludes that the heirs must attend to the mitzvah and pay the congregation the amount their ancestor pledged, or sell the privilege and pay the congregation any shortfall. If they sell it for more, the profit belongs to the heirs. Olas Tamid, end of siman 144, objects that one may not profit from mitzvos funded by charity, and therefore the profit belongs to the congregation. In my humble opinion, the principle that one may not profit does not apply here. There, as stated in Kiddushin, one does not profit from consecrated property because “there is no poverty in a place of wealth,” and from charity funds because poor people may arrive and there may be nothing available to give them. None of those reasons applies here. Since he purchased the privilege for himself and paid the price to charity, it became his. Indeed, this occurs every day: even the owner of a privilege sometimes sells it to another when necessary and profits from it. This is also implied by Magen Avraham, siman 306, seif katan 15. Responsa Nachalas Shivah, siman 4, writes that money raised from the sale of mitzvos is fundamentally intended for the poor and not for sacred utensils, although the gabbaim may redirect it. See there also regarding using it for taxes. Emek Berachah, siman 5, states that one may not retain the mitzvah money for himself, but must give it to the gabbaim. Responsa Tzemach David, siman 72, writes that if one wished to purchase a privilege for twenty and another bid thirty, but the second bidder is not financially reliable, the gabbai may compel the first to take it for twenty. Magen Avraham, end of siman 155, copied his words concerning a case where the second bidder later withdrew, and wrote that this is not the accepted practice. I am astonished, for the responsum itself clearly states that when the second bidder withdraws the practice is to compel him to pay the entire amount. Only where he is financially unreliable and the gabbaim refuse to allow him to purchase it must the first bidder take it for twenty, for the second is treated as nonexistent. It is possible to follow this ruling. Yam Shel Shlomo, Beitzah chapter 5, writes that the pious of Austria practiced that whatever a person uttered as a bid, even if another then increased the price and retained the privilege, he nevertheless gave to charity the amount he had already pronounced. If one purchased a privilege for a year and during that year the king expelled the Jews, if he knew of the danger at the time of purchase he should have stipulated. See Responsa Menahem Azariah, siman 64, and Magen Avraham there. Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes that where the custom is to purchase the right to fill vacancies, or if the purchaser himself travels to another city or becomes ill, he may not appoint an agent in his place.
