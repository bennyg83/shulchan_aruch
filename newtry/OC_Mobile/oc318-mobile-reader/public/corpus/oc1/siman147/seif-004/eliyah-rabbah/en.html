Magen Avraham likewise writes that if one changed their form and made a different utensil from them, it is permitted, for the laver was made from the mirrors of the women who assembled. This proof is difficult, because there the reason, as Rashi explains at the end of Vayakhel, was that those mirrors were more beloved than all the other donations, since through them the women established many hosts of Israel. Ramban, Gur Aryeh, and Divrei David further explain that they were polished copper, and even after being made into the laver their appearance did not change. See there. Magen Avraham would have done better to prove the point from the nose-rings and earrings mentioned earlier. Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes that the restriction applies specifically to accessories of sanctity, but accessories of a mitzvah, such as a bag for a tallis, are permitted. Beis Hillel, siman 139, writes that even the tallis itself is permitted, as is also implied by Responsa Bach, siman 17. Taz in Yoreh Deah, siman 282, cites Responsa Maharil as ruling that accessories of a Sefer Torah should not be made from a garment used by an ordinary person, deriving this from the exposition concerning the altar, and that those who do so should be protested. I examined the responsum itself, siman 122, and it does not say that one should protest. Rather, it states: “Although people commonly make them from old garments, Mar bar Ravina did not sign his approval of them. When asked, I tell them to purchase new garments for this purpose; therefore, new is preferable.” This implies that one should not protest. When asked, one tells them not to purchase old garments, but if they already purchased them perhaps they are permitted. His conclusion, “new is preferable,” likewise implies that old garments are not forbidden. It appears that for this reason Levush included the case in his first clause together with a torn cloth, where there is no prohibition. He was also precise in writing that the rule was supported by a verse, rather than using Maharil’s expression that it was derived from the verse, since it is only an asmachta. This will be explained further at the end of siman 153. Taz there writes that the restriction applies only to the accessory of sanctity itself, while a secondary accessory mentioned in siman 154 may be made from old materials. Magen Avraham objects that our paroches is a secondary accessory, yet Maharil forbids making it from old materials. Perhaps in Maharil’s time they would sometimes place the Sefer Torah on the paroches, as Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes, making it a direct accessory. However, Mordechai’s explanation in the name of Ri is difficult. He writes that although regarding a tallis we would not forbid a garment that an ordinary person had used, and so forth. This implies that in matters of sanctity even a secondary accessory is forbidden. Perhaps he mentioned a tallis only to teach the greater novelty in the latter clause, that one made by non-Jews is forbidden. If an accessory of sanctity was already made from such material, it is forbidden afterward to make from it a secondary accessory. Although we are stringent not to make from it a direct accessory of sanctity, we do not therefore say leniently that no sanctity ever took effect upon it. See Responsa Bach there. Taz there, seif katan 8, writes that if one says, “This money is for an accessory of a Sefer Torah,” the rule that a declaration to sacred use is binding does not apply, since he may sell the item. That rule applies specifically to charity. I found the same in Responsa Maharil there: even if one purchased fabric for a wrapping cloth, he may retract, although he originally vowed to provide a cloth for the Sefer Torah. Nevertheless, he must fulfill his vow by purchasing another mantle. See below, siman 153, seif katan 21.
