Know that what is written in Shulchan Aruch se'if 1, 'and even if it grew in the wilderness,' etc. is the view of the Rosh and his teachers. But Tosafot disagree, and this is from what is taught on daf 33b: 'arvei nachal' - I know only aravot of a river; from where do I know rain-fed ones and mountain ones? The verse says 'arvei' in any case.
Tosafot hold that the inclusion of rain-fed and mountain aravot is from the extra expression 'arvei.' Rabbi Akiva and the Rabbis disagree: according to Rabbi Akiva, one aravah is sufficient; and according to the Rabbis, two are required, because it is written 'arvei' in plural language.
Since we rule like the Rabbis, who require two aravot from the verse's word 'arvei,' we can no longer derive from the word 'arvei' to include rain-fed and mountain aravot, for one cannot learn two derivations from one inclusion. Therefore they hold that rain-fed and mountain aravot are invalid. But the Rosh wrote like the baraita, holding that rain-fed and mountain aravot do not need a verse, because the implication of the verse 'arvei nachal' already implies this even without an inclusion: the same applies to rain-fed and mountain ones. What then does 'arvei nachal' mean? It means the species whose majority grows by the river, excluding tzaftzafah, for that species does not grow by water. But a species whose majority grows by water is valid even if it grew in the wilderness or in the mountains, and 'arvei' comes to include that we require two. The Taz wrote that it is still difficult regarding the teachers of the Rosh: why were they not careful to take specifically from what grows by the river, in order to fulfill according to all opinions, meaning even according to Tosafot, who hold that the baraita includes rain-fed aravah from the extra word 'arvei,' as above? He answered that it is like what is written in Berachot, that from a person's blessings it is recognizable whether he is a Torah scholar. Therefore, in siman 168 it is explained that we bless 'hamotzi lechem min ha'aretz' and not 'motzi,' even though if he said 'motzi,' etc., he fulfills according to all opinions, whereas regarding 'hamotzi' there is a dispute between the Rabbis and Rabbi Nechemiah. The reason is to teach the novelty that even if he said 'hamotzi,' he fulfills.
And likewise in siman 432, where we bless 'al bi'ur chametz.' Even though if he said 'leva'er,' according to all opinions he fulfilled the obligation, while regarding 'al bi'ur' there is a dispute, nevertheless we bless 'al bi'ur' for this reason.
The same applies to one that grew in the mountains: they were not particular, in order to teach the novelty that even with mountain and rain-fed aravot one fulfills the obligation.
Nevertheless, they were not particular to take specifically one that grew in the mountains or was rain-fed; if one that grew by a river came to their hand, they would also take it. It is enough that they were not particular whether it was from a river or rain-fed.
Magen Avraham se'if katan 2: that was cut off. Etc. The Maggid Mishneh's explanation refers to what is written in the Shulchan Aruch, 'most of its leaves fell off.' This is the view of the Rosh, who explains that which we learned, 'its leaves were broken apart, it is invalid; some leaves fell off, it is valid.' The Rosh explained that 'were broken apart' and 'fell off' are one matter, namely that the leaves broke apart and were detached from the stem; only the first clause, which speaks where most of the leaves broke apart, calls it 'were broken apart,' and the latter clause, which validates and speaks where a minority broke apart, calls it 'fell off.' The Maggid Mishneh wrote: some explain that what is taught, 'its leaves were broken apart, it is invalid,' means that its leaves were loosened from the stem, which is like the Rosh's explanation. Others explain that they split, etc. The Maggid Mishneh concluded that both explanations are true in practice.
