As long as the strings remain attached to the tallis, however, one may not use them. The gloss explains that it is forbidden to use for personal needs an object with which one is meant to fulfill a mitzvah; for example, using strings attached to a tallis to tie something; smelling a hoshana; or eating the esrog used for the mitzvah. Tur in the name of She'iltos, Parashas Shelach Lecha. The 'hoshana' mentioned here means a hadas, as R' Eliyahu Mizrachi writes in his glosses. Hadas, lulav, and aravah are all called hoshana, as we find in many places in the chapter Lulav HaGazul, because they are waved while the Hoshanos are recited. Maharil, Hilchos Lulav in his Derashos. A Baraisa explicitly equates them: 'These are objects used for a mitzvah: sukkah, lulav, shofar, and tzitzis.' We see that a sukkah and lulav are forbidden for personal use during the period of their mitzvah; the same therefore applies to tzitzis. Even when one is not wearing them, one may not use them, since they remain designated for fulfillment of the mitzvah. The Baraisa equates all of them: Just as a sukkah and lulav are forbidden throughout all seven days of Sukkos, even when one is neither waving the lulav nor sitting in the sukkah, so too with tzitzis. Removing tzitzis from a tallis in order to sell it was forbidden because this cancels the mitzvah, just as removing a mezuzah when leaving a house is forbidden because it cancels the mitzvah. Permission was given only to remove them from one garment and immediately place them on another. She'iltos, Parashas Shelach Lecha, implies likewise. The same applies to every object designated for fulfillment of a mitzvah: one may not use it for personal needs. The author of Ateres Zahav discusses this at length in his work.
