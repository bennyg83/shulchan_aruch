And some permit studying quietly. This is the view of Tosafos and the Rosh. In truth, however, this position appears very difficult. What is gained by studying quietly so as not to disturb the others listening to Krias HaTorah? He too is obligated to hear Krias HaTorah, and if he studies his own subject he will not pay attention to the reader. Even if ten people are listening, what does that accomplish for him? The obligation enacted by Ezra rests upon every individual. Indeed, the early authority Shibolei HaLeket already challenged this position in siman 39, as cited by Beit Yosef, and therefore rejected it from practical halachah. Were I not hesitant, I would say that this view refers to one who has already fulfilled his obligation of Krias HaTorah. Even then he may not study aloud, so as not to disturb the other listeners. The Ba’al Halachos Gedolos, cited afterward, may likewise be speaking only in such a case. This is also somewhat implied by the Rosh’s rulings, for quiet study alone is not a sufficient basis for leniency unless combined with the fact that Torah is his occupation, through which he is intrinsically exempt from Krias HaTorah according to the Rif’s view. See there and see Peri Chadash. According to this, the Mechaber may refer only to the case immediately preceding it, where he has already completed the weekly portion, as I wrote in the Mishnah Berurah, meaning that he has already fulfilled the reading. However, the Mechaber’s words at the end of this seif, “All this does not relate,” etc., do not appear to support this. This requires study.
And to read the verse twice, etc. See Magen Avraham, who cites Terumas HaDeshen that this is permitted even when there are not ten people listening to the Sefer Torah, since he too is occupied with the same subject. In truth, however, Terumas HaDeshen’s conclusion is not at all compelled. The proof he brings from Or Zarua, who writes that this is permitted according to all opinions, does not mean even where ten are not listening. Rather, it refers to what precedes it. Or Zarua first cites Rabbeinu Chananel, who permits this only for one whose Torah study is his occupation, like Rav Sheshes, while nowadays we have no one whose Torah study is his occupation. Therefore, even where ten are listening, that status does not help, as is evident from the Rif in the chapter HaKorei Omed. He then cites Rabbeinu Simchah, who explains that Rav Sheshes acted as he did because he was blind. It is on this that Or Zarua adds that reading the verse twice and once in translation is permitted according to all views because one is at least occupied with the same subject. Yet all this may apply only where ten are listening. Where ten are not listening, it may not help, since he is reading independently from a Chumash and sometimes is in different sections and verses, and does not hear the portion being read from the Sefer Torah. It further appears to me that Or Zarua’s initial citation of Rabbi Yitzchak son of Rabbi Shmuel, that quiet study is permitted, likewise applies only where ten are listening, as stated in Semak, siman 155. This is also what he later writes in the name of the Rif, referring back to the same point. Although the Rif does not discuss quiet study, the comparison is valid: just as the Rif requires ten listeners even where Torah is one’s occupation, as Hagahos Maimoniyos writes in his name, so too according to Rabbi Yitzchak’s view that quiet study helps, it helps only where ten are present. This also resolves very well the words of Hagahos Ashiri in Berachos 8a, who cites Or Zarua that quiet study is permitted only when ten are present. At first glance, he appears to combine two distinct answers in Or Zarua; according to what we have written, however, it is entirely consistent. The same is implied by Hagahos Maimoniyos, who writes concerning one whose Torah is his occupation, in the name of the Rif, that there too ten listeners are specifically required, and otherwise it is forbidden. He then writes that nowadays this status never helps, since we have no one whose Torah is his occupation like Rav Sheshes. Afterward he writes in the name of Or Zarua that reading the verse twice and once in translation is permitted, which implies that he refers back to the case where ten are listening. This is even more apparent at the end of his words. The same is implied by Kol Bo and Semak, siman 155, who permit even reviewing the weekly portion only where ten are listening. It emerges that Terumas HaDeshen has no source for his ruling in Or Zarua, while Semak explicitly states the opposite. This is certainly true according to Taz and those who agree with him in siman 55, seif katan 4, who hold that one who is asleep, or speaking and not listening, does not count for prayer because ten listeners are required, in accordance with the Rosh cited in siman 124, seif 4. In our case too, ten people must be listening to the reading from the Sefer Torah, and one cannot permit it merely because he is occupied with the same subject. Taz writes there that even in the case of seif 6, where one is occupied with prayer itself, it is permitted only because he can interrupt and remain silent, and hearing is considered responding. Even according to the Shulchan Aruch there, who is lenient, perhaps that leniency applies only to prayer and not to Krias HaTorah, which specifically requires a congregation. Proof is that for matters of prayer, one person may gather ten men who have already prayed and fulfilled Barchu and Kedushah, then lead them and recite Barchu and Kedushah while they respond. We do not find this with Krias HaTorah, that one who did not hear the reading may gather ten who already read and have it read again solely for him. See siman 142 in Biur Halachah. It must therefore be that Krias HaTorah was instituted only with an actual congregation. Indeed, we are compelled to say that according to the Shulchan Aruch Krias HaTorah is more stringent, for in our case Ba’al Halachos Gedolos and the Rif explicitly require ten listeners, and one who sits and studies, even Torah, does not count. Yet according to the Shulchan Aruch there, merely sitting in the synagogue suffices, as explained by Magen Avraham, seif katan 8. It follows that reading is more stringent. This also resolves the doubt of Peri Megadim cited above in Mishnah Berurah, siman 55, seif katan 33, whether a sleeping person counts for Krias HaTorah. The words of Ba’al Halachos Gedolos and the Rif imply that he does not.
And the proper practice regarding all the portions, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, beginning “It is proper,” etc. This entire seif is an open rebuke to those who abandon their souls and engage in idle conversation during the reading. If even between one aliyah and the next, while the Sefer Torah is closed, it is forbidden to converse with another person, and according to several poskim even Torah study is forbidden, how much greater is the prohibition while the Sefer Torah is open. It is a disgrace to turn one’s ear away from hearing the word of Hashem, and his sin is exceedingly severe. Even concerning one who leaves in the middle of the reading, the Gemara applies the verse, “Those who forsake Hashem shall perish.” All the more so one who stands in the synagogue and refuses to incline his ear to the Torah. In addition, this often causes a public desecration of Hashem’s Name, for example when he stands at the eastern wall of the synagogue and his wrongdoing is visible to everyone. There is then concern for the prohibition, “You shall not desecrate My holy Name.” Frequently, his stories are also mixed from beginning to end with lashon hara and rechilus. In addition, there is the prohibition of idle conversation in a synagogue or beis midrash, which is itself very serious, as explained below in siman 151. Consider how many prohibitions he violates through this. His prayer too becomes abominable before Hashem because of it, as it is written, “One who turns his ear from hearing Torah, even his prayer is an abomination.” Fortunate is one who accords honor to the Torah, as it is stated, “For those who honor Me I will honor,” etc.
