1) [Se'if 1] He should wash his hands, etc. Tolaas Yaakov writes that Zohar states: One who walks four amos in the morning without washing his hands is liable to death. Bach; Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur 4; Halachah Berurah 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, second edition, siman 1:7; Birkei Yosef, siman 1:1; Shalmei Tzibbur, page 18a; Siddur Beis Yaakov 1. Pri Etz Chaim by Maharchu and Olas Tamid by Mahari Tzemach, page 17a, likewise state that one may not walk four amos without this morning washing, as is written in the Zohar on [the verse] 'to whomever Hashem brought it with His hand.' Matsas Shimurim, page 3a, and Ben Ish Chai, Toldos 6, write likewise. Regarding Bach's words, see Birkei Yosef there. Eliyah Rabbah, siman 1:4, cites Damesek Eliezer concerning Tolaas Yaakov's statement: 'Perhaps this applied only in their times, as with uncovered liquids and pairs.' Birkei Yosef writes that this is unconvincing. Shaar HaKavanos also implies that it applies nowadays, as cited below in paragraph 12. Every person should therefore prepare the vessel beside his bed so that he need not walk four amos. Ben Ish Chai writes likewise.
2) If one rose from his bed, did not find a washing vessel nearby, and cannot ask someone else to bring it, Birkei Yosef writes that he heard from holy sages who customarily walked in increments of less than four amos, like the rule stated concerning Shabbos. Moreh BeEtzba, in Kesher Gudal 1:4; Shalmei Tzibbur, page 18c; Zechor LeAvraham 3 in the aforementioned authority's name; Ruach Chaim 1; Pischei HaDevir 1:1 and its Kuntres Acharon, Chaim Shenayim Yeshalem 2; and Ben Ish Chai, ibid. 7, write likewise. This is unlike Siddur Beis Yaakov 3 and Shaarei Teshuvah, siman 1:2, who write that, on the contrary, it is a mitzvah to run to the water so as to remove the power of impurity more quickly, etc. Ruach Chaim there rejects their position, as does Pischei HaDevir; see there. Ruach Chaim concludes that he should walk less than four amos, sit briefly, and remain seated for as long as he walked; see there. The aforementioned poskim, however, mention only stopping, implying that sitting is unnecessary.
3) If one rose from bed and went to the nearest washing vessel, but it contained insufficient water to wash each entire hand three times - that is, through the wrist - he should use the small amount of water to wash through the finger joints, so that he does not walk four amos without washing. This resembles washing on Yom Kippur: the poskim agree that although one washes only through the finger joints on Yom Kippur, it is considered washing and one recites the berachah Al Netilas Yadayim. Travelers may likewise be lenient in this manner under conditions of water scarcity. Shalmei Tzibbur, page 18c; Shaarei Teshuvah 6; Ikrei HaDat 1:12. Kitzur Shulchan Aruch 2:3 likewise writes that in pressing circumstances washing through the finger joints suffices. See Shulchan Aruch HaRav 7. It appears that only if no water is available to him at all, even at a distance, should he recite a berachah over this washing. If water is available farther away and he washed through his finger joints merely to avoid walking four amos without washing, he should wait until he reaches the water, wash through the wrist, and then recite the berachah.
4) This prohibition against walking four amos without washing one's hands applies whether one rises with no intention of sleeping again or rises intending to return to sleep. Its fundamental reason is the evil spirit, and the evil spirit rests upon him throughout the night. Zechor LeAvraham.
4) The prohibition against walking four amos without washing one's hands applies specifically to one who slept past midnight. One who rises at midnight but did not remain asleep past midnight need not take this precaution. Divrei Shalom, question 48. Regarding its statement that he need not wash three alternating times, Emes LeYaakov, question 41, writes that the good and proper practice is to wash three alternating times; see there.
5) There: He should wash his hands. Although strict law requires only washing the hands, it is nevertheless a mitzvah to wash one's face as well. Shalmei Tzibbur, page 19a; Kitzur Shulchan Aruch 2:3; Ben Ish Chai, ibid. 11. Shaar HaKavanos, first discourse on the night, likewise says to wash one's face, as does Pri Etz Chaim, Shaar Tikkun Chatzos, chapter 3. Although Shaar HaKavanos there also says to wash one's feet, Pri Etz Chaim does not, nor does Shaar HaKavanos in its discourses on the morning berachos. Yafeh Shaah 2 explains that we lack the power to drive the external forces from the feet, for their hold is strongest there. The kohanim alone, by virtue of the Temple service, are told, 'Aharon and his sons shall wash their hands and their feet.' This also explains washing the feet on Erev Shabbos, for the merit of Shabbos can protect. Ben Ish Chai, end of paragraph 1, cites this. Solel Boneh 2 and Shalmei Tzibbur, page 4c, write likewise.
6) There: He should recite the berachah Al Netilas Yadayim. Rosh explains that because hands are active, it is impossible that they did not touch soiled flesh during the night. The Sages therefore instituted the berachah before one recites Krias Shema and prays. Rashba, however, explains that each morning one becomes like a new creation, as it says, 'They are new every morning.' We must thank Hashem and purify ourselves for this service like a kohen from the laver; they therefore instituted washing at that time. Beis Yosef; Perishah 1; Taz 1. According to Orchos Chaim, however, morning washing is required because of Shivta and the 'king's daughter,' to remove the evil spirit that rests on the hands at night, based on Shabbos 109a. According to Zohar, one who sleeps tastes death for sixty breaths, and upon awakening the Other Side adheres to his hands. Neither rationale would warrant reciting Al Netilas Yadayim, because one does not recite a berachah over protection from danger or an evil spirit, but only over mitzvos. Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham 1. Practical differences follow. According to Rosh, strict law requires neither a vessel nor water valid for washing before a meal, and if one's hands are soiled before Minchah he recites Al Netilas Yadayim. According to Rashba, a vessel, human force, and all other requirements of washing before a meal are necessary, while at Minchah one does not recite Al Netilas Yadayim. One who remained awake at night is subject to Orchos Chaim's rationale but not Zohar's; one who slept sixty breaths during the day is subject only to Zohar's rationale. Eshel Avraham, ibid. See Mishbetzos Zahav 1. Another practical difference arises if one slept wearing gloves and knows with certainty that he touched no soiled place: according to Rosh he does not recite the berachah, while according to Rashba he does. Eshel Avraham and Mishbetzos Zahav, ibid.
7) There: He should recite Al Netilas Yadayim. One who has a wound on one hand and therefore washes only the other hand nevertheless recites Al Netilas Yadayim. Responsa at the end of Zivchei Tzedek 2:13, which adduces proof from Magen Avraham 170:2; see there. Mahari Chaim also rules this way in Rav Pe'alim 1:8; see there.
8) There: He should recite Al Netilas Yadayim. Seder HaYom writes that one should recite the berachah immediately after washing and not wait until drying. Knesses HaGedolah cites this in Hagahos HaTur, as do Olas Tamid 2 and Eliyah Rabbah 5. Birchas Avraham 3 likewise writes that drying is not indispensable in this washing; Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur 6, and Magen Avraham 6 cite it. Matsas Shimurim, page 3c, also says to recite the berachah before drying. Machazik Berachah 1, however, writes in Maharam Nigrin's name that the evil spirit does not depart until after drying. Accordingly, he concludes, one should not recite the berachah until after drying, unlike Knesses HaGedolah in Seder HaYom's name; see there. Ben Ish Chai, ibid. 5, writes: 'According to our master the Arizal, once one has properly washed three alternating times, the evil spirit is entirely uprooted. He must therefore recite the berachah immediately, before drying, unlike the later authorities who write that it is not uprooted until after drying and therefore hold that the berachah is recited afterward. Rabbi Rashash's work also states explicitly that the berachah is recited before drying.' I heard that meticulous people customarily pour a fourth time so the first water runs off, eliminating any remaining concern according to all opinions. This is the proper practice. See also below, paragraph 12.
8) Machazik Berachah 2 further writes: 'According to the foregoing, he should first dry his hands and then wash his face. The aforementioned rabbis write likewise. It would accordingly appear that even if he dried his hands, if he had not washed his face and then entered the lavatory and washed his hands, he must dry them and only afterward wash his face, in view of Zohar's statement that a particular spirit is present in the lavatory.' Shalmei Tzibbur, page 18a; Zechor LeAvraham 50; and Siddur Beis Yaakov 6 cite this. Here too, some customarily pour a fourth time and wash their faces before drying.
9) There, in the gloss: Some say that one should also recite Asher Yatzar, etc. Rabbeinu Abudraham writes this, as Maran cites in Beis Yosef, where he questions it. Rama, Darkei Moshe 3, reconciles his words, as do Levush, Bach, Perishah at the end of the siman, and Magen Avraham 2. Maamar Mordechai 1, however, writes: 'I found that Rabbeinu Yerucham, part 3 of path 1, states explicitly, "Asher Yatzar, if he relieved himself," etc. This clearly accords with Maran: one who did not relieve himself does not recite Asher Yatzar.' Olas Tamid 2 likewise writes that Shiltei HaGibborim, chapter HaRoeh, rules in accordance with Maran, and that this is correct. Shaar HaKavanos states: 'A person must arrange and recite every day all eighteen berachos from Al Netilas Yadayim through the end of Birchos HaTorah, even if he did not personally incur their obligation, because they all allude wondrously to supernal lights, etc. The exceptions are Al Netilas Yadayim and Asher Yatzar; if he did not incur these obligations, he may not recite them.' Pri Etz Chaim, Shaar HaBerachos, chapter 1; Rashash, Nehar Shalom, page 87a; and Shalmei Tzibbur, pages 45 and 46, write likewise. Zechor LeAvraham 1, Hilchos Berachos 300. Birkei Yosef, siman 6:3, concludes that the practical ruling follows the Arizal and those who agree with him: one who did not relieve himself may not recite the berachah in the morning. Yafeh LaLev 1:4 extensively cites many Rishonim and Acharonim who agree that it should not be recited; see there.
10) There: Water invalid for washing before a meal - see the gloss below, siman 160 - is valid for washing before prayer; nevertheless, some say, etc. Magen Avraham 3 writes: 'Rama means specifically water whose appearance changed or with which work was performed, as described in siman 160. A vessel and human force, however, are not indispensable, and he recites a berachah, as stated in se'if 7.' This follows the emendation of Baer Heitev 3, Yad Ephraim, and Levushei Serad. Yeshuos Yaakov 2 questions Magen Avraham, writing that he does not know the source for reciting a berachah; he later found that Perishah says not to recite it. Maamar Mordechai 2 likewise rejects Magen Avraham's words. One who requires a vessel and human force also requires valid water, while one who does not require the former does not require the latter. This is implied by the poskim cited in Beis Yosef, stated explicitly by Rama in Darkei Moshe, and implied by Levush. This is the primary ruling, because in cases of doubt concerning berachos we rule leniently, even against Maran and even against the majority, as Zechor LeAvraham writes in volume 1, Hilchos Berachos 60, and volume 3:60.
11) Water brought into a lavatory for cleaning oneself is invalid for washing the hands because it is repulsive. Bach in siman 160, cited by Baer Heitev 8, similarly rules that water from which a pig or dog drank is invalid for washing because people find it repulsive to drink. Although Maamar Mordechai 6 there disputes Bach, one must nevertheless be concerned for doubt regarding berachos. Ben Ish Chai, Acharei Mos-Kedoshim 13, likewise rules that water brought into a lavatory is invalid for washing the hands.
11) Regarding washing one's hands after using the lavatory, Zechor LeAvraham 3, entry nun, writes: 'I observe that most people, upon leaving the lavatory, pour out the water in the vessel they took into the lavatory and do not wash their hands with that water.' The great Rav writes likewise in Zivchei Tzedek 2:116:48: 'In our locale we have designated vessels that always remain in the lavatory, and we do not use their water to wash our hands. It is proper for everyone to act this way.' The pious members of Beis El, may it be rebuilt speedily in our days, likewise do not wash their hands with water brought into the lavatory. In pressing circumstances, however, one may permit it, as Zechor LeAvraham writes, since no berachah is recited over this handwashing.
11) There: Nevertheless, some say that one should not recite a berachah over it. The unqualified wording implies that no berachah is recited at all - neither Al Netilas Yadayim nor Al Nekiyus Yadayim. Levush and Perishah, however, write that he does not recite Al Netilas Yadayim but does recite Al Nekiyus Yadayim. Magen Avraham 5 suggests that Maran's statement that no berachah is recited may concern a person who does not presently intend to pray. Pri Tzaddik, page 74a, however, writes: 'He cannot recite Al Nekiyus Yadayim, because that berachah was instituted only when he does not wash with water but cleans his hands with something that cleans. It is inapplicable when he washes with water. Accordingly, se'if 1's statement that water invalid for washing is valid for morning washing, except that no berachah is recited over it, means that no berachah at all is recited. This is unlike Levush and Magen Avraham, who follows him in saying that Al Nekiyus Yadayim is recited if one intends to pray immediately. That is incorrect, for this berachah does not apply when washing with water.' Maamar Mordechai 3 writes likewise at length; see there, as does Neveh Shalom 2. This is the primary ruling, because doubt concerning berachos is resolved leniently.
