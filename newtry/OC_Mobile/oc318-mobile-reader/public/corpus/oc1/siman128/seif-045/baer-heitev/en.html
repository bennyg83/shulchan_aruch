A blessing. This is difficult, for 'to you' is not the end of a blessing, yet they turn their faces then. It appears that both conditions are needed: the end of a blessing and a blessing addressed directly to the congregation. In Yevarechecha and Veyishmerecha, and likewise in Eilecha and Vichuneka and the second Eilecha, the congregation is addressed directly; the kohanim therefore turn south and north to bless them. Shalom, however, is merely a noun and does not address anyone directly. The word lecha is therefore added to Shalom, making the end of the blessing a direct address like the others. Peri Chadash. And the prompter. The chazzan leading the prayer obviously may not say Ribbono shel Olam, because it would interrupt the prayer. Even a different prompter should not say it, because of concern that he may become confused. When no kohen is present, Vese'arev is not recited. Maharam Lublin, siman 30.
With a kohen. Many therefore refrain from employing a kohen as a servant. Knesses HaGedolah. An unlearned man should take care not to marry a kohenes. Gemara, Pesachim.
He waived it. Taz asks: We rule that 'you shall sanctify him' applies against the kohen's will, for if he marries a divorcee we compel him to divorce her; why not say that he waived his sanctity? Taz concludes that a kohen may waive honor because he benefits from the service. Accordingly, when the kohen receives no benefit from working for another, it is forbidden to use him even if he waived his honor. Taz overlooked Mordechai in the chapter HaNizakin, citing Maharam: The fact that the rule derives from 'you shall sanctify him' does not require coercion. Coercion applies when he marries a prohibited woman, but being called first and reciting a blessing first are merely honors, which he may waive. This distinction also answers Taz's question against Levush. Mordechai therefore implies that a kohen may always waive his honor even without receiving benefit, unlike Taz. Magen Avraham brings proof from the passage in the chapter HaZero'a concerning Rava's kohen servant. If, as he understood, 'he said to his servant' means Rava's servant, it is no proof, for Rava was also a kohen, and perhaps a kohen may use another kohen; see se'if katan 8. Rashi explains that it means the householder's servant, to whom the priestly gifts were given. This does prove the point, since the householder was an Israelite; perhaps Magen Avraham intended this but wrote briefly. In siman 201, se'if katan 4, Magen Avraham writes that we are not expert today in priestly lineage, and therefore are not careful about a kohen's sanctity. Knesses Yechezkel, end of siman 56, writes that Heaven forbid one should cast aspersions upon contemporary priestly lineage.
