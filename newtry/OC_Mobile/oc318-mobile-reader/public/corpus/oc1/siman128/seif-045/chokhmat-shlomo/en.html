Se'if 45, gloss: It is forbidden to make use of a kohen even nowadays, as this resembles misuse of consecrated property, unless he waives it. Taz permits it because 'you shall sanctify him' is for the kohen's satisfaction, and he would not be satisfied if forbidden to serve a Torah scholar. Tosafos, Sotah 40b, is difficult for this: even a king may not stand before a Torah scholar, because 'a mitzvah is different' applies only where that mitzvah exceeds royal honor; here the command that the king's awe be upon you is greater. Why not say the king gains satisfaction from standing? Evidently royal honor is not his to waive. Kohanic honor is likewise not his; indeed 'you shall sanctify him, for he offers your God's bread' expressly makes it Heaven's honor. Nor should a kohen be able to violate 'you shall sanctify him' for a scholar when the scholar is commanded to sanctify the kohen and not conversely. Saying the verse excludes scholars would likewise allow a king to stand, since royal awe would apply only to ordinary people and the king remains commanded in reverence for Hashem. Tosafos therefore implies that a kohen's waiver does not help. This explains King Shapur's words to Rav Huna bar Nasan when he raised his belt: 'A kingdom of priests and a holy nation is written about you.' 'A holy nation' is necessary. A Jewish king is himself within the holy nation; compared with a Torah scholar he has only the additional royal distinction, so royal honor takes precedence. A Jewish Torah scholar has two distinctions (kingdom of priests and holy nation) whereas the gentile king has one. Honoring the scholar is therefore the greater mitzvah, allowing the king to waive his honor. Thus Shapur acted consistently whether honoring Torah or royal dignity. Regarding Rema's ruling, I do not understand Taz and Magen Avraham's questions from Rabbeinu Tam's incident, Rava's kohen slave, or the rule that a kohen slave's ear is not pierced. There is a distinction between a Torah scholar and an am ha'aretz. An am ha'aretz may not use a kohen, but a Torah scholar is greater than an ignorant kohen; the Sages even say he should not yield precedence to such a kohen as a fixed kohanic right. Nedarim calls Torah scholars kohanim from 'David's sons were kohanim,' which Rashi explains as first precedence. Since a kohen may use another kohen, certainly a Torah scholar, himself called a kohen and superior to an ignorant kohen, may use him even without waiver; Taz's satisfaction rationale is unnecessary. Rema and Semag, cited by Magen Avraham in siman 201, allow waiver. Taz says waiver alone does not help, except where serving gives the kohen honor. This is difficult: universal custom lets a kohen honor another with leading Birkas HaMazon, although first blessing is also derived from 'you shall sanctify him.' If waiver helps there, why not for service, when both derive from one verse? The matter requires consideration.
