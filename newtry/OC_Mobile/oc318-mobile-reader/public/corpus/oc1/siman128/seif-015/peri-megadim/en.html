It is preferable. See Taz. His meaning is that here he retracts what he wrote in his longer work. Otherwise, 'preferable' would mean as follows: When another person concentrated on the prayer, that is preferable to relying on an assured chazzan, because an assured chazzan says Sim Shalom while the prompter calls Yevarechecha. Some authorities actually forbid this unless the prompter listened from beginning to end; see Beis Yosef. When the chazzan is not assured and another person did listen, it is preferable for that person to prompt Yevarechecha and say Sim Shalom, having listened throughout. This cannot be correct: How could a procedure not mentioned in the Mishnah be called preferable to the one it records? Taz therefore retracts and says only that, even when the chazzan is assured, it is preferable for the prompter also to finish Sim Shalom. A careful reader of Beis Yosef will see that he initially explained Hagahos Maimoniyos as referring to an unassured chazzan. After citing Agur's reconciliation of Rashi and Rambam (that Rashi concerns a case with no other kohen) he no longer explained Rambam as dealing with lack of assurance and thus retracted his initial statement. Netziv understands likewise; this is Taz's point in saying, 'And if from Beis Yosef...' Bach writes that with an unassured chazzan the prayer would be broken into segments. Taz was not satisfied: Lack of assurance merely forces the other person to take over, and he could not have intended this from the beginning. Regarding the anger directed at Levush in se'if 21, I examined his words. In my humble opinion, Levush means that Shulchan Aruch did not retract his longer work, and 'preferable' is still apt in a case where the unassured chazzan improperly ascended while he was the only kohen. If another person listened, that person should finish Sim Shalom; if not, the chazzan must finish it. Initially, however, this procedure is not permitted, which is why the Mishnah gives no such initial arrangement. For an assured chazzan there is no preference; the chazzan himself finishes. Eliyah Rabbah, letter 42, sensed this, so I shall not elaborate.
From Maharam of Rothenburg one can prove that a lone kohen violates the Torah command even without being told to ascend, as Taz writes in letter 3. Otherwise, how could Maharam infer from the Mishnah's phrase 'if'? Perhaps it applies specifically where another kohen is present: Kohanim is called and he ascends to avoid transgression, whereas a lone kohen is not called and does not violate. It follows that he violates even without a call. Yet this also proves that the Sages upheld their rule despite the command; otherwise he would violate it. According to Magen Avraham, letter 34, there is therefore no proof for Taz, letter 3. There is no need to elaborate.
