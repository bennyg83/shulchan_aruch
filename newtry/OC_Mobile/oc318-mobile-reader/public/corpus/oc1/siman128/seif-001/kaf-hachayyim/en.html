1) [Se'if 1] The Priestly Blessing is not recited with fewer than ten, etc. If they began with ten and some left, they complete it, as stated above in Shulchan Aruch, siman 55, se'if 2. This comes from the Yerushalmi, Megillah, chapter HaKorei, and is cited by Rif and Rosh there and by Rambam, chapter 8 of Hilchos Tefillah, as Beis Yosef writes above in siman 55. Knesses HaGedolah, in this siman, os 3, cited by Olas Tamid, os 1, wonders why the poskim did not explain this. That objection is surprising, for Rif, Rosh, Rambam, and Beis Yosef wrote and explained it above in siman 55. If his objection is that Shulchan Aruch does not state it explicitly, it follows automatically and is already explained in Beis Yosef; why must Shulchan Aruch spell out an obvious consequence? I later saw that Eliyah Rabbah, os 1, also objects to Knesses HaGedolah; see there. His words may nevertheless be reconciled by explaining that he refers to the later authorities after Maran, who did not explain the rule in the laws of the Priestly Blessing. When we say that if some left they complete it, this applies only if a majority remains, as Shulchan Aruch states there. So write Peri Chadash here, os 2, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 1. See our words there, os 35.
2) There: The Priestly Blessing is not recited with fewer than ten, etc. Knesses HaGedolah, os 1, questions whether the Priestly Blessing must be recited specifically where a Torah scroll is present. Mahari Ayash writes similarly at the end of Beis Yehudah, page 109d, in the customs of Algiers, siman 4. Eliyah Rabbah, os 2, however, writes that it is obvious that even those praying in a house without a Torah scroll recite the Priestly Blessing, and says Olas Tamid inclines to this view. Peri Chadash, os 10, likewise writes that this is the custom; Yad Aharon cites him in Hagahos Beis Yosef. Responsa Masas Moshe, part 1, Orach Chaim, siman 3, rules that wherever the blessing is ordinarily recited, any ten who assemble in a temporary house recite it, even aboard a moving ship. Yad Aharon cites him in Mahadura Basra, Hagahos Beis Yosef, os 1, as do Shaliach Tzibbur, page 142b; Birkei Yosef, os 8; Zechor LeAvraham, os 50; Beis Menuchah, os 1; and Nehar Shalom, os 1, who agrees. So rule Chayei Adam 32:15; Pesach HaDevir, os 5; Kaf HaChaim, siman 15, os 72; and Ben Ish Chai, Tetzaveh, os 13. This also follows from Arizal in Shaar HaKavanos, discourse 5 on the repetition of the Amidah. He gives a mystical reason for the Priestly Blessing within the repetition and writes that it accomplishes a particular aspect of the prayer. The law therefore does not depend on the presence of a Torah scroll. Wherever ten are present and the repetition is recited, the Priestly Blessing must be recited to complete that aspect, so the prayer will not be deficient. This is the proper practice and should not be changed.
3) There: With fewer than ten, etc. The ten must be adult males, thirteen years old with two pubic hairs, free men specifically. This follows either because the Priestly Blessing is a sacred matter or from the verbal analogy of 'the children of Israel,' as Ran writes in Megillah. Even so, that exposition is an asmachta and the requirement is rabbinic. Mishbetzos Zahav, os 1. See above, siman 55, se'if 1, and our words there, os 6.
4) There: With fewer than ten, etc. If ten are present in the synagogue, but instead of a separate repetition the congregation and prayer leader recite the Amidah together aloud, and the kohanim finish their Amidah before the congregation reaches Retzeh, they may ascend the platform and recite the Priestly Blessing. Even if the prayer leader calls them, this is not an interruption because it is needed for the prayer. To avoid any concern, another person who has completed his prayer should call them before Retzeh. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, os 2, writes that witnesses reported this as the custom in the western lands; Shaliach Tzibbur, page 142d, cites him. See the beginning of Maaseh Rokeach, page 1b, in the novellae to Rambam composed by his son Rabbi Avraham. It appears there that when the congregation prays aloud together with the prayer leader, the kohanim ascend even if they did not finish before Retzeh. Beis Menuchah, os 8, cites this. However, in light of what we wrote above in siman 124, os 2, in Arizal's name; that the repetition is obligatory and possesses greater stature than the silent Amidah; one should not rely on this to cancel the repetition except when unavoidable, such as before departing on a journey or when insufficient time remains to recite it.
5) There: The kohanim count toward the quorum. Beis Yosef writes in Rabbeinu Manoach's name that the kohanim are included in the blessing, just as Kesubos 8b says that the wedding blessings require ten and the groom counts among them. Levush explains from the verse, 'And I will bless them': Hashem says, 'Who blesses the kohanim? I bless them when they bless Israel.' They therefore count toward the ten. See Sotah 38b and Midrash Rabbah, Naso, end of chapter 11.
6) There, gloss: A non-kohen may not recite the Priestly Blessing, etc. Regarding one who comes today and says, 'I am a kohen,' Maran writes at the beginning of Even HaEzer, siman 3, that he is not believed and cannot establish his priestly status through his own word. He is not called first to the Torah and may not recite the Priestly Blessing. Rama there, however, cites Ramach that he is believed for being called first and for the Priestly Blessing today, when we have no Torah-level terumah and need not fear that this status will lead people to give him terumah. Rama says this is the present custom wherever terumah is not practiced. Chelkas Mechokek, os 2, writes that Rama did not accept every aspect of Ramach's view. Where even rabbinic terumah is practiced today, such as Eretz Yisrael under Yoreh Deah 331:2, one must fear that being called first will lead to his receiving rabbinic terumah. Where terumah is not practiced at all, he may be called because no harm can result, as Ran and Maggid Mishneh explain the custom. Beis Shmuel, os 2, objects that Ran and Maggid Mishneh mention only being called to the Torah, which entails no prohibition, not reciting the Priestly Blessing. A non-kohen who blesses appears to violate a Torah prohibition, for the verse says, 'So shall you bless'; you, and not non-kohanim. He answers that we do not suspect a person of committing a prohibition from which he gains nothing. We therefore fear only that he may eat terumah, not that he will recite the Priestly Blessing or be called to the Torah. In Eretz Yisrael, where terumah is practiced under Yoreh Deah 331, he is not believed even today. He concludes that lineage requires two valid witnesses according to all views, while rabbinic terumah requires one witness and cannot be established by the person's own word. Why did the authorities not also fear that people might give him the money of pidyon haben, from which he benefits? A father does not give that money to just any kohen; he first determines that the recipient has established lineage and is learned, so the mitzvah is properly fulfilled. Terumah today, by contrast, is burned and may be given even to an unlearned kohen, as stated there in se'if 19; hence the concern. See our Chukei Chaim, discourse 1 on pidyon haben. Bayis Chadash nevertheless writes that he is believed today even in Eretz Yisrael; Baer Heitev there, se'if katan 1, cites him. Maharit, part 1, siman 149, writes that perhaps one who comes from a known place and says he is a kohen may be called first and recite the Priestly Blessing, because where travelers are common a person would not lie about something that could later bring him disgrace. But one who comes from a distant place and is unknown; such as forced converts arriving from a distant land where they did not observe Jewish law; is certainly not believed on his own, only through others. See Emes LeYaakov, laws of being called to the Torah, os 12, and Ledavid Emes, siman 5, os 12. It appears that present practice is as follows: If we see that he is learned, G-d-fearing, and says he is a kohen from a specified place, he is believed on his own to be called first and recite the Priestly Blessing, even in Eretz Yisrael. If he comes from a distant land with which travel is uncommon and is also unlearned, he is not believed even outside Eretz Yisrael without testimony from others. See further below, siman 135, os 17.
7) There: A non-kohen may not recite the Priestly Blessing, etc. A child was taken captive among non-Jews and redeemed, and his parentage is unknown. One witness says with certainty that he is a kohen, while another says with certainty that he is an ordinary Israelite. Pesach HaDevir, os 4, rules that he may not recite the Priestly Blessing when he matures; see his full discussion. See also there where he rejects Responsa Shevus Yaakov, part 1, siman 93, which appears to permit a doubtful kohen to recite it. Mishbetzos Zahav, os 27, likewise rules that one whose paternity is uncertain; whether he is the nine-month child of the first husband or the seven-month child of the latter; may not recite the Priestly Blessing. Pesach HaDevir cites and agrees with him.
8) There: A non-kohen may not recite the Priestly Blessing, etc. Kesubos 24b states that a non-kohen who recites it violates a positive commandment. Rashi explains that the verse says, 'So shall you bless'; you, and not non-kohanim; a prohibition inferred from a positive command is treated as a positive command. Rama specifies 'even together with other kohanim' because Shabbos 118b records Rabbi Yose saying, 'I never disobeyed my colleagues. I know that I am not a kohen, but if they told me to ascend the platform I would ascend.' Tosafos writes that Ri did not know what prohibition a non-kohen violates by ascending, other than a blessing in vain, since the Torah commanded the kohanim, not Israelites, to bless. Darkei Moshe, os 1, asks from Kesubos and suggests that Ri discussed only one who ascends with other kohanim, whereas one who ascends alone violates the positive command. Rama therefore rules stringently that even together with other kohanim it is forbidden. Bach answers that the positive prohibition applies only when he recites the blessing in the prescribed manner of the Priestly Blessing; merely ascending the platform without doing so does not violate it. In my humble opinion, the answer is that a non-kohen violates the command only when he intends to fulfill the mitzvah of blessing Israel as kohanim were commanded. If he merely intends an ordinary blessing such as one person gives another, he does not violate it. This is what Tosafos means by saying Ri did not know the prohibition. Haflaah on Kesubos cites Sefer Chareidim that an Israelite has a positive mitzvah to receive the blessing. A non-kohen who ascends the platform does not receive it, for se'if 24 below says those behind the kohanim are not included in the blessing. Shaarei Teshuvah, os 2, cites this.
9) There: Even together with other kohanim. It is forbidden even if he does not recite the preceding blessing together with them. That blessing is obviously forbidden as a blessing in vain, because the Torah commanded kohanim, not Israelites, to bless, as explained in the preceding os. So writes Olas Tamid, os 4.
10) There: Even together with other kohanim. Likewise, a chalal, whom se'if 42 says may not recite the Priestly Blessing, is forbidden to do so even together with other kohanim. Mishbetzos Zahav, os 2. Peri Chadash there in se'if 42 likewise writes that a chalal who recites it violates a positive command; Mishbetzos Zahav cites him. See Beis Menuchah, os 38, and our words below, os 251. The same applies to a tumtum and an androgynos: They may not ascend the platform even without reciting the preceding blessing and even together with other kohanim. Mishbetzos Zahav there.
