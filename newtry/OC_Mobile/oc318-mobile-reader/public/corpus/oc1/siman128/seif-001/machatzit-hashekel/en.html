Magen Avraham wrote in Even HaEzer, etc. This requires examination, for Ramach, from whom Rama's ruling there derives, asks: What concern is there? We learned in Gittin 59b that a kohen reads first for the sake of peace, so that people should not quarrel and each say, 'I will read first.' Since the kohen reads first, there is no quarrel when one who is presumed a kohen by his own statement does so, and there is no prohibition. See siman 201. Ramach must hold like Tosafos and Tur that the exposition from 'you shall sanctify him' requiring that he bless first is only a support. Yet Magen Avraham there necessarily regards it as a full exposition, since the Gemara asks why the Mishnah attributes the kohen's reading first to peace when it is a Torah law from 'you shall sanctify him,' and answers that peace is needed to teach that the kohen may not waive his honor. If it were merely a rabbinic law supported by a verse, what would the Gemara's question be? Perhaps they supported it with the verse only because of peace. If it is truly a Torah law, however, he should not be believed on his own testimony even merely to read first.
Chelkas Mechokek and Beis Shmuel there answer that Rama proved that, according to Ramach, he is believed even to raise his hands. Ramach wrote that we do not fear he will be elevated from reading the Torah to terumah, because terumah today is rabbinic and is not practiced at all outside Eretz Yisrael. It nevertheless remains difficult: We should fear that he will be elevated to duchaning. It must therefore be that he may indeed raise his hands. Since there is no concern about terumah, he gains no money through the claim, and we do not suspect him of lying.
Ri did not know the meaning of Rabbi Yose's statement in Shabbos 118b: 'I never transgressed the words of my colleagues. I know that I am not a kohen, yet if my colleagues tell me, Ascend the duchan, I ascend.' Tosafos asks what prohibition applies to a non-kohen who ascends the duchan other than a blessing in vain. Since he knew he was not a kohen, he certainly would not have recited the blessing; what, then, was the praise that he never disobeyed his colleagues?
For in Kesubos 24b:
A non-kohen who raises his hands violates a positive commandment, as it is written, 'Speak to Aharon and to his sons, saying: Thus shall you bless'; you, and not non-kohanim. A prohibition derived from a positive commandment has the status of a positive commandment.
The case in Shabbos must therefore concern ascending together with other kohanim. Were he to ascend alone, he certainly would not do so, for he would violate a positive commandment.
Perhaps Ri holds that the positive commandment violated by a non-kohen who raises his hands is the mentioning of the Divine Name in vain and the recitation of a blessing in vain, unlike Rashi's explanation. Thus Tosafos asks that, were it not a blessing in vain, no positive commandment would be violated. Although Berachos 33a says that one who recites an unnecessary blessing violates 'You shall not take,' Magen Avraham in siman 215 cites the dispute: Rambam regards it as a Torah prohibition, while Tosafos regards it as only a Scriptural support. In Temurah 4a the prohibition against pronouncing the Divine Name in vain is derived as a positive commandment from 'You shall fear Hashem your God,' whereas the exposition from 'You shall not take' is only a support. It is therefore a positive rather than a negative commandment, and there is no distinction between pronouncing the Name in vain and reciting a blessing in vain. Accordingly, the statement in Kesubos that a non-kohen violates a positive commandment is correct.
Women may perform semichah voluntarily. The verse excludes them only from obligation; it does not forbid the act. Ri therefore challenges Rabbi Yehudah's view: 'Thus shall you bless' does not prohibit a non-kohen from duchaning; it merely commands kohanim and leaves non-kohanim uncommanded.
This accords with Rabbi Yehudah, who says that one may establish lineage from priestly blessing. The Gemara asks whether duchaning establishes lineage: A non-kohen who eats terumah incurs death at the hand of Heaven and is therefore believed, whereas duchaning involves only a positive prohibition, about which a person may not be meticulous. Rabbi Yehudah also holds that terumah establishes lineage. The Gemara's reference to a positive prohibition is therefore sound, because Rabbi Yehudah holds that women may not perform semichah voluntarily.
