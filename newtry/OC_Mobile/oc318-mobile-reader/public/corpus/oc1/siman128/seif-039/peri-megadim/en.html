Because this siman contains many laws; some in which one who ascended must descend, some in which he need not descend, some in which even after the fact the act profanes Temple service and duchaning, and some in which it does not; I shall explain a portion of them, with Hashem's help.
Know that the Torah's positive commandment is for a kohen to raise his hands once each day; afterward there is no further mitzvah. Not every kohen is fit for duchaning. Some are disqualified by Torah law, some by the Prophets, some by rabbinic law, some by custom, and some because a required condition is absent. I shall enumerate them, with Hashem's help.
All the disqualifiers are fifteen.
1. A non-kohen.
Second: a chalal.
3. One who is sitting.
Fourth: an onen.
5. An apostate to idolatry.
6. A kohen who killed a person.
7. One who marries women forbidden to a kohen or becomes impure through the dead.
8. One who has a conspicuous blemish on his face or hands.
9. One who has drunk wine.
10. One whose speech is unclear and who cannot articulate the letters properly.
11. A minor who has not manifested two pubic hairs.
12. One who did not wash his hands for duchaning.
13. A kohen who did not uproot his feet during Retzei; he may no longer ascend.
They added to these an uncircumcised kohen according to Bach; see Magen Avraham, letter 54. This is also the view of Knesses HaGedolah and Peri Chadash, se'if 39. It is also obvious that one who apostatizes by desecrating Shabbos or who commits another sin specifically to provoke is like a non-Jew and may not duchan until he repents. See Peri Chadash, se'if 39. Thus, by our count there are fifteen disqualified categories. Apart from these, other conditions that disqualify Temple service do not disqualify duchaning: corpse impurity and similar impurity, uncut hair, torn garments, and an interposition between the kohen and the floor. Rambam enumerates these in Hilchos Bi'as HaMikdash 9:15. It is true that Megillah 24a says that one whose garments do not properly cover him may not raise his hands, and Peri Chadash, se'if 30, suggests that Shulchan Aruch relied on siman 53:13. There, however, the issue is congregational dignity, which some say the congregation may waive. Moreover, it applies specifically when his arms are exposed; one whose garments are merely torn may duchan.
According to Taz, letters 27 and 38, the Torah establishes a full comparison between duchaning and Temple service. Six categories would therefore be disqualified by Torah law and would invalidate both service and duchaning: a non-kohen, a chalal, one sitting, an onen on the day of death, one who drank wine, and an uncircumcised kohen. Even after ascending, such a person must descend. We have already written what appears to me from Taanis 26b and that Sotah 38a indicates a full comparison. This is also why duchaning is not performed at night, as discussed in siman 129. See Magen Avraham, letters 64 and 54. For the same reason, one mourning a father or mother, who does not cut his hair until his friends rebuke him (three months, and a festival does not help; see Yoreh Deah 390:4) would likewise be considered one with uncut hair. Although such a condition does not invalidate Temple service after the fact, it disqualifies initially; see Rambam, Hilchos Bi'as HaMikdash 9:15. This supports Rama's custom in se'if 43. It does not apply to mourning other relatives, which lasts only thirty days and by then he generally has cut his hair; for a father or mother the Sages made no distinction. See Magen Avraham, letter 66.
Regarding the improperly covered person mentioned in Megillah 24a, Rashi explains that the issue is congregational dignity. If the congregation waived it, he might duchan according to the view that permits waiver; this would not help if the law derives from Temple service, as Peri Chadash holds. Taz answers similarly concerning a blemished kohen in letter 27. Failure to wash one's hands might also disqualify for this reason, and se'if 6 requires washing to the wrist, as for Temple service. Orach Chaim cites Baal HaTamid that they customarily washed their feet as well, perhaps for this reason. Yet this creates a difficulty: We are all impure through contact with the dead. One cannot say the comparison is to service at a private altar, for there a non-kohen is valid while an impure person is disqualified; see the end of Zevachim. One might answer with difficulty that one impure through a corpse may perform a communal offering and that duchaning is perhaps a communal need. This is forced for several reasons. Moreover, what would one say regarding zav impurity and similar states?
According to the dissenters, all these laws are rabbinic and the comparison is only a Scriptural support. The only Torah-level disqualifications are a non-kohen, a chalal, and one sitting, because the comparison requiring standing is full. See Magen Avraham, letter 34, in the name of Semag. Regarding wine, anyone whose drinking does not invalidate Temple service is fit for duchaning, as Beis Yosef and Magen Avraham, letter 54, write; see Taz, letter 35. A chalal, however, is disqualified here even though he does not invalidate Temple service, as Rambam writes in Hilchos Bi'as HaMikdash 6:10; see Peri Chadash, se'if 42. Perhaps Rambam refers to one discovered to be a chalal only after serving, while one who was a chalal from birth invalidates the service. Tosafos on Taanis 17b, however, implies that even a chalal from birth does not invalidate it, and Rambam in 9:15 does not even list a chalal among those disqualified initially. I do not know why. Levush in se'if 42 writes that a chalal never had the status of a kohen and therefore may not duchan; perhaps he alludes to this. In any event, Peri Chadash rules that he may not duchan, clearly even if his status was discovered only later after he had duchaned many times. In siman 128 they prohibited duchaning at Minchah of a fast that also has Ne'ilah because of other Minchah prayers. If drinking were only a rabbinic disqualification, this would be a decree upon a decree.
Accordingly, in the following cases; one who did not uproot his feet during Retzei, one with a blemish on his hands, one whose pronunciation is unclear, or one whose hands are dyed; if he ascended, he need not descend, as Peri Chadash, se'if 33, and Magen Avraham, letter 10, write. A non-kohen, a chalal even if his status was discovered only later, one sitting who cannot stand without actual support, an uncircumcised kohen, an onen, and an apostate to idolatry are disqualified by Torah law as explained above; if any ascended, he must descend. The same applies even to one who killed a person, whose disqualification is from the Prophets, and even to a rabbinic disqualification, because whenever the Torah says not to do something, the act is ineffective even regarding rabbinic law; see Sema, Choshen Mishpat 208. Only blemishes of the hand and similar matters that cause distraction do not require descending after ascent. A kohen who did not wash his hands may, if he washed in the morning, rely upon that washing after the fact and need not descend once he ascended.
A kohen who raised his hands at night has accomplished nothing. If he was told to ascend by day and did not, he violates three positive commandments; the same applies to one who duchaned while sitting and similar cases. A kohen who refuses to ascend is flogged until he is prepared to die. After the time passes, he receives lashes for rebellion. Even a prohibition without an action may incur such lashes, as appears from Even HaEzer 21:1; see Peri Chadash on Orach Chaim 471 and 495 and elsewhere. We wrote about this in Sefas HaYam, klal 40. His eligibility as a witness requires examination, for perhaps the Sages established their law on the pattern of Torah law and require an action. With Hashem's help this will be explained in Sefas HaYam. There is no need to elaborate here.
