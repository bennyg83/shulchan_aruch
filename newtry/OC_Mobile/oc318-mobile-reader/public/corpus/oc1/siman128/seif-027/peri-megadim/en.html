In letter 3 he writes a novel rule that such a kohen nevertheless does not violate the command, deriving it from Megillah 24b, which says that one familiar in his city 'may' duchan; Berachos 34a likewise says 'may.' Yet according to his view in letter 3, a lone kohen is obligated even without a call, so 'may' cannot be precise. Perhaps it is precise only to exclude a case in which other kohanim are present, when he may not. See what I wrote in se'if katan 20. Regarding the question and answer, see Eliyah Rabbah, letter 52. I explained it in letter 9: The passage in Taanis 27a follows the first Tanna. The verse says 'the sons of Aharon,' not merely Aharon, because 'sons' naturally includes descendants with blemishes. Even the word kohanim does not exclude a blemished kohen; all the more so 'his sons' denotes biological descent even with a blemish. This is stronger than the comparison of blessing to service. See Re'em on Emor. A chalal may not duchan, as in se'if 2 from Rambam, Hilchos Tefillah 15:5. Although 'sons' would include chalalim were it not for 'the kohanim,' that word excludes a chalal. Since the Torah permits him to become impure, it reveals that he is like a non-kohen and is excluded wherever 'his sons' appears, for he is not a son in priestly status. One born after a doubtful nine-month pregnancy from the first husband or seven-month pregnancy from the latter is a doubtful kohen and may not duchan. When ten kohanim are present and one woman alleges illicit relations while the husband rejects the claim, the child is rabbinically silenced from priestly status and may not eat sacred foods; whether he may duchan requires examination. See Rambam, Hilchos Issurei Bi'ah 20:20, and Even HaEzer 3:9. If an unmarried woman had relations and both acknowledge that the child's father is a kohen, the child is a kohen in every respect. This requires examination. With Hashem's help I shall explain further in letter 38 and Magen Avraham, letter 54.
