But in front of them and on their sides, etc. — See in Mishna Berurah; and know that although we wrote that sides directly [adjacent to him] are included in the blessing like sides in front of him, nevertheless there is a distinction between them — namely, that for sides in front of him his face must be toward the east specifically; but for sides directly [adjacent], it seems that facing east does not help, for "face to face" we derive in the Gemara from "Say to them" — like a person who speaks to his fellow; and it is not common at all that two people sit exactly level and speak, and one's face would turn east and the other's west [all this I learned from the teaching of our Rabbi, the Taz; and although one can reject it somewhat, nevertheless the truth is so] — rather he should turn his face toward the south so that his face inclines toward the faces of the kohanim. And it is possible that even if he stands level with the faces of the kohanim — that is, his face toward the west — he is also included in the blessing, for this too is included in their statement, in the manner that people sometimes sit level and speak.

Even an iron partition, etc. — See in Eliyah Rabbah, as is stated above in siman 55, seif 20, regarding answering Kaddish, Kedushah, and Amen, that we require that filth not interrupt — the same applies here. And now that Sefer HaEshkol has been published, I found that he wrote explicitly, after citing the view of Rav Achai Gaon that filth interrupts, and these are his words: "And it appears to us that specifically for answering Amen is there an interruption; but regarding Birkat Kohanim for people in the fields who need not answer, there is no interruption"; so far. And perhaps Eliyah Rabbah's intent is where one must answer Amen, such as one standing in the courtyard of the synagogue / outside the synagogue but near the synagogue; and see.

If they are under duress, etc. — See in Mishna Berurah; and it is from Rashi; and know that according to this it appears that that which we say in the Talmud, "with those behind the kohanim," is not precise; rather, likewise if he is in the city and sits in his house and does not go to the synagogue — he too is not included in the blessing, even if his house stood facing the kohanim, since the blessing is not important enough to him that he come and hear; and so is implied at the end of Rosh Hashana, that the Gemara compares Birkat Kohanim to the law of prayer; see there — and in prayer it certainly does not help in this manner; see in the Rif there; and so is implied somewhat in Sotah 38b in the tosafot, s.v. "Ha de'anisei," that they did not distinguish in this. And what is stated in the Shulchan Aruch, that those in front of them and on their sides are included in the blessing even if not under duress — that is when he stands near the synagogue from outside, hears the blessing, and desires to be blessed, even though he was not under duress in a matter where he could have come from inside — nevertheless he is included in the blessing since he stands at least facing the kohanim or on their sides. Not so in our case, where he does not want to go to hear — he is included in "with those behind the kohanim." And the Gemara did not mention "behind the kohanim" except as an extension — that even if he is in the synagogue and answers Amen, he is not included in the blessing since he does not want to come facing them as required, as Rashi explained; and to my mind Rashi also alluded to this in s.v. "dela anisei," and he explained "those standing in the synagogue," etc.; and so is implied somewhat in the Tur in what he wrote, "To whom do they bless — their brothers in the north and in the south" — and is not this matter referring to their brothers in the fields who are under duress? And it is known that north and south are included in "their sides," which is like "in front of them"; and nevertheless if not under duress he would not be included in the blessing, since he does not incline his ear to come hear the blessing; and see in the Prishah; and to my mind the Tur wrote thus intentionally. And although the superficial reading of the poskim seems apparently not as we say, indeed the essential matters prove as we wrote, and in particular the sugya at the end of Rosh Hashana cited above; and one may reconcile even the words of the poskim on the path we wrote; and afterward I found in Meiri in Sotah that it reads as we wrote.
