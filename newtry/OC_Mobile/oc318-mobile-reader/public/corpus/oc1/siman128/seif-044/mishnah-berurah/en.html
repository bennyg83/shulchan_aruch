160) He does not recite the Priestly Blessing. Nevertheless, all agree that a married man may recite it even when his wife is not with him.
161) The custom is that he recites it. This applies even when he is the only kohen present.
162) Although he is unmarried. We are stringent specifically in mourning, when he is in sorrow. An unmarried man, though not joyful, is not in sorrow.
163) When 'Kohanim' is called. In truth, he must leave during Retzeh, as stated repeatedly. He must also remain outside during the Priestly Blessing, lest his absence from the platform be taken as evidence of disqualification, as explained above in se'if 4.
164) Throughout these lands, etc. In Eretz Yisrael and throughout Egypt, the custom is to recite the Priestly Blessing daily, and the poskim praised this custom.
165) Only on Yom Tov because, etc. This applies whether Yom Tov falls on a weekday or Shabbos. Some places do not recite the Priestly Blessing even on Yom Tov that falls on Shabbos, but many later authorities write that this custom has no basis. The kohanim customarily enhance the mitzvah by immersing on erev Yom Tov in preparation for the next day's blessing. Independently, a person should purify himself for a festival. Nevertheless, failure to immerse does not invalidate the blessing after the fact.
166) Possess the joy of Yom Tov. The Priestly Blessing is also recited on Yom Kippur, which contains the joy of forgiveness and pardon.
167) And about cessation of their work. The wording is difficult; Heaven forbid that holy Jews should be distressed on Shabbos by cessation of work on the sacred day. The correct text appears to be, 'and because of cessation of their work.' This reason refers to the preceding statement that the Priestly Blessing is not recited on other days. Darkei Moshe likewise separates the rationales: Preoccupation with thoughts and concerns explains why it is not recited on Shabbos, while cessation of work explains weekdays. See there.
