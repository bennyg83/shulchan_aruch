I shall further explain that Shulchan Aruch's phrase 'as though he violates three positive commandments' is Rambam's wording in Hilchos Tefillah 15:12; Tur says he actually violates three. Rambam writes in his principles concerning putisa, a composite creature, that multiple prohibitions do not create multiple sets of lashes unless their subjects differ. The same may apply to positive commandments: Since their subjects do not differ, they count as one. See Hilchos Tefillin 4:26, where Rambam counts the arm and head tefillin as two positive commandments because they do not impede one another. The four passages might be four commandments although they do impede one another, but this is not the place. Practically, if the kohen encounters another positive commandment such as returning a lost object, according to Tur duchaning may take precedence because it contains three commandments; according to Rambam, returning the object may take precedence because it includes both a positive and a negative commandment. If a corpse is in the synagogue and the kohen was told to duchan, the positive commandment of duchaning apparently does not override the positive and negative commands of impurity. If there are three positive commands, one might say they override it, since neither positive command is intrinsically stronger. Tosafos on Sotah 38b, however, cites Yerushalmi that duchaning does not override impurity. Perhaps this refers to one who deliberately failed to leave: A positive command created through negligence does not override, as Tosafos on Eruvin 100a writes. Even if he did not know or the person died suddenly after he was called, the passage implies that he leaves. Corpse impurity in an adjacent house, however, is rabbinic, while duchaning is a Torah law; even if he knew, he must duchan. See Eliyah Rabbah 126:3. See the commentary to Yerushalmi Nazir, chapter 7, page 21b, concerning a communal positive command. Even if we call duchaning communal, perhaps that principle applies only where one cannot avoid the obligation; here, if he is not called, he does not violate it. Yet for purposes of overriding another law it can certainly override. Tzitzis with mixed fibers proves this, although it is a personal obligation and one could avoid wearing the garment. See Kesubos, Eilu Na'aros, concerning 'if she says I do not desire it,' which is not comparable. Know that Yerushalmi's reading asks whether a Kohen Gadol may become impure for duchaning; the commentary deletes 'Gadol,' and Tosafos in Sotah appears likewise. One might retain it: An ordinary kohen has at least two positive commands, whereas a Kohen Gadol has another positive command and thus two commands concerning impurity, prompting the question which is stronger. This is not the place. Perhaps even according to Rambam one violates two positive commands just as one violates two negative commands, though the punishments are not counted separately.
What the commentator wrote, that the question applies although he was not in the synagogue, is difficult. Although a person is punished in a time of divine anger for avoiding such a positive command, as with wearing a linen cloak in summer in Menachos, it still cannot override another law; the question should never have arisen. See Chemed Moshe and what I shall write on this, with Hashem's help, in the following letter.
What Taz wrote concerning Tur requires study; I shall explain it to the best of my ability, with Hashem's help. At first glance, Tur and Rabbeinu Peretz depend on the Tur; Rambam dispute whether one is 'as though' he violates three positive commandments or actually violates three. According to Tur, 'Say to them' retains its plain command and refers to Aharon and his sons, so even a lone Aharon violates it if called. Rabbeinu Peretz follows Rambam that 'Say to them' is used only for the exposition 'when they tell them'; consequently one kohen is not called and does not violate even if called. This seems difficult in light of Shulchan Aruch, se'if 2, who says every kohen, even one alone, is as though he violates three commandments. One might answer, with some difficulty, that the rules 'for two call Kohanim, for one do not' are all rabbinic supports, so by Torah law even two need no call. Tosafos on Menachos 44a, however, indicates that by Torah law two are called and one is not. Taz says that had Scripture not written it; perhaps meaning that for one a call is unnecessary, not forbidden; see Chemed Moshe; even if it was written to prohibit calling one, how could the command 'Thus shall you bless' initially address Aharon alone when without a call there is no command? A case where someone improperly called him would be an after-the-fact case and would not be written as the Torah's command. Taz also asks from se'if 13, where Tur distinguishes that even for one kohen each word is prompted. This cannot be the plain meaning, so why should the exposition 'to them' exclude a lone kohen from calling? His answer is that Tur's three actual positive commandments require the plain meaning plus two expositions. The extra wording provides all three. The alef of 'amor' is vocalized with a kamatz; a simple future command would use a sheva. The infinitive with kamatz can sometimes indicate a prior action, teaching 'when they tell them': The positive command 'Thus shall you bless' applies when they were first addressed. It also teaches that each word is prompted. Otherwise 'amor' with kamatz could have been written before 'Thus shall you bless,' or with a sheva afterward. Since the call to Kohanim precedes the blessing, placing the kamatz-form after 'Thus shall you bless' teaches that they are prompted within the blessing as well. Even if Yevarechecha itself is not prompted, as in se'if 13, the point remains. Were only this lesson intended, it could have used a sheva. Thus all are derived: two expositions and the plain positive command, making three actual positive commandments. In my view the word 'or' should be inserted in Taz: 'It should write amor with a sheva [or] before the blessing.' It follows that a lone kohen violates the command if he does not ascend even without a call. Netziv writes likewise in se'if 10. Prompting each word for one kohen follows the reason (preventing error) which applies to one as well; here 'to them' was certainly not expounded as specifically two. Taz concludes that all this concerns whether he violates the command, but a spiritual defect certainly occurs even if he was outside the synagogue when Kohanim was called, as in se'if 4. With Hashem's help I shall explain more in Magen Avraham. Peri Chadash, se'if 10, likewise writes that a lone kohen possibly violates the command even without a call. See what I wrote in letter 8.
