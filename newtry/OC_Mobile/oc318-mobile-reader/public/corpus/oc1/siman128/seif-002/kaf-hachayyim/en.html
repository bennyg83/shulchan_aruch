11) [Se'if 2] Every kohen who has none of, etc. A kohen may not contract impurity in order to recite the Priestly Blessing. Tosafos, Sotah 38b, in the name of the Yerushalmi, Nazir, chapter Kohen Gadol; cited by Eliyah Rabbah, os 3, and Mishbetzos Zahav, os 3. Mareh HaPanim on Nazir explains that contracting impurity violates both a positive and a negative command, and one positive command cannot override both. Pesach HaDevir, os 1, cites him. Eliyah Rabbah writes that even if the kohen has already washed his hands to ascend and then hears that impurity is present, he must leave the synagogue. So too Har HaMoriah, os 2: Even if told to ascend, he does not contract impurity for the blessing. Mishbetzos Zahav says that if the impurity is in an adjoining house he need not leave, because transmission in that case is rabbinic while the Priestly Blessing is biblical, even when he knows he must bless. See our words above, siman 126, os 13, where we wrote that according to some, if an opening one tefach wide connects them, the impurity is biblical. If he knows or is informed of it, he must therefore leave.
11) A synagogue stands in a courtyard surrounded by projecting roofs and porticoes. A corpse, Heaven preserve us, lies in one house, all of whose doors are locked. Since the rule that impurity is destined ultimately to leave through an entrance is rabbinic, a kohen may enter the synagogue to recite the Priestly Blessing, because he enters for a mitzvah. Responsa Ramaz, siman 15. In siman 16 he writes that he sent this ruling to Rabbi Shmuel Aboab, who agreed in practice. It is cited by Ikarei HaDat, siman 5, os 58, and Beis Menuchah, os 2. See Responsa Devar Shmuel, siman 223, which cites Ramaz.
11) Impurity was present in a house adjoining the synagogue and entered through the women's-section window. The sages of Eretz Yisrael ruled on Shabbos that the window, which measured a tefach by a tefach, could be closed with wool. The wool was suitable for lying upon and therefore not muktzeh, and closing it did not violate building, because adding to an existing structure is permitted. They likewise permitted closing it with a wooden lattice. This was both for the congregation's honor, so they would not pray where impurity entered through the window, and because they should not be left without the Priestly Blessing recited with love. Leket HaKemach, Yoreh Deah, page 123c; Shaliach Tzibbur, page 140a; Beis Menuchah, os 3.
11) From Rabbi Leket HaKemach's statement that the sages of Eretz Yisrael ordered the window closed on Shabbos because the congregation should not be left without the Priestly Blessing, it follows that if repair had been impossible, the kohanim could not have blessed. They may not remain in the synagogue because impurity enters through the window, and the mitzvah of the Priestly Blessing does not override impurity. This also follows from Devar Shmuel, siman 204, and Taz, Yoreh Deah 371:3. So writes Heichal HaKodesh in Shaliach Tzibbur there and Beis Menuchah, os 4. See Heichal HaKodesh's lengthy discussion there and our words above, siman 126, os 13.
11) Regarding the Priestly Blessing outside the Temple in Temple times, where the explicit Divine Name was not pronounced, and likewise today: Keren Orah, in his novellae to Sotah 38, discusses whether it is biblical or rabbinic and concludes that it is biblical. This follows from Rambam's unqualified language in Hilchos Tefillah; Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's statement that every kohen who does not ascend violates three positive commands; the Yerushalmi cited by Tosafos there asking whether a kohen may contract impurity for the blessing; and the entire discussion in Kesubos, chapter HaIshah Shenisarmlah, about using the Priestly Blessing as evidence of lineage. Yalkut likewise cites Sifrei Zuta interpreting 'in every place where I cause My Name to be mentioned' as including outside the Temple. Peri Megadim appears to agree in Eshel Avraham, os 1, and Mishbetzos Zahav, os 3. Although Mor Uketziah repeatedly writes here that the blessing outside the Temple in Temple times, and likewise today, is only rabbinic and adduces several proofs, the great scholar Pesach HaDevir, os 4, already addresses his words. He proves clearly from many Talmudic passages and from leading early and later commentators and poskim that it remains a biblical positive command today. Mor Uketziah himself did not maintain his position, for Responsa She'eilas Yaavetz, part 1, siman 54, calls it a biblical positive command today. Pesach HaDevir's addenda, page 217d, notes that Mabit states explicitly in Kiryas Sefer, chapter 14 of Hilchos Tefillah, that it is biblical outside the Temple as well. So rule Maharshach in the newly printed Binyan Shlomo, siman 10, and his brother in Reishis Bikkurim, page 10b. Sedei Chemed, part 3, Maareches Divrei Chachamim, siman 64, Orach Chaim, os 3, cites them. This also appears from Maran's words here and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 3.
12) There: Among the matters that prevent, etc. Even when a condition prevents him only rabbinically, he does not violate a positive command by remaining below. The person calling 'kohanim' intends only qualified kohanim, not those disqualified, even if only rabbinically. He therefore need not leave before the call. Bach rules accordingly. Magen Avraham, se'if katan 4, comments that this implies that if he is personally told to ascend, he must do so, because otherwise he violates the positive command. Yet Beis Yosef's wording in se'if 2 implies that one possessing any disqualifying condition does not violate the command even if told to ascend. This appears correct: He does not refrain on his own; the Sages barred him, and they have authority to do so, as stated in siman 191. Peri Chadash, os 4, likewise rules that a kohen with a rabbinic disqualification may not ascend even when told, because a rabbinic court may suspend a Torah law through passive nonperformance, as stated in Yevamos, chapter HaIshah Rabbah. So too Olas Tamid, os 6. If only disqualified kohanim are present, Eliyah Rabbah, os 4, says they should leave before Retzeh. Olas Tamid, however, says that even when only disqualified kohanim are present and the prayer leader will certainly intend his call for them, they need not leave because the Sages disqualified them. Nevertheless, the person calling 'kohanim' should preferably intend to call only those fit for the blessing and free of even a rabbinic disqualification.
13) There: Although he has neglected one positive mitzvah, etc. This is Rambam's language at the end of Hilchos Tefillah. Sotah 38b, however, says in Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's name that every kohen who does not ascend violates three positive commands: 'So shall you bless,' 'Say to them,' and 'They shall place My Name.' Why does Rambam say he is 'like one who violates three positive commands' rather than that he actually violates them, as the Gemara says? Maran explains in Kesef Mishneh there and Beis Yosef here that only 'So shall you bless' is phrased as a command. Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's word 'violates' is therefore not exact; he is like one who violates three commands. So write Levush, Soles Belulah in Mikdash Me'at, os 1, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 3. See Mishbetzos Zahav at the end of the siman: A kohen who refuses to ascend is compelled until his life is at risk; if the prescribed time has passed, he receives lashes for rebellion. Know also that when possible he must ascend specifically onto the platform, not remain on the floor, because ascent is mentioned in the Gemara as explained above. This also follows from the poskim's unqualified language and should not be changed. See below, os 120.
14) There: If he was in the synagogue, etc. This states explicitly that any one of the three conditions is sufficient for violation: He was in the synagogue when the prayer leader called 'kohanim'; another congregant told him to ascend; or someone told him to wash his hands. So write Maamar Mordechai, os 4; Shulchan Aruch HaRav there; and Chayei Adam 32:1. Levush concludes, 'But if he is not there, or they did not tell him to ascend or wash his hands, he does not violate.' Maamar Mordechai says this wording is inexact because it sounds as though he is exempt whenever any one condition is absent, contrary to Beis Yosef and the poskim. It should read: If he was not there and they did not tell him either to ascend or to wash his hands, he does not violate. In my opinion Levush can be read that way: 'If he is not there' means he was absent from the synagogue when the prayer leader called; 'or they did not tell him to ascend, etc.' means that even if present, the prayer leader did not call 'kohanim' and no congregant told him either to ascend or wash. Only if neither request was made does he not violate; if either was made, he violates.
15) There: If he was in the synagogue, etc. This excludes kohanim who leave before the cantor calls 'kohanim.' They then violate nothing, not even the command 'So shall you bless.' It rejects Rabbeinu Manoach, cited by Beis Yosef, who says that a kohen not asked to bless avoids only the three-command violation but still violates 'So shall you bless,' because kohanim are inherently commanded to bless Israel. The poskim disagree, as Beis Yosef explains. Olas Tamid, os 6, says this is proven by the Yerushalmi: When Rav Adda bar Pazi was weak and could not hold up his head, he stood behind a pillar; Rabbi Elazar went outside. They did so to avoid ascending when the cantor called 'kohanim.' Under Rabbeinu Manoach's view, this accomplished nothing, for they would violate a command even without being called. Peri Chadash, os 4, likewise says Rabbeinu Manoach's view need not be considered. Ran in Megillah, chapter HaKorei Omed, writes that one outside the synagogue does not violate even if people call him to ascend.
16) There: Or if they told him to ascend, etc. Even before the prayer leader calls, if people tell the kohanim to ascend or wash their hands, they must ascend. Mordechai writes that even when other kohanim are present, one who does not ascend violates the command; this is obvious. Olas Tamid, os 7. This implies that without being told he does not violate, even if he never ascends all year. Responsa Rabbi Yaakov of Marvege, siman 38, whose answers came from Heaven and are printed in Radbaz, part 5, writes differently: Where kohanim fit to bless are present but the community does not recite the Priestly Blessing even once a year, both groups violate; the Israelites, who do not call them and appear indifferent to their Father's blessing, and the kohanim, who do not bless voluntarily. The verse says, 'I will bless those who bless you,' from whose positive formulation a negative consequence is inferred. Chesed LaAlafim, os 1, writes that one who does not bless forfeits much good and shows that he neither desires the mitzvah nor the blessing; contempt for the matter will bring harm. A G-d-fearing kohen who delights in His commandments should therefore never omit blessing and giving satisfaction to his Maker, for Hashem desires Israel to be blessed. How good is the custom of places where kohanim bless daily, and every place should adopt it rather than seek devices to cancel a biblical positive command. Although a kohen violates only when called, the Priestly Blessing is an auspicious time that brings blessing to the upper and lower realms. Its intentions and deepest mysteries are ancient; in general, kohanim should intend that the Supreme One establish the work of their hands and that they act with the intention of Aharon HaKohen and all the holy ones, may their merit protect us. See Shaar HaKavanos, discourse 5 on the repetition, which explains that the Priestly Blessing should be recited during the repetition every day and gives its mystical meaning.
17) There: Or if they told him to ascend, etc. Rabbeinu Peretz writes that if only one kohen is present, he does not violate, because this is derived from 'Say to them,' whose plural form implies multiple kohanim. Tur cites him but disagrees: 'Them' refers to Aharon and his sons, and means to say it to each of them. Maran's unqualified language here and that of the later authorities follows Tur, so even a lone kohen violates. So write Olas Tamid, os 8; Maamar Mordechai, os 5, who says this is Maran's view; and Beis Menuchah, os 8.
18) There: Or if they told him to ascend, etc. This applies only if he moved his feet during the blessing of Avodah, or if before Retzeh they told him to ascend. If he did not move his feet, he may not ascend, as stated in se'if 8 and Magen Avraham, se'if katan 2. That is, if he moved during Avodah and was told to ascend but did not, or if before Retzeh they told him to ascend so that he could move during Retzeh, he violates by failing to ascend. If he did not move during Retzeh and was told afterward, he does not violate, because se'if 8 says he may no longer ascend. So Magen Avraham, se'if katan 30; Maamar Mordechai, os 4; Machatzis HaShekel, se'if katan 2; and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 3. Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 12, however, questions whether the rule that one who did not move may not ascend applies only when acting voluntarily, while one explicitly told to ascend must do so. Eliyah Rabbah, os 16, and Eshel Avraham, os 2, cite him. It therefore appears that one who did not move his feet during Retzeh should leave the synagogue, both so no one tells him to ascend and so he does not hear the cantor call 'kohanim' and enter a doubtful obligation, and also so he will not appear disqualified. See below, os 22.
19) There: Or to wash his hands. If the person bringing water for the kohanim approaches a prayer leader who is a kohen and reminds him to wash, the prayer leader must recite the Priestly Blessing even when other kohanim are present; if he does not ascend, he violates three positive commands. The water-bearer should therefore be taught not to remind him when other kohanim are present, so that he will not violate if he does not bless. Beis Yosef in Mordechai's name; Rama cites it below in the gloss to se'if 20; Peri Chadash, os 4. The person calling 'kohanim' should likewise be taught not to intend the prayer leader when others are present, in deference to Maharam Mintz's view that an explicit request obligates him even if he did not move his feet during Retzeh, as stated in the preceding os. Olas Tamid, os 42, says excluding the prayer leader from the caller's intent is ineffective, citing the Yerushalmi about Rabbi Shimon ben Pazi standing behind pillars when ill. That proof is not compelling: There the purpose was to avoid being seen as disqualified. No such concern applies to the prayer leader, because everyone knows he does not ascend precisely because he leads the prayer. It is therefore best that the caller not intend him. See Eliyah Rabbah, os 40.
