6) There, gloss: A non-kohen may not recite the Priestly Blessing, etc. Regarding one who comes today and says, 'I am a kohen,' Maran writes at the beginning of Even HaEzer, siman 3, that he is not believed and cannot establish his priestly status through his own word. He is not called first to the Torah and may not recite the Priestly Blessing. Rama there, however, cites Ramach that he is believed for being called first and for the Priestly Blessing today, when we have no Torah-level terumah and need not fear that this status will lead people to give him terumah. Rama says this is the present custom wherever terumah is not practiced. Chelkas Mechokek, os 2, writes that Rama did not accept every aspect of Ramach's view. Where even rabbinic terumah is practiced today, such as Eretz Yisrael under Yoreh Deah 331:2, one must fear that being called first will lead to his receiving rabbinic terumah. Where terumah is not practiced at all, he may be called because no harm can result, as Ran and Maggid Mishneh explain the custom. Beis Shmuel, os 2, objects that Ran and Maggid Mishneh mention only being called to the Torah, which entails no prohibition, not reciting the Priestly Blessing. A non-kohen who blesses appears to violate a Torah prohibition, for the verse says, 'So shall you bless'; you, and not non-kohanim. He answers that we do not suspect a person of committing a prohibition from which he gains nothing. We therefore fear only that he may eat terumah, not that he will recite the Priestly Blessing or be called to the Torah. In Eretz Yisrael, where terumah is practiced under Yoreh Deah 331, he is not believed even today. He concludes that lineage requires two valid witnesses according to all views, while rabbinic terumah requires one witness and cannot be established by the person's own word. Why did the authorities not also fear that people might give him the money of pidyon haben, from which he benefits? A father does not give that money to just any kohen; he first determines that the recipient has established lineage and is learned, so the mitzvah is properly fulfilled. Terumah today, by contrast, is burned and may be given even to an unlearned kohen, as stated there in se'if 19; hence the concern. See our Chukei Chaim, discourse 1 on pidyon haben. Bayis Chadash nevertheless writes that he is believed today even in Eretz Yisrael; Baer Heitev there, se'if katan 1, cites him. Maharit, part 1, siman 149, writes that perhaps one who comes from a known place and says he is a kohen may be called first and recite the Priestly Blessing, because where travelers are common a person would not lie about something that could later bring him disgrace. But one who comes from a distant place and is unknown; such as forced converts arriving from a distant land where they did not observe Jewish law; is certainly not believed on his own, only through others. See Emes LeYaakov, laws of being called to the Torah, os 12, and Ledavid Emes, siman 5, os 12. It appears that present practice is as follows: If we see that he is learned, God-fearing, and says he is a kohen from a specified place, he is believed on his own to be called first and recite the Priestly Blessing, even in Eretz Yisrael. If he comes from a distant land with which travel is uncommon and is also unlearned, he is not believed even outside Eretz Yisrael without testimony from others. See further below, siman 135, os 17.
