35) [Se'if 6] Although the kohanim washed, etc. Sotah 39a states in Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's name: A kohen who has not washed his hands may not recite the Priestly Blessing, for it says, 'Raise your hands in holiness and bless Hashem.' Beis Yosef understands Rambam to require washing only when the hands are impure, such as when one did not wash in the morning. If he washed then and knows of no later impurity, he need not wash again. This was the Egyptian custom. Orchos Chaim likewise cites Rabbi Avraham ben Rambam that in practice before his father Rambam and other Torah teachers, every kohen standing in prayer proceeded to the blessing relying on his washing for Shema and the Amidah. Tosafos in Sotah cites a similar view in Rashi's name but says it was copied from a student's gloss; Rashi's own words, 'who did not wash before ascending,' imply washing immediately beforehand. Tosafos further proves from the Bavli and Yerushalmi that even one who washed in the morning must wash again near the blessing, with no delay exceeding the time to walk twenty-two amos. Beis Yosef cites this and says Tur agrees with Tosafos. This is Maran's ruling here, and so rule Levush; Bach; Peri Chadash, os 6; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 8; Chesed LaAlafim, os 3; and Ben Ish Chai, Tetzaveh, os 7. It also follows from the Zohar cited below, os 40, and is the custom here in holy Jerusalem. Only when impossible; such as a prayer leader for whom washing would interrupt the Amidah; may one rely on Rambam. See the next os.
36) There: Although the kohanim washed their hands in the morning, etc. If he washed in the morning, even only to the ends of his finger joints, and has no water for washing before the platform, Rambam and his followers may be relied upon. He may bless provided he did not divert his attention from his hands. Olas Tamid, os 12; Eliyah Rabbah, os 8; Chayei Adam 32:8; Mishnah Berurah, os 20. If he did not wash even in the morning, he may not bless. Cleaning the hands by another method suffices for prayer but not for the platform, which requires water. Olas Tamid and Eliyah Rabbah there; this also appears to be Peri Chadash's view. See below, os 39.
37) Orchos Chaim writes in Baal HaTamid's name: We have found no author who requires washing the feet, but this was the custom in those lands, and Jewish custom has the force of Torah. Beis Yosef cites him. Peri Chadash says the custom may have been modeled on the priestly sanctification in the Temple, of which the verse says, 'They shall wash their hands and feet.' It is no longer practiced, correctly so: The present verse says 'your hands,' not 'your feet,' and the Zohar likewise says that he must sanctify his hands. Beis Yosef says we have never seen anyone practice it, and Rama in Darkei Moshe, os 6, says it is not the custom in his lands either. See our words above, siman 4, os 5.
38) There: Although the kohanim washed their hands in the morning, etc. The Egyptian custom is not to wash again at Shacharis but to wash at Mussaf because attention has meanwhile been diverted. Radbaz, part 1, siman 147; Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef; Magen Avraham, se'if katan 6. Nevertheless, one who fears Hashem and delights in His commandments should wash again even at Shacharis, thereby satisfying Maran, those who agree with him, and the Zohar.
39) There: Up to the joint connecting the hand and arm. Rambam, Hilchos Tefillah 15:5, explains that this parallels sanctification for the Temple service; Tur and Beis Yosef cite him. Chullin 106b likewise says that the Temple sanctification of hands and feet extended to the joint, which Rashi defines as the upper joint where hand and arm meet. So writes Levush. If only enough water is available to reach the ends of the finger joints, Olas Tamid, os 12, permits one who did not wash in the morning but prayed after cleaning his hands to ascend after this limited washing, and says Maran may agree. See the end of siman 161 and our words in siman 4, os 3. On Tishah BeAv and Yom Kippur, therefore, one should wash for the Priestly Blessing only to the ends of the finger joints. This follows from Rama 613:2 and Maran's unqualified language there, which does not distinguish morning washing from washing for the blessing. Peri Megadim, Eshel Avraham 613:3, and Yafeh LaLev, os 6, agree. See Dagul Merevavah's gloss there, cited by Yafeh LaLev, os 4, and Yafeh LaLev's further discussion. He concludes that we follow Maran and Rama: Both morning washing and washing for the platform extend only to the finger joints on those days, and he heard that distinguished kohanim observe this. It is a proper custom, since passive restraint is preferable in a dispute, especially when Maran and Rama agree and this is common practice. See above, os 36.
40) There: The Levi pours water, etc. Zohar, Naso 146a, teaches: A kohen who spreads his hands must add holiness to his holiness by sanctifying his hands through a holy hand. That holy hand is the Levi's, from whom the kohen receives the sanctifying water, for it says, 'You shall sanctify the Levites'; they are holy. It also says, 'And also your brethren, the tribe of Levi.' Thus every kohen who spreads his hands must be sanctified by a holy person, adding holiness to his holiness, and should not receive the sanctifying water from an unholy person. Tzinius DiSifra further teaches that the Levi through whom the kohen is sanctified must first sanctify himself. Why specifically a Levi rather than another kohen? Another kohen might be deficient, and a complete kohen should not be impaired through one who is incomplete. A complete Levi, however, was fit to ascend the platform and serve in the Tabernacle, and is called holy, as it says, 'You shall sanctify the Levites.' Beis Yosef cites this, and it is the basis of Maran's words here. The Zohar implies that when no Levi is present, neither an Israelite nor another kohen should wash his hands; he should wash them himself. Bach, however, cites Maharil that in the absence of Levites, a firstborn who opened his mother's womb should pour for the kohanim. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, os 3; Olas Tamid, os 13; Taz, se'if katan 3; Ateres Zekeinim, os 1; Magen Avraham, se'if katan 7; and Eliyah Rabbah, os 10, cite this. Magen Avraham notes that this means specifically a maternal firstborn; a paternal firstborn alone has no recognized sanctity today. Shaliach Tzibbur, page 143a; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 10; Chayei Adam 32:8; Chesed LaAlafim, os 3; Beis Menuchah, os 11; and Ben Ish Chai, Tetzaveh, os 7, cite it. I have not seen anyone follow this. They apparently rely on the Zohar's plain implication that only a Levi pours; without one, each kohen washes his own hands.
41) There: The Levi pours water, etc. If the kohanim are unlearned and the Levi is a Torah scholar who refuses to pour for them, he bears no guilt, even if he is the only Levi. This remains so even if he previously followed the practice and then changed his mind. Rabbi Yaakov HaLevi, siman 39; Rabbi Yitzchak HaLevi, his son Rabbi Shmuel HaLevi, and Rabbi Chaim Shabsai, simanim 40-41. Rabbi Leib Sarval, however, writes in siman 40 that a Levi may not exempt himself from pouring unless the kohanim are unfit for the blessing, such as people of Beis She'an and Beis Haifa or minors. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur; Olas Tamid, os 14; Magen Avraham, se'if katan 7; and Eliyah Rabbah, os 10, cite the dispute, and Eliyah Rabbah says one should follow the latter stringent view. Peri Chadash, os 8, disagrees. Since a Levi's pouring is not indispensable; a city consisting entirely of kohanim has everyone ascend though no Levi is present; a learned Levi who declines to pour for unlearned kohanim acts correctly even when he is the only Levi. On the contrary, he incurs death at Heaven's hands if he serves an unlearned kohen. Knesses HaGedolah further cites Rabbi Shmuel HaLevi, siman 41, that if learned and unlearned kohanim are both present the Levi must pour for all. Olas Tamid, Magen Avraham, and Eliyah Rabbah cite this, but Peri Chadash rejects it because of the liability just mentioned. Yad Aharon, Hagahos Beis Yosef; Shaliach Tzibbur, page 143b; Eshel Avraham, os 7; Chesed LaAlafim there; and Kaf HaChaim, siman 15, os 71, cite the discussion. Chesed LaAlafim says that although some permit it, caution is required and passive restraint is preferable, even when he is the only Levi. Ben Ish Chai rules likewise.
42) There, gloss: The Levites did not customarily wash, etc. If the Levi diverted his attention from his hands, it is proper that he wash first; certainly so if he touched his body or shoes. Bach; cited by Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, os 2; Olas Tamid, os 15; Magen Avraham, se'if katan 8; Eliyah Rabbah, os 11. Knesses HaGedolah says the widespread custom among them is for Levites to wash even while pouring water over the kohanim's hands. Every G-d-fearing person should follow this: Wash his own hands and then pour for the kohanim, even where Levites did not customarily wash, thereby fulfilling the Zohar's words.
43) Magen Avraham writes that the water must not have changed from its natural state, as stated in siman 160, and must be poured by human force, paralleling the kohanim's Temple service. Soles Belulah, os 9; Shaliach Tzibbur, page 143c; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 8; Chesed LaAlafim, os 4; Ben Ish Chai, Tetzaveh, os 8. It appears that a vessel and a revi'is are also required, as for hand washing under the beginning of siman 159 and siman 160:13. Although Rambam, Hilchos Bias HaMikdash 5:11, permits sanctification from a vessel holding less than a revi'is, that case differs because the water comes from a source containing the requisite measure, as Kesef Mishneh explains. One washing directly from a vessel requires a revi'is in it.
44) Taz 511:8 writes: I saw communities pour scented oil into water on Yom Tov for washing the kohanim's hands before the platform. They act improperly, because this creates fragrance in the water. Even if poured before Yom Tov, the kohanim create fragrance on Yom Tov when it transfers to them; they desire and intend this, so the practice must be stopped. Magen Avraham there, se'if katan 11, and here, se'if katan 8, agrees. Leket HaKemach, part 2, siman 20, likewise forbids washing face and beard with rosewater on Shabbos because it creates fragrance in the beard hair, and hair resembles clothing. Eliyah Rabbah here, os 8, says that because the oil itself is mixed with the water, this is no more creating fragrance than smelling the oil; it may be poured either into the water or onto the hands. Chacham Tzvi, siman 92, likewise disputes Taz and Magen Avraham and holds that creating fragrance does not apply to human flesh, foods, or drinks. Ginas Veradim 3:16 permits applying it to hands, face, and beard, but agrees with Taz and Magen Avraham that rosewater may not be added to water. Nechpah BaKesef, siman 4, defends the custom of adding rosewater to wine on Shabbos and Yom Tov and permits it plainly. He is stringent only about adding fragrance to water intended for washing; if it was added before Shabbos, washing is permitted. Mahari Ayash agrees at the end of Shevet Yehudah. Chida cites them in Birkei Yosef 511:5 and Machazik Berachah there, os 5. Likkutei Peri Chadash 511 says any intent to create fragrance is forbidden. Batei Kehunah, part 1, siman 293, likewise appears to forbid intentional creation of fragrance on a person or object, permitting it when unintended. Responsa Shoel UMeishiv, second edition, part 2, siman 7, discusses the dispute and rules like Taz. Shelah's summary of Yom Tov laws and Shulchan Aruch HaRav at the end of siman 511 rule likewise. Therefore, practically, anything intended to create fragrance is forbidden; for example, putting rosewater into washing water, or washing one's face, hands, and certainly hair with rosewater itself; because the prohibiting authorities are the majority. It is permitted to add it to foods and drinks to sweeten them, or to pour it into one's hand in order to smell the rosewater itself, as some communities do at celebrations so each person can recite the fragrance blessing, since he does not intend to perfume his hand. Rav Pe'alim, part 2, siman 51, rules accordingly.
