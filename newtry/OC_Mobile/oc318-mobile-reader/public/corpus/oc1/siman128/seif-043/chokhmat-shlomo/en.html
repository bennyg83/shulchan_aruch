Se'if 43: After seven days of mourning he raises his hands; during the seven days he should leave the synagogue when kohanim are called. Magen Avraham writes, 'all the more so for us,' etc. Even if aninus does not apply because he does not intend burial today, he should apparently not raise his hands, since he lacks joy, as Magen Avraham says. This depends on the two rationales: according to Shach, once he is not an onen he may raise his hands; according to Magen Avraham he may not. Under Shach's view, a case I encountered can be resolved. A deceased kohen had a kohen brother; this occurred on Yom Tov, and the synagogue was so close to the house that other kohanim could not raise their hands there. The brother could seemingly enter and perform Birkas Kohanim. Some opinions in Shulchan Aruch permit relatives to become impure even not for burial needs. Although we rule otherwise, it remains a doubt. Shach, Yoreh De'ah 371, also treats impurity transmitted through an adjacent handbreadth as rabbinic. Magen Avraham disputes him and calls it Torah law, but Pnei Yehoshua in Berachos 3 and Sukkah 1 disagrees with Magen Avraham, preserving a second doubt. Thus perhaps non-burial impurity is permitted; and even if not, perhaps adjacent impurity is only rabbinic. Within a rabbinic matter one may rely on the permitting view, a double doubt like that used regarding blood in eggs. Even if he is an onen, if he does not intend burial today (or on Shabbos where Birkas Kohanim is performed) the brother may raise his hands in that adjacent synagogue when the others cannot. Under Magen Avraham's lack-of-joy rationale, however, he remains forbidden. The egg-blood comparison itself is difficult, since an opposite double doubt exists: perhaps the blood was in the prohibited place, and even if not, perhaps egg blood is Torah-prohibited. The matter requires consideration. My allowance to enter for Birkas Kohanim does not permit entering the adjacent house for a discretionary purpose. One may not deliberately create a rabbinic doubt or double doubt, comparable to deliberately nullifying prohibition. For a mitzvah, however, one may create such a doubt, just as siman 626 permits deliberate nullification for a mitzvah because the ordinary prohibition does not apply. Thus entry is permitted for the mitzvah but forbidden for personal purposes. I wrote similarly in my Yoreh De'ah responsa, siman 201. One may also say this is a double doubt involving something that will become permitted later, which ordinarily does not help; for a mitzvah it is not considered later-permissible, since tomorrow the mitzvah opportunity is gone.
