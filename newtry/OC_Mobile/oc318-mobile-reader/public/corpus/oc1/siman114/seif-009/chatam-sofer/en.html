In the Mechaber, seif 9: Ninety times. Note: See Responsa Chasam Sofer, Orach Chayim, siman 20. There it states that the Yerushalmi teaches at the beginning of the first chapter of Taanis: One who prayed and does not know what he mentioned, Rabbi Yochanan said that throughout the first thirty days the presumption is that he mentioned what he was accustomed to mention, and so forth. Hagahos Maimoniyos writes in the name of Maharam of Rothenburg that one who wishes to avoid having to repeat the prayer because of doubt should say the passage from 'You are mighty' through 'Who sustains life' as many times as it is said during thirty days. He did not write ninety times, because Rabbi Yochanan's statement may be founded upon the Gemara's teaching in Chagigah 9b that one who reviews his chapter one hundred times cannot be compared to one who reviews it one hundred and one times. This calculation relates to Pesach outside Eretz Yisrael, even though Rabbi Yochanan lived in Eretz Yisrael. See Tosafos to Shabbos 9b, s.v. ha lan, that his practice was to teach halachah to students from outside Eretz Yisrael. There are therefore one hundred and one recitations. During thirty days there are ninety prayers, Shacharis, Minchah, and Maariv. Subtract Maariv and Shacharis of the first day of Pesach, since the phrase is not changed until Mussaf, leaving eighty-eight. Add the eight Mussaf prayers of Pesach, three Mussaf prayers for the three Shabbosos, and two Mussaf prayers for the two days of Rosh Chodesh Iyar. These thirteen, together with the eighty-eight, total one hundred and one prayers. The Tur, however, writes ninety times. See what the Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 12, and it is possible that he intended the calculation stated here. This is also how I rule initially, that one should say it one hundred and one times. Nevertheless, if one said it only ninety times and afterward was uncertain whether he made the proper mention, I cannot rule that he must repeat the prayer against the implication of the Gemara and the Rama.
