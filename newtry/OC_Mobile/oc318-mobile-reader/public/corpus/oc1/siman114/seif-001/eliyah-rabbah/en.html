[1] In the second blessing, etc. Rabbi David Abudraham explains why the resurrection of the dead is mentioned three times in this blessing. First, when a person awakens from bed, Hashem restores his soul. Second, “Who revives the dead with mercy,” through rain, which is equivalent to the resurrection of the dead. Third, we conclude, “Blessed are You, Hashem, Who revives the dead,” referring to the resurrection in the future. This resolves Tosafos’ question in Bava Metzia 85, s.v. ki mata, concerning the words “Who revives the dead.” See there and examine carefully. One may further say that two additional references are counted: “Who keeps His faith with those who sleep in the dust,” where the yud in “sleepers” is vocalized with a sheva, referring to resurrection, for they are sleeping, as it is written, “Many of those who sleep in the dust shall awaken”; and “the King Who causes death and restores life.” This yields five references, corresponding to the four who are considered as dead and the fifth, actual resurrection. We always count the praise from the lower level upward. Know that resurrection corresponds to Yitzchak at the Akeidah, as Rabbi David Abudraham writes. Rain is mentioned in “You are mighty” because it is called the might of rain: every drop descends from a high place, yet the wind does not scatter it, and it falls only where the Holy One, blessed is He, desires. This is incomparable might. Mateh Moshe writes that the letter gimel in “Atah gibbor” should be pronounced with a dagesh. The Tur implies that this blessing alludes to the matters not entrusted to an agent. Their mnemonic is MAFTEIACH: rain, livelihood, resurrection, and childbirth. Perishah writes that childbirth is alluded to in “Who releases the bound,” because the child and mother were confined. For this reason, the blessing at a bris includes “Give thanks to Hashem,” etc., since four people must give thanks, one of whom is one released from imprisonment. One should have this intention when saying “Who releases the bound.”
[2] In the Mussaf prayer, etc. Levush writes that they begin specifically at Mussaf and not at Maariv because not everyone is present in the synagogue at night. They therefore do not begin or cease during Shacharis, lest those absent from the synagogue the previous night think the congregation had already begun at Maariv and err the following year. This is difficult, for Beit Yosef, citing the Yerushalmi, explains that this reason applies only to the first night of Pesach, when people are occupied with work or with matzah and maror. During Chol HaMoed Sukkos, however, work is prohibited and everyone comes to the synagogue at night, as Rabbi David Abudraham also writes. Rather, the reason is so that all the festivals should conclude with dew. The fact that they begin and cease on those days is likewise based on this reason, as Beit Yosef explains from the Yerushalmi. Levush, however, altered the explanation and wrote that it is because a rainy day is difficult. It appears that he wrote this according to our custom, under which we do not say “Who causes the dew to descend” during the summer. Nevertheless, even for this reason, that rain is difficult, the Sages did not establish the change on Yom Tov. It therefore appears to me that this is why we do not begin at Maariv on Shemini Atzeres. Examine carefully. Another reason for mentioning it in Mussaf is the Yerushalmi’s exposition: “Hear my song” refers to Torah; “give ear to my prayer” refers to prayer; “without deceitful lips” refers to Mussaf. What is written immediately afterward? “Let my judgment come forth from before You.” This shows that Mussaf is the primary prayer.
