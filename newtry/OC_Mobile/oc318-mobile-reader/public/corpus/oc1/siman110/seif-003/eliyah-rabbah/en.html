[6] The needs of Your people, etc. The Levush gives the following text: "Master of the worlds, the needs of Your people Israel are many and their understanding is limited. May it be Your will, Hashem my God, that You provide each and every one with sufficient livelihood, and each and every body with all that it lacks. Do what is good in Your eyes. Blessed are You, Hashem, Who hears prayer." Malbushei Yom Tov writes that opening with "Master of the worlds" is appropriate, though he did not find this in the Gemara or the poskim. Perhaps they relied upon the principle that a person should always first arrange praise of Hashem, etc. Lechem Chamudos, folio 26, writes similarly. In my humble opinion, one may answer that since he is in a place of danger, the Holy One, blessed is He, does not desire his praise, analogous to the verse, "Why do you cry out to Me?" Furthermore, regarding the text in the Mishnah, "Save, Hashem, etc.," one cannot say that he should begin, "Master of the worlds, save, Hashem." It is also possible that the essential prayer begins with "May it be Your will," and therefore it begins that way, like the other prayers discussed below in siman 230. However, I found in Abudraham, folio 147, the Levush's version beginning "Master of the worlds." Regarding "and do what is good in Your eyes," Rabbeinu Manoach explains: do not give us what appears good in our own eyes, for perhaps its ultimate result will not be good. Rather, give us what is good in Your eyes, which is complete good. The Kesef Mishneh cites this in chapter 4 of Hilchos Tefillah. See also what the Rambam wrote in his Commentary to the Mishnah, in the chapter Tefillas HaShachar: one may recite this prayer while walking, riding, or in whatever posture circumstances permit, and it contains no bowing. See below, at the end of this siman.
[7] And if he is able to stand, etc. Nachalas Tzvi explains the reason as follows: the Rambam's text of the Gemara, Berachos 29, reads, "A short prayer may be recited even while walking," implying that ideally it is preferable to stand. This is imprecise, for the Kesef Mishneh in chapter 4 already wrote in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah that the Rambam derived this from the incident in which Rav Chisda and Rav Sheshes were traveling. Rav Chisda stopped and prayed, and Rav Sheshes also stopped, applying the principle, "Since it can be done in the better manner, do not be called bad." The Rashba explains this explicitly in his chiddushim. There is, however, difficulty with the Rashba's statement: "This prayer was a short prayer, for if it had been Havineinu, standing would not derive merely from the principle of doing the better thing, because by law Havineinu must be recited standing." The plain meaning of the Gemara indicates that the discussion also refers to Tefillas HaDerech, since Rav Sheshes permits its recitation while walking, as is also clear from the Beit Yosef. It appears that the Kesef Mishneh did not mean a short prayer specifically, but rather any prayer analogous to a short prayer, because they share the same law. This is also implied by Abudraham, folio 131. Yet there remains a difficulty, for the Gemara on 29 says, "The halachah is that Havineinu is recited standing," and the expression "the halachah" implies that there is a dispute. Perhaps his text did not contain the word "the halachah," as it is also absent from the Rif and the Rosh.
