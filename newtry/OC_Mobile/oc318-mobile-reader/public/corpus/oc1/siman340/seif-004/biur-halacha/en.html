With liquids on the table. See Mishnah Berurah, where we copied the laws of writing and erasing, mainly from the Rambam, chapter 11, and from the Gemara. It is also evident there that if one wrote with mei tarya, which Rashi explains either as a fruit liquid or as rainwater, or with lead-water as concluded in Gittin 19, or with shichor, he is liable. The Tosefta adds that if one wrote with walnut shells or pomegranate peels, meaning with the liquid in which they were soaked, substances used for dyeing, he is liable. All these are called writing with something lasting, and the Rambam included them in "and the like." See Rashba, Shabbat 115b, who writes that for Shabbat liability we do not require writing that will last forever; it is enough that it lasts somewhat, the way people write things not meant to last forever, such as memoranda. For Shabbat, it is melechet machshevet and need not last forever like ink. Tosafot imply similarly. According to this, one who writes with the lead pencil commonly used today by merchants for accounts and reminders is plainly liable. It is not like writing with lead discussed in Gittin as not Torah writing, for we see that a pencil mark is recognizable and lasting. Writing with charcoal mixed in water also implies liability in the Gemara, all the more this, which lasts longer. According to the Mechaber in Even HaEzer siman 125, who rules like the Rambam that lead and shichor are valid in all cases, here one is certainly liable in all cases. Regarding writing with something non-lasting, the Mishnah there also mentions road dust and scribes' dust; the Rambam took some examples, and the same applies to anything whose mark does not stand. Regarding the requirement to write on something lasting, the Tosefta states that if one wrote on olive, carob, or gourd leaves, or on congealed blood or milk, he is liable, while on lettuce, leek, onion leaves, or vegetables he is exempt because it is not lasting. Rashba cites this Tosefta as law. The Rambam mentioned the common case and included the rest in his phrase that one must write with a lasting substance on a lasting substance. Why he omitted blood and milk, which is a great novelty, requires some study.
I saw a surprising matter in Peri Megadim, who wanted to distinguish between Shabbat and gittin against the Rashba, based on the Rambam and Yerushalmi that one is exempt for writing on vegetable leaves, while Gittin 17a says one may write on an olive leaf. He overlooked this Tosefta, which says that on Shabbat too one is liable for writing on an olive leaf.
Know further that if one wrote letters on foods he is also liable, for this is called writing on something lasting, as is evident from Gittin 19a: according to the Rabbis, if one wrote a get on them it is valid, since it is called lasting; only Rabbi Yosi HaGelili excludes them for a get by verse, because it must be like a book. Mordekhai in Klal Gadol explicitly writes that erasing applies to them, and consequently writing applies as well.
Regarding writing one letter, see Mishnah Berurah: it is specifically where he completed a book. It sounds that if he completed only a word he is not liable, though the Tosefta states the opposite; see Rashba. Know that although Rashi explains "and he completed a book" as the final letter of one of the twenty-four books, this is not necessarily literal. The same applies if a letter was missing in the middle of the book, so that it was not a complete book, and he corrected it; he is liable, because through him the book is completed. This is also evident from Rashi s.v. "Rava said," and so writes the Riaz cited in Shiltei HaGibborim.
Regarding correcting one letter, we copied the Rambam's words in Mishnah Berurah. The Gemara gives another case: he removed the roof of a dalet and made it a reish; this is cited by the Rosh and Rabbenu Yerucham. The Rambam did not copy it because Rava must be speaking where through this the whole book was corrected and no other error remained; therefore he is liable because it is like completing the book, as Rashi explains, and it is included in the law he already copied regarding completing a book.
Regarding writing identical letters, we copied the Rambam's language, namely where some word is thereby formed. If the letters are identical and no word is formed, such as alef alef or bet bet, the Rambam's language implies exemption, as the Maggid Mishneh writes. His view is that all the Tannaim of the baraita imply that one is not liable for alef alef of "a'azerkha"; how could the Tanna of the Mishnah disagree with all of them? Therefore when the Mishnah says "from one name," it means specifically cases such as gag or tat. He rules like the anonymous Mishnah and like Rabban Gamliel, Rabbi Yehudah's teacher, not like Rabbi Yehudah. Perhaps he also follows Rav Yaakov's explanation that the Mishnah is Rabbi Yosi, who obligates because of marking, and therefore obligates in all cases, whereas he rules like the baraita that disagrees. But Rashi, Rabbenu Yerucham, and Rav Ovadiah Bartenura hold that even for alef and alef one is liable. This also appears from the Rif and Rosh, who copied the Mishnah's plain statement, "whether from one name or from two names," and did not copy Rabbi Yehudah's baraita in the name of Rabban Gamliel; this implies that even alef alef is liable.
One who studies Rashi's view carefully through the sugya will see that he holds the Tannaim of the baraita do not disagree with the Mishnah at all. All agree that if one initially intended to write two alefs and the like, he is liable. When they say he is exempt, it is specifically where he intended initially to write a large word; although the Torah revealed through "one from one" that even writing part of it can be liable, that part must still be some word matching his original intent. Therefore he is exempt for the alef alef of "a'azerkha," because there is no word in it at all and his thought was not fulfilled. Rabbi Shimon, who exempts until he writes the whole name, also speaks in that kind of case, as is evident from Rashi s.v. "et kulo." But if he initially intended to write only two letters, all agree he is liable, even for alef alef and even according to Rabbi Shimon.
Regarding two marks, we copied from the Rambam that he is liable. At first glance this is difficult, for the Yerushalmi says: who is the Tanna who obligates for two marks? Rabbi Yosi; meaning, since he obligates even for two markings, one is liable even without letters, for mere signs. It implies that according to the Rabbis he is exempt. If so, since the Rambam rules unlike Rabbi Yosi, as he rules that one who writes with his left hand is exempt, he should also exempt for two marks. One can say that the Rambam holds the Bavli disagrees. Proof: on Shabbat 103 the Gemara challenges Rav Yaakov's explanation, "since the end is Rabbi Yosi, the beginning is not Rabbi Yosi"; this implies that according to Abaye the difficulty is resolved. But according to him too, the same question should apply to the Mishnah's ruling that two marks are liable. It must be that the Bavli holds even the Rabbis obligate, because marks are like letters. Regarding "read with one another," one may doubt whether if one wrote one letter and then another above it, this is called two letters, since they are not read with one another. One cannot prove from "one on the ground and one on the beam" being exempt, for perhaps the Mishnah states a not-only-this-but-even-that sequence. See the Yerushalmi; the commentators explain it as writing over writing, meaning one letter over another, exempt because they are not read together. Their explanation is not compelled, as I wrote in my novellae, and our Gemara also does not accept that explanation, for otherwise how would it know the Mishnah is not Rabbi Yehudah?
Regarding making marks, we copied the Rambam's language. Its source is the Yerushalmi in Klal Gadol: the first one who draws a form is liable for writing, and the second for coloring. This means the first marked the form with lead or the like, and the second passed color over it; therefore the first is liable only for writing because he made the image-mark, while the second, who applied the color to the marked place, is merely coloring. The Rambam likewise means that he made the outline of the image, as he writes, in the manner painters mark. The Rambam does not state explicitly whether two forms are required like two letters, or whether one form suffices because it is independently significant. From this Yerushalmi it is clear that one form suffices. Eliyah Rabbah asks from this Rambam against what he rules in chapter 10, copied in Mishnah Berurah, that one who draws a form on a vessel is liable because of makeh be-patish. According to our explanation it is somewhat resolved: in chapter 10 he is not speaking of a mere mark as here, where the decorative work is not yet complete, but of making the form at once; therefore writing does not apply, for it is a different matter. Here he only made the initial outline so that the painter will later know what to color, resembling writing, whose letters indicate something. Why the Rambam does not obligate there because of coloring may be because he is not speaking of painting with color, but engraving a form in a vessel, as is done with silver vessels. Therefore our Gemara on 76b also mentions only makeh be-patish, not coloring. With this the Magen Avraham's question in s.k. 6 is removed.
Since we are dealing with the laws of writing, I saw fit to raise one matter that troubled me. Rashi on the Mishnah of writing on two tablet leaves says they are coated with wax, implying that wax is called lasting. This is reasonable, for wax is no worse than congealed milk, regarding which the Tosefta says one is liable for writing on it. Yet below in siman 545:7 it says that wax tablets are not lasting, and Hagahot Ashri writes that even on Shabbat one is exempt. Regarding erasing, I copied the essence from the Rambam. What he wrote, "in order to write in the place erased," appears learned from the Gemara and the Rambam's ruling that demolishing in order to build in its place is demolishing; see Shabbat 31b. Peri Megadim writes that the requirement of erasing in order to write means that he intends it to be fit whenever he wants to write two letters there, not that he intends to write there on Shabbat itself. This is obvious.
