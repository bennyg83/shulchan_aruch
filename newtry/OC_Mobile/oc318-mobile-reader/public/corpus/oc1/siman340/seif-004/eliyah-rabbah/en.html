[11] [Levush] But, etc. The text is missing the Shulchan Aruch's statement that one should take care not to write with liquid on a table or in ash. If one writes with a finger in honey or congealed fat in the manner of writing in ash, it is prohibited, as stated by the Perishah. I found in Tosefta, chapter 12, that one who writes with nutshell dye, pomegranate peel dye, congealed blood, congealed milk, on an olive leaf, carob leaf, gourd leaf, or anything enduring is liable. Writing on leaves of lettuce, leeks, onions, vegetables, or anything non-enduring is exempt. The rule is that writing an enduring substance on a non-enduring surface, or a non-enduring substance on an enduring surface, is exempt; liability exists only for enduring writing upon an enduring surface. The same plainly applies to erasing. Rambam, chapter 9, states that leather, parchment, paper, wood, and similar materials are enduring surfaces, while ink, black pigment, sikra, gum, vitriol, and similar substances are enduring writing materials. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 63, writes that liquids, fruit juices, and dry dust are non-enduring, but are nevertheless prohibited initially because the mark is visible temporarily. Even writing a non-enduring substance on a non-enduring surface is at least prohibited, as Knesset HaGedolah writes. The Rambam there says one who writes on his flesh is liable because flesh is like leather. Although bodily heat will later remove the writing, this resembles writing that is subsequently erased. But one who incises the shape of writing on his flesh is exempt. This requires study, because Rabbenu Yerucham, page 87, implies liability. One who cuts or incises a form of writing on leather is liable. One who marks signs and drawings on a wall, document, or similar surface in the manner of artists is liable because of writing, and one who erases them is liable because of erasing. See seif katan 8. If a minor holds the pen and an adult holds the child's hand and thereby writes, the adult is liable. If the adult holds the pen and the minor holds his hand and writes, the adult is exempt. The Tosefta there similarly says that if an unwitting person holds the pen while an intentional violator holds his hand and writes, the intentional violator is liable. One who draws upon bean peels or lupine peels is exempt. One who rules lines in order to write two letters is liable. One who corrects a letter, such as removing the roof of a chet and thereby making two zayins, is liable. Rabbenu Yerucham further writes that one who removes the roof of a dalet and makes it a resh, thereby correcting the book, is liable. Shiltei Giborim in Perek HaBoneh cites the Riaz similarly. In the Yerushalmi, Perek Klal Gadol, I found that one may write a single point and be liable both for writing and erasing, or erase a single point and be liable for writing and erasing. For example, changing a dalet into a resh or a resh into a dalet. So too it appears from the Rokeach, siman 86. Writing with the left hand, back of the hand, foot, mouth, or elbow is exempt. Perhaps the same applies to erasing. Therefore it appears preferable to break the aforementioned cakes with the left hand, and likewise to open the aforementioned books with the left hand. A left-handed person's right hand has the status of everyone else's left hand. One who is equally skilled with both hands is liable with either.
[12] In the air, etc. We do not say that he is training his hand in writing. The same applies that one may watch a craftsman work on Shabbos even though this teaches him the craft, as stated by Terumat HaDeshen. The rule is not limited to the air. The same applies to tracing on a dry board or table when no visible mark remains, because that is like writing in the air.
[13] [Levush] And it is not a letter either, etc. The Bach asks that in Perek HaBoneh the Gemara states one is liable for making a mark. If the mark is non-enduring, one should at least be exempt but prohibited, like writing. The Bach therefore disagrees and rules that even marking parchment is prohibited; the Magen Avraham cites him. It appears to me that the Levush resolves this by saying that the Gemara's 'mark' means specifically a mark in the form of letters. A mere sign used to mark an error found in a book is permitted. So too it appears from Nachalat Tzvi. The Taz answers that because he makes only one mark and it is also non-enduring, there are two reasons for leniency. Even if he makes two marks, each is a separate sign and permitted; the Gemara concerns two marks serving one matter. Maharshal cites the Semak as prohibiting. They explain this specifically on paper, where the mark endures, but on parchment it is permitted. Malbushei Yom Tov writes that he found the Or Zarua prohibits making a fingernail mark on a margin as a reminder, and that Rabbi Yose, whose reasoning is generally accepted, prohibits even a mere mark. According to this, there is no difficulty with the Tur, for perhaps he does not rule like Rabbi Yose, as the Bartenura writes. The Semak may follow the Or Zarua. The Yerushalmi in Perek HaBoneh discusses whether engraving is included: one teaching says not engraving, another even engraving. Rav Chisda explains that the one excluding engraving speaks of raised writing, such as on a coin, while the one including engraving speaks of writing in a tablet. The Magen Avraham writes that Gittin 20 implies that attaching silver letters to a garment is called writing, so perhaps doing so on Shabbos is prohibited.
