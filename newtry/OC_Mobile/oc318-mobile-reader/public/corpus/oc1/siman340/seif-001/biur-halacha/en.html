Whether by hand. See Mishnah Berurah, that by hand one is exempt but it is forbidden, following the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Eliezer. See the Ran, who writes that Rabbenu Chananel rules like Rabbi Eliezer because the Rabbis explain his reason. I did not understand him: the first statement of Rabbi Eliezer is specifically against the Sages, and what does "explain his reason" mean? In the Rosh, Klal Gadol siman 6, it is explicit that Rabbenu Chananel holds that pulling wool by hand is exempt like the Sages. Now that we have merited Rabbenu Chananel's commentary, I found explicitly that his words refer to Rabbi Eliezer's second law, concerning braiding and painting the eyes, where several Rabbis explain why one is liable. But on Rabbi Eliezer's first law, Rabbenu Chananel explicitly rules like the Sages; see there and on 74b. Apparently the Ran did not have the Rabbenu Chananel commentary in our hands, only an abridgment copied to him in error.
And he is liable, etc. Tosafot write that this is only according to Rabbi Yehudah, who obligates for melakhah she-einah tzerikhah legufah, but according to Rabbi Shimon one is exempt even with a tool. Therefore the later authorities wondered at the Rosh and Tur, who copied the law though they rule like Rabbi Shimon, and at the Mechaber, who states the law plainly while in siman 316:8 he inclines to the first opinion that exempts. Magen Avraham explains that according to the view exempting melakhah she-einah tzerikhah legufah, the case is where he needs the hairs. But this is difficult, for then why did he rule that one who plucks white hairs from black is liable even for one? He does not need the hair itself; and even if he did, one hair alone is not the measure for liability. Can the beautification accomplished by plucking one hair complete the measure of two hairs needed for their own sake? I later found one who asked this. It seems because of this difficulty the Gra turned away from Magen Avraham's explanation and marked the law of plucking black from white as simply following the Rambam, who rules like Rabbi Yehudah in melakhah she-einah tzerikhah legufah. The same issue would force Magen Avraham to explain the case of hangnails and nails above in siman 328:31 as where he needs them, which is very forced, and Chacham Tzvi forcefully rejects this.
The Gra writes that the Shulchan Aruch here rules plainly like the Rambam, that melakhah she-einah tzerikhah legufah is liable. But how shall we answer the difficulty that Tur and Shulchan Aruch contradict themselves? The Rivash, siman 394, writes that the melakhah of shearing is considered a melakhah according to all even when he does not need the hair, because shearing in the Mishkan was not for the wool or hair but for the hide, such as the tachash skins. Therefore, whenever it is for the body's need, one is liable even though he does not need the hair; it is a melakhah tzerikhah legufah. His teacher the Ran writes similarly: even though he does not need the shearing, since he needs to beautify himself by it he is liable for shearing, like one who shears a hide, who is liable though he needs not the wool but the hide to remain hairless for tanning. One can say this is the view of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, unlike Tosafot. Chacham Tzvi agrees that their view follows the Rivash, not Tosafot. Sidrei Taharah objected that the Rosh in Bekhorot and the Ramban hold that pulling wool from an animal before slaughter, or feathers from a bird, is melakhah she-einah tzerikhah legufah. But that is no proof, as he himself later rejects: there the pulling is not for the hide but for the slaughter; where it is for the hide, even if he does not need the shearing, they hold he is liable. So too here, since he removes nails or hair to beautify himself, it is called needed for the body.
Since we have clarified that the sugya on Shabbat 94b necessarily speaks where one does not need the hair and nails, we are forced to say that several great Rishonim also agree with the Rivash and Ran: Rabbenu Chananel, Ramban, Semag, and Rosh. Rabbenu Chananel rules like Rabbi Shimon and nevertheless rules explicitly here that with a tool one is liable a chatat. Ramban proves in Kol Kitvei that the Rif rules like Rabbi Shimon, yet the Rif himself copies the sugya of hangnails and nails. The Rosh and Semag also explicitly rule like Rabbi Shimon and nevertheless copy the sugya of nails and of plucking black hairs from white as involving chatat liability. Thus all these poskim must hold like the Ran and Rivash, that this is considered a need for the body, and therefore the Shulchan Aruch states the law plainly.
I saw that the Gra in this siman writes that this sugya is a proof for the Rambam's ruling that melakhah she-einah tzerikhah legufah is liable. But according to all the geonim cited above, who hold that it is exempt and nevertheless copy this sugya, they must hold like the Ran and Rivash. See the Gra at the end of siman 316:8, where he inclines that the law is that melakhah she-einah tzerikhah legufah is exempt; he is evidently showing the basis for each major view. Regarding a woman who forgot to cut her nails before Shabbat and her immersion night falls on Shabbat, lechatchilah she should certainly tell a non-Jewish woman to remove them by hand and not with a tool, for with a tool it is a full melakhah according to the Rambam. If this is impossible, Magen Avraham inclines to permit through a non-Jew even with a tool, following Nekudot HaKesef in Yoreh De'ah siman 198, because here it is truly melakhah she-einah tzerikhah legufah: her intent is not beautification but immersion. For toenails, it appears better to permit cleaning by scraping than through a non-Jew; see Pitchei Teshuvah there in the name of Rabbi Daniel. Incidentally, what I wrote simply in Mishnah Berurah, that one who plucks hair from a dead animal is liable, is from the Taz and the Yerushalmi. Although Ba'er Heitev cites Yad Aharon against him, the law follows the Taz. He is not alone: so it appears from the Rosh in Bekhorot in the name of Ramban, explicitly in the Rosh in Klal Gadol, in Rabbenu Chananel on 74b, and in Rabbenu Yerucham. Yad Aharon's proof from the Rambam's wording, "one who shears," is no proof, for the Rambam chose shearing because he wished to conclude "whether from the living or the dead"; plucking is liable only after death, when plucking is its normal manner like shearing, whereas while alive plucking from an animal is exempt because it is not the normal manner and pains it. His proof from the Rambam mentioning plucking a bird's wing is also no proof, because plucking a bird's wing applies even while alive, as evident in Bekhorot, while with an animal alive liability is only by shearing. Therefore his claim that the Yerushalmi is not halakhic because our Gemara implies otherwise is also no proof: our Gemara speaks of plucking from the living, where only a bird is normal, not an animal. From a dead animal, plucking too is normal like shearing, as the Yerushalmi says, "its plucking is its shearing." We therefore should not move from the Yerushalmi and the poskim who follow it.
For two hairs. See Mishnah Berurah, from Magen Avraham, and this is the consensus of the later authorities for prohibition, unlike Olat Shabbat. The Rambam writes explicitly in chapter 18 halakhah 23 that all Shabbat melakhot, if one performs half the measure, involve prohibition; this includes writing one letter, removing one hair, and the like. Whether this is Torah or rabbinic: Magen Avraham implies it is Torah law, like every half-measure of forbidden foods, where we rule like Rabbi Yochanan that it is Torah-forbidden because it can combine to a full measure. That reason applies here too. Even if the source is "all fat," it is reasonable to derive from it to the entire Torah, as Tosafot write that Rabbi Yochanan's reason is that it can combine; so too for anything that can combine. Rashi on Shabbat 74, Hagahot Ashri, Or Zarua, Meiri, and Rabbenu Yerucham write similarly. One should not object from the rule that all Shabbat exemptions are exempt but forbidden, which Rashi explains as rabbinic, for Rashi meant to include all exemptions, at least rabbinically; some are indeed Torah-prohibited but exempt from chatat. This also resolves the Rambam's words in chapter 1. For law, one should not move from all the poskim cited.
And one plucks white hairs from black. One must examine whether this is only where there was one such hair, so that plucking it beautifies him, but if many remain perhaps not; or perhaps he is still somewhat beautified. In the Yerushalmi, Klal Gadol, regarding borer, Rabbi Yudan says that if one sits on a pile and selects all day he is not liable, because he accomplished nothing, since pebbles remain. Apparently the same would apply here according to the Yerushalmi. This is also somewhat implied in Makkot 20b by the commentator there. See Tur Yoreh De'ah siman 182 and Beit Yosef there, who incline that even one single white hair alone is a violation of lo yilbash; if so, the same should apparently apply to Shabbat liability, since the baraita equates them.
Even for one, he is liable. Kol Bo writes that here one is liable even by hand. See Eliyah Rabbah's reason, that the weekday manner is to pluck it by hand, though he remains with a difficulty from the Gemara. In my humble opinion, when it says that the Sages agree with Rabbi Eliezer, it means even by hand and even for one; the Rif's reading proves this.
