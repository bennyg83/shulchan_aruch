1) [Seif 1] It is forbidden to remove, etc. One who removes hair or nails by hand, whether from himself or another person, is exempt but the act is forbidden. If he uses a utensil, he is liable to bring a sin offering, and liability for hair requires two hairs. Tur. Rambam writes the same in chapter 9, halachos 8 and 9. One who uses a utensil is liable even when removing another person's hair. Bach; Magen Avraham at the beginning of the siman; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 1. Erekh HaShulchan, paragraph 1, likewise writes that since we rule like the Sages, there is no distinction between cutting one's own hair and another's: with a utensil one is always liable, while by hand one is always exempt. He adds that Yerushalmi, chapter Elu Devarim, proves that all opinions exempt one who removes another person's hair even with a utensil; see there. In practice, we follow the Babylonian Talmud, with practical relevance to the required rectification. See Tosafos, Shabbos 94b, beginning with the word 'But,' who writes that liability follows Rabbi Yehudah, who holds one liable for labor not needed for its own purpose. Rabbi Shimon exempts. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 1, writes that this is Shulchan Aruch's view; see there. Rivash, siman 394, however, holds that one is liable here even though the labor is not needed for its own purpose, because in the Mishkan they sheared the hides of techashim and did not need the hair, but only the hides; see there. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 1, cites him, and in paragraph 2 writes that Shulchan Aruch follows Rivash, not Tosafos; see there. Nishmas Adam, klal 21, paragraph 3, likewise writes that Tur follows Rivash; see there. This also appears to be the view of Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 2, and Chayei Adam there, paragraph 4. See below, paragraph 6, and above, siman 278, and examine carefully.
2) Taz writes in Yoreh De'ah, siman 198, that if a woman forgot to cut her nails before Shabbos and her immersion is Friday night, she should not ask a non-Jew to cut them. She can clean beneath them instead, so why should instructing a non-Jew be permitted? Moreover, she moves her hand and assists, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, siman 181. She should therefore clean them thoroughly. Shach in Nekudos HaKesef there, however, discusses the matter at length and permits it, because assistance has no substance, as stated in siman 328 at the end of seif 17 in the gloss, and because it is labor not needed for its own purpose, as Tosafos writes. Nevertheless, if they can be removed by hand, that is preferable. These are his words. It appears to me that she should tell the non-Jew to remove them by hand, because this creates two layers of rabbinic prohibition, which are permitted for a mitzvah, as stated in siman 307, seif 5. If removal by hand is impossible, they may be cut with a utensil. Magen Avraham at the beginning of the siman; see there. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1. According to what we wrote in the preceding paragraph, however, Tur and Shulchan Aruch follow Rivash that cutting hair or nails with a utensil incurs Torah liability even when one does not need the hair or nails. A non-Jewish woman may therefore be permitted only to remove them by hand or teeth, creating two layers of rabbinic prohibition permitted for a mitzvah. This is Shulchan Aruch HaRav's view in paragraph 2; see there. Ben Ish Chai, Parashas Ki Sisa, paragraph 16, writes that the practice in his city follows Shach, permitting a non-Jewish woman to remove them, but she is instructed to draw the Jewish woman's hand toward herself to cut, rather than having the Jewish woman bring her hand forward. Where this is the custom, it remains the custom. If no non-Jewish woman is available, it appears that in pressing circumstances one may rely upon thoroughly cleaning beneath the nails. For toenails, even when a non-Jewish woman is available, cleaning beneath them is preferable. So write the later authorities.
3) One who shears wool or hair from a domesticated or wild animal, whether living or dead, or even from its hide, is liable. Likewise, one who plucks a feather from a bird, whether living or dead, as Chayei Adam, klal 21, paragraph 2, writes, performs a derivative of shearing. One who spins wool directly from a living animal, however, is exempt because this is not the ordinary manner of shearing. Rambam, chapter 9, halachah 7. This implies that one who plucks wool from an animal is exempt because this is not the ordinary manner of shearing. Magen Avraham, paragraph 3; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 4, distinguishes that one who plucks from a living animal is exempt because its normal method is shearing, while one who plucks from a dead animal is liable. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 5, writes the same, based on Taz at the end of siman 336 in the name of Yerushalmi; see there. We already wrote there in paragraph 67 that Yad Aharon proves from the Babylonian Talmud and Rambam, chapter 9, contrary to Yerushalmi, that even plucking from a dead animal incurs no liability to bring a sin offering but only a rabbinic prohibition; see there. Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah here writes the same; see there. Some, however, hold that one who plucks from an animal is liable for uprooting something from its place of growth, as stated in Beis Yosef, siman 498, and Magen Avraham there, paragraphs 22 and 23; see there. Eshel Avraham here, paragraph 3, writes the same; see there. Chayei Adam, klal 21, paragraph 1, however, writes that reaping does not ordinarily apply to skin except with a utensil; see there. Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah writes the same in the name of Chiddushei Rashba; see there. All this applies when one needs the wool or hair. If he does not need it, this is labor not needed for its own purpose. Magen Avraham, paragraph 3; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 4. See above, paragraph 1, where we wrote that some hold one liable for shearing even when not needed for its own purpose, because in the Mishkan the hides of techashim and rams were sheared although their hair and wool were not needed; see there and our words on siman 278.
4) The minimum measure for liability is enough fiber to spin a thread twice the breadth of a sit, as stated in Shabbos 105b. Rashi explains a sit as the distance between the index and middle fingers. Rambam, chapter 9, explains it as the distance from the thumb to the index finger when spread with full force. It is close to two thirds of a zeres. See Maggid Mishneh there.
5) There: Whether by hand, etc. By hand one is exempt but the act is forbidden; with a utensil one is liable for two hairs, as stated above in paragraph 1. The same applies when cutting with one's teeth: one is exempt but the act is forbidden, as stated in the Gemara there.
6) There: And he is liable for two hairs. According to the view that one is exempt for labor not needed for its own purpose, this discusses when he needs the hairs. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1. See above, paragraph 1, where we cited the dispute and that some hold one liable even when he does not need the hairs; see there.
7) There: And he is liable for two hairs. This applies when he cut them with a utensil. If he used his hand or teeth, he is exempt but the act is forbidden, as stated above in paragraphs 1 and 6.
8) There: And he is liable for two hairs. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 1, writes that specifically two hairs are forbidden, while removing one hair entails no prohibition, although he leaves the practical law requiring examination. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, disagrees and writes that removing one hair is forbidden like any half measure, contrary to Olas Shabbos. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 3; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1; Birkei Yosef, paragraph 1, write the same. Erekh HaShulchan, paragraph 2, cites a dispute whether the Torah prohibition of half a measure applies specifically to eating prohibitions or to all prohibitions. He then cites Mahari Ayash in Hagahos Ara DeRabbanan, paragraph 41, who proves at length from Rambam that half a measure is forbidden by the Torah only in eating prohibitions and those resembling them; see there. All this concerns cutting one hair with a utensil. Cutting one hair by hand or teeth certainly entails only a rabbinic prohibition, since even removing many hairs that way is only rabbinically forbidden. Ben Ish Chai, Parashas Ki Sisa, paragraph 14, writes the same.
9) Therefore, one should take care not to scratch his beard extensively or occupy himself with it, lest he detach a hair. Women with much hair on their heads likewise should not scratch extensively with their nails, because they will certainly detach hairs. Hairs are common inside the nose, so one should gently remove somewhat dried nasal matter to avoid uprooting them. Similarly, when cleaning oneself after relieving himself, one should use abundant water and wipe gently. If too little water is used and he wipes forcefully, fine hairs will certainly be detached. Ben Ish Chai there. See above, siman 303, paragraph 127.
10) From here comes a life-giving rebuke to those who grow their hair to resemble non-Jews. This is forbidden even during the week, and on Shabbos they add further prohibitions by combing it, detaching hairs, and applying oil or similar substances. Even when done by a non-Jew, it is rabbinically forbidden and warrants excommunication. The excommunication may be treated leniently if the act was inadvertent because the person did not know it was forbidden, but he must nevertheless observe forty fasts, as Rema writes above in the gloss to siman 334. Magen Avraham there, paragraph 33, writes even more strongly that one should fast for violating a rabbinic techum, and even an inadvertent transgressor must fast, as Zera Emes, volume 2, Yoreh De'ah, siman 89, writes; see there. From here I warn others when I see them removing earwax or nasal matter on Shabbos in a manner that detaches hairs. Although an unintended result is permitted, one must be stringent when the result is inevitable. When using a cloth, squeezing may also be a concern if it is wet. Similarly, one washing his face who runs his hands over his beard and sidelocks and grasps them cannot avoid detaching hair. The incident involving Arizal is known: he inadvertently placed his hand into his beard and did not remove it until after Shabbos. For this reason, the precise phrase is, 'Anyone who observes Shabbos according to its law,' meaning that no halachic dispute should remain, but it should be observed in a manner universally accepted as lawful and without any halachic doubt. Ruach Chaim, paragraph 1.
11) There: And he is liable for two hairs. For nails, however, one is liable even for one nail. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2. This also appears from siman 328, seif 31; see there. This applies when it was removed with a utensil, as stated above.
12) There: And one who plucks white hairs, etc. The same applies to one who plucks black hairs from among white hairs. Tosefta, chapter 10 of Shabbos.
13) There: Even for one hair, he is liable, etc. This means that on Shabbos he is liable to bring a sin offering. Rashi explains that one is particular even about a single hair so as not to appear old. During the week, the act entails only the negative prohibition of wearing what is associated with the other gender, and so forth. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 1.
14) There: Even for one hair, he is liable, etc. Chayei Adam, klal 21, paragraph 6, writes that this applies even if he plucked it by hand. Kol Bo, page 27, writes the same, and Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3, cites him. Eliyah Rabbah adds, however, that the halachic authorities and Gemara imply that one who uses his hand is exempt; see there. Rashi in Makkos 20b also appears to imply this; see there. Tosafos there writes in the name of Rashbam that this follows specifically Rabbi Yehudah, who holds one liable for labor not needed for its own purpose; see there. We already wrote above in paragraph 1, however, that some hold one liable for shearing even when the labor is not needed for its own purpose; see there. Nevertheless, when one plucks a white hair by hand and does not need the hair, it is sufficient for a transgressor seeking rectification to address a rabbinic prohibition. This is in addition to rectifying the negative prohibition against a man wearing what is associated with a woman, which applies no less than during the week. See the end of siman 334, where we wrote some forms of rectification.
