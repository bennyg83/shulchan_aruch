[1] Whether by hand, etc., he is liable, etc. The Shulchan Aruch writes this without qualification, implying that even by hand one is liable. This requires study, because the Rambam in chapter 9 and the Tur write that one is liable specifically when using a utensil, but by hand one is exempt, whether removing one's own hair or another's, and whether hair, nails, or teeth. Perhaps the Shulchan Aruch follows Rabbenu Chananel, cited by the Beit Yosef, who holds that even by hand one is liable. Yet the Beit Yosef appears not to accept that position, and in siman 328, seif 31, the Shulchan Aruch rules that by hand one is exempt. One can answer that there the loose skin had already separated. Otherwise Rabbenu Chananel would contradict the law from Shabbos 94 that the halachah follows Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar. Rabbenu Yerucham, page 86, also rules like Rabbenu Chananel and immediately afterward rules that if the skin already separated, removal by hand is exempt, supporting this distinction. Yet another difficulty is that the Gemara there says everyone agrees that when done to another person one is exempt. Perhaps the Shulchan Aruch took account of the Rambam and Bartenura, who write that one is liable when removing another's hair by hand, although this is surprising because it contradicts the explicit Gemara, and the Rambam himself in his code rules that regarding another one is exempt. Lechem Chamudot in Perek HaMatznia distinguishes that hair incurs liability but nails do not, whereas the Rambam implies liability for both. See Tosafot Yom Tov. One must therefore say that the Shulchan Aruch's word 'liable' does not refer to removal by hand. Yet the Tur, Rabbenu Yerucham, and Levush rule in siman 278 that a melachah not needed for its own purpose is exempt, while this is such a melachah, as Tosafot on page 94 writes. Perhaps they hold it is needed for its own purpose; so Rashi's view appears from Tosafot there. Examine carefully. In Nekudot HaKesef, siman 198, I found that the present case is where he needs the hair or nails themselves. One must then explain siman 328, seif 31 similarly, though this is strained. Nekudot HaKesef there and the Magen Avraham further write that a woman who forgot to cut her nails before immersion should tell a non-Jew to cut them with the non-Jew's hands or teeth. Although I concluded that according to Rabbenu Chananel one should be stringent even by hand, for a mitzvah it is permitted. Indeed, Nekudot HaKesef writes an even greater leniency: if this is impossible, the non-Jew may cut them with a utensil, since the melachah is not needed for its own purpose. The conclusion of the Magen Avraham also somewhat implies this.
[2] Two hairs, etc. Olat Shabbos infers from the Shulchan Aruch's use of the plural 'his hair' that with one hair there is not even a prohibition. The Magen Avraham disagrees and says that one hair is prohibited like every half-measure. The proof of Olat Shabbos appears incorrect. The wording follows the Mishnah. According to Rabbi Eliezer, who holds one is liable for one hair, the same plural wording must still include one hair; the Sages then say it is prohibited rabbinically. Moreover, since Rabbi Eliezer holds one hair incurs a sin offering, how could the Sages permit it entirely? The law is explicit regarding writing: two letters incur liability, while one letter is exempt but prohibited. Likewise the text says 'his nails,' yet surely one nail can incur liability.
[3] He is liable even for one, etc. Kol Bo, page 27, says even by hand. This requires study, because Rashi there explains that cutting by hand is not the usual manner, and Rashi himself rules one exempt when removing hair or nails by hand. One must say that selecting white hairs from black hairs is ordinarily done by hand even on weekdays. Yet the Rambam and other authorities imply exemption by hand. According to Kol Bo, the Gemara on Shabbos 94 also becomes difficult: why does it not say that the Sages agree with Rabbi Eliezer concerning the Mishnah? The Taz at the end of siman 336 writes that one must take care not to peel skin from his body by hand or elsewhere, because it is like something attached to the ground, as implied at the beginning of Sanhedrin. This requires study. Even regarding hair and nails, liability is because of shearing, as with an animal or beast.
[4] Whether with a utensil, etc. The Maggid Mishneh, chapter 9, questions the Rambam because at the end of Eruvin it appears that if the loose skin is moist and removed with a utensil one is liable. Olat Shabbos answers that the Rambam holds a melachah not needed for its own purpose is exempt. This is astonishing, because just before this the Rambam imposes liability, and at the end of siman 328 the Tur and other authorities explicitly write that the Rambam holds such a melachah incurs liability. In my humble opinion, the Rambam there discusses dry skin and relies, regarding moist skin, on his later statement that in the Temple one may not cut it with a utensil. That must involve Torah liability, because there is no rabbinic prohibition in the Temple. So too it appears from Rashi and the Ran. The Magen Avraham did not address this. The Rambam writes that one who shears wool or hair from a domesticated or wild animal, whether living or dead, even from its hide, is liable, with the measure being approximately two-thirds of a zeret. Tal Orot explains that 'hide' means flayed hides whose wool was removed but that were not yet processed. The Taz at the end of siman 336 writes that one must take care with a garment made from animal skin not to pull hair from it, because this can incur a sin offering. He cites the Yerushalmi that one who pulls hair from a dead animal is liable because pulling is its form of shearing. The Magen Avraham, however, writes that one who pulls wool from a living animal is exempt; see siman 498. If he does not need the wool or hair, it is also a melachah not needed for its own purpose. It appears that this exemption applies specifically to a living animal, whose normal method is shearing rather than pulling, unlike a dead animal. For the same reason, pulling a feather from a bird incurs liability because birds are not normally shorn. So too it appears from the Yerushalmi in Perek Klal Gadol.
