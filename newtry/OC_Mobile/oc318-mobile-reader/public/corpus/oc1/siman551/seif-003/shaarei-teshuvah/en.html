And the baal berit. See Baer Heitev. See Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, vol. 3, siman 37, who challenged Baer Heitev and wrote that one should not infer from the fact that he did not write a permission to cut hair; on the contrary, since he also did not write a prohibition, one may say that he relied on what he had written permitting one to wear white garments, and the same applies to cutting hair, since they customarily cut hair because it is his festival day. In Machazik Berachah he wrote in the name of Responsa Zera Emet that his view is to prohibit haircutting and wearing white garments during the week in which Tishah BeAv falls, and he challenged the Pri Megadim, writing that the Rama permitted only Shabbat garments but not white garments, etc. But in the name of Bnei Yehudah he wrote to prohibit haircutting and permit white garments; see there in Mahadura Batra, letter 5. See Responsa Or Neelam, siman 10, who wrote that where Tishah BeAv is on Sunday and the circumcision is on the preceding Thursday, since there is no week in which Tishah BeAv falls and no prohibition by the law of the Gemara, one may permit the mohel and baal berit to cut their hair in honor of the circumcision. See Pri Megadim there, who challenged Baer Heitev for understanding what is written in Yoreh Deah siman 394, "to shave," as referring to cutting the hair of the head; this is not so, for it refers to cutting nails. Likewise, in Darkei Moshe he did not mention cutting hair, and so too in Levush, etc. But he challenged him for no reason, for Baer Heitev concluded there, "and see what I wrote in Beer HaGolah," and in Beer HaGolah there in Yoreh Deah he wrote that Darkei Moshe did not mention cutting hair, and perhaps it refers to nails, etc. In Baer Heitev there he brought the words of Levush and wrote that Beer HaGolah objected that it should have said "and to cut them," etc.; see there.
(See Eliyah Rabbah and Eliyah Zuta, from whom Baer Heitev copied, except that he omitted what is written there, that the ruling in Yoreh Deah siman 393 concerns nails, because he referred to Baal HaNefesh. See there in Eliyah Rabbah and Zuta, that before the week in which Tishah BeAv falls one may also permit haircutting, and if it falls on Shabbat and is postponed they rule, following the poskim, that it does not have the status of the week in which Tishah BeAv falls; see there.) See also Pri Megadim, who wrote to defend the custom that mourners serve as shoshvinim to bring the bride and groom to the chuppah and to eat there. The Taz wrote that one should not permit eating there, and all the more so those who are lenient have whom to rely on, namely the words of the Rosh in Perek HaCholetz, etc. Although other mourners have the custom to prohibit, nevertheless shoshvinim are considered a great mitzvah, etc., and therefore they customarily ruled leniently; see there. In our country the custom is to be stringent like the Taz, except that when they are relatives they are like attendants during the meal, like members of the feast, and afterward they eat with them. Nevertheless it is not proper to be there to hear instrumental music, and all the more so one mourning for his father or mother, or a woman mourning her husband; even if she is serving there, they should refrain from hearing singers and songstresses. See Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 391 regarding cutting nails for immersion, in the Taz and Shach there and Nekudot HaKesef there. What the Magen Avraham wrote, that the one who brings the infant in and the one who brings him out are not included in this, applies likewise and all the more so to the other honorees who pass the infant from one to another, those who place him on the chair of Eliyahu z"l, and those who place him on the knees of the sandak or hold the infant while "Asher kidesh yedid" is recited. There would be some reason to be lenient for the one who places the infant on the sandak's knees, because the sandak's legs are compared to the altar. What is written in Yoreh Deah siman 265, that he is like one who offers incense, see Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 26, who explains why this does not contradict Mahari Weil's ruling that one should not give one sandak two of his sons for this reason: perhaps the concern applies to the sandak and not to the mohel, for if the sandak's legs are like the incense altar, then the mohel is like the one who offers the incense, etc.; see there. At first glance there is a difficulty with the very statement that the sandak, who resembles the altar, should not be repeated because it brings wealth, for we find this only with the one who offers the incense, derived from "they shall place incense," etc. Nevertheless there is some support from Yoma 43, where R. Yehudah says there was no lottery for the coal-pan, etc.; the Gemara suggested that since it was uncommon and brought wealth, they should institute a separate lottery for it, etc. Thus even the coal-pan on which the incense is placed brings wealth like the one who places the incense. But I have not seen people follow this practice, and in the holy community of Brody the custom is that the father of the child places the infant on the sandak's knees, and it is a fine custom. The father of the child's father and the mother's father also customarily rule leniently for themselves and their wives to wear Shabbat garments. It seems that the custom is to be lenient also for other relatives who, if it were other days, would normally wear Shabbat garments; they wear them now too, and this requires study. It appears that it is distressing for the father of the child and the mother when these relatives do not participate in their joy by wearing white garments, but other relatives should be stringent. See Machazik Berachah in the name of Zera Emet, that a baal berit may not wear new garments during that week. The women who carry the infant to the synagogue, and the one who passes the infant to the men's synagogue, called kvater shaft, also wear Shabbat garments, since they have no other mitzvah in the circumcision except this one. See Pri Megadim.
