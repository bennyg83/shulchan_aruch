Fourth or fifth, etc. Some authorities are lenient after several generations because the force of the original forbidden agent has ceased, but the Magen Avraham is astonished at relying on this where the agent itself set the food before Pesach.
Where the setting was caused by both forbidden and permitted agents, it may be a case of this and this causing, which is permitted. But if the forbidden agent alone had sufficient power to set or leaven, it is not considered this and this causing and remains forbidden.
And the Radbaz wrote. The cheese was not necessarily forbidden because the stomach lining was a setting agent, for the prohibition in the lining may have come only from absorbed chametz taste. Rather, it may have been forbidden because there was not sixty, or because it was made on Pesach when chametz forbids in any amount.
And it appears that its law is like something cooked, etc. Whatever leniency the Rema gives for food cooked in a chametz vessel applies to taste absorbed through soaking in a chametz vessel, for soaking is no worse than cooking in this regard.
This applies specifically where the stomach lining remained in the chametz vessel for a full twenty-four hours, so that the taste was absorbed through soaking, as explained in Yoreh De'ah.

If there is no kezayis among all the pieces, it sounds from the Semak that one need not destroy them, unless there is another reason to require destruction, such as concern that they will gather together.
According to the poskim who disagree with the Semak, the Gemara's cases must be explained as pieces attached like something made to strengthen, or as pieces that are somewhat soiled and therefore not subject to the same concern.
And what is written in siman 460, se'if 3. Although crumbs generally become nullified on their own, in dough they gather together and therefore do not become automatically nullified; that is the distinction explained there.
And the Hagahos Maimoniyos wrote in the name of Re'em. The leniency applies only where the chametz is somewhat unfit or soiled. If it remains somewhat fit to eat, the Taz asks that one should still require destruction lest he come to eat it.
The conclusion depends on whether the pieces are fit to eat, attached, made to strengthen, somewhat soiled, or likely to gather. Where they are fit and likely to be eaten or to combine to a kezayis, destruction is required; where they are attached or soiled and less than a kezayis, there is basis for leniency as explained by the Magen Avraham.
