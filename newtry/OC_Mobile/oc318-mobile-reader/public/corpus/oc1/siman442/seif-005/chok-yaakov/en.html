So too ruled responsa Maharam Lublin regarding dough that became leavened with beer dregs that passed over Pesach. This is also the agreement of the later authorities in siman 447, seif 11. The same appears in the gloss of the Netz in this siman in the name of Rav Nosson Katz regarding mead that was set with beer dregs. So too wrote the Rashba in responsum 850, even if one set mead with mead dregs that had themselves already been set with beer dregs. See the Taz, who discussed at length how to conduct oneself with mead. The conclusion is that some are lenient to drink on the last day of Pesach mead from the whole year, as the Rama wrote in siman 447. The same applies to anything cooked in a chametz vessel, where one may be lenient on the last day of Yom Tov. It appears that this is because there is a doubt whether the vessel had been used that day, and we rule that ordinary vessels are presumed not to have been used that day, and on the last day of Yom Tov we say this. But if there is doubt whether it was fermented with beer dregs, there is no room to be lenient. Certainly in places where they cook mead all year in cauldrons used for making brandy, which is sharp and strong and gives a favorable taste to the mead, as is known, the fact that the vessel was not used that day does not help, and there is no doubt at all. Therefore one who guards his soul should distance himself from this even on the last day of Yom Tov, and all the more from buying mead from a non-Jew on the last day of Yom Tov, lest he mix beer into it. Although an agent that causes standing and something made for taste are not nullified only D'Rabbanan, as Tosafot in the chapter Kol HaZevachim and the Issur VeHeter wrote, nevertheless, since an agent that causes standing is like something present in its own substance, it is forbidden even after the fact if he kept it until after Pesach. It is possible that this is the view of the Tur. The Magen Avraham wrote that in the Tur's view an agent that causes standing is not nullified according to the Torah, but his words are not necessary. In Sefer Beis Hillel he wrote about a vessel designated for kneading dough, in which sourdough was placed and bread baked before Pesach; a little dough remained stuck to it, they forgot to clean it well and did not sell it, Pesach passed, and after Pesach they forgot and kneaded dough in it and baked bread. He answered that the bread is permitted for eating, since it is nullified in sixty, and if the stuck dough is less than sixty he should cast its value into the river. He further wrote that although sourdough is made for its taste and is not nullified even in a thousand, the dough nullifies the sourdough, citing Terumas HaDeshen. These words are very difficult. First, they contradict themselves, for initially he wrote to nullify in sixty and then wrote that it is nullified in a thousand. Second, his proof from Terumas HaDeshen is against the Gemara and all the poskim, for something that leavens or seasons forbids in any amount, as the Rambam wrote and as all early and later poskim agree, even by D'Rabbanan prohibitions. His proof from Terumas HaDeshen falls away, for that source only wrote that the dough is not nullified to the sourdough with respect to the obligation of challah, but certainly the sourdough itself is not nullified. However, if the sourdough did not have enough strength by itself to leaven, then it is nullified, as is explicit in Yoreh De'ah, siman 98, seif 1, and at the end of siman 87, that where this and that together cause the effect, it is permitted if the prohibition alone does not have enough strength to leaven. So too regarding items made for taste, they forbid only as long as their taste is sensed. Nevertheless, it appears to me that perhaps all these cases in which we say that something made for taste is not nullified, or that an agent that causes standing is not nullified, are no worse than a mixture of less than sixty that passed over Pesach, where it suffices to cast the benefit of the prohibited part into the Dead Sea, as explained below in siman 447, seif 11 in the gloss. However, from some later authorities that I cited it is clear that the entire item is forbidden, because it is like the prohibited substance itself. Still, in a case of great loss, one may perhaps permit benefit from it by casting the benefit of the standing agent into the Dead Sea. The later authorities wrote in the name of responsa Radbaz that cheese set with a stomach that had absorbed from a chametz vessel is forbidden to eat but permitted to keep.
