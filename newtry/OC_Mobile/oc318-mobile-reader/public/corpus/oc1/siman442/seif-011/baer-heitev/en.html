For dough. This is also the custom regarding boards used for filled pastries, rolling pins, and all utensils used with dough. Maharil. Knesses HaGedolah writes that our women scrape them thoroughly and put them away. Even regarding a kneading trough, Bach implies that this is only a stringency. Kneading troughs made from a single piece of wood and having no crack may certainly be scraped clean. In utensils. This refers to wicker baskets. A sack. Levush writes that shaking and laundering are not sufficient for the sacks themselves. Eliyah Zuta writes that the same applies to a new sack used for Pesach flour. If one wishes to wash it and reuse it for Pesach flour, the seams and all patches must be opened. My grandfather, the great authority, would teach this publicly. It requires study whether, after the fact, if flour was placed in it during Pesach without opening the seams, the flour may be permitted even for eating. Bach, at the end of siman 452, rules stringently even regarding an old sack without patches and seams. My grandfather acted leniently, in accordance with the implication of Shulchan Aruch. Knesses HaGedolah likewise writes that this is the accepted custom. Magen Avraham writes that mere rinsing is ineffective. On the contrary, the dough adheres to the sack. It therefore requires washing with hot water and ashes, together with beating. Tobacco is ordinarily soaked in beer and must be locked in a room or placed behind a partition. The same applies to saffron. See Magen Avraham, siman 467, seif katan 10. It appears to me that everything depends upon the practice of the particular country.
