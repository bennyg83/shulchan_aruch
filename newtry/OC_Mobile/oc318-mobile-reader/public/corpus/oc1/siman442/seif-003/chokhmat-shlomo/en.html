And below it will be explained, etc. In my humble opinion, even according to those who disagree, one should forbid eating this theriac, lest the chametz in it is what sets it, like the case of setting cheeses nearby, where nullification before Pesach does not help. And likewise if he set cheeses, etc. The reason is that since it is the setting agent, a thing that sets is considered as if it is present in its own substance. Therefore, if he set honey-water called mead with beer yeast, all the mead is forbidden on Pesach, just as with wine of non-Jews in Yoreh De'ah siman 123, se'if 15, where the whole dough is forbidden. If he also set it with yeast that is not chametz, this is a case of this and this causing, and is permitted according to the law stated in Yoreh De'ah siman 87 regarding the stomach lining. But it is difficult: in Yoreh De'ah siman 87 we say that if one set cheese with a kosher stomach lining, it is nullified in sixty; why not say the same here, that before Pesach it is nullified in sixty? One must say that chametz is stricter here: because the power of the setting agent is active during Pesach, we say that it reawakens, even though with ordinary chametz we do not always say it reawakens. Regarding our mead during the rest of the year, it appears to be complete chametz for two reasons. First, it is cooked in a kettle in which beer is cooked; if the beer cooking was within twenty-four hours, the mead absorbs chametz taste, though if it was not within twenty-four hours the taste is impaired and is permitted before the first night of Pesach. Second, it is fermented with beer yeast, which makes all the mead forbidden, just like a whole dough set by forbidden matter in Yoreh De'ah siman 123. Therefore anyone cooking mead for Pesach must be careful to ferment it with yeast that is not from chametz. Even yeast of mead from the rest of the year may itself be chametz, because that mead may have been fermented with beer yeast. If it is fermented in the special vessel called a kvasnik in Russian, which leavens all mead poured into it, the absorbed leavening power in that vessel came from earlier chametz mead. There is some leniency regarding the kettle where there is doubt whether the beer was cooked within twenty-four hours, because we generally say that ordinary utensils are not assumed to have been used within twenty-four hours, even Jewish utensils, because of a double doubt, and impaired taste is permitted after the fact. One might therefore permit if he fermented the mead with yeast from ordinary mead. But in siman 447, se'if 5, it will be explained that regarding chametz we do not necessarily say that ordinary utensils are not ben yomo. More importantly, the second concern remains: ordinary mead may sometimes have been fermented with beer yeast, and then all its yeast is chametz. Since this is a Torah doubt, one should be stringent even on the last day of Yom Tov. The custom is already to be careful not to ferment Pesach mead with year-round mead, and to use a kashered utensil, or to fill it three times, each time for a full day, with fresh water, as in Yoreh De'ah siman 135, se'if 12, regarding wine vessels of non-Jews. Some are lenient to drink year-round mead on the last day of Yom Tov, as the Rema in siman 447, se'if 5, is lenient on the last day regarding foods cooked in chametz vessels. That leniency seems to rest on doubt whether the vessel was ben yomo, but where there is doubt that it was fermented with beer yeast there is no basis to be lenient, and certainly not where the mead is cooked in kettles used all year for distilled spirits, which are sharp and strong and improve the mead's taste, so the lack of ben yomo does not help. Therefore one who guards his soul should distance himself from it even on the last day of Yom Tov, and all the more so from buying mead from a non-Jew on the last day, lest he mixed beer into it.
