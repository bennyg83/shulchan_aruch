And anything similar. See Baer Heitev, and see Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 20, who wrote that whisky made from malt is forbidden according to the Torah. Even though the taste of the malt changed, nevertheless since the essence of the malt is to make the whisky, it is like very astringent fruits that are not fit to eat raw but are eaten by cooking in water or with honey and sugar; even though their taste changes through cooking and frying, they remain in their identity, etc.; see there. In Pnei Yehoshua, in Kiddushin, in the discussion of chadash, he elaborated on this, as did Responsa Shev Yaakov, part 10, siman 41. See Responsa Beis Ephraim, siman 49, one responsum to the Gaon Rabbi Yehoshua, author of Menoras HaMaor, who strongly upheld the permissive view for whisky because it is merely vapor. In the work Be'er Yaakov he discussed this at length with his fellow geonim. See Responsa Noda BiYehuda Mahadura Tinyana, siman 64, from whose words it appears that he did not want to firmly rule a practical permission on this basis, even together with the permission of nullification. See Responsa Beis Ephraim, where I wrote in the name of the Gaon author of Sedei Yehoshua, who inclined to permit the sale of malt because malt is chametz nukshah; see there. If so, what is implied from the beginning of the Chacham Tzvi's words, that one transgresses bal yera'eh and that it is Torah-forbidden, is not compelled according to several poskim who hold that nukshah is rabbinic. Nevertheless, there I explained that everything depends on the circumstances: whether it became so spoiled that it is not fit to eat at all or not; see there. Accordingly, one can say that the Chacham Tzvi speaks of malt whose taste changed but that is still somewhat fit to eat after grinding. However, from his comparison to astringent fruits, this does not sound so, and it requires analysis.
