(13) That were laundered, etc. Meaning, even close to Pesach.
(14) With wheat starch. This applies even if a small amount of actual chametz is visible on them.
(15) And likewise papers, etc. This likewise applies even if a small amount of actual chametz is visible on the outside. The reason in all these cases is that its original form has already been destroyed, as the Mechaber explains.
(16) It is permitted to retain them. Nevertheless, one may not spread garments laundered with wheat starch upon the table, since a small amount of actual chametz is sometimes found on them and some of it might crumble into the food. All the more so, one may not place Pesach flour in them. Women’s scarves that are commonly stiffened with flour until they become somewhat thick, and from which particles commonly crumble, should not be worn while eating. Those who are meticulous are accustomed not to launder garments or scarves with wheat starch during the thirty days before Pesach, but only with substances that are not among the five species of grain.
(17) And some are stringent, etc. They hold that when the substance is visible from the outside, the form of the chametz is still considered intact even though it has dried. According to them, pasting papers with chametz is permitted only when no actual chametz is visible from the outside. When pasting paper to a window, they therefore require that the dough be made very thin and that all of it be inserted between the joined surfaces so that none protrudes at all. If it was done more than thirty days before Pesach, however, all agree that there is no concern even if it is visible from the outside, since it has certainly hardened and become repulsive, and is comparable to bread that became moldy until it was unfit for a dog. Likewise, if all the chametz paste on the paper was less than a kezayis, all agree that it need not be destroyed even when it was applied within thirty days of Pesach.
