3) Maggid Mishneh writes there that Rambam rules in chapter 1 that eating a chametz mixture does not violate a prohibition unless one consumes an olive-sized quantity of chametz within the time needed to eat a pras. A pras is three eggs according to Rambam in chapter 1, which is also the opinion of the Shulchan Aruch; some authorities say four eggs, as stated above in siman 368, se'if 3, and siman 612, se'if 4. See there. Certainly, according to Rambam, one likewise transgresses here only when the mixture contains an olive-sized quantity within the time needed to eat a pras, and this is the primary ruling. Some Geonim maintain that one transgresses even with less than this. I have also seen one who explains Rambam to mean that although one does not violate the eating prohibition, one must eliminate the mixture, because it contains an olive-sized quantity of chametz; although mixed into many olive-sized quantities of other food, one transgresses by retaining it. See there. Maran likewise writes in Kesef Mishneh there that for this matter there is no distinction between whether or not it contains an olive-sized quantity within the time needed to eat a pras. See there. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1, writes that this also appears from Ran and is the primary ruling. This also appears to be the opinion of Magen Avraham, subsection 1, and Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1, writes that the later authorities agree that even without an olive-sized quantity within the time needed to eat a pras, one violates the prohibition against chametz being seen. Mekor Chaim, subsection 1; Vilna Gaon; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1; Chayei Adam, rule 121, subsection 1, write likewise. This applies specifically to a mixture of different kinds, which is not nullified by a majority under Torah law according to those who maintain that the flavor of a prohibited substance is prohibited like its substance under Torah law. In a mixture of the same kind, however, such as flour from grain that became chametz mixed with flour from grain that did not become chametz, the prohibited flour is nullified by a majority under Torah law and may be eaten. It may certainly be retained under Torah law. Rabbinically, however, retaining it is forbidden lest one eat it during Pesach, and it must be eliminated. Nevertheless, if one transgressed and retained it, it is permitted after Pesach, even for consumption according to Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 13, because one did not violate the prohibitions against chametz being seen and found, as explained below in simanim 447 and 467 by the later authorities. The same law applies to a mixture of different kinds when the permitted substance is sixty times the chametz. Chok Yaakov, there; Chok Yosef, subsection 1. See below, siman 447, subsection 244. Wheat flour mixed with barley flour, and the like, is considered a mixture of different kinds. Eshel Avraham, subsection 1.
