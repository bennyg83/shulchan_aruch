(s.k. 1) Mixtures, etc. See siman 612 regarding the measure of eating a peras: some say it is four eggs, and some say it is three large eggs. 'For there is a kezayis of chametz there' means that only with regard to eating do we require a kezayis within the time of eating a peras. If the chametz were present by itself and one ate a little, paused, and ate a little more until he completed a kezayis, but more than the time of eating a peras passed from the first eating to the last, the amounts do not combine. All the more so where it is mixed: if within four eggs, according to one view, or within three eggs, according to the other view, there is less than a kezayis, then to eat a kezayis of chametz he must eat more than four or three eggs, and the eating of the kezayis of chametz takes longer than the time of eating a peras, so it does not combine. But regarding bal yera'eh, in any event he has a kezayis in his house, even if it is mixed among many eggs, and therefore he transgresses.

For according to the Torah it is permitted, etc. For we hold like Rabbeinu Tam, who rules like the Sages that according to the Torah a substance mixed with its own type is nullified by a majority, while rabbinically it requires sixty.
But a substance mixed with a different type, etc. For we hold that taste is like substance according to the Torah, except that one is not lashed for it. It combines automatically, according to the second view brought above and as the Kesef Mishneh agreed.
See the Tur according to Rashi's view, who ruled the opposite: a substance mixed with its own type forbids in any amount according to the Torah, while with unlike types, the rule that taste is like substance is not according to the Torah. Thus, according to Rashi, the law is exactly the reverse of Rabbeinu Tam's view.
But we rule like Rabbeinu Tam in both matters, both regarding a substance mixed with its own type and regarding taste being like substance. Therefore the law is as the Magen Avraham wrote. The practical difference is that one does not transgress bal yera'eh, even according to us, who rule like Tosafos that even something forbidden only rabbinically to eat must nevertheless be destroyed, as the Magen Avraham wrote in this se'if katan. Still, if he transgressed and kept it, where he did not transgress bal yera'eh it is permitted after Pesach, as explained at the end of siman 447, because chametz that passed Pesach is forbidden only as a penalty, and where he did not transgress bal yera'eh they did not penalize him. A case that has the taste of chametz means that there is not sixty against the chametz, but there is no actual substance of chametz, for example where chametz fell into hot permitted food, the permitted food received the taste of chametz, and the chametz itself was removed.
Although it is certainly forbidden to eat. However, regarding bal yera'eh, the Eliyah Rabbah holds that one does not transgress. Therefore after Pesach, even if one is stringent to forbid eating it, it is at least permitted for benefit. He wrote that for this reason his father-in-law, Maharash of Prague, permitted butter in which bread and flour had been soaked and that had been kept until after Pesach: one should cast the benefit of the bread and flour into the sea, and the butter may be sold to a non-Jew. This refers to the practice of cooking butter to remove the scum, placing bread and flour into it to absorb the scum, and then straining the butter. Chok Yaakov wrote below, siman 447, s.k. 17, that if crumbs fell into wine and one strained it before Pesach, it is permitted on Pesach according to all. Even if some fine crumbs enter the wine through the straining, since they are very fine and mixed as liquid with liquid, if they were nullified before Pesach they do not reawaken on Pesach. The same applies to this butter, which is normally strained before Pesach. Although before straining there was not sixty in the butter against the bread and flour, after straining, even if a little passed through, there is certainly sixty against that little. Since it is considered liquid with liquid and was nullified before Pesach, it does not reawaken on Pesach. If one would forbid the butter, it would have to be only because of the taste it absorbed before straining, when there was not sixty; that is taste without substance. In such a case, initially it is rabbinically forbidden to keep and must be destroyed, but if he did not destroy it he did not transgress bal yera'eh, and after Pesach it is at least permitted for benefit through redemption; see the end of siman 447. The Tur's statement that it is permitted to mix means not only that if it was already mixed it may be kept according to the view that a chametz mixture is permitted to eat according to the Torah, though rabbinically forbidden, but that one may even initially mix it in order to keep it, since it is still a time of permissibility, as the Magen Avraham wrote below in siman 626, s.k. 3. The same applies, according to the Tur, to chametz nukshah in its own substance where its eating is not forbidden by Torah law.
The words of the Beis Yosef here require examination. He wrote on the Tur's statement that according to Rabbi Eliezer a chametz mixture is under a negative commandment without kareis, and according to that it is forbidden to keep on Pesach; according to the Sages, even though there is no negative commandment, there is still some prohibition, and according to them it may be kept. The Beis Yosef understood from this wording that according to the Sages there is no negative commandment but there is nevertheless a Torah prohibition in eating, and therefore he wondered why the Tur permitted keeping it. But this cannot be the Tur's intent, for afterward he explicitly wrote that the Rif ruled like the Sages, that there is no negative commandment either in mixtures or in chametz nukshah, only a rabbinic prohibition, and therefore it is permitted to mix and keep it. Thus it is clear that when he first wrote 'only a prohibition,' he meant a rabbinic prohibition. The Taz already noted this and added further challenges; see there.
But Tosafos at the beginning of the chapter, etc. They wrote reasons for stringency with chametz even after nullification: either because of concern that one may eat it, unlike other prohibitions, or because chametz remains with him all seven days and there is a greater concern he may come to eat it, or because the Torah was stricter with chametz than with other prohibitions. Therefore the Sages required destruction even where the eating prohibition is only rabbinic.

And the Peri Chadash wrote. The conclusion is that where there is less than a kezayis and it is either attached, made to strengthen, or somewhat soiled, one has grounds not to require destruction; but where it is gathered or fit to be eaten, there remains concern that it must be destroyed, either because of bal yera'eh when it combines to a kezayis or because one may come to eat it. Each case follows the distinctions explained by the Magen Avraham and the authorities cited there.
