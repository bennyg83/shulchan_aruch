1) [Se'if 1] One transgresses regarding a mixture containing chametz, etc. One is not flogged for violating the prohibitions against chametz being seen or found unless one acquired chametz during Pesach or caused a substance to become chametz, so that one performed an action. If one possessed chametz before Pesach and Pesach arrived without one eliminating it, but one merely left it in one's domain, then although one violated two prohibitions, one is not flogged under Torah law because one performed no action. One is instead given lashes for rebellious conduct. Rambam, chapter 1, halachah 3. Regarding the rectification for one who transgressed, see above, siman 334.
2) There. One transgresses regarding a mixture containing chametz, etc. The Shulchan Aruch uses the language of Rambam in chapter 4, who rules according to Rabbi Eliezer that a chametz mixture is subject to a prohibition, while hardened, inedible chametz is not subject to any prohibition. It therefore does not also write that one transgresses regarding hardened chametz, despite the unattributed Mishnah in the chapter Eilu Ovrin. This is also explained below in siman 447, se'if 12. See there. Nevertheless, initially it is forbidden to retain it during Pesach. Chok Yaakov, subsection 2. If one retained it, it is not prohibited after Pesach, as stated below in siman 447, se'if 12. See there.
3) Maggid Mishneh writes there that Rambam rules in chapter 1 that eating a chametz mixture does not violate a prohibition unless one consumes an olive-sized quantity of chametz within the time needed to eat a pras. A pras is three eggs according to Rambam in chapter 1, which is also the opinion of the Shulchan Aruch; some authorities say four eggs, as stated above in siman 368, se'if 3, and siman 612, se'if 4. See there. Certainly, according to Rambam, one likewise transgresses here only when the mixture contains an olive-sized quantity within the time needed to eat a pras, and this is the primary ruling. Some Geonim maintain that one transgresses even with less than this. I have also seen one who explains Rambam to mean that although one does not violate the eating prohibition, one must eliminate the mixture, because it contains an olive-sized quantity of chametz; although mixed into many olive-sized quantities of other food, one transgresses by retaining it. See there. Maran likewise writes in Kesef Mishneh there that for this matter there is no distinction between whether or not it contains an olive-sized quantity within the time needed to eat a pras. See there. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1, writes that this also appears from Ran and is the primary ruling. This also appears to be the opinion of Magen Avraham, subsection 1, and Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1, writes that the later authorities agree that even without an olive-sized quantity within the time needed to eat a pras, one violates the prohibition against chametz being seen. Mekor Chaim, subsection 1; Vilna Gaon; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1; Chayei Adam, rule 121, subsection 1, write likewise. This applies specifically to a mixture of different kinds, which is not nullified by a majority under Torah law according to those who maintain that the flavor of a prohibited substance is prohibited like its substance under Torah law. In a mixture of the same kind, however, such as flour from grain that became chametz mixed with flour from grain that did not become chametz, the prohibited flour is nullified by a majority under Torah law and may be eaten. It may certainly be retained under Torah law. Rabbinically, however, retaining it is forbidden lest one eat it during Pesach, and it must be eliminated. Nevertheless, if one transgressed and retained it, it is permitted after Pesach, even for consumption according to Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 13, because one did not violate the prohibitions against chametz being seen and found, as explained below in simanim 447 and 467 by the later authorities. The same law applies to a mixture of different kinds when the permitted substance is sixty times the chametz. Chok Yaakov, there; Chok Yosef, subsection 1. See below, siman 447, subsection 244. Wheat flour mixed with barley flour, and the like, is considered a mixture of different kinds. Eshel Avraham, subsection 1.
4) If there is no tangible chametz at all, but only its flavor, such as a dish cooked together with chametz in one utensil that absorbed the flavor of chametz but contains none of its substance, Olas Shabbos writes there that it is forbidden to retain it during Pesach and that one violates the prohibition against chametz being seen. Chok Yosef, there, holds likewise. Chok Yaakov, there, however, writes that the authorities imply otherwise. Even according to the position that the flavor of a prohibited substance is prohibited like its substance under Torah law, that applies only to eating, derived from the law of soaking, but not to the prohibition against chametz being seen. He concludes that it is nevertheless rabbinically forbidden to retain it, and if it is found during Pesach it must be eliminated. If one transgressed and retained it, however, it should not be prohibited; at least benefit from it should be permitted, while prohibiting its consumption is merely an added stringency. He writes that the same law applies to all substances that may be retained under Torah law but that the Sages prohibited one from retaining initially: if one finds them during Pesach, one should burn them rabbinically, but if one transgressed and retained them, they remain permitted. Mekor Chaim, subsection 1, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 7, write likewise.
5) Olas Shabbos further writes there that one should be stringent not to retain during Pesach even food cooked in a utensil used for chametz that same day. See there. Chok Yaakov, there, disputes this as well and writes that there is no reason to be stringent and that it may be retained. See there. Mekor Chaim, there; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 8; Chayei Adam, rule 121, subsection 1, write likewise. Responsa Chasam Sofer, siman 108, nevertheless concludes that a sharp food may not be retained, while other foods may be retained. See there. Mekor Chaim writes there that this applies only if the food will not remain soaking in a chametz utensil during Pesach, meaning that it was poured into a utensil used for Pesach. It appears that 'a utensil used for Pesach' is not literal, but means a utensil suitable for use on Pesach because it was used with chametz only when cold, as stated below in siman 421, se'if 22. See there. Responsa Har HaCarmel, siman 14, writes that preserves or fat kept in chametz pots may be retained. See there. Sha'arei Teshuvah, subsection 1, cites this ruling.
5) If the food was cooked or soaked during Pesach, even in a utensil that had not been used for chametz that day, it must be eliminated. Chayei Adam, there. This follows the authorities who prohibit during Pesach even a detrimental absorbed flavor, as stated below in the gloss to siman 447, se'if 10. According to the Shulchan Aruch there, however, it is permitted even for consumption, as will be explained there, with the help of Heaven. Regarding the practical ruling, see our words there in subsection 231.
6) The Tur writes that anything whose consumption is not prohibited under Torah law may initially be mixed and retained. Sefer Mitzvos Katan writes likewise. Tosafos at the beginning of Pesachim, however, write that it is forbidden, because the Sages made no distinction. Magen Avraham, subsection 1, writes that this is the opinion of the Shulchan Aruch. See there. See below, siman 447, subsection 241.
7) In a mixture of the same kind, even when one liquid became mixed with another liquid in a ratio of less than sixty, it may be retained. Chametz that functions as a setting agent, however, is prohibited after Pesach even after the fact. Magen Avraham here and in siman 447, subsection 11; Mekor Chaim, subsection 1.
8) There. One transgresses regarding it, etc. A Jew who owns a mill and grinds fine flour throughout the year, etc. Must eliminate everything. If not, the owner violates the prohibitions against chametz being seen and found. Responsa Radbaz printed in Furth, siman 589; Machazik Berachah, subsection 2.
9) There. Such as fish brine and Babylonian kutach, etc. Babylonian kutach is prepared by crumbling bread crumbs into it to ferment it. Median beer is prepared by adding barley and water and allowing it to ferment. Tur. Fish brine likewise contains an admixture of chametz. Levush.
10) There. Such as fish brine and Babylonian kutach, etc. All these contain an admixture of water, for otherwise the established law is that fruit juice does not cause leavening. Rif and Rosh write likewise, as cited by Beis Yosef. See below, at the beginning of siman 462.
11) There. And all similar substances, etc. This includes Edomite vinegar, Egyptian zythum, and all the other substances listed in the Mishnah at the beginning of the chapter Eilu Ovrin. Chok Yaakov, subsection 4.
