A mixture of chametz. The Tur wrote that Rabbi Eliezer makes a chametz mixture subject to a negative commandment but not kareis, and according to him it is forbidden to keep on Pesach; according to the Sages it has no negative commandment, only a lesser prohibition, and according to them it may be kept. The Beis Yosef understood that even according to the Sages there is a Torah prohibition in eating, and therefore wondered why the Tur permits keeping it. But the Tur's words later show that he means only a rabbinic prohibition: the Rif rules like the Sages that there is no negative commandment in mixtures or in chametz nukshah, and therefore it may be mixed and kept. Thus the Beis Yosef's difficulty is not correct. The Taz explains that the main dispute is whether keeping a mixture follows eating. If eating is not forbidden by Torah law, the Tur holds that keeping is permitted, whereas Tosafos require destruction rabbinically because the Sages did not distinguish in chametz and because one may come to eat it. The Mishnah's statement that 'these transgress' can be understood either as removal from the table or as actual bal yera'eh, depending on the view. The Rambam's formulation indicates that for bal yera'eh he rules stringently in mixtures, while for eating the liability depends on the usual eating measures. The distinction also depends on whether the mixture has actual chametz taste. If the chametz was inserted only to sour or prepare the mixture and there is no taste sufficient to create liability, it is less severe; but if the taste of chametz is present, taste is like substance in unlike mixtures and the mixture is forbidden. A thing intentionally made in that manner is not treated like an accidental mixture that becomes nullified, as the Rashba writes regarding drinks made from vinegar and honey and as is seen from Babylonian kutach. Even if according to the Torah the mixture might be permitted without a kezayis within the time of eating a peras, rabbinically it may still be forbidden and may require destruction. The Taz further analyzes chametz nukshah: it is not full chametz, such as scribes' paste or dough that has begun but not completed leavening. If it was once full edible chametz, it would remain forbidden until unfit for a dog; therefore the cases of chametz nukshah are either never fully edible or only somewhat fit. Nonetheless the Sages were concerned one may come to eat it. He also distinguishes keeping before Pesach, mixing during the permitted time, and the penalty after Pesach: where one did not transgress bal yera'eh, the item is not forbidden after Pesach as a penalty, though eating may remain forbidden. The conclusion is that the Mechaber follows the stricter approach for mixtures of chametz, while the Tur's leniency applies only according to the view that the mixture is not Torah-prohibited and only before Pesach or for keeping where no bal yera'eh applies.
