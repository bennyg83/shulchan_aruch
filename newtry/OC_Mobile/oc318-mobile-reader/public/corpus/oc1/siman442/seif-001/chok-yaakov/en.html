A mixture containing chametz. The agreement of the later authorities is in accordance with those poskim that even if there is not an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf, one violates the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found. This applies specifically to a mixture of one type with another, which according to those who hold that taste is primary according to the Torah is not nullified according to the Torah by a majority. But with the same type mixed into the same type, since according to the Torah it is nullified by a majority and one may eat it, certainly according to the Torah one may keep it. D'Rabbanan, however, it is forbidden to keep it, lest he come to eat from it on Pesach, and he must destroy it. Nevertheless, if he transgressed and kept it, it is permitted after Pesach, since he did not violate the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found, as is explained below in simanim 447 and 466. The same law applies where there is sixty even in a mixture of one type with another. Where there is no actual substance of chametz, but only chametz taste, the Olas Shabbos also wrote that one violates the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found. In my view, this law requires great doubt, for the implication of the poskim is not so. So too wrote the Minchas Kohen in Sefer HaTaaruvos. It appears that even according to the view that taste is primary according to the Torah, even without substance, that is only regarding eating, which is derived from the law of nazirite soaking. But regarding violation of the prohibition of chametz being seen, since there is no form of chametz here at all, this is not comparable, even though Beis Hillel derives destruction from eating at the beginning of Beitzah. They are not equated for every matter. See the responsum of the Rashba, siman 30, where the questioner wanted to permit such a case by sixty even for eating. This is also clear from Rav Natronai, cited by the Tur in this siman, and so it appears from the responsa of Maharit. When the Tur writes that if it has the taste of chametz one is liable for it, because taste is primary according to the Torah, that liability refers to eating, not to keeping it. Alternatively, it refers to all those items in which the body of the chametz is mixed but there is not an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf, unlike what the Bach wrote. The Bach and Rosh Yosef wrote similarly, and the words of the Beit Yosef are difficult. Greater than this, the Olas Shabbos wrote that even if it was cooked in a vessel used that day, one should be stringent not to keep it. I do not know the source of this stringency. On the contrary, the Agudah at the beginning of Pesachim explicitly wrote that because of absorption from vessels it does not require destruction. This is especially so since many poskim hold that one does not violate the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found with any mixture of chametz, and even according to Rashi and those with him, taste is primary is not according to the Torah. Some also say that before Pesach, when it is still permitted, vessel absorption is considered a secondary taste of a secondary taste. All the more so according to what is explained below in siman 447, according to Rashi and Tosafot in the chapter Kol Sha'ah, that absorption from vessels is considered only a minute prohibition that was nullified before Pesach. Therefore even if we say that taste without substance entails violation of the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found, with vessel absorption one should not be stringent at all, and it is permitted to keep it. Where its taste remains but not its substance, it nevertheless appears to me that it is forbidden to keep it D'Rabbanan and it must be destroyed if found during Pesach. But if one transgressed and kept it, one should not forbid it, and at least it should be permitted for benefit. The prohibition of eating in this case is only a mere stringency. The same law applies to all cases that according to the Torah are permitted to keep but D'Rabbanan are forbidden to keep initially: he should burn them during Pesach D'Rabbanan, but if he transgressed and kept them, they are permitted, as is explained below in siman 447. So wrote the Magen Avraham. For this reason, the famous gaon, my wife's grandfather, ruled, as cited by my brother-in-law in Eliyah Zuta, siman 447, seif katan 21, regarding butter in which bread and flour had soaked and which one transgressed and kept during Pesach, that he should cast the benefit value of the bread or flour into the sea, and he permitted the butter for benefit. It appears to me that this speaks where the flour was fine flour, or where water was mixed with the butter, for otherwise fruit juice alone does not become chametz. Our beer, over which one violates the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found, as the Rosh wrote, is because its essence is barley, so it has both taste and substance, and it is also made for its taste, as the Rosh wrote in klal 2 regarding the prohibition of new grain. And anything similar to these items. This refers to Edomite vinegar, Egyptian olives, and all those listed in the Mishnah at the beginning of the chapter Eilu Ovrin. Brandy made from soured grain, called malt in the vernacular, was written in responsa Masas Binyamin, siman 58, to be actual chametz and worse than a mixture containing chametz. See there, where the matter is reasoned. So too wrote Rav Menachem of Tiktin in a responsum printed at the beginning of the Rif, Krakow edition, Seder Nashim. There is no distinction between brandy made from soured grain and brandy made from beer dregs, unlike what one scholar thought, whose question to me I will quote. He asked about Reuven, who had brandy made from the dregs of barley beer, forgot to sell it before Pesach, and remembered during Chol HaMoed. The brandy was in the house of a non-Jew, in a room that belonged to the Jew, and the loss was great. At first glance, he wrote, it appears simple that it is chametz that passed over Pesach and must be destroyed. The Mishnah lists Median beer among the items over which one violates on Pesach, and Rashi and Riva explain that this refers to the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found. The Rosh cited Rabbeinu Tam that Median beer is specifically where barley is placed in it, whereas our beer in Ashkenaz, whose essence is barley, is actual chametz and has an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf. So wrote the Tur and all the later authorities. The same should apply to brandy made from grain. Nevertheless, the questioner sought grounds for leniency, distinguishing between brandy made from grain and brandy made from dregs. Since dregs are merely acidity and not true wine, as is stated in Bava Basra regarding wine dregs, perhaps beer dregs are likewise mere acidity. He also argued that this is not a case of a fermenting agent, since the dregs did not cause another item to stand or ferment, but only produced an exudation. He added that the owner was inadvertent, had nullified his chametz, the item was not in a Jewish house, and there was great loss. He therefore inclined to permit benefit from it, provided another decisor agreed. My response is as follows. Although I do not presume to decide easily in the severe matter of chametz, I will not leave the question unanswered. First, the assumption that there is no prohibition of chametz being seen and found with a mixture containing chametz is not simple, for the Shulchan Aruch at the beginning of this siman rules explicitly that one violates a negative commandment over a mixture containing chametz even when there is not an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf, and this is the consensus of the later authorities. The Taz's difficulty on the Beit Yosef in siman 442 is not a difficulty, for the Mishnah itself states that these are in the warning of a prohibition, proving that it is not merely removal from the world. Furthermore, even if one were to require an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf for a mixture, brandy is not called a mixture containing chametz but actual chametz, for vapor that emerges from a liquid has the status of that liquid, as is proved from several Mishnayos in Machshirin and from the responsa of the Rosh and the Rivash. The Masas Binyamin and Rav Menachem of Tiktin wrote plainly that brandy made from chametz is actual chametz, not merely vapor, and is treated like the prohibited substance itself or like an agent that causes standing. As for distinguishing beer dregs from wine dregs, this comparison has no basis. Sense and reason testify that wine dregs whose wine has been removed may be only acidity, but beer dregs still contain the strength and substance of the grain and make a stronger drink when boiled and distilled. The proofs from wine dregs therefore do not apply to beer dregs. Nor does the fact that there is no intent to use them as a fermenting agent help, for the product itself comes from the chametz dregs and is made for taste and strength. The additional arguments also do not suffice. Forgetfulness after Pesach does not remove the fine of chametz that passed over Pesach where the chametz was owned by a Jew. Nullification does not help once the Sages required removal and sale, especially for a significant item that a person intends to preserve, and keeping it in a non-Jew's house does not help where it remains the Jew's property. Therefore the ruling is that brandy made from beer dregs is forbidden after Pesach as chametz that passed over Pesach, and certainly one should not rely on the proposed leniencies. The printed responsum of Rav Menachem of Tiktin that forbids both kinds is correct. Only where the case is of something permitted according to the Torah and forbidden only because of a D'Rabbanan concern may one discuss benefit after Pesach, as explained in the relevant simanim. But in this case, where the essence of the product is from chametz grain or its dregs and is made for taste and strength, it is treated as actual chametz. It follows that the gloss later in seif 4, beginning with the words 'and below,' also refers to all of these cases taught here.
