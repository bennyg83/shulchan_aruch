(1) A mixture containing chametz, etc. The Mechaber is discussing only bal yera’eh and bal yimatzei. Regarding these prohibitions, he holds that one transgresses even when the mixture does not contain a kezayis of chametz within the time needed to eat a pras. Concerning eating, all agree that one is liable to kares only when that concentration is present, and some hold that even a regular negative prohibition does not apply without it. Regarding bal yera’eh and bal yimatzei, however, all portions of the mixture are held in one vessel and therefore combine when the total contains a kezayis of chametz. This applies specifically to unlike substances, where the prohibited substance is not nullified according to the Torah by a majority according to those who hold that taste is treated as substance according to the Torah. With like substances, such as flour from leavened grain mixed into unleavened flour, the chametz is nullified according to the Torah by the majority and may be eaten according to Torah law, and certainly may be retained. Rabbinically, however, it may not be retained, lest one eat it on Pesach. It must be destroyed before Pesach, and if remembered on Pesach it must then be destroyed. If it was retained until after Pesach, it is permitted, since bal yera’eh and bal yimatzei were not violated, as explained at the end of siman 447. The same applies to unlike substances when there is sixty times the chametz, for although chametz on Pesach is not nullified even in a minute amount, that rule is rabbinic. Other items whose retention is prohibited only rabbinically must likewise be destroyed before Pesach and, if remembered on Pesach, destroyed then. If they remained until after Pesach, they are permitted for eating. If no actual chametz remains and only its taste remains, such as food cooked with chametz from which the chametz was removed, and there was not sixty times against it, eating is forbidden according to the Torah because taste is treated as substance. Chok Yaakov nevertheless inclines to say that bal yera’eh is not violated because no actual prohibited substance remains. It is certainly forbidden rabbinically to retain it and it must be destroyed if found during Pesach. If retained until after Pesach, it is not prohibited in benefit, since the Sages did not penalize retention that involved no Torah prohibition. Regarding eating, it may be proper to be stringent. Others disagree and hold that since eating is prohibited according to the Torah, destruction is also required according to the Torah, and if it remained through Pesach it is prohibited in benefit. In a case of substantial loss one may rely on the first opinion. All this concerns food that absorbed the taste of chametz itself. Food cooked before Pesach in a pot in which chametz had been cooked that day may be retained until after Pesach even when the food does not contain sixty times the absorbed chametz. It should be hidden in a room that one does not ordinarily enter, as explained in siman 451, and it must be transferred to a Pesach vessel so that it will not remain soaking in a chametz vessel during Pesach. If it was cooked or soaked during Pesach in a chametz vessel, even one that had not been used that day, it must be destroyed. Chayei Adam.
(2) One transgresses regarding it, etc. One does not transgress bal yera’eh and bal yimatzei regarding chametz nuksheh, but it must be destroyed rabbinically. Chametz nuksheh means material that is not full chametz, such as paste made from flour and water that scribes use to attach papers, dough that has not yet developed cracks but whose surface has turned pale, or material that was never more than barely edible. If it was once edible and later spoiled, it must become unfit even for a dog, as explained in seif 2. If it remained through Pesach, see the end of siman 447.
(3) Muryas. This is a food made from fish fat, lightly roasted flour, and water. Kutach is made from salt and whey, with bread crumbs mixed into it. Median beer is made by adding barley and water so that it ferments.
(4) And anything similar, etc. Examples include Edomite vinegar and the other items listed in the Mishnah, Pesachim 43a, that contain mixtures of chametz. The later authorities agree that distilled liquor made from any of the five grains is full chametz and is more severe than an ordinary chametz mixture. See Chok Yaakov and Mekor Chayim. The same applies when it is made from beer sediment. See Peri Megadim.
(5) It is not fit for eating. We do not decree lest one eat it, since it is completely unfit for eating. Chametz nuksheh, however, remains somewhat edible and may not be retained.
(6) It may be retained. The hides cause it to become foul immediately.
(7) He must destroy it. It is still fit for eating when Pesach begins. Once the obligation of destruction took effect, if he failed to destroy it and it remained into Pesach, he must still destroy it even if it has since spoiled, as explained in the next seif regarding moldy bread.
(8) They may be retained on Pesach. Their permissibility for eating is explained below in seif 4.
