A mixture of chametz. The Maggid Mishneh wrote in chapter 4 that this is specifically where there is a kezayis of chametz within the time of eating a peras, and this is primary; see siman 612. Some say that although one does not transgress by eating it, he is obligated to destroy it, for even though it is mixed among several olives, he transgresses since there is a kezayis of chametz there; the Kesef Mishneh agreed with this view. It appears to me that if it was mixed with its own type, such as flour with flour, then if it was nullified by the majority he does not transgress it, for according to the Torah it is permitted to eat it initially, as written in Yoreh De'ah siman 98, and certainly it is permitted according to the Torah to keep it. But with a mixture of unlike types, even though where there is not a kezayis within the time of eating a peras he is not lashed for eating it, that is because the kezayis does not combine when he does not eat it at once; nevertheless he is obligated to destroy it, because it combines by itself. See the Tur according to Rashi's view. The Tur wrote that anything not forbidden to eat according to the Torah may be mixed initially and kept; the words of the Beis Yosef here require examination. So too wrote the Semak. But Tosafos at the beginning of Pesachim wrote that it is forbidden, for the Sages did not differentiate, and they proved this from the Mishnah that se'or must be burned, even though it is chametz nukshah and there is no Torah prohibition in eating it, and all the more so in keeping it, yet it must be burned rabbinically. The Ran wrote likewise. According to the Tur, one must explain that the case of se'or is on Yom Tov, when he cannot bake it, for it is rabbinically forbidden to eat and, if left as it is, it will become complete chametz; cold does not help once it has begun to become chametz, and therefore it must be burned. The Shulchan Aruch's view is like Tosafos, for at the end of siman 447 he ruled that with chametz nukshah one has not transgressed bal yera'eh according to the Torah; if so, he holds there is likewise no negative commandment in eating it, since he ruled like the Rambam and Rashi that the phrase 'these transgress' refers to bal yera'eh, and like Rabbi Eliezer, for the beraisa supports him. Yet he ruled that chametz nukshah may not be kept, except that if one kept it, it is not forbidden for benefit, since he did not transgress bal yera'eh. With a chametz mixture, however, the Shulchan Aruch ruled like the plain Mishnah that 'these transgress,' and like the Rambam. Chametz nukshah means that it is not complete chametz, such as the paste with which scribes glue their papers, made from flour and water, or dough whose surface has paled but has not become complete chametz. Rashi and the Ran explain it as bad chametz not fit for eating; one must say that it was never fit for eating, for if it had been complete chametz fit for eating it would remain forbidden until it became unfit for a dog, as written in se'if 2. Still, it must be somewhat fit to eat, for the Ran wrote that the decree is lest one come to eat from it. The Tur wrote that this is specifically where the mixture has no taste of chametz, as with all these cases where the chametz is put only for sourness; therefore one needs a kezayis within the time of eating a peras. But if it has the taste of chametz, one is liable for it, since taste is like substance according to the Torah. This speaks of unlike types, as explained above; see the end of siman 447. In Yoreh De'ah siman 134, the Rashba is cited that beverages made from wine vinegar and honey are forbidden. Do not say that the vinegar is mixed with the honey and nullified in its minority, because anything whose very purpose is to be made this way is not nullified, like the four mixtures of Medina, such as Babylonian kutach. Only where something became mixed incidentally did they not forbid until it gives taste; but where one's intent is to do this, they forbade it and it is not nullified in its minority, like preserves into which wine is normally placed. And if you ask, the Gemara establishes Babylonian kutach as having a kezayis within the time of eating a peras, implying that otherwise it would be permitted; one must say that according to the Torah it is permitted, but rabbinically it is forbidden. Riaz wrote: if it was mixed before Pesach it is nullified in sixty, specifically where he did not mix it deliberately; but if he mixed it deliberately, because he needed to prepare it with chametz, such as the almudi mentioned above, it is forbidden even after Pesach and must be destroyed before Pesach. Otherwise it is permitted. It implies there that even if he did not mix it intentionally for the purpose of keeping it, it is forbidden; so too wrote the Rosh and the Tur. See what I wrote in se'if 8 and at the end of siman 447.
