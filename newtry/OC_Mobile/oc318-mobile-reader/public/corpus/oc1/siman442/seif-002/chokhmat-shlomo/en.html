And likewise papers, etc. The Rambam ruled this way, and one may ask: from the paste of scribes the Mishnah forbids. The Ravyah, brought by the Tur, answers that the paste of scribes means the paper itself was processed with chametz in a recognizable form, but where we paste parchment onto a board and the chametz is not recognizable, it is permitted. The Maggid Mishneh explained differently: the paste of scribes refers to before the papers were pasted, when the chametz remained a hard substance in itself and was forbidden to keep; the Rambam speaks after the papers were pasted, similar to a vat in which hides were placed, where he says the form of chametz no longer remains. It is clear that after pasting it is permitted to keep. The Beis Yosef therefore wrote that we do not need the Ravyah's words. In my humble opinion, the Ravyah's distinction is surprising: if there is a chametz prohibition, what does covering it with paper help, since bal yera'eh applies even to chametz hidden in a pit where one does not see it? Terumas HaDeshen, siman 117, therefore wrote that one who wishes to paste windows within thirty days before Pesach should paste with very soft dough and insert all the dough between the joints so that none protrudes outward; this is the Rambam's case, where the form of chametz does not remain. One who is stringent should plaster it with clay. This is the stringency brought here by the Rema, following the Ravyah, but it is difficult for the reason I wrote. Also, even with garments laundered many times in starch-water, some substance may appear somewhat on the outside, yet it certainly does not forbid, because no chametz remains there at all, as the Maggid Mishneh explained. Therefore one who wishes to be stringent should remove even what the paper covers, for there is no distinction. What helps in se'if 6, plastering with clay, is only a stringency, because even without plastering it is permitted there since one cannot dig after it and it is like chametz covered by a collapse. But here, where one is trying to repair the prohibition through plastering, that is not reasonable unless the plaster itself nullifies the form and taste of the chametz. Regarding paper pasted to windows, one should follow Terumas HaDeshen's first instruction: cover it with extremely thin dough so that after drying there is no substance at all; there is no distinction between what is visible outside and what is inside.
