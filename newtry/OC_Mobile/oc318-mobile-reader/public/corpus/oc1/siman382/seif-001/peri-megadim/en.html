In two houses. See Taz and Beit Yosef. This requires study, for the wording implies that even when each person eats his own food and a partition separates them, as in siman 370, seif 4 in the gloss, so long as there are not two distinct houses, a non-Jew does not prohibit the eruv, even though one could conceive of a case in which the Jewish residents prohibit one another. This also appears from Beit Yosef's inference from the words “two houses,” for otherwise the statement that they prohibit one another would itself be sufficient. I do not know whether “two houses” means three rooms or literally two separate houses. See Rashi, Eruvin 61a. The case of two men married to two women who prohibit one another is no proof, for one may say that everything depends upon whether they prohibit one another. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 2. The word “but” implies that the requirements form one combined rule, while the primary factor is whether they prohibit one another.
