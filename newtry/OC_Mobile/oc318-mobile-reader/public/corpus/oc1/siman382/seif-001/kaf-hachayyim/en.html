1) [Se'if 1] One who resides with a non-Jew in a courtyard is not prohibited, etc. A non-Jew's residence does not intrinsically prohibit carrying. The Sages decreed because the Jewish resident might learn from the non-Jew's conduct. They did not impose the decree when only one Jewish person lives there, since it is uncommon for one to live with a non-Jew because of concern that the non-Jew might kill him, as they were suspected of bloodshed. When more than one Jewish person lives there, however, they are not afraid and may reside with the non-Jew. The Sages therefore decreed that they must rent the non-Jew's rights. The non-Jew will be unwilling to rent them because he is concerned about sorcery, and the Jewish resident will consequently leave. Gemara. According to this reasoning, it appears that it is certainly forbidden to live with a Jewish person who publicly commits transgressions, because of the concern that one might learn from that person's conduct. The laws of a Kusi and an apostate are explained below in siman 385. See there.
2) There. Does not prohibit carrying for him, etc. If many houses belonging to non-Jews open into a courtyard, a Jewish person may carry into and out of that courtyard, provided that no other Jewish person resides there. Tosafos on Eruvin 62a.
3) There. A Jewish person may remove his hat in the courtyard of nobles and enter the reception hall. Whenever no Jewish people establish their Shabbos residence there, carrying is permitted. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1. If Jewish people reside there, it is forbidden to carry into the reception hall, but it is permitted to carry within the courtyard. Eshel Avraham, subsection 5.
4) There. Does not prohibit carrying for him, etc. If two Jewish people live in a non-Jew's house and the non-Jew also lives there, but one Jewish resident cannot reach the other because a gate between them is locked at night, there is uncertainty whether they must establish an eruv chatzeiros, since they cannot reach one another at night. The law is that when one Jewish person lives with a non-Jew in a courtyard, the non-Jew does not prohibit carrying for him. They should therefore establish an eruv chatzeiros because of the doubt, without reciting a blessing. Responsa Shoel UMeshiv, third edition, volume 3, siman 102. See there. Orchos Chaim, subsection 2.
5) There. They reside in two houses. If they reside in one house, however, even if there are many people and they do not rely upon one table, but each person has independent possession, they are nevertheless considered one because they reside in a single house. Beis Yosef; Taz, subsection 1; Olas Shabbos, subsection 1; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1; Tosefes Shabbos, subsection 2; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1. This implies that if they reside in two rooms within one house, then since they prohibit carrying for one another, the non-Jew likewise prohibits carrying for them. See above, siman 370, se'if 4, in the gloss.
6) There. And they prohibit carrying for one another, etc. This excludes brothers who receive their sustenance from their father and sleep in their own houses, and those other cases stated in siman 370. Although they reside in separate houses, they are considered one because they do not prohibit carrying for one another. Rambam, chapter 5, halachah 16, likewise writes this. Beis Yosef; Magen Avraham, subsection 2. The later authorities write likewise.
7) There. Renunciation by the non-Jew is ineffective, etc. This is for the reason stated above in subsection 1. The enactment was intended to prevent the non-Jew from renting his rights because he is concerned about sorcery, so that the Jewish resident will leave. Beis Yosef; Taz, subsection 1.
8) There. Rather, they must rent from him. This must specifically be done as a rental. If the non-Jew gives his rights as a gift, it is ineffective. Chayei Adam, rule 75, subsection 1. If they rent his rights, the rights to his house are included. This is unlike renunciation of rights in siman 380, where the rights to the house are not included. One who renounces rights does so ungenerously, whereas one who rents rights transfers them generously. Shemen HaMaor.
9) There. Rather, they must rent from him. Renting a non-Jew's rights is effective only in a city entirely surrounded by a wall whose gates are fit to be closed at night. If the city contains breaches, the alleyway must first be rectified, after which the rights may be rented from the non-Jew. Nechalas Tzvi, end of siman 399; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1. Responsa Sefer Yehoshua, end of siman 8, however, writes that this was not the accepted practice. Nowadays, when the rights are purchased, it may be said that, on the contrary, subsequently installing a tzuras hapesach constitutes taking possession. See there. Responsa Rivash, siman 427, however, appears to agree with Nechalas Tzvi even where the rights are rented. See there. Orchos Chaim, subsection 4, cites both opinions.
10) There. Gloss: A Jewish person who rented, etc. This applies even if the Jewish owner retains no right of use there. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1. If the Jewish owner retains a right of use in the non-Jew's house, some authorities maintain that rental is unnecessary in any event, as stated below in se'if 12. See there. This applies specifically when the Jewish person rented or lent the property to the non-Jew. If the property was sold to the non-Jew, the non-Jew certainly prohibits carrying. Responsa Chaim V'Chesed, siman 92.
11) There, in the gloss. Does not prohibit carrying for him. This means that even if other Jewish people reside there, the non-Jew does not prohibit carrying. If only the Jewish owner resides with the non-Jew in the courtyard, the non-Jew does not prohibit carrying in any event, as stated in the Shulchan Aruch. If the intent were merely that the non-Jew does not prohibit carrying for the owner but does prohibit it for the others, then once the others were forbidden to carry, the householder would also be forbidden because they prohibit carrying for him. What purpose would the statement that the non-Jew does not prohibit carrying for him serve? It must therefore mean that the non-Jew does not prohibit carrying either for him or for the other residents. Levush writes likewise. Birkei Yosef, subsection 2; Ma'amar Mordechai, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2.
12) There, in the gloss. Does not prohibit carrying for him. It appears to me that even if the Jewish lessor does not live in this courtyard but rented the courtyard's houses to other Jewish people and to a non-Jew, the non-Jew nevertheless does not prohibit carrying. Here too we say that the Jewish owner did not rent a house to the non-Jew with the intent that the non-Jew prohibit carrying for the Jewish residents. Chayei Adam, rule 75, subsection 9. Birkei Yosef, subsection 1, however, writes that Rashba's words imply somewhat that this applies specifically in a courtyard where the Jewish lessor resides. If he does not reside in the courtyard, perhaps he does not care whether the non-Jew prohibits carrying, and the matter can be debated. Chemed Moshe in siman 384 likewise writes that if the Jewish owner of the dwelling does not reside there, the non-Jew prohibits carrying. See there. The later authorities write that one should rule stringently, since even without this issue some authorities question the underlying ruling, as stated by Even HaOzer.
13) There, in the gloss. For he did not rent or lend, etc. Although we do not apply this reasoning when the lender or lessor rents or lends to a Jewish person, as stated in siman 370, se'if 2, the Sages ruled leniently regarding a non-Jew. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, there.
14) There, in the gloss. For he did not rent or lend, etc. This applies when the Jewish owner can remove the non-Jew whenever he wishes. If he cannot remove him, the non-Jew prohibits carrying. Gra. The Gra writes this to reconcile the Shulchan Aruch's words at the beginning of siman 384. Rashba, however, writes another reason in Avodas HaKodesh: it is uncommon for a Jewish person to rent or lend his house to a non-Jew, and the Sages did not impose their decree in an uncommon case. See there. According to this reason, it appears that even if the Jewish owner cannot remove the non-Jew, the non-Jew does not prohibit carrying. See further below, at the beginning of siman 384.
15) There, in the gloss. And the non-Jew resides in the house with him. This means in a separate room, in a manner that causes the non-Jew to prohibit carrying for him. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2. If the Jewish resident has the right to use the entire house, including the non-Jew's room, the situation is no less favorable than that of the non-Jew's hired worker or attendant, who does not prohibit carrying, as stated in se'if 16. Ma'amar Mordechai, subsection 2, writes likewise.
16) There, in the gloss. Renting the house is ineffective, etc. One should not think it sufficient for this Jewish person merely to contribute a share to the eruv, so that the situation resembles five people residing in a courtyard, where one person may rent the non-Jew's rights on behalf of everyone, as stated in se'if 9. The gloss teaches that this is not so. A separate rental is required for purposes of the eruv because the non-Jew resides there. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1. Ma'amar Mordechai, there, writes likewise.
17) There, in the gloss. A Jewish person who lent, etc. This refers to a Jewish person who owns two houses in a courtyard in which a non-Jew resides. The owner lives in one house and lent or rented the second to another Jewish person, while retaining a right of use there, namely, utensils that may not be moved on Shabbos. Although the two Jewish residents need not establish an eruv with one another, as stated in siman 370, they must nevertheless rent the non-Jew's rights. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 3.
18) There, in the gloss. It is ineffective. It is ineffective to render them a single resident in the place of a non-Jew, for it is no better than if they had established an eruv. Magen Avraham, subsection 3; Tosefes Shabbos, subsection 4; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, there. This somewhat resolves the objection raised against this ruling from the case of a father and his children, who need not rent from the non-Jew. That case is different because the place of eating is primary. Retaining a right of use, however, is not as significant and therefore is no better than establishing an eruv together, in which case they must rent from the non-Jew. See the explanations of the Gra and Ma'amar Mordechai, subsection 3, and examine the matter carefully.
