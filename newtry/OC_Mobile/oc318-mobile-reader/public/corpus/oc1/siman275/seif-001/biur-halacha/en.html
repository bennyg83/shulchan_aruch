(1) He cannot read by its light. There is room to examine how much concentration is considered close examination. Pri Megadim implies from Magen Avraham's citation of the Ran that even slight concentration is forbidden. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, however, implies that only great concentration is forbidden. The Ramban and Ritva appear to agree with the Ran, based on the Yerushalmi. The practical ruling therefore requires consideration.
(2) By the light of a lamp. Regarding the lamps common today, some have questioned the custom of reading by their light. One might suggest that since their fuel is repulsive, they resemble the Raavan's allowance regarding tallow because a person naturally recoils from touching it; gas is even more foul-smelling and provides a strong light. This is not persuasive, however, for in se'if 12 the Shulchan Aruch follows the Rambam, who forbids close examination even by naphtha light. The Ramban explains that the leniency concerning a repulsive substance applies only to inspecting whether a utensil is clean, and the Raavan is a lone view. One reliable expedient is to attach near the adjustment mechanism a conspicuous note stating, 'Today is Shabbos; it is forbidden to kindle.' This resembles Maharshal's practice of locking the lamp, Machatzis HaShekel's unusual knot, and other visible reminders, such as reciting Bameh Madlikin; a reminder is effective just as another person may remind him. Masgeres HaShulchan ultimately permits reading by modern lamps for another reason: an ordinary wick sometimes dims and must be tilted or drawn up, whereas these lamps ordinarily burn steadily until the fuel runs low; at that point adjustment is useless or worsens the flame by producing soot. If one set sufficient light before Shabbos, there is therefore little concern. It is advisable to raise the wick well before Shabbos so it will burn properly. Although this rationale is not entirely certain, it may be relied upon for Torah study, especially in a public study hall, where one may also combine the Or Zarua's view that many people studying the same matter are like two people who remind one another. At home, when another light is readily available, it is proper to use the written-reminder method.
(3) Even if it is ten stories high. Taz explains that where the Sages prohibited an object they made no distinctions, whereas a person physically unable to tilt the lamp was never included in the decree. Thus, one whose two hands are amputated or securely bound may read by its light.
(4) And likewise by a wax candle. See Magen Avraham in the name of Maharshal. If an oil lamp and a wax or tallow candle stand beside one another, reading may not be permitted even according to Maharshal, nor according to Magen Avraham, who is lenient above in siman 264 when a valid lamp stands beside an invalid one; see Tosefes Shabbos and Pri Megadim for the reason. With an oil lamp and a stearin candle, however, it appears permissible to read before the oil lamp as long as the stearin candle is burning.
