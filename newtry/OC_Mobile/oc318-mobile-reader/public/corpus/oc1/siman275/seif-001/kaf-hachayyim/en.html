1) [Seif 1] One may not inspect garments, etc. The Gemara explains that the reason is lest one tilt the lamp, meaning that he might tilt the oil toward the top of the wick so that it burns well, thereby kindling on Shabbos. Beis Yosef.
2) There: One may not inspect garments, etc. Inspecting the hair of one's head is likewise forbidden. Orach Chaim, paragraph 1, in the name of Menorah HaTehorah.
3) There: And one may not read, etc. The same applies to inspecting tzitzis. Mateh Moshe and Maharshal; Magen Avraham, paragraph 1. Anything requiring some close examination may not be done by the light of an oil lamp, even when it uses valid oils. When invalid oils are used, no use may be made of the light at all, even for an activity requiring no close examination. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1.
4) There: And one may not read, etc. If it is still daytime and he can read without a lamp, he may read. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 1, in the name of Hagahos Maimoniyos.
5) In a house that receives daylight, if there is a lamp in a dark area, one may not read facing it. The same applies when a permitted lamp and a forbidden lamp are present: one may not read by the forbidden lamp's light. Halachos Ketanos, volume 2, siman 27. See below, paragraph 12.
6) There: Even if it is enclosed in a lantern. If it is enclosed in a lantern locked with a key, there are grounds not to forbid it. Maharash similarly writes that he heard that Maharam's students would place a lamp in a lantern on Shabbos night, lock it with a key, and then read by its light. Rabbi Avraham Stein also acted this way. Bach, however, disagrees and writes that we adopt the stringent ruling, contrary to Maharam's students and Rabbi Avraham Stein; see there. Taz, paragraph 1, writes the same. Olas Tamid, paragraph 1, similarly writes that it remains forbidden even if he closes the lantern with a lock and gives the key to another person who is not in the house, because the Sages made no distinction. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2, however, writes that giving the key to another person is effective. Yad Yosef similarly permits it in accordance with Eliyah Rabbah, as does Responsa Nachalah LeYisrael, siman 18; see there. Machazik Berachah, paragraph 1, writes that one should be stringent even in this case. Shulchan Atzei Shittim, in the laws of extinguishing, siman 3, seif 2, similarly forbids it even if the lamp is locked with a key, and permits leniency only in a case of great need, because the Sages made no distinction; see there.
7) Machatzis HaShekel, paragraph 1, writes that according to those who permit use of a key, an unusual knot likewise permits reading. Regarding articles sent through a non-Jew that require a seal, an unusual knot is superior to a key, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, simanim 118 and 130; see there. Petach HaDevir, paragraph 2, cites this; see there.
8) If a window or opening exists in the wall between a person's house and his neighbor's, and a lamp in the neighbor's house shines through that window or opening and illuminates the person's house opposite it, it appears that he may read in his house opposite that window or opening. We do not decree lest he enter his neighbor's house and tilt the lamp. If, however, one person has two rooms, with a lamp in one room whose light passes through a window or opening into the second, he may not read even in the other room where no lamp is burning, opposite the window or opening through which the first room's lamplight enters. Beis Menuchah, paragraph 2.
9) Similarly, if a non-Jewish neighbor kindled a lamp in his house to perform his work, and the lamp shines into a Jewish person's house through a window strongly enough that the Jewish person can read comfortably by its light, he may read alone. We are not concerned that he might enter the non-Jew's house to tilt the lamp. Terumas HaDeshen, paragraph 3.
10) Similarly, one may read sacred writings on Friday until nightfall. We are not concerned that his study might draw him onward and cause him to continue reading by lamplight after nightfall. Reading at night itself is prohibited only by decree, and forbidding it before nightfall would constitute a decree upon a decree. Rabbi David Pardo in Chasdei David, chapter 1 of Shabbos, folio 51 at the end of the second column; Beis Menuchah, paragraph 4.
11) There: The same applies to a wax candle. Bach writes in the name of Maharshal that the custom is to be lenient and inspect utensils and tzitzis, and to read the Haggadah, by the light of a wax candle, because there is no concern that one might tilt it. Magen Avraham, paragraph 3, cites this. Taz in siman 278 likewise permits reading by the light of wax and tallow candles; see there. Magen Avraham writes there that although there is concern that one might trim or cut the top of the wick, as Beis Yosef writes, one can answer that this entails no Torah prohibition. Nevertheless, one should be stringent when there is no need, because some say that extinguishing is labor forbidden by Torah law, as stated at the end of siman 334; see there. Magen Avraham further writes that Raavan is lenient regarding a tallow candle because it is repulsive. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 4, cites this. Machatzis HaShekel writes that Rif and Rosh give two reasons concerning a naphtha lamp, one that its light is bright and another that it is repulsive, demonstrating that they disagree with Raavan; see there. Thus, whether one may read by a wax or tallow candle is disputed. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1, writes that when necessary one may rely upon those who permit it. Nesiv Chaim similarly writes that it is sufficient for us to be lenient regarding Torah study by a wax candle, but other activities may not be performed by its light; see there. Ben Ish Chai, parashas Noach, paragraph 18, likewise writes in the name of the later authorities that one should not protest against those who are lenient regarding a wax candle wrapped around its wick, because they have authorities upon whom to rely. Therefore, such a lamp may be permitted for the sake of a mitzvah, namely Torah study, when he cannot appoint a person to watch him. If he can appoint a watcher, however, he should not rely upon the leniency even for a wax or tallow candle wrapped around its wick. He further writes: Know that some tallow candles wrapped around their wicks are not expertly made and require constant trimming while they burn, as we observe directly. These may not be permitted. The same applies to poorly made wax candles that require trimming; they may not be permitted at all. Similarly, glass lamps containing water, oil above the water, and a wick burning in the lamp's center may not involve tilting, but they do require trimming and are therefore forbidden. Although we stated that when one cannot appoint a watcher, he may rely upon those who permit a wax or tallow candle wrapped around its wick, which is not ordinarily trimmed, a person who fears Heaven should nevertheless do the following when possible: place that wax candle inside a glass vessel with an opening, close the opening before Shabbos with an iron lock and give the key to another person, or close it with an unusual knot. He should do so to fulfill the decree of our Sages without any doubt. These are his words.
12) It is obvious that even if a wax candle and an oil lamp stand beside one another, one may not read facing them. This law should not be compared to Magen Avraham's ruling in siman 264 that when a valid lamp stands beside an invalid lamp, one may use their light. There the discussion concerns other activities that do not require close examination, for which the valid lamp alone suffices. We are therefore unconcerned that one might tilt the invalid lamp, since the valid lamp is sufficient. Here, however, the discussion concerns activities requiring close examination, for which even valid oil creates concern that one might tilt the lamp. Although there is no such concern with the wax candle, in this case we are certainly concerned that he might tilt the oil lamp. This is very obvious. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 4. Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 8, writes the same. We already wrote in the preceding paragraph, however, that one should be stringent even with the wax candle itself when possible. See above, siman 264, paragraph 100.
