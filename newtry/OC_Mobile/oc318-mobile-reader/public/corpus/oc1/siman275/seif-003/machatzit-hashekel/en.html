Paragraph 3: With a lamp, etc. Although we may fear he will trim the wick, which entails extinguishing; siman 514 also says this entails repairing a utensil.
For this entails no Torah prohibition. Tur rules like Rabbi Shimon that one is exempt for labor not needed for its primary purpose. Extinguishing, unless done to char the wick or make charcoal, is such labor. Tevuos Shor writes that if a wax candle and oil lamp stand beside one another, reading by them is plainly forbidden. This differs from siman 264, where Magen Avraham permits use when a valid lamp stands beside an invalid one. There the other uses require no close examination, and a valid lamp initially suffices; the invalid lamp will not be tilted because the valid one suffices. Here the activity requires close examination, which is forbidden even by a valid lamp lest he tilt it. A wax candle beside it does not help: although a wax candle cannot be tilted, he may tilt the other lamp. This is very clear.
For some hold that extinguishing is Torah-prohibited. This is Rambam's view, who rules like Rabbi Yehudah that one is liable for labor not needed for its primary purpose.
See se'if 12. Raavan's ruling that repulsiveness eliminates concern that one may tilt depends upon the dispute in se'if 12 among Rashi, Rif, and Rosh. Under their text, a servant may inspect utensils by a naphtha lamp. Rashi explains that because it is repulsive, we do not fear he will tilt it, agreeing with Raavan. Although naphtha may be more repulsive than tallow, Rashi indicates generally that no decree applies when it is repulsive. Rif and Rosh give two reasons: its light is strong and it is repulsive. Beis Yosef says both are necessary: strong light removes concern of tilting, because no tilt is needed; repulsiveness removes concern he may take some fuel, as with olive oil. Since repulsiveness alone does not suffice for them, they evidently still fear tilting despite it, contrary to Raavan.
