That entered an inspected house. The Gemara and poskim imply that no distinction exists even if the loaf is small enough for a mouse to eat, and even if it was nullified. We do not rule leniently on the basis of a Rabbinic doubt, because a doubt cannot displace an established certainty of prohibition. We know with certainty that the mouse dragged the chametz before us, and a doubt cannot displace that certainty, as Pesachim 9a states explicitly. Bach writes similarly, unlike Rosh Yosef, who writes that the case concerns a large loaf and that otherwise one should rule leniently in a Rabbinic doubt.
And found crumbs. If he found a whole loaf, however, he need not inspect, as is explicit below in siman 439. This applies specifically where he nullified the chametz. If he did not nullify it, we do not attribute the matter leniently and he must still inspect the entire house. Tur writes this explicitly in siman 439. Olas Shabbos leaves this law requiring study and concludes, “Nevertheless, from Tur’s ruling concerning a mouse that entered,” etc. He overlooked Tur’s explicit statement of this law. The other poskim agree. See below, siman 439, seif katan 8.
And inspect the house. Meaning all rooms whose doors are open and into which the mouse could enter, even through a hole, as explained in siman 434. Bach rules that we do not suspect the mouse dragged the chametz through a hole. Magen Avraham disagrees and writes that “house” means the room into which we saw the mouse enter. The other rooms present the Gemara’s unresolved question of a mouse entering a courtyard without knowing whether it entered a house. The conclusion is that even if it was very close to the house, we attribute the matter leniently and no inspection is required if he nullified the chametz or if the loaf was not large. In truth, Rambam in chapter 2 of Hilchos Chametz, Tur in siman 434, Rokeach, and the other poskim write: “He should take care with the chametz he leaves over and hide it so that he will not require another inspection, as where a mouse takes it before him, in which case he must inspect the entire house again.” The phrase “the entire house” indicates that all rooms the mouse could reach require inspection. The Gemara, Pesachim 9, likewise infers from the Mishnah, “We are not concerned that a weasel dragged chametz from house to house,” that if we actually saw it take the chametz, inspection would be required. Even where there are two inspected houses and we do not know into which one the mouse entered, both require inspection according to Tur, siman 439. Magen Avraham’s proof from the courtyard case is no proof. There the courtyard itself was not inspected, and we may attribute the chametz to the uninspected place, just as where one house is uninspected and one inspected we attribute it to the uninspected house, as explained in siman 439. It is also possible that the mouse entered neither house, but left the courtyard for the public domain, or that birds and ravens commonly found in a courtyard took it, as explained above in siman 433. This also resolves Rosh Yosef’s question below, siman 439, against Tur’s ruling concerning a courtyard. His answer is very strained, while this explanation is straightforward.
Because it is not the way of a mouse to crumble. The same applies to a dog or chicken. The later authorities in the name of Rokeach.
If a child entered, this applies specifically to a young child. Regarding a minor with understanding, see the end of siman 434.
Because his presumption is that he ate it. This applies even if the chametz was not nullified. Bach and my brother-in-law in Eliyah Zuta rule this way, unlike Olas Shabbos. I find a clear proof from Tosafos, Pesachim 10b, under the heading “Because it is the way of a child to crumble.” Tosafos asks that even according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who requires inspection of the entire field, this case is different because there are grounds to assume that the crumbs came from the loaf, since the child entered immediately beforehand. If the leniency concerning a child applied only where the chametz was nullified, there would be no question at all from Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, whose case concerns a Torah-level doubt involving impurity. Here, after nullification, the issue is merely Rabbinic. Tur and Beit Yosef, siman 439, explicitly distinguish in that manner concerning one who entered and inspected and found something. It is therefore clear that no distinction should be made.
