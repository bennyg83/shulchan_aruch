A new fruit. Since the 2 days are regarded as 1 long day, how can one recite Shehecheyanu over Yom Tov twice on 1 day? This concern applies initially. If, however, he has no new fruit, he should nevertheless recite Shehecheyanu, as on other Yamim Tovim. The only special distinction of Rosh Hashanah concerns an egg and something attached to the ground, so that what is prohibited on 1 day remains prohibited on the other; this does not apply to other matters. The reason is that even when beit din fixed the calendar, there were times when they observed 2 days although there was no uncertainty, because the second day was the principal day, yet they completed the first day in sanctity so that people would not treat it lightly in a later year. Regarding Shehecheyanu, either way it is recited: if the day is doubtful, one recites it because perhaps this is the principal Yom Tov; and if we rely upon the practice of beit din, today and tomorrow are both treated as holy, while the second day is the principal Rosh Hashanah because the calendar was reckoned from it. Question. A certain congregation had a shofar on Erev Rosh Hashanah. Robbers came, plundered them, and took the shofar as well. The congregation sent to another community to send them a shofar, but the messenger was delayed by circumstances beyond his control and did not arrive until the end of the second day. Rosh Hashanah had fallen on Thursday and Friday. By the time the shofar arrived they had already prayed the Shabbos Maariv prayer, but there was still much daylight. May they blow the shofar at that time, without concern for the rabbinic restriction against blowing on Shabbos? Answer. Mordechai in Perek Bameh Madlikin writes that we establish the Mishnah to mean that during bein hashmashot one may make an eruv chatzeirot. Rabbeinu Yitzchak and Rabbeinu Chananel explain accordingly, and so is explicit in the Yerushalmi. The same applies to eruv tavshilin: during bein hashmashot one may make the eruv. Rabbeinu Yoel therefore ruled that even after the Maariv prayer one may make an eruv if it is still day, because answering Barchu is comparable to the shofar blasts by which people accepted Shabbos before bein hashmashot in order to add from the weekday onto the holy. Even so, eruv chatzeirot remained permitted during bein hashmashot, and likewise after answering Barchu. Mordechai then records that Rabbeinu Shemaryah ruled similarly. Some of Rabbeinu Yoel's students disagreed and distinguished between a person who actually accepted Shabbos upon himself and one who had not. The Rav later retracted and prohibited it, citing the Tosefta: after the third shofar blast, even if a person holds a kettle he must place it down. Yet during bein hashmashot the Mishnah permits making an eruv and insulating hot food. This would show that personal acceptance of Shabbos is more stringent than mere doubtful darkness. I am not worthy to decide the dispute, but this proof from the Tosefta is difficult. The Mishnah's statement that one may insulate the hot food means mere insulation, without cooking. The Tosefta, however, speaks of placing a kettle on the fire to cook, which is forbidden after the third blast. Indeed, Shabbos 38 states that when the third blast began, one who needed to remove a pot removed it and one who needed to insulate insulated it. Thus ordinary insulation remained permitted after the third blast. When the Tosefta says that the kettle must be placed on the ground, it must concern a more severe act. Beit Yosef in siman 342 cites Rambam: all matters forbidden only because of shevut were not prohibited during bein hashmashot where there is a mitzvah need or pressing circumstance. For example, one may climb a tree during bein hashmashot to bring a shofar. Without a mitzvah or pressing need this remains forbidden, so one may not tithe definite tevel, although tithing on Shabbos is itself only a shevut; demai, however, may be tithed. Rambam's formulation that the prohibition of shevut applies only to the day itself implies that it does not automatically apply with full force to the voluntary addition of sanctity that a person accepted upon himself. Even those who are stricter regarding personal acceptance than bein hashmashot in discretionary matters should therefore agree that for a mitzvah the acceptance is lighter. Regarding a discretionary act such as eruv, once he voluntarily accepted additional sanctity, the prohibitions of Shabbos take effect upon him. During bein hashmashot without personal acceptance, there is room for leniency because perhaps the sanctity of Shabbos has not yet begun. For a mitzvah, however, the logic reverses. During bein hashmashot there is concern that it may actually be night. When the person merely accepted Shabbos early, however, we can say that he never intended his acceptance to prevent fulfillment of a mitzvah whose time remains today. Had he known that the shofar would arrive, he would not have accepted Shabbos in a manner that canceled the mitzvah. This resembles an acceptance made in error on a cloudy day, as explained in siman 263. Moreover, even if he knew the shofar would later arrive and nevertheless accepted Shabbos, his acceptance cannot uproot the obligation of shofar. His acceptance is no stronger than an oath, and an oath does not take effect to violate a mitzvah. The stringent position in Mordechai concerns discretionary matters. This does not contradict the rule concerning one who accepted Shemini Atzeret early and then eats before nightfall without a blessing on the sukkah. There the person has no active obligation to eat in the sukkah, since he could simply refrain from eating. Here the shofar obligation is a positive active obligation, and his voluntary acceptance cannot nullify it. All the more so in our case, where he did not know that the shofar would arrive. There is an explicit proof from brit milah. Beit Yosef in Yoreh De'ah, siman 262, cites Sefer Mitzvot Gadol: the law does not depend on prayer at all; if people accepted Shabbos while it was still day, this is neither a basis for leniency nor for stringency. If a child was born on Shabbos after the Maariv prayer while it was still day, we do not say that prayer made it night so that his brit milah would have to be on Sunday; rather, the brit milah remains on Shabbos. Conversely, if he was born on Friday after the congregation accepted Shabbos through prayer while it was still day, we do not say that prayer made it night so as to permit performing the brit milah on the following Shabbos. Thus the mitzvah's halachic time is not changed by prayer. The same applies where the brit milah could not be performed earlier on Friday and only at the very end of the day, after he had already prayed the Shabbos Maariv prayer, did it become possible. Since it remains daytime and the mitzvah is presently obligatory, he must perform it; the prayer by which he accepted Shabbos does not cancel the mitzvah. This is unlike the laws of mourning in Yoreh De'ah, siman 402, or the beginning of 7 clean days after an early Maariv, where the authorities are stringent because postponement causes no loss of a mitzvah at its fixed time. The general rule is therefore that where an obligation of mitzvah exists at this very time and cannot properly be fulfilled otherwise, a person's acceptance of Shabbos cannot cancel that obligation. Thus in both the shofar case and the brit milah case, he is obligated to perform the mitzvah while it is still day even after he has prayed Maariv of Shabbos. Regarding shofar, however, there is an apparent contradiction in public conduct, because everyone knows that shofar is not blown on Shabbos and this person has already treated the time as Shabbos. Since blessings do not prevent fulfillment of the mitzvah, he should therefore blow without a blessing. Brit milah presents no such contradiction, because a brit milah in its proper time may itself be performed on Shabbos. Nor is blowing shofar itself a Torah melacha on Shabbos; the prohibition is only a rabbinic decree lest a person carry it in a public domain. Thus the act of blowing could theoretically be a voluntary act and does not visibly contradict his accepted Shabbos status. Reciting the mitzvah blessing, however, would clearly declare that he is treating the time as the obligatory Rosh Hashanah blowing, and that creates the contradiction. This appears clear. Afterward I saw the Tosefta, chapter 5, in a different wording: the third blast separated the people from melacha. If one had taken a scroll to read, it was taken from him; one removed the cooked food from the stove, insulated a kettle, and lit the lamp. When the blowing ended, even if he held a kettle he did not insulate it, but put it down. The phrase "finished reading" in the text means finished blowing, as appears also in Sefer Mitzvot Gadol, Hilchot Shabbos. Since the Tosefta compares this insulation to lighting a lamp, whereas our Gemara permits ordinary insulation during bein hashmashot but forbids lighting, the Tosefta must be speaking of a more severe type of insulation, such as insulating a cold, not-yet-cooked dish so that it will subsequently cook. Accordingly, the students' proof does not establish any distinction between personal acceptance of Shabbos and bein hashmashot. So appears to me, David the small, HaLevi.
