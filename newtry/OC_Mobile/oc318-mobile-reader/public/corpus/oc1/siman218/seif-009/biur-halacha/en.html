And there is one who disagrees. See Magen Avraham, who questions this dissenting opinion, for one certainly cannot recite “Who performed miracles for me” over an ordinary natural occurrence. Biur HaGra similarly asks that, according to this view, every woman after childbirth would have to bless “Who performed miracles for me,” and her children would likewise have to bless forever. According to what Radbaz writes in responsa, part 3, siman 572, cited in Nishmas Adam, this question is resolved. Certainly no authority maintains that ordinary childbirth or ordinary illness constitutes the kind of miracle discussed here, since most sick people and women in childbirth survive. According to all opinions, an actual miracle is required. The dispute is only whether the salvation must be above the natural order, like the miracles performed for our ancestors, or whether any event that Hashem providentially brings about at that precise moment through natural means is likewise called a miracle. An example would be robbers or thieves attacking a person, creating a near certainty that they will kill him, when other people or some other cause suddenly appear and frighten them into fleeing. Although nature was not altered, it is nevertheless a miracle that the Holy One, blessed is He, arranged that cause at precisely that moment. Nishmas Adam appears to understand that, according to the first opinion, only a case of thieves is excluded because they come merely for money and there is no danger to life. This is not convincing, for according to halachah one may kill a burglar because there is a presumption that he comes prepared to kill, as is well known from the law of one tunneling into a house.
