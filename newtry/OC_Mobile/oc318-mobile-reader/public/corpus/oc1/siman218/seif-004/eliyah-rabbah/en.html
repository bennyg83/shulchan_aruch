And all his descendants, etc. Magen Avraham writes that this includes even those born before the miracle, as he states in seif 6. It appears to me that he reasons from Shulchan Aruch's ruling that one blesses over a miracle done for his teacher just as over a miracle done for his father, showing that the reason is honor rather than shared participation, so it makes no difference whether they were born before. In my humble opinion this is incorrect. Shulchan Aruch derives the rule from Ramban cited by Beit Yosef, who reads the Gemara as referring specifically to a son and grandson, because a father's mercy extends that far, whereas other descendants do not bless. Rashba, however, includes all descendants because of their shared participation in the ancestor's existence. If they were already born when the miracle occurred, that shared participation does not apply, as Olat Tamid writes. Clear proof is Rashba's statement that although the Yerushalmi leaves unresolved whether one blesses over a miracle of the public, it can be resolved from Rav Chana, who blessed over a miracle of Rabbi Yehudah his teacher. Why did Rashba not resolve it as Ramban did from a blessing over one's father? Necessarily because Rashba follows his own view that the Gemara includes all descendants and therefore understands their blessing as based on shared participation, from which a teacher cannot be derived. This appears correct in practical law. Regarding a son and grandson, however, one should rule that they bless even if born before the miracle, relying on the majority of poskim who explain the Gemara as referring only to son and grandson, not all descendants, so the reason is not participation. Moreover, even according to the view that includes all descendants because of participation, one can say that son and grandson have an independent obligation apart from that reason. This also explains why Shulchan Aruch elaborates and links the law of a teacher to that of a father rather than simply saying one blesses over one's teacher. Responsa Rabbi Yom Tov Tzahalon, siman 87, writes that if a miracle occurred to someone in an upper story and he lives in the house below, whenever he goes up to that story after thirty days he blesses.
[Levush] To our fathers, etc. Lechem Chamudot is dissatisfied with this wording, arguing that even when several descendants are present they can only say "to our father." It appears to me that the Levush agrees and speaks of descendants of several different people to whom miracles occurred. It also seems that if two grandsons descend from one grandfather, they can say "to our fathers," because their own father is included in the miracle of the grandfather through shared participation. According to this, a lone grandson may say "to my fathers." This appears from Rambam, Semag, Semak, Talmidei Rabbenu Yonah, and the remazim, whose wording otherwise seems difficult. Kol Bo, siman 87, however, says that a grandson should say "to my grandfather."
