Seif 2: The Sages prohibited them. This means unlike Rashi at the beginning of Ein Tzadin, who holds that these labors are prohibited by Torah law because they could have been performed before Yom Tov. This explanation is difficult, because we find a distinction between what could and could not have been done beforehand only regarding preparatory labors for food according to Rabbi Yehudah, based upon the verses “it alone” and “for you.” This implies that no such distinction exists regarding the preparation of food itself. The Sages permitted bringing a knife and an animal to the slaughterer, although on Shabbos carrying a knife for circumcision is forbidden. The same applies to the Korban Pesach. They also permitted crushing salt with a deviation even though salt does not lose its flavor, as explained above in seif 1. Rather, the prohibition follows Tosafos, Rosh, and Ran there. Since a person ordinarily performs such activities for many days at once, they resemble weekday labor. Although the Jerusalem Talmud derives the prohibition from a verse, that derivation is an asmachta. Rashba, however, holds that the prohibition is from the Torah. He writes in Avodas HaKodesh: By Torah law, laborious work is forbidden on Yom Tov just as on Shabbos, as the verse states, “You shall perform no laborious work.” What constitutes laborious work? Any labor not needed for food preparation, such as plowing, sowing, building, weaving, and similar labors. Only food preparation was permitted. Yet the Torah did not permit every form of food preparation, because after permitting it, the Torah limited it with the words “only,” “it alone,” and “for you.” These are exclusions. The Torah entrusted their definition to the Sages. The Sages stated that reaping, detaching produce, threshing, selecting, grinding, sifting, trapping, milking, making cheese, and squeezing are forbidden. They also prohibited by Rabbinic law removing honeycombs, etc. Why were these particular labors listed? Because the Torah did not permit work ordinarily performed to prepare provisions for many days. Nor did it permit only activities performed with items already in one’s possession, rather than uprooting something from its place of growth, because most fruit is gathered for an extended period. Certainly trapping living creatures not under one’s control is excluded, since it requires exertion. There are also other labors, and even forms of carrying, that the Sages prohibited because they resemble ordinary weekday conduct, as will be explained.
