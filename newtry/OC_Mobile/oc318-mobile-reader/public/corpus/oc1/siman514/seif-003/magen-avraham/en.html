Before he lights it. The Rosh writes that although indirectly causing extinguishing is permitted, as stated in siman 334, seif 22, this applies only when he does not touch the burning substance. Oil and the wick, however, both sustain the flame, and one who reduces either of them hastens its extinguishing. Therefore, after lighting it, one may not place around it something that will prevent it from continuing to burn. These are the words of Beit Yosef. This requires study, for a cloth that caught fire may be surrounded with water, as stated in siman 334, seif 24. One cannot answer that the latter permission is only to prevent a loss, because the Rosh permits indirect extinguishing even without a loss; otherwise his proof from indirect extinguishing would pose no difficulty. It therefore appears to me that the Rosh forbade only removing part of the burning substance itself. Placing something outside it, however, is permitted. This is also implied by his wording there and by the Tur, who omitted the words 'before he lights it,' indicating that it is permitted even after lighting. The remaining piece is not forbidden as muktzeh, since it was not set aside at twilight. Moreover, he could have caused it to extinguish indirectly, and therefore he did not remove it from his mind. See siman 279. Nevertheless, it appears to me that a candle lit for a mitzvah on Yom Tov remains forbidden for benefit even after it goes out, since it was set aside for its mitzvah, like decorations of a sukkah. Tosafos write on Shabbos 42b, s.v. Ve'ein Ne'osin Mimenu, that even according to Rabbi Shimon, who does not accept muktzeh generally, oil that drips from a lamp is forbidden for benefit because it was set aside for its mitzvah. Although according to Rabbi Shimon one may benefit from it after it goes out, according to our ruling it remains forbidden throughout the day, because anything set aside at twilight remains set aside for the entire day. See Shabbos 44a, s.v. Shebaner Veshebake'arah. What Olas Shabbos writes here, that we do not enact one decree upon another, is not precise, for many times we find such a decree and say that all of it constitutes one enactment.
Meaning, that he lights it. This means specifically when he intends to light both parts together. If, however, he acts in order to detach the first part so that the second side will not ignite, it is forbidden. Yam Shel Shlomo. The Rama's wording, however, implies that even if he does not need both parts and acts only to shorten the candle, it is permitted. This is unlike seif 8, where his intent is to make two wicks from one, and it is therefore forbidden unless he needs both, because he thereby fashions a utensil. Here, by contrast, he intends only to shorten the candle so that it will go out sooner, and this is permitted as indirect extinguishing. According to this view, it is plainly permitted to insert a burning candle into sand. The Rama therefore writes, 'Some permit,' because they disagree with the first view. The Bach rules likewise. As I wrote above, even according to the first view it is permitted. See seif 2, seif katan 9, and siman 509, seif 3.

And it is permitted to position it. It appears to me that this is inferred from Rambam's wording. As stated above in siman 277, seif 2, it is forbidden to open a door opposite a bonfire even when only an ordinary wind is present. This implies that when no wind is present at all it is permitted, even though wind will eventually come. This requires study. Regarding a bonfire, an ordinary wind is actually permitted by law, and the Sages prohibited it only as a decree lest an unusually strong wind occur. All the more so, one might think they should prohibit opening the door when no wind is presently blowing lest one do so when the wind is blowing, since wind changes from moment to moment and the distinction is difficult to discern. This is also implied at the beginning of siman 277, which forbids opening a door or window opposite a lamp without distinguishing whether wind is present. See siman 680. What is written in siman 277, seif 2, 'even when there is only,' etc., refers specifically to a bonfire. There, an ordinary wind is permitted by law and was prohibited only as a decree lest an unusually strong wind occur, so when there is no wind at all it is permitted. With a lamp, however, it is always forbidden. It cannot be permitted as indirect extinguishing, because that was permitted only to prevent additional fuel from burning, whereas causing a flame that is already burning to go out is forbidden, as stated in seif 2. See the end of siman 265. The custom on Yom Tov night is therefore not to carry the synagogue candles home as is done on Motzaei Shabbos, for the reason I have written. This is contrary to Shelah, who writes that it is forbidden to insert the candle into sand and that one should instead carry it home. The opposite is correct.
