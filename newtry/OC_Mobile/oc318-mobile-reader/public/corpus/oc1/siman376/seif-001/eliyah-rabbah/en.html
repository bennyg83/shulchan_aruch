Recognizable, etc. So that the bucket should not enter his fellow's domain. Why are we not concerned with this regarding a balcony at the beginning of siman 355? Magen Avraham answers that there he stands above and lowers the bucket straight through the opening, so we are not concerned that it will go outside. The Bach answers that there the water is generally deeper than ten tefachim and therefore constitutes a makom patur. In truth, however, it appears to me from the Shulchan Aruch and Levush that the requirement that a tefach of the partition extend into the water is to provide a proper recognizable marker, not because of concern that the bucket will stray. Accordingly, with a balcony there is already a recognizable marker, as Tosafot 87 s.v. Bein explain, since the opening is four by four and a partition is made around it. It also appears to me that since there is such a marker, one will presumably take care that the bucket not pass outside it.
The Levush writes: This is difficult for me, etc., that it is not precise, etc., but it is strained, etc. In my humble opinion, there is no difficulty at all. With a partition we do not apply the principle that its edge descends and closes, as stated in Avodat HaKodesh, p. 20, and therefore at least one tefach must extend into the water. But with the edge of a roof we certainly say that it descends and closes completely, as is also implied by Shiltei Giborim in the name of Ri'az and by Rabbeinu Yerucham, p. 95. Even apart from this, there is no difficulty, for the Rashba and Rabbeinu Yonatan write that the principal reason a beam four tefachim wide permits is that the bucket does not travel more than four tefachim, and therefore one will not draw outside the beam. The same applies to a partition four tefachim wide: it need not have a tefach submerged in the water. Only a partition less than four tefachim wide must be submerged or extend a tefach above, so that the bucket not pass beyond it. The Levush, however, apparently does not accept this rationale, as stated above.
