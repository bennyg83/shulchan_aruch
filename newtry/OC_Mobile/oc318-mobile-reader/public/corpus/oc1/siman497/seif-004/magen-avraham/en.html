Because it is a double doubt. This applies only when both doubts concern the object's own status. A double doubt created through mixtures is forbidden, as Shach writes in Yoreh Deah, seif katan 10, contrary to Taz. The primary ruling, however, requires study. A non-Jew who brings a gift of a species that is attached to the ground is forbidden even on the second day of Yom Tov, although we do not know that it was detached that day, as stated in siman 515. The same is implied by siman 513, end of seif 6, which states that a non-Jew speaking incidentally is believed on the second day of Yom Tov that the eggs were laid the previous day. This implies that if he says nothing, they are forbidden because of the doubt. Terumas HaDeshen writes this explicitly. He states that although regarding a gift brought by a non-Jew where there is concern of trapping or detaching from the ground we forbid it even on the second day of Yom Tov, which is D'Rabbanan, while something brought from outside the techum is permitted to another person, that case is different. That decree applies specifically to Yom Tov, and therefore the Sages did not distinguish between the first and second day, just as they did not distinguish in any Yom Tov prohibition, as written in siman 496. By contrast, the rule that a non-Jew speaking incidentally is believed was not stated specifically regarding Yom Tov, but is a general rule throughout the Torah that he is believed in D'Rabbanan matters. See what I wrote in siman 515, seif 3. Yam Shel Shlomo, siman 4, rules stringently on the second day of Yom Tov even in a case of doubt, and this is the principal ruling. Kenesses HaGedolah writes: See the language of Rosh of Lunel cited in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 120. He writes there that on the second day of Rosh Hashanah doubtful preparation is forbidden, and the same applies to a second day of Yom Tov adjacent to Shabbos.
