In a covered enclosure, trapping does not apply to birds at all, even if it is very large, except for a free bird, to which trapping applies even in a covered place. Regarding wild animals, if one can reach it in a single run, trapping does not apply and it is permitted even if the animal is rebellious. Where trapping according to the Torah applies, however, it is forbidden even merely to drive the animal into a house. This requires study. Whenever one cannot catch the animal in a single run, it is forbidden even in a covered enclosure, and all the more so regarding a bird. Tosafot explicitly write that the test, “Bring a net and let us catch it,” refers to birds in a covered enclosure. The same is implied by Yam Shel Shlomo and all the poskim, who do not distinguish between covered and uncovered places. Rather, whenever a net is needed it is forbidden. Rambam likewise implies this in chapter 10, where he writes that one who drives a bird into a large enclosure is exempt. This must refer to a covered enclosure, because in an uncovered enclosure one would be exempt even if it were small, since the bird can fly out. Thus he necessarily refers to a covered enclosure, and nevertheless in a large enclosure the bird is not considered trapped. It follows that trapping applies when the bird is already in a large enclosure. This is the intent of the poskim, who omit the special case of the free bird and instead give the general rule that the place must be one in which the creature is trapped, meaning that no net is required. This is also Shulchan Aruch's view at the beginning of siman 316, where he writes, “into a house in which they are trapped.” See there. Rambam likewise writes that one who brings a bird into a tower is liable, whereas one who brings a free bird into a house is exempt. He then writes that one who drives a bird into a large enclosure is exempt. He first says “bird” to include every bird as considered trapped in a tower. In the middle clause he says “free bird,” meaning that specifically a free bird is not considered trapped in a house, while other birds are considered trapped because a house is small. In the final clause he again says “bird,” teaching that in a large enclosure no bird is considered trapped, even if it is covered, as explained above. The Gemara likewise implies this.
