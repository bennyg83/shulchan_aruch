Seif katan 14, and he found three, etc. We do not say that one is nullified in two, because living creatures are significant and are not nullified. Furthermore, it is something that has a permitted alternative. So it is in the Ran.
According to this, if one of them was eaten, etc. They are permitted, etc. This is according to the view of the Mechaber there, who permits something that has a permitted alternative by a double doubt, namely a third mixture; so too he permits where one fell away. But according to the Rama there, who permits something that has a permitted alternative through a third mixture only for need or significant loss, all the more so he permits only for need or significant loss where one fell away, which is worse than a third mixture, as the Shach wrote there in seif katan 57. Regarding whether one may eat them all together, and by one person, even according to the Mechaber, see there. But in the Gemara there is another concern, etc. The Gemara's language is: whichever way you look at it, if they are others, they are others, and if they are not others, there is one mixed among them.
Rashi explained: whichever way you look at it, meaning that for this reason they are forbidden whichever way you might try to permit them. End quote. One must say that the Ran, of blessed memory, who cited only that there is one mixed among them, should have cited also the second reason, which creates stringency regarding one that fell away. For according to the conclusion, etc., they tend to hop, etc. One can say that the Ran explains this whichever way you look at it, if they are others, etc., as the Bach wrote, unlike Rashi. After this the Gemara brings a baraisa in which Rabbi and the Sages disagree about second tithe coins: if one left one hundred of second tithe and found two hundred, they are chullin and second tithe mixed, according to Rabbi, for we say that another person mistakenly placed one hundred chullin beside the one hundred of second tithe. The Sages say all is chullin, for we say that he took the one hundred of second tithe and forgot, and placed two hundred chullin there instead. The Gemara initially thought that the law of fledglings is the same as the law of second tithe, the dispute of Rabbi and the Sages there. Therefore it said that if he designated two and found three, they are forbidden whichever way you look at it, whether according to Rabbi or according to the Sages. It said: if they are others, meaning according to the Sages, who say regarding second tithe that he took the hundred of second tithe and placed there two hundred chullin coins, so too here we say the designated ones went elsewhere and these are others; if so, they are others and all three are undesignated, for they are others. And if they are not others, meaning according to Rabbi, who says chullin and second tithe are mixed, so too here we do not say these are others, but that one fledgling was added from elsewhere, as Rabbi holds; in any case, there is one mixed among them, as Rabbi holds. According to this, what we say, if they are others, etc., is to prohibit even according to the Sages, and that is only according to the initial assumption that fledglings are like second tithe. But according to the conclusion, even the Sages agree regarding fledglings that there is no concern to say these are others, but only that one is mixed among them. The Ran cited only the conclusion, which appears at the end of the Mishnah: if he designated three and found two, they are permitted. What is the reason? We say these are the same ones and one of them went elsewhere. Shall we say this follows Rabbi and not the Sages?
For it was taught: he left one hundred and found two hundred, etc. If he left two hundred and found one hundred, then one hundred remains and one hundred was taken, according to Rabbi, for we say that from the two hundred he took one hundred and left one hundred of second tithe in its place. The Sages say all is chullin, for we say he took all two hundred and placed one hundred chullin. The Gemara answers: you may even say it follows the Sages, for it was stated about it that Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Elazar both said: fledglings are different because they tend to hop. Meaning, since they move on their own, one can say that one hopped away and the other two remained. But coins do not go by themselves unless a person took them, and when he took them he took both, as Rashi explained. According to this, since the Sages agree regarding fledglings in the latter clause, where he designated three and found two, the same applies in the first clause, where he designated two and found three: they agree with Rabbi that we do not say they all went away and these are others, but since they hop, they agree with Rabbi that we say only that one is mixed among them, even though in the first clause regarding second tithe, where he left one hundred and found two hundred, they disagree with Rabbi and hold that all is chullin. Nevertheless, with fledglings they agree.
If so, the only reason in the first clause, where he designated two and found three, that they are forbidden is because one is mixed among them. Therefore it is well understood that the Ran cited only this reason, and therefore if one of them fell away, the remaining ones are permitted.
For it is not the way, etc. Beside another person's coins is not precise; rather, it means beside second tithe coins.
And see seif 13 in the gloss, that even when tied, we say they tend to hop. What he wrote, siman 439, perhaps intends to resolve a slight difficulty in the Bach's explanation. For it is stated regarding the answer that fledglings hop that Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Elazar said about it: one says the dispute is with two pouches, but with one pouch all agree that it is chullin; and one says the dispute is with one pouch, but with two pouches all agree that one hundred remains and one hundred was taken. If so, according to the one who says that with two pouches the Sages agree that one hundred remains and one hundred was taken, why did Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Elazar need to answer that fledglings are different because they hop? Fledglings resemble two pouches. Rabbi Elazar said: here we deal with tied fledglings and tied pouches. Meaning, when we say the dispute is with one pouch, this does not mean literally one pouch, but two pouches similar to one pouch, namely where they are tied, and the Sages hold that in such a case all is chullin. They wanted to establish our Mishnah here as speaking of tied fledglings. Therefore according to the Sages one must say that fledglings are different because they hop and become separated from one another. These are the Gemara's words. If so, the Gemara's words on the first clause, with two found as three, where it says whichever way you look at it, if they are others, etc., according to the Bach's explanation mean according to the Sages. But since the Gemara had not yet thought of the distinction that fledglings hop, certainly it had not yet thought of Rabbi Elazar's answer that it speaks of tied fledglings. If so, according to the one who says that with two pouches the Sages agree, then with untied fledglings, which are like two pouches, the Sages agree with Rabbi that we do not say these are others. How then could it say, if they are others, etc., according to the Sages? It would have had to say that the Gemara follows specifically the one who says the dispute is even with two pouches. To this he hinted by saying to see what the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 439, seif katan 7, that the statement that the dispute is with one pouch, meaning two pouches similar to one pouch, that is, two tied pouches, while with truly two pouches that are not tied the Sages agree, applies specifically in the latter clause, where he left two hundred and found one hundred. But in the first clause, where he left one hundred and found two hundred, the Sages disagree even with two untied pouches. This resolves what was stated according to the initial assumption: if they are others, etc., according to the Sages, even according to the one who says that with two pouches the Sages agree. See what I wrote, with Heaven's help, there in siman 439. The Bach asked: since Rabbi Elazar establishes the latter clause as speaking of tied ones, the same should apply to the first clause, where he designated two and found three. If so, whichever way you look at it, if he found two tied and the third not tied, why are the two forbidden? We know that the untied one is the one that came from elsewhere and the two tied ones are those he designated. If he found all three tied, it is obvious they are forbidden, for we see that all three are not those he designated. And if he found all of them untied, why speak specifically of two found as three? Even if he found only two untied, they would be forbidden, for perforce these are others, since he designated tied ones. He answered that in truth even if he found two they are forbidden because they are untied, and it mentioned finding three because it speaks in all cases, whether he designated tied ones or untied ones; when he designated untied ones, they are forbidden only if he found three, and therefore it mentioned finding three. But the Taz, with whom the Peri Chadash agrees, disagrees and holds that if he designated two tied fledglings and found two untied, they are permitted, for they became separated from one another and are the same ones.
