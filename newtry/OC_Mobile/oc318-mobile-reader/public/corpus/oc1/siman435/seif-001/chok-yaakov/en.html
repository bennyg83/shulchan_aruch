On the fourteenth day. The inspection is performed by candlelight. The same applies to all later inspections, even after Pesach, so that no distinction is made between one inspection and another. This is implied by the poskim above, siman 433.
If he did not inspect throughout the fourteenth. It requires study whether, if a person failed to inspect before the prohibition began and then died, the heirs are obligated to inspect and remove the chametz. Perhaps a prohibition is not inherited, as Ameimar says in Gittin 40 concerning one who freed his slave and died. Although the halachah there does not follow Ameimar, that may be only a Rabbinic stringency, as Rosh explains there. Moreover, chametz itself is a novelty. It is not legally in a person’s possession, yet Scripture treats it as though it were in his possession in order to impose liability, as stated in the first chapter of Pesachim and in Bava Kamma. We should therefore limit the novelty to its explicit scope and not impose the obligation upon heirs. If the heirs use the house, however, they must cover the chametz with a utensil or make a partition so that they do not stumble through it, as with another person’s chametz, explained below in siman 440. Nevertheless, it is proper to remove it, since it is already prohibited for benefit and they gain nothing by retaining it. They should not recite a blessing over the inspection and removal, because they are not obligated to inspect and remove chametz that is not theirs.
During Pesach. Even if he already nullified it, and even on Yom Tov itself, he inspects. Although he cannot burn it on Yom Tov, he can cover it with a utensil until after Yom Tov. Taz and Magen Avraham agree, unlike Bach.
Because it is prohibited for benefit. This is the wording of Tur, Rambam, Semag, and the other poskim. It implies that the stringency of inspection and removal was imposed specifically because chametz is forbidden for benefit, in addition to being something from which people do not separate themselves throughout the year. Had it been permitted for benefit but prohibited only for eating, inspection and removal would not have been required. This is explicit in Ran and implied by Tosafos at the beginning of Pesachim. If it were permitted for benefit, one could retain it at home for benefit, provided he made a recognizable safeguard against eating or selling it. Accordingly, if one nullified before Pesach, and according to Baal HaItur such chametz is permitted after Pesach, then in his view there would be no need to inspect it after Pesach. He must therefore interpret the Mishnah’s phrase “after the appointed time he should inspect” as referring to after the sixth hour, not after Pesach, as Rashi explains. Bach below, siman 448, follows this approach. I discussed it at length in Toras HaShelamim, responsum 6, contrary to Shaar Ephraim.
For an inspection after Pesach he does not recite a blessing. Although the inspection is a Rabbinic enactment, no blessing is recited because it exists only as a secondary safeguard, lest one confuse this chametz with other chametz. The prohibition of chametz that remained over Pesach is itself only a Rabbinic penalty. This is unlike Pri Chadash in Yoreh Deah, addenda to siman 13. The wording implies that specifically an inspection after Pesach is performed without a blessing. An inspection during Pesach requires a blessing, because the chametz is then prohibited for eating and benefit by Torah law. Even if he nullified it and no longer violates the prohibitions of seeing or finding chametz, he should recite a blessing because the mitzvah to remove it remains upon him. Tosafos at the beginning of Pesachim implies this, as do Kol Bo and Agudah, who write that one always recites a blessing except when inspecting after the appointed time, meaning after Pesach. There is also some proof from the ruling above, siman 432, that one who forgot the blessing may recite it at the time of removal, because removal is the primary completion of the inspection. If one who nullified could not bless over removal, how could this person bless then? He presumably nullified properly on the night of the fourteenth. The daytime nullification is only a preferred enhancement. Nevertheless, he blesses over the removal. All the more so should one who never inspected recite a blessing when he finally inspects. This is the primary ruling, unlike Taz, who remains uncertain. Magen Avraham also implies that a blessing is recited despite prior nullification. Where one inspected and nullified, but then found chametz during Pesach, Magen Avraham leaves unresolved whether a blessing is recited over its removal. In my opinion, one should recite a blessing. Magen Avraham himself rules plainly at the beginning of siman 446 that one blesses over removing an item that became chametz during Pesach or chametz discovered during Pesach. This applies to actual chametz. If only a minute trace of chametz is found during Pesach, no blessing is recited. Magen Avraham writes this below, at the beginning of siman 446.
