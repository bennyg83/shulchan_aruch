In his house. If the chametz is in the public domain, it is like ownerless property, and it is forbidden to move it even when it causes harm to the public. Rivash.
And he should destroy it. He recites the blessing over destroying chametz, even though he already nullified it. All the more so if dough became chametz for him on Pesach, since this was not included in the nullification, he should recite a blessing over its destruction. This applies specifically to actual chametz; but with a wheat kernel found in a cooked dish, which is not actual chametz, it appears to me that he should not recite a blessing over it. See above, siman 435, s.k. 3, what I wrote there. So too, one should not recite a blessing over a mixture involving a mashehu.
A utensil. The Tur's view is that even if he did not nullify it, such as where it became chametz on Yom Tov and he transgresses bal yera'eh, nevertheless it is forbidden to destroy it. They did not permit even moving it, such as throwing it into the sea or scattering it to the wind. In truth, however, the view of the other great halachic decisors is explained as follows: specifically where he nullified it, so that he does not transgress bal yera'eh, covering it with a utensil is enough; but without nullification, the prohibition of bal yera'eh comes and at least overrides rabbinic moving, and even burning is permitted, for we say that since kindling was permitted for a Yom Tov need, it was permitted even not for a need. So ruled the Bach. But the consensus of most later authorities is that he has no remedy except covering it with a utensil, and they did not permit even telling a non-Jew to throw it into the sea; so concludes the Magen Avraham. Nevertheless, where the practice is to be lenient, the practice stands, and they have authorities on whom to rely; see Magen Avraham and Chok Yaakov. See also siman 325 se'if 2, where the Rema wrote, such as removing chametz from his house on Pesach; examine it.
