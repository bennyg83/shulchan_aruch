Levush: But if he did not nullify it, etc. Since the later authorities collected somewhat from my work Eliyah Zuta improperly, I must copy it, and this is my language: according to this, if something became chametz on Yom Tov, which certainly was not nullified, he must burn it; see the end of siman 436. So ruled the Bach, except that he wrote that one should throw it into the river. This applies specifically to actual chametz, but a cooked dish in which a wheat kernel was found on Yom Tov is forbidden according to everyone, since prohibition by a mashehu is rabbinic. But my elder, the Gaon, of blessed memory, acted in practice like the plain ruling of the Shulchan Aruch: even where he did not nullify it and it is actual chametz, it is forbidden to move it, for the Sages upheld their words by requiring passive inaction even to uproot a Torah matter. So ruled Maharam of Lublin, siman 20, and Nachalas Tzvi. End of my language. Recently the Taz and Magen Avraham were printed, and they too ruled this way, for other reasons. In Chok Yaakov he proved from the Talmud, folio 7, where Rav says: one who checks must nullify. What is the reason? A decree lest he find a fine cake and his mind be upon it, and he transgress bal yera'eh. This proves that it is permitted where he did not nullify, for if not, let the reason be lest he forget on Yom Tov. End of his words. In my humble opinion, from this there is no proof at all, for the Magen Avraham wrote in the name of Tosafos, folio 29, that one who keeps his chametz while intending to destroy it does not transgress. Therefore the Talmud needed to say that his mind is upon it, meaning that he intends not to destroy it, and therefore he transgresses. One can further say based on what Kesef Mishneh wrote in chapter 3, that the Torah said bal yera'eh only when it is in his power to destroy it, but if he is prevented because of a rabbinic prohibition, such as on Yom Tov, he does not transgress. One can further say that it was preferable for the Talmud to give a reason that applies even on a weekday. The Shelah wrote on folio 141 that this law of covering it with a utensil speaks where there is no non-Jew present; but where there is a non-Jew present, they should tell him to remove it, even chametz that he nullified, for the positive commandment of tashbisu se'or comes and overrides the rabbinic prohibition of telling a non-Jew, which is a shevus. End quote. In Eliyah Zuta I wrote that one should not rule this way for several reasons. It also leads to a stumbling block, for they may give it to him to eat; and most times they will not go with him, and he will certainly eat it. Even to throw it into the river one should not be lenient through a non-Jew.
