Open space is more stringent than invalid s'chach. Thus, in a small sukkah, open space invalidates at three tefachim, whereas invalid s'chach invalidates only at four tefachim. The Taz explains that a visible gap is more conspicuous.
Specifically when it runs. This rule applies when the open space is less than three tefachim; the law of three tefachim is explained below.
His head and the greater part of his body. Ran's text reads, "his head or the greater part of his body," and Ritva has the same reading.
It divides the sukkah lengthwise. Since the strip crosses the entire sukkah, each side is left with only one and a half walls, and both sides are invalid.
A valid measure remains. That is, a valid measure of sukkah remains on the side adjoining the middle wall, whether the intervening strip is invalid s'chach or open space.
That place is valid. It has three walls. The outer section on the other side is invalid because it has only two walls. If the invalid s'chach between the middle wall and the end is less than four amos, the outer section is also valid through the principle of a bent wall. If the interruption is open space, however, the outer section is invalid, because a bent wall cannot extend through open space.
Even the outer section. Sometimes the outer section is valid, whether the interruption is invalid s'chach or open space, when it remains connected at the sides. The inner and outer sections then combine, even if the inner section alone lacks the minimum measure of a sukkah. Magen Avraham. One may not, however, sit beneath the gap or invalid s'chach. Bikkurei Yaakov questions this combination.
