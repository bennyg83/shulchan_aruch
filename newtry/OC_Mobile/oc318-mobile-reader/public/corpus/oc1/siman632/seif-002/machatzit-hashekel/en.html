(Small paragraph 2) The s'chach, etc. Although it does not extend, etc. Meaning, it is not distant in width.
Since the height, etc. We do not say chavot and lavud to be stringent. Meaning: first we would have to say chavot rami, as above in siman 631:5, and through this it would be as though the roof were placed below. Afterward we would have to say lavud of the roof with the beam. To be stringent, we do not say both. Granted, the Magen Avraham holds in siman 502, and likewise at the end of this siman, that we say lavud to be stringent, unlike the Rema; but he agrees that we do not say chavot and lavud together to be stringent. See what the Ran wrote about the dispute on page 11a, where Rabbi Eliezer and Ravina disagree, and everyone holds that if there was open air of three handbreadths in the sukkah and he reduced it with invalid s'chach, it is valid, for the invalid s'chach has no measure to invalidate and the open air too has no measure to invalidate, since three handbreadths of open air did not remain, and we say lavud. All this is when such a case is from the side. But if such a case is in the middle of the s'chach, they disagree whether we say lavud in the middle or not. The Ran asked: why did the dispute take this case, where the open air became less than three handbreadths because he reduced it with spits? It should have taken the simple case: there was originally open air of less than three handbreadths in the middle of the s'chach, whether we say lavud. He answered that it comes to teach a novelty according to the one who says there is no lavud in the middle: even when there had been three handbreadths of open air from the side and he reduced it with invalid s'chach, he agrees that we say lavud only if he placed the invalid s'chach next to the valid s'chach and the open air remained next to the wall; in such a case we say lavud from the side. But if he placed the invalid s'chach next to the wall, we do not say lavud, for this is lavud in the middle. Although one could say that the invalid s'chach placed next to the wall is bent wall, and if so the open air is from the side and one can say lavud, nevertheless we do not say two principles, bent wall and lavud. These are the contents of his words. Certainly the amoraim do not disagree about this; even according to the one who says that we say lavud in the middle, and that is how we rule, presumably he agrees that we do not say lavud and bent wall together. Although it appears from the Magen Avraham's words that we would say chavot and lavud together were it not that this is to be stringent, one must distinguish between bent wall with lavud and chavot with lavud. Nevertheless, it is surprising to me that the Beis Yosef did not bring the above words of the Ran as practical law, for we do not find poskim who disagree with the Ran, and one can also bring proof from the Talmud as above. Even if the Beis Yosef found someone who disagreed with the Ran in this, in any event it should at least be a dispute. Perhaps one can say that the Ran's intent is specifically according to the one who says there is no lavud in the middle; since one cannot say lavud unless one first says bent wall, and through this it becomes lavud from the side, in such a case we do not say it because the two principles need each other. But according to us, who say lavud in the middle, we say in one sukkah both bent wall and lavud if that sukkah needs bent wall and also lavud, since they do not need each other. Therefore the Beis Yosef did not bring the Ran's words for us. However, this is not so. Even if we say this reasoning, nevertheless there is a practical difference for us too, for sometimes one must say bent wall and also lavud, and they do need each other. According to what the Magen Avraham wrote at the beginning of this siman in the name of the Ran, that we say bent wall only when the walls reach the s'chach, if so the same applies where the walls are high until the s'chach, but the s'chach is distant across the width of the sukkah and there is open air there; according to the Ran one cannot say bent wall. But if that open air is not three handbreadths wide, one could first say lavud, so it is as though the walls reach the s'chach, and afterward say bent wall. They need each other, for one cannot say bent wall unless one first says lavud. What difference is there whether one must first say lavud and afterward bent wall, or the reverse, first bent wall and afterward lavud? Likewise such a case is found when the walls are distant from the s'chach less than three handbreadths and there is open air there; one must also first say lavud and afterward bent wall. If so, even according to us there is a practical difference in the Ran's words, that in such a case we do not say both, and the Beis Yosef should have brought the Ran's words.
