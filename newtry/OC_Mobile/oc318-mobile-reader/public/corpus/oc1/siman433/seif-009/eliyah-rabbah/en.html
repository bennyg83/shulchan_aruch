Levush: When the time of its prohibition arrives, etc. This resolves Rama’s difficulty from siman 445, seif 3, and Levush therefore omitted the difficulty. Examine this carefully. Eliyah Zuta. Chok Yaakov overlooked this point. The statement in Shulchan Aruch comes from Agudah. It does not discuss definite chametz after the time of prohibition. Before the time of prohibition, one may throw chametz into a place where birds are commonly found, as Hagahos Maimoniyos writes. Nevertheless, Chok Yaakov treats Rama and Bach’s position as an error and infers this from Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 193. In my humble opinion, the opposite is true. Rama derived his reasoning from that responsum. Mahari Weil writes: “Definite chametz must certainly be removed, as stated in Hagahos Maimoniyos. Therefore, one should be careful before Pesach not to throw grain to chickens in a damp place, lest it ferment and remain there during Pesach.” At first glance this is difficult, because Hagahos Maimoniyos explicitly permits throwing it there before the time of prohibition. One must therefore say that Mahari Weil disagrees with Hagahos Maimoniyos, which permits doing so before the time of prohibition but forbids it afterward. Accordingly, he holds that definite chametz must be inspected for lest it remain after the time of prohibition begins. It appears that he derived this from Agudah, which writes: “This applies to doubtful chametz, but definite chametz must be removed.” The implication is that definite chametz requires action even before the prohibited time. Chok Yaakov writes that when the first clause mentions doubtful chametz, the same law applies to definite chametz, but the term bedikah is appropriate only for doubt, as Beit Yosef writes in siman 438. This point may be questioned. In my humble opinion, one may say the reverse. The latter clause says “must be removed,” rather than “must be inspected,” because it concerns definite chametz. Nevertheless, it still concerns the period before the prohibition, like the first clause. Since the word bedikah could not be used, the author simply used the term removal, because this action is needed for removal. See siman 436, seif katan 2. The words of Shulchan Aruch, “but definite chametz,” plainly imply this. Magen Avraham and Chok Yaakov challenge Bach from Pesachim 8: Why does the Gemara ask from Rava’s ruling that the middle of a courtyard does not require inspection? Perhaps the baraisa concerns definite chametz. In my opinion, the Gemara challenges the clause teaching that one does not inspect by sunlight. As already explained, the term inspection refers specifically to doubtful chametz. Magen Avraham answers that Semak’s statement, “but not definite chametz,” means that with definite chametz we do not assume the birds ate it. If birds are commonly found in the courtyard, it still requires inspection. If ravens come there, however, they certainly dragged it elsewhere. Chok Yaakov asks that the Gemara’s difficulty remains. Perhaps the baraisa concerns definite chametz in a place where ravens are not commonly found. It appears to me that Rava says without qualification that a courtyard does not require inspection because ravens are commonly found there. This indicates that ravens are found in every courtyard. Otherwise, he should have said that a courtyard where ravens are common does not require inspection. When Magen Avraham writes, “if birds are common in the courtyard,” his intent is to derive from this that a chicken coop does require inspection. I later found in Sefer Amarkal, written on parchment: “It appears obvious to me that only in a doubtful case is no inspection required. If it is definitely known that chametz lies in the courtyard, it requires inspection, as we learn from the Mishnah concerning chametz covered by a collapsed structure, although dogs are found there. What difference is there between dogs and ravens? Rather, that case is different because the chametz is definitely there. We likewise say that we do not concern ourselves that a weasel dragged it away, because who says that it ate definite chametz?” According to this, all the suggested answers to the Gemara fall away. One must say that the Gemara preferred to establish the baraisa even in a doubtful case, corresponding to the clause concerning torchlight. This requires study. The explanations offered for Agudah and Shulchan Aruch are therefore incorrect. Their intent certainly follows Sefer Amarkal, as Rama understood. At the end of siman 445, the case is different because the chametz was placed in ownerless territory, as Hagahos Maimoniyos states explicitly. Nevertheless, if it is found there after the prohibited time, one need not remove it merely because one sees that his chametz is still lying there. This is entirely unlike giving chametz to non-Jews after the time of prohibition, because there it is in the non-Jews’ possession. This is unlike Taz at the end of siman 445.
