2) There: In the evening, one recites two blessings, etc. The first begins, 'Who by His word brings on evenings,' etc., and concludes, 'Who distinguishes between day and night. Blessed are You, Hashem, Who brings on evenings.' This is the Sephardic text. The Ashkenazic text reads: 'Who distinguishes between day and night; Hashem, Master of Legions, is His Name; the living and enduring God shall always reign over us forever and ever. Blessed are You, Hashem, Who brings on evenings.' R' Yechiel, of blessed memory, writes that the Sephardic text is more correct, because the Ashkenazic wording does not contain a theme resembling the conclusion immediately before the conclusion. To justify the custom, Rosh, of blessed memory, would say that we pray that Hashem reign forever and ever, that the verse, 'Toward evening there shall be light,' be fulfilled, and that He illuminate for us. This therefore somewhat resembles the conclusion immediately before the conclusion. Tur. Taz, paragraph 1, and Magen Avraham at the beginning of the siman cite this. Bach likewise gives a reason justifying the custom; see there. The phrase, 'Hashem, Master of Legions, is His Name,' also resembles the conclusion, as it is written, 'Who turns dawn into darkness; Hashem, Master of Legions, is His Name.' Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3. Nowadays Sephardic communities practice saying after, 'Who distinguishes between day and night,' the phrase, 'Hashem, Master of Legions, is His Name. Blessed are You, Hashem, Who brings on evenings,' because it is mentioned in Shaar HaKavanos, folio 52b, which gives a mystical reason; see there.
