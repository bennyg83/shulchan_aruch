Magen Avraham wrote, citing Olas Tamid, that one should pause between “in the firmament,” etc. Meaning, the word “as He wills” should be connected to what precedes it: “and arranges the stars in their watches in the firmament as He wills.” For we say at the end of Shabbos that Israel is not subject to mazal, and Tosafos write that at times, through great merit, Hashem changes a person's mazal. Thus we find that the Holy One, blessed is He, said to Avraham: “What is your view, that Jupiter stands in the west? I shall place it in the east.” We see that at times it is His will, blessed is He, to overturn the constellations and stars, and it is therefore fitting to say that He arranges them in the firmament as He wills. But if the words “as He wills” are connected to what follows, “as He wills, He creates day and night,” this is inappropriate. Certainly He has the power to change day into night or night into day, to command the sun not to rise, but He has already promised that “day and night shall not cease,” except as an exceptional occurrence, as with the darkness in Egypt, which was not throughout the world but only in Egypt, and likewise the stopping of the sun for Moshe, Yehoshua, and Chizkiyahu, which occurred only exceptionally. By contrast, overturning the order of the stars certainly occurs in every generation for the righteous of the generation, and these are among the hidden miracles.
It may be said that we pray, etc. “And it shall be toward evening that there shall be light,” while “darkness shall cover the nations.” This is indeed of the same theme as the conclusion “Who brings on evenings,” meaning that evening and darkness should befall the nations, just as He brings on evening each day.

For in Pesachim 117b we say that Rabbi Yochanan stated: Krias Shema and Hallel conclude with “Who redeemed Israel,” whereas the Amidah concludes with “Who redeems Israel,” because it is a prayer for mercy. Rashi explains that it is a prayer for mercy because we pray concerning the future. But Krias Shema, and likewise Hallel on the night of Pesach, are praises concerning the past redemption from Egypt. The Bach concludes there: “Rather, one must say ge'alo, without a vav between the gimel and alef, for this properly refers to the redemption that already occurred.”
However, at the end of the first chapter of Berachos, 12a, Ravah bar Chinena Sava said in the name of Rav: Whoever does not say Emes Veyatziv in the morning and Emes Ve'emunah in the evening has not fulfilled his obligation, as it is stated, “To declare Your kindness in the morning,” etc. Rashi writes: “The verse states, ‘To declare Your kindness in the morning,’ and the blessing Emes Veyatziv is entirely about the kindness He performed for our forefathers by taking them out of Egypt. The blessing Emes Ve'emunah, however, also speaks of the future, for we await His fulfillment of His faithfulness and promise to redeem us from the hand of kings,” and so forth. Tosafos there, after citing Rashi's words, write: “At the end of the blessing it returns to Pharaoh's redemption in order to adjoin it to ‘Who redeemed Israel.’” Thus, even in Maariv the conclusion refers to the past. Perishah likewise asks that the Tur above, in siman 66, cites Rashi's words, while here he writes that one must say “Who redeemed Israel,” not “Who redeems,” because it refers to the past redemption. He answers on his own reasoning that although the blessing discusses the future redemption, the conclusion must nevertheless refer to the past redemption. This is cited in Eliyah Rabbah; see there.
Seif katan 1: A need, etc. See siman 167, seif 6, that something required for the meal is not considered an interruption between washing and HaMotzi. The same applies to something required for prayer.
This is unlike Maharshal, who writes that since nowadays we have accepted Maariv as obligatory, the Rashba's reason no longer applies, and therefore one may not interrupt with an announcement. Bach disagrees, holding that the Rashba's principal reason is that the announcement is needed for the prayer, which applies nowadays as well. The Rashba's statement that Maariv is optional is merely an additional consideration, not the primary reason.
Specifically on Rosh Chodesh, which is D'Oraisa. Meaning, although we rule that at Maariv one who forgot Yaaleh Veyavo is not required to repeat the Amidah, Rosh Chodesh itself is nevertheless D'Oraisa. Likewise, regarding “Grant dew and rain,” although the insertion is not D'Oraisa, it has the significant feature that one who omits it must repeat the Amidah. This differs from Chanukah and Purim, which are D'Rabbanan, and one who omitted Al HaNissim does not repeat the Amidah.
It appears to me that between the sections, etc. But interruption between Krias Shema and its blessings is forbidden according to all opinions. Although he already agreed above that the fact that Maariv is optional is only an additional consideration, while the primary reason is that the announcement is needed for prayer, and that reason applies between the sections as well, his intent is nevertheless that it is preferable to interrupt with the announcement after Yir'u Eineinu rather than between the sections. After Yir'u Eineinu, some poskim permit interruption for all matters, since in any event the verses of Yir'u Eineinu already interrupt between redemption and prayer, as the Tur writes in their name. By contrast, even at Maariv all agree that one may not interrupt between the sections. It is therefore preferable to interrupt between redemption and the Maariv Amidah rather than between the sections.
