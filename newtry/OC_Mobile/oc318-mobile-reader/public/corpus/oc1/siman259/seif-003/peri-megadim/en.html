I have seen fit. I have seen fit to record here some of the laws of shehiyah and hatmanah from simanim 253, 254, 257, and 259. With God's help, they will be explained further in the Magen Avraham.
First. The law of shehiyah requires clarification. We follow the lenient custom based on the second opinion in siman 253, se'if 1, that food which has reached ma'achal ben Derusai, meaning half cooked according to the Magen Avraham in siman 254, se'if 2, letter 8, may remain even in our ovens, which are neither swept out nor covered with ash, if continued cooking harms it. If continued cooking improves it, it must be fully cooked according to Rashi of blessed memory, which is also implied in siman 253. It is therefore necessary to determine which foods are mitzamek v'yafeh lo and which are mitzamek v'ra lo. The Rosh in the chapter Kirah quotes the Rif that a turnip dish, dates, porridge, and similar foods are mitzamek v'ra lo, whereas cabbage, beans, and minced meat are mitzamek v'yafeh lo. The Gemara on folio 37b states the rule that anything with a marrow is mitzamek v'ra lo, except turnips containing meat when they are not intended for guests, which are mitzamek v'yafeh lo. If they are intended for guests, or contain no meat, they are mitzamek v'ra lo. This implies that even without marrow, when there is no meat they are mitzamek v'ra lo. According to another version, figs, dates, and porridge are mitzamek v'ra lo. Lechem Chamudos there, letter 1, states that cabbage, beans, and minced meat are mitzamek v'yafeh lo. See Rashi there. Hot water is mitzamek v'ra lo, as is explained there. Regarding roasted or cooked eggs, however, there are differing opinions. The Milchamos and Ran write that eggs are mitzamek v'yafeh lo, based on the statement on folio 38a that contracted eggs are mitzamek v'yafeh lo. The Yerushalmi in the first chapter of Shabbos, halacha 10, however, lists meat, onions, and eggs as mitzamek v'ra lo. To reconcile this with the Bavli, I wrote in siman 254 in the Taz, letter 1, and in my novellae to Shabbos, that the Bavli may be discussing eggs contracted in wine or a similar liquid, as in Chullin 6a, where eggs contracted in wine were brought before him. Eggs by themselves, however, are mitzamek v'ra lo. See Eliyah Rabbah, siman 318, letter 10, in the name of Minchas Kohen, that whenever one cannot determine clearly whether a food is mitzamek v'ra lo, it is proper to be stringent. The same applies to cooking under siman 318, se'if 4. Moist meat that is mitzamek v'ra lo may be warmed because there is no prohibition of cooking after cooking in it, and this appears to be the view of the Mechaber there in se'if 8. See the Magen Avraham, letter 27. If a pot contains one item that is mitzamek v'ra lo and another that is mitzamek v'yafeh lo, it appears that the majority determines the status. This follows all the more from the rule that a cold dish containing both liquid and congealed material is judged by the majority, as Eliyah Rabbah writes in siman 318, letter 11, in the name of Minchas Kohen. This is learned from the Beit Yosef in siman 253, in the name of Rabbeinu Yeruham citing Rabbeinu Yonah, that where the majority is gravy, the dish is mitzamek v'yafeh lo and, when cold, may not be returned. I explained this in siman 253 in the Taz, letter 13, and in siman 254 in the Taz, letter 1. The same rule applies when a mixture contains elements that are mitzamek v'yafeh lo and mitzamek v'ra lo: the majority determines its status. Whenever the majority is mitzamek v'ra lo, one will not stoke the coals and thereby spoil his food. As stated, ordinary gravy is considered mitzamek v'yafeh lo. With God's help, this will be explained further in siman 318.
And all. All this is based on custom rather than strict law. Likewise, where the pot is uncovered above, the custom is to be lenient, as in siman 253, se'if 1. Strictly speaking, however, if the pot touches the coals one should be stringent, as I will explain briefly, with God's help, among the laws of shehiyah and hatmanah. Consequently, the only clear basis for permission is the Rama's ruling in siman 254, se'if 1, that this is the established practice because a person remembers when the oven is sealed with clay. There is a difference between the phrases 'such is the custom,' 'they practiced,' and 'this is the established practice.' The last expression implies that one may rely on this rationale plainly even to be lenient. It therefore deserves consideration whether the permission applies only when the oven is thoroughly sealed with clay, as Rashi explains in Shabbos 18b, 'sealed with clay,' and as the Rama writes in siman 254, se'if 1, and siman 257, se'if 8. Sealing it with rags may have only the status of closing the oven with a board and would not provide the reminder created by clay sealing. Tosafos there, s.v. 'In a cauldron,' distinguishes when it is sealed thoroughly, and even according to our practice clay may therefore be required. See Magen Avraham, siman 257, letter 19. It would thus be better with our cholent to place it in the oven very close to nightfall, so that it has the status of raw food. However, since people put into it a pie called kugel and also insulate a pot of legumes, buckwheat, or similar foods that cook quickly, this permission does not apply, as stated in siman 254, se'if 8, and in siman 257 in Magen Avraham, letter 19. According to the Mechaber in siman 254, se'if 1, sealing with rags may perhaps suffice.
And in an oven. Although an oven sealed with clay is permitted, this may apply only where there is no true hatmanah. If the cholent pot is completely covered with coals, as is sometimes done, sealing the oven with clay does not permit it, because Maharashal writes in responsum 60 that with hatmanah the Sages prohibited olive waste because of hot ashes. See there. The Rama's statement in siman 257, se'if 8, that our manner of hatmanah in an oven sealed with clay is permitted means that it is not actually hatmanah, since the pot does not touch the oven walls. This appears from the flow of the Taz, letter 6, and Magen Avraham, letter 19, as I explained there. The Nechalas Tzvi at the end of siman 257, however, writes that since the Sages permitted it only when the oven is sealed, the person will remember. See there. His proof from siman 254 concerning roasting where there is clay sealing may be answered by saying that roasting is merely shehiyah, not hatmanah. For shehiyah we do not decree where stoking is not relevant, whereas hatmanah remains different. In any event, closing the oven opening with rags provides no reminder, because that is not called true sealing. There is no prohibition, however, against closing it with rags. This is unlike siman 259, se'if 7 in the gloss, which concerns clay.
And when Yom Tov falls. When Yom Tov falls on erev Shabbos, it is proper to place the cholent in the oven early enough that it reaches at least ma'achal ben Derusai, meaning half cooked, before nightfall, according to what the Magen Avraham writes in siman 527 that according to our practice the permission rests only on the principle of ho'il. See there. It is permitted on Yom Tov that falls on erev Shabbos to seal the oven opening with clay. See siman 507, se'if 7. If rags are soaked in clay, the water is squeezed from them, and they are then used to seal the oven on Yom Tov that falls on erev Shabbos, one may argue that this is forbidden. Although the rule that soaking is laundering does not apply to such rags because they are dirty, as explained in siman 302, se'if 10, in Magen Avraham, letter 22, squeezing nevertheless applies to them, as the Magen Avraham writes there and in Shaar Efrayim, siman 46. It is therefore better to use clay than rags, lest one squeeze them, since he is particular that they not contain excessive water that will drip into the oven. Because the oven can be sealed without squeezing, squeezing is not sufficiently necessary for food preparation, and we are concerned that he may come to squeeze. If he has no clay, he should place the rags in water before Yom Tov and not squeeze them on Yom Tov. Rather, he should place them wet around the oven. Even though squeezing may then occur inevitably, it is permitted when done for food preparation, but he should not squeeze them directly by hand, since it is possible to proceed without squeezing. With God's help, this will be explained in simanim 301, 302, and 503. This is not the place to elaborate. See siman 299 regarding this matter. One may nevertheless argue that it is better to use rags without squeezing than clay, because smoothing clay is a melacha according to the Torah. See Levush, siman 507. If possible, soaking the rags before Yom Tov is preferable. If squeezing is inevitable but he does not desire it, the primary law will, with God's help, be explained in simanim 301 and 302. In the laws of Yom Tov, with God's help, it will also be explained whether melacha on Yom Tov is considered completely permitted or merely overridden. There are three categories concerning raw food. Raw meat may be left on the fire, as stated in siman 253, se'if 1. Vegetables and legumes that cook quickly may not, as stated there in Magen Avraham, letter 4. Fruits that are good both raw and cooked have the status of ma'achal ben Derusai and may be left according to the second opinion in siman 253, se'if 1, as stated in siman 254 in Magen Avraham, letter 12.
Hatmanah. According to the Mechaber in siman 253, se'if 1, whenever the pot touches and adheres to the coals, even if the pot is uncovered above, it is considered hatmanah and is forbidden. The same applies to all substances that add heat. This is his view. See Chiddushei HaRan to Shabbos 49a regarding a basket in which one insulated a pot. The materials that add heat are listed in siman 257, se'if 3. From the Taz in siman 258 we learn a novel point: even hot water in a kli sheni that is at yad soledes bo heat, apparently specifically when it is at yad soledes bo, is considered to add heat when one insulates something there for a short time. He distinguishes between insulation for an extended period and insulation for a short time. See there. What is the measure of hatmanah? The Rama writes in siman 253, se'if 8, that it applies specifically when the pot is not exposed above. It must be considered whether he means only when the opening of the pot is also covered, or whether whenever the entire body of the pot is insulated it is hatmanah even if its opening remains exposed. The Taz in siman 258 implies the latter. In Chiddushei HaRan to Shabbos 48a regarding a basket, he writes that one may remove the cover and the insulating material falls away, and that this applies specifically where the material does not add heat. This implies that the pot is considered insulated even though its opening is exposed. One might argue that this is only when the entire pot other than its opening is insulated, but the implication remains as stated. Regarding a stove swept free of coals, see Magen Avraham, siman 257, letter 18, who suggests that it is all the more so cooling. According to the Taz in siman 258, however, insulation there is forbidden for a short time. A stove whose coals are covered with ash certainly does not permit hatmanah. Even if one places an empty pot or similar object upon it, places another pot on top, and covers it with garments, it is forbidden. This is not comparable to siman 253 in Magen Avraham, letter 38. There the discussion is shehiyah, whereas here it is hatmanah, and as long as fire remains beneath it, it certainly adds heat.
I have found a solution. I have found a case in which one may perform an initial hatmanah on Shabbos in something that does not add heat. If a pot was insulated in an oven sealed with clay and completely surrounded by coals, as is sometimes done with cholent, it is permitted because the person remembers, as stated in siman 257 in Magen Avraham, letter 19, and Taz, letter 6, as I explained there. Therefore, if he removes it from the oven on Shabbos intending immediately to place it in pillows and cushions, this is permitted because it is not considered an initial hatmanah, as stated in siman 257, se'if 4. This is all the more so here. It is not comparable to siman 253, se'if 2, in Taz, letter 14, and Magen Avraham, letter 25, because there the pot in the oven was not considered insulated, since there was air around it. Here, however, if it had been insulated in the oven among the coals, or if the oven walls were narrow and touched it, it appears that this return is permitted, as I have written.
