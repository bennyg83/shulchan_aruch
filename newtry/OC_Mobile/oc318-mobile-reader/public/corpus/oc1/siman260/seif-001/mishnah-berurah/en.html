(1) It is a mitzvah, etc. This is in honor of Shabbos. Nevertheless, it is not an absolute obligation. One who fulfills it receives reward, while one who does not is not punished. Great care must be taken that the mitzvah of bathing not lead to possible desecration of Shabbos. Regrettably, many people stumble in this during the short winter days, remaining in the bathhouse until nearly dark. Even if the bathing itself was completed permissibly, a prohibition may still arise, for sometimes one combs his hair at the end, which is a melacha forbidden by the Torah on Shabbos. It is also common that after all the bathing is finished, one wrings the towel of the water absorbed in it, which is included in laundering, as will be explained below in siman 302. Anyone who has fear of Hashem in his heart should restrain the public from this. The merit of the many will depend upon him, and he will also avoid being held accountable, God forbid, for their sins. The teaching of the Sages is well known: whoever can protest against the members of his household and does not do so is held accountable for their sin, and whoever can protest against the people of his city and does not do so is held accountable for their sin.
(2) To wash. The mitzvah of washing also applies to women. During the short winter days, when they lack time, they should fulfill it by washing their faces and hands. This is also sufficient for men when they lack time. See Eliyah Rabbah.
(3) His entire body. Meaning, with hot water.
(4) And his feet. Today people are not accustomed to be careful about this. Perhaps it applied specifically in their countries, where people commonly walked barefoot and therefore washed with water because of dust and dirt, unlike in our countries. Peri Megadim writes similarly above in siman 4, Eshel Avraham, s.k. 1; see there.
(5) On erev Shabbos. The same applies to all these matters on erev Yom Tov. Thursday does not display honor for Shabbos, unless he cannot wash on erev Shabbos. In that case, the closer it is to Shabbos the better. The same applies to taking a haircut.
(6) On erev Shabbos. People are particular not to cut the nails on Thursday because they begin growing again on the third day after they are cut. It is therefore not a preparation in honor of Shabbos, since they begin emerging again on Shabbos. Some write that the fingernails and toenails should not be cut on the same day, and that they should be cut only on erev Shabbos or erev Yom Tov. The proper practice is to cut the toenails on Thursday and the fingernails on Friday, as stated by the Magen Avraham. The Gemara states that one who burns his nails is pious, one who buries them is righteous, and one who throws them away is wicked, lest a pregnant woman step over them and miscarry. Therefore, in a study hall, where women are uncommon, or a bathhouse intended only for men, it is permitted to throw them away. The Gemara also states that they can cause harm only in the place where they originally fell, but if they are later gathered and thrown elsewhere there is no concern. The Prisha, siman 241, writes that if a person cut his nails at home where women are present and some fell from his hand, he should sweep that place and move them, after which there is no concern. Eliyah Rabbah writes that perhaps only moving them outside the room is considered a change of place. One should wash his hands after cutting the nails, as stated above in siman 4.
(7) To cut his hair. With a razor or scissors, so that he should not enter Shabbos unkempt. In some places people do not take haircuts or cut nails on Rosh Chodesh even when it falls on erev Shabbos, because R' Yehuda HaChasid instructed this.
(8) He should not cut, etc. The Tashbetz states that Maharam was not particular about this, and the same is written regarding the Arizal. Nevertheless, one should be careful l'chatchila, as stated by the Magen Avraham.
