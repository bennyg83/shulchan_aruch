The Magen Avraham wrote, and the Bach wrote, etc. That there is a sound reason. This refers to what the Magen Avraham writes at the end of this se'if katan in the name of Sefer HaGan. The Magen Avraham's difficulty from the second and fourth chapters of Taanis is as follows. The second chapter refers to the Mishnah on folio 15b concerning the men of the ma'amad. The daily offerings brought each day belonged to all Israel, and a person's offering cannot be brought while he is not standing over it. The early prophets therefore divided the kohanim, leviim, and Israelites into twenty-four groups. The kohanim and leviim were called the men of the watch, and the Israelites were called the men of the ma'amad. Each group served for one week in rotation. When the time of a group arrived, the kohanim and leviim went up to Jerusalem. The Israelites who lived in or near Jerusalem also went up, while those far from Jerusalem gathered daily in the synagogues of their towns, read the account of Creation each day, fasted throughout the week except Sunday and Friday, and prayed that the offering be accepted favorably before Hashem. They were called men of the ma'amad because they served as the agents of all Israel to stand over the offering. They were forbidden to take haircuts or launder clothing during their week, so that they would prepare beforehand and not enter their watch unkempt. On Thursday they were permitted because of the honor of Shabbos. Rashi explains that most people take haircuts on Thursday, etc. Thus the men of the ma'amad took haircuts on Thursday and did not wait until erev Shabbos.
The fourth chapter of Taanis begins on folio 26b. The Mishnah states that during the week in which Tisha B'Av falls, it is forbidden to take a haircut or launder clothing, but on Thursday it is permitted because of the honor of Shabbos. Rashi explains that if Tisha B'Av falls on erev Shabbos, laundering is permitted on Thursday. This applies when the calendar was fixed by observation, since in our calendar Tisha B'Av cannot fall on erev Shabbos. This is not a proof against the Bach and Maharshal, who forbid taking a haircut on Thursday. That case is different because one cannot wait until Friday, which is Tisha B'Av, and therefore Thursday was permitted. Ordinarily, however, it may be preferable to wait until erev Shabbos. The proof is rather from Rashi's concluding words on folio 26: when Tisha B'Av falls on Wednesday, the Mishnah need not teach that laundering is permitted on Thursday, because the Gemara states that the restriction applies only before Tisha B'Av, while afterward it is permitted. Rashi thereby explains why he establishes the Mishnah when Tisha B'Av falls on Friday rather than on Wednesday. From this the Magen Avraham can indeed prove that taking a haircut on Thursday is ordinarily permitted. Otherwise, why was Rashi troubled? Perhaps the Mishnah concerns Tisha B'Av on Wednesday, and it was necessary to permit a haircut on Thursday, since one might otherwise have required waiting until Friday. If Tisha B'Av fell on Friday, however, Thursday had to be permitted. It follows that Thursday is ordinarily permitted. There is also a proof from the Rambam. The Rambam omitted the law that Thursday is permitted. Lechem Mishneh explains that he follows his commentary to the Mishnah, where he writes that before Tisha B'Av no leniency was granted even for the honor of Shabbos. The Mishnah therefore concerns Tisha B'Av on Wednesday and follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who forbids even afterward. Thursday was permitted only for the honor of Shabbos. Since we do not rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, the Rambam omitted this law. According to us, a special permission for Thursday would arise only if Tisha B'Av fell on Friday. If it fell on Wednesday, permission on Thursday would be obvious because we do not rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. Yet if it fell on Friday, Thursday would actually be forbidden because it precedes Tisha B'Av. Thus, according to the Rambam, the Mishnah concerns Tisha B'Av on Wednesday, when one could wait until Friday, and nevertheless the Sages permitted Thursday, apparently against the Bach in the name of Maharshal. In my humble opinion, however, no proof may be brought from the fourth chapter according to either Rashi or the Rambam. The permission on Thursday may refer only to laundering and not to taking a haircut. Rashi on folio 26b explicitly says that if Tisha B'Av falls on erev Shabbos, laundering is permitted on Thursday. He mentions only laundering and omits haircutting. This actually supports the Bach and Maharshal, that haircutting is forbidden on Thursday, whereas laundering was permitted in accordance with Ezra's enactment that clothing be laundered on Thursday. See the Taz here for the reason. The proof from the second chapter, however, is sound, for Rashi writes there that on Thursday of their watch they were permitted, since most people take haircuts on Thursday rather than erev Shabbos because of the burden. Rashi explicitly applies the permission to haircutting and says this is common practice. Perhaps, since Rashi explained the permission in the second chapter as including haircutting, the same wording in the fourth chapter also includes it, and his mention of laundering there is not exclusive. This is somewhat forced, and the Magen Avraham's resolution does not fully address it.
It is possible that nevertheless the optimal mitzvah, etc. The men of the ma'amad were not free on erev Shabbos. Since they were occupied throughout their week, they had to use erev Shabbos for other Shabbos needs, and the Sages therefore permitted them to take haircuts on Thursday. Although Rashi writes that most people take haircuts on Thursday, most people may not be particular about the optimal manner, since Thursday is permitted by law and they advance it because of the burden. The question remains why the Sages permitted the men of the ma'amad to take haircuts on Thursday rather than insist on the optimal manner. The Magen Avraham answers that the Sages were lenient for them because they were occupied. If the Magen Avraham's proof were also from the fourth chapter, however, this answer would not suffice.
And the fingernails on Friday. Since the fingernails are exposed and visible, greater concern is given to the honor of Shabbos, and their cutting is placed closer to Shabbos. This also prevents them from beginning to grow on Shabbos.
Because they grow on Shabbos. Meaning, since they are cut to remove the excess, it is not an honor to Shabbos that the excess should begin growing again on that very day.
One who burns his nails, etc. The Gemara in Niddah, in the chapter Kol HaYad, explains that a pregnant woman might step over them and miscarry, for this is a property of cut nails. In the chapter Elu Megalchin it is stated that in a study hall, where women are uncommon, they may be thrown away, because there is no concern. Although the study hall might later be swept and the nails carried outside where a pregnant woman could step over them, the Sages were not concerned, because once the nails have been moved from the place where they first fell at the time of cutting, they no longer cause harm. Therefore, if nails accidentally fall to the floor while being cut, one should sweep that place. However, Pri Chadash in Yoreh De'ah siman 115 writes that if, while cutting them, one placed them in one place with the immediate intention of moving them elsewhere, then the second place assumes the property of the first and they can cause harm. He brings a proof; see there. Tosafos explain why burning them is more pious than burying them: burning eliminates the possibility that they will later be uncovered. The Aruch explains that burning a nail harms the person, as does burning anything that comes from the human body. He is therefore considered pious because he is stringent to burn them even though it harms him. Regarding bathing, Tevuos Shor writes that Pesachim 111b states that one should not put on his shoes while his feet are still wet from bathing, because this endangers the eyesight. People are not particular about this, and 'Hashem protects the simple.'
