Seif katan 5, "the scalpel," etc. Rama in Yoreh De'ah understood Rabbeinu Yerucham to permit moving the scalpel after the brit milah, because once it came into one's hand permissibly it may be returned to a place of safekeeping. Magen Avraham, however, cites Rabbeinu Yerucham's language and explains it differently. Maharshal also understood Rabbeinu Yerucham in the lenient manner, yet he goes further and permits moving the scalpel even after it was put down. The argument that there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos means that at twilight the scalpel was not muktzeh, because it was needed for the brit milah. Even though after the procedure there is no further need for it, perhaps it should not become muktzeh because during part of Shabbos it was fit for permitted use. Magen Avraham writes, however, that it appears proper to be stringent. This case is not governed by the principle that there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos. Yerushalmi, cited in siman 651 regarding a lulav, indicates that once the blessing was recited and the mitzvah fulfilled, after the lulav is put down it is again forbidden to move it. This shows that the principle does not apply to a case of this type. The rule that there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos applies where an object temporarily became unfit and later became fit again, such as figs that were moist and edible at twilight, later became unfit while drying, and then completed drying and became edible again. Since they were fit at twilight and later returned to fitness, the temporary interval does not create muktzeh. A brit milah scalpel is different. Its ordinary status is muktzeh machmat chisaron kis. Before the procedure it is permitted only because it is needed for the mitzvah. Afterward it returns to its normal restrictive status. The same is true of a utensil whose melacha is forbidden. Even though one permissibly used it for its body or place, afterward one may not move it merely from sun to shade. Ramban's ruling concerning burial implements is similar: their continued permissibility depends on the possibility that they may still be needed that day. Once it is known that they will no longer be needed, their ordinary muktzeh status returns. Maharit writes that because the scalpel is still in the mohel's hand after the brit milah, this itself permits him to carry it to a place of safekeeping. Magen Avraham therefore derives that the leniency applies specifically so long as he has not put it down. Once he puts it down, moving it anew is forbidden. Taz in Yoreh De'ah, siman 266, gives another reason for leniency. If the mohel had to throw away the scalpel immediately after use, he might refrain from performing the brit milah out of fear that the instrument would be lost. This is stronger than ordinary cases in which the Sages permitted the end of an act because otherwise people would refrain from beginning it. Taz nevertheless limits the leniency to placing the instrument safely in the same room, not carrying it elsewhere when unnecessary. According to Magen Avraham, however, so long as the scalpel remains in the mohel's hand he may carry it to any place he wishes. Once it has been set down, it may no longer be moved even merely within that room. In a case where 1 mohel performs both the cutting and priyah and must therefore put down the scalpel after the cutting, there is room to permit picking it up again because of the concern that otherwise he may refrain from undertaking the mitzvah. Eliyah Rabbah suggests handing it directly to another person, though it is difficult to understand how this helps, because for the second person this would be a new act of moving muktzeh. Beit Yosef writes that one may not carry the infant from his house through an unjoined courtyard to the place of the scalpel. The principle that a living person carries himself does not apply to an infant of this age in the relevant way. Through a non-Jew there is room for leniency where no Torah-level public domain is involved. Even so, it is preferable to bring the scalpel to the infant rather than bring the infant to the scalpel, because after the brit milah one would otherwise need a second rabbinic leniency to return the infant to his mother. Where bringing the infant to the synagogue would require carrying through an unjoined courtyard, it should not be done merely for the honor of performing the brit milah in the synagogue if the procedure can be performed at home. If absolutely necessary and no other method is possible, the authorities discuss reliance upon a non-Jew and the general leniencies of a double rabbinic prohibition for a mitzvah.
