"Twilight." See Mishnah Berurah, where I cited Radbaz. This is not treated as a classic double doubt, 1 doubt whether it was still day and another doubt within twilight itself. Pri Megadim suggests that perhaps the mother's prior status as pregnant is relevant, though that presumption is not especially strong for determining the precise halachic time of birth. Where there is uncertainty whether the head emerged before or during twilight, one cannot automatically say that we establish the mother in her prior status until the later moment. Once the head emerged, the Torah may already regard the child as born. Thus, if a known interval elapsed between emergence of the head and completion of the birth, the timing of the head's emergence must itself be evaluated. Several authorities discuss a case in which the child was fully born after 3 medium stars were visible, but there was a delay from the emergence of the head. If the witnesses can estimate that the head clearly emerged while it was still day, one follows what they saw. If they cannot determine the time, there is substantial reason to be stringent and not allow the brit milah to override Shabbos. "Born without a visible foreskin." The term refers to a child who appears so even without erection. If the child appears to have a foreskin when the organ is not erect, but after erection it appears otherwise, the authorities discuss whether this status is considered present from birth. The practical law depends upon whether there is truly hidden foreskin tissue that requires cutting or merely hatafat dam brit. "Caesarean section." A child removed through the abdominal wall rather than through ordinary birth does not have the same law concerning an eighth-day brit milah overriding Shabbos, because the Scriptural formulation connects the eighth day with the mother's status of birth impurity. "2 foreskins." The authorities explain this either as 2 organs or as 2 superimposed foreskins. In either interpretation the brit milah does not override Shabbos because of the unresolved doubt concerning the Torah's application to the case. The common denominator of these cases is uncertainty concerning whether the Torah's ordinary rule of an eighth-day brit milah applies in a form strong enough to override Shabbos. Where the mother's birth does not create tumat leidah, such as Caesarean delivery or a non-Jewish mother who converts only afterward, the exclusion follows the Scriptural relationship between the mother's impurity and the son's eighth day.
