"A scalpel from before Shabbos," etc. It is written in Yoreh De'ah, siman 266, that one may move the knife after the brit milah. This is based on Rabbeinu Yerucham, who writes that after the procedure one may return the knife, because once the utensil came into one's hand permissibly he may carry it to any place he wishes; Ramban writes likewise. The intent is the general rule that when one already has a muktzeh object in his hand in a permitted manner, he need not immediately drop it. Maharshal also permits moving the knife, but he appears to disagree with Rabbeinu Yerucham's limitation and permits it even after it was put down. He relies on the principle that there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos. It appears to me, however, that one should be stringent. The Yerushalmi cited in siman 651 regarding a lulav shows that after the mitzvah was completed and the lulav was put down, it becomes muktzeh again. The rule that there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos applies where the object became temporarily unfit and then returned to fitness, as explained in siman 310. Here the knife's ordinary status is muktzeh machmat chisaron kis. Its permitted use for brit milah does not permanently remove that status. After the procedure, when it is no longer needed, it returns to its ordinary prohibition. Likewise, a utensil whose melacha is forbidden may be moved for use of its body or place, but afterward one may not move it from sun to shade. Ramban's discussion of burial implements is similar. They remain movable only so long as they may still be needed for another deceased person that day. Mahari Beit writes that rinsing the scalpel after the brit milah is forbidden when this is done only for preservation after Shabbos, but there is no muktzeh problem while the instrument is still in his hand. One should therefore be stringent not to put it down until he has carried it to a place of safekeeping. Beit Yosef writes that it is forbidden to carry the infant from his house through a courtyard to the scalpel. Through a non-Jew there is room for leniency where no Torah-level public domain is involved. It is nevertheless clear that one should not bring the infant to the synagogue through an unjoined courtyard merely for the ceremony, since the brit milah can be performed at home. Where there is no alternative, one may rely upon the authorities who permit even stronger forms of non-Jewish assistance for the needs of brit milah.
