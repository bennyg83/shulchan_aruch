Rama there rules that where there is uncertainty whether an infant is a 7-month or an 8-month child, even if his hair and nails are not fully developed, the brit milah is performed because either he is a viable 7-month child and the mitzvah applies, or he is an 8-month nonviable child and cutting his flesh is of no consequence. Taz, however, writes that one should not be lenient. Regarding desecrating Shabbos to save such an infant, certainly one does not desecrate Shabbos where he is of doubtful 7- or 8-month status and his hair and nails are not complete; see Shach there, seif katan 15. This implies that rabbinic prohibitions may nevertheless be performed by a Jew, just as the brit milah itself is performed under the either-or reasoning, even though moving the child is rabbinically prohibited. One should not argue that moving is lighter, for wounding involves the melacha of extracting, and although where the blood itself is not needed the prohibition is rabbinic, it is nevertheless a rabbinic prohibition, yet the act is permitted because a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently for the mitzvah of brit milah, since perhaps he is a 7-month child. The same reasoning should apply to rabbinic acts for pikuach nefesh. See Even HaEzer, siman 4, Beit Shmuel, letter 19, regarding the rule that a child is sometimes treated as a 7-month child even where only 5 calendar months separate the relevant dates. The same can affect the Shabbos calculation. Regarding a woman who gives birth in the ninth month there is a dispute there. It is possible that brit milah presents no Torah-level concern because of the either-or argument and a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently; the matter requires study. Regarding a doubtful 7- or 8-month child whose hair and nails are incomplete on the second day of Yom Tov in the Diaspora, the matter likewise requires study. See Shach, Yoreh De'ah siman 266, seif katan 8, who inclines to say that even a doubtful case overrides the second day of Yom Tov. All the more so here, where there is an either-or argument and perhaps the halacha follows Rama there in seif 11. If a child of doubtful 7- or 8-month status is born already lacking a visible foreskin and requires only hatafat dam brit, there may be a Torah-level issue of extracting blood because the blood itself is needed for the mitzvah. This resembles wounding for a therapeutic purpose, and one must analyze Rambam, chapter 8, concerning wounding where the blood is desired, as well as the discussion in Kesubos where the act is considered extracting even though the blood is not desired for its own sake but for determining a fact. This may remove the either-or basis for leniency on the second day of Yom Tov. Know also that brit milah overrides tzaraat, as ruled in Yoreh De'ah, siman 266. In a doubtful 7- or 8-month child the same either-or logic can apply because if he is an 8-month nonviable child the cutting is legally insignificant. It requires study whether those whose brit milah does not override Shabbos, such as one with 2 foreskins or an androgynos, nevertheless have tzaraat overridden for their brit milah. Taz in siman 588, seif katan 5, writes that where the Torah explicitly permits an act on Shabbos by the word 'day,' the Sages have no power to decree against it, for 'on the day' implies even Shabbos or Yom Tov. Otherwise one could have said that 'day' comes only to include Yom Tov. See there and Tosefet Shabbos. One who was born without a visible foreskin and is of doubtful 7- or 8-month status is not treated leniently on Shabbos according to Rama in Yoreh De'ah, siman 266, seif 11. One cannot create a double doubt in favor of leniency, because even if he is an 8-month child, the act of drawing covenantal blood is not automatically free of prohibition, while if he is a 7-month child perhaps there is no hidden foreskin. At minimum there remain rabbinic concerns rather than a true double doubt toward permission. Everything the Mechaber writes in seif 5 concerning cases whose brit milah does not override Shabbos applies likewise to Yom Tov. Shach in Yoreh De'ah, siman 262, letters 1 and 2, seeks to prove from Menachos 72a that one who performed brit milah before the eighth day must, after the fact, perform the mitzvah again, because otherwise why would brit milah override Shabbos? This proof requires consideration, for one who acts before the eighth day has certainly failed to fulfill the positive commandment 'on the eighth day,' whereas in the Menachos case the essential mitzvah was accomplished and only the preferred time element was lacking. Moreover, the Scriptural derivations concerning harvesting and brit milah are not identical. Brit milah is especially weighty, being associated with 13 covenants and having an explicit Scriptural permission. I was uncertain whether one who intentionally performed a brit milah not at its proper time on Yom Tov receives lashes. Shabbos 133a states that Yom Tov contains both a positive and a negative commandment and that a positive commandment does not override both. It is possible that the mitzvah nevertheless overrides the negative commandment itself, leaving only the positive commandment of Yom Tov, in which case there would be no liability for lashes and the person might remain qualified for testimony. The Yerushalmi at the end of Lulav HaGazul, and the discussions of the authorities concerning whether a positive commandment overrides the Shabbos prohibition in exceptional configurations, bear on this question. The matter has many ramifications and requires further study.
