Seif 1, one performs all the needs of milah on Shabbat, etc. NB: Regarding the question whether there should be only two mohalim or three, the Yeshuot Yaakov wrote in this siman that this is an error. But in a responsum that I wrote to the holy community of Barzan, which found its place in my work in the omissions to tractate Niddah, I discussed the reason for the matter at length, with the help of Heaven; see there on fol. 113 and examine carefully. Now, concerning the dispute of the Baal HaMaor and the Ramban, cited by the Ran in the chapter Rabbi Eliezer de-Milah, in the case where the hot water needed for the milah spilled, whether we follow the final consequence or the present moment, see my responsum to Lvov, which found its place in the second omissions in Hilkhot Shevuot, siman 239, where I discussed this at length with the help of Heaven; see there, and see in the Ran the proof they brought from Beitzah. There appears to be a kind of proof from the Gemara in Niddah 38, regarding the early pious ones who would not have relations from Wednesday onward lest they come to chillul Shabbat. Why do we not say that now he is acting permissibly, and afterward it will be a case of pikuach nefesh, which overrides Shabbat? Rather, since we know that afterward it may lead to chillul Shabbat, one may not initiate it. One cannot say that they were merely stringent upon themselves, for if we knew with certainty that this would happen and lead to chillul Shabbat, it would be forbidden by law; therefore, because they were pious, they were stringent even in a case of doubt. But if even with certainty it would be fully permitted, there would have been no reason to be stringent in a doubt. Thus it appears that where the later outcome is certain it is forbidden by law, and therefore they were stringent even where it was doubtful; examine carefully. As to the Razah's proof from slaughtering on Yom Tov, it would seem that the Ramban holds like those who say that pikuach nefesh is permitted in the case of a sick person, not merely overridden. If so, the matter works well: with kisuy hadam, after one has slaughtered he has no permission to cover except by force of a positive command overriding a prohibition, and since that is only by way of dechuyah, while he has not yet slaughtered and may not need to reach such overriding, he must avoid it. But with pikuach nefesh, the permissibility on Shabbat is a true heter, so we properly follow the present moment: he circumcises lawfully, and afterward it is pikuach nefesh that overrides Shabbat. The Razah holds that pikuach nefesh is only dechuyah for a sick person, and therefore he properly brings proof from kisuy on Yom Tov. This is not the place to lengthen further; see my novellae to chapter 5 of Yoma, in Mahadura Tinyana, fol. 55b, Tosafot s.v. "ha-sholeach," where I proved that Tosafot hold like the permissive view cited by the Ran, that we follow the present moment, and their words are compelled in the Gemara according to that opinion; see there and examine carefully. See also what I wrote in a responsum to Yoreh De'ah siman 373 concerning tumat kohen; see above siman 244, and in siman 248 regarding setting sail, where it is likewise evident that we follow the present moment. In my work on Avodah Zarah, Avodat Avodah, fol. 10b, I also wrote a proof from Tosafot there for the permissive view that we follow the present moment; see there. One who studies the Ran there will see that the Ran holds like those who do not follow the present moment, but rather the final outcome; he only distinguishes between milah, which is fixed on Shabbat, and slaughtering on Yom Tov. At first glance this is difficult, because his words appear to contradict what he wrote in Nedarim 16 concerning substitutes for issurei hana'ah, where a debtor and creditors may come and collect. There it implies that initially it is permitted to borrow, for there is no benefit now, and when the creditor later comes and collects, that is already after the fact and permitted bediavad. Thus it appears that we do not follow the final outcome but the present moment: since now there is no prohibition, it is permitted to act, even though we know that afterward the prohibited result will occur, because later it will be in a permitted framework, as bediavad. Therefore the Ran's words would seem contradictory. One must distinguish between Torah and rabbinic law: in Nedarim he deals only with a rabbinic prohibition, as the Ran writes that the prohibition of exchange in that case is only rabbinic, and for rabbinic law we follow the present moment. But in Shabbat, where a Torah prohibition is involved, and likewise in slaughtering on Yom Tov where a Torah melakhah may be needed, we follow the final outcome. Since this is a broad rule in many places, I recorded it here. Perhaps God will grant me another opportunity to explain it further. See the Taz in Yoreh De'ah siman 117 concerning the slaughter of goats, where it is also evident that he holds like those who follow the present moment; he did not address all the above points or the dispute cited by the Ran. According to what I wrote above, his words may be sustained according to those who hold that the prohibition of commerce in neveilot is only rabbinic, and there is proof for his position from the Ran cited above; see what I wrote there in my novellae to Yoreh De'ah, and examine carefully.
Now, what the Shulchan Aruch wrote, that one performs all the needs of milah on Shabbat. NB: These are the words of the Rambam in chapter 2 of Hilkhot Shegagot, halakhah 8: "And similarly, one who had two infants, one to circumcise on Shabbat and one to circumcise on Friday or on Sunday, and he forgot and circumcised both on Shabbat, is exempt from a chatat, for he had permission to circumcise one of them on Shabbat, and Shabbat is dechuyah for him, and it is a positive mitzvah, even though they are two separate bodies, since its time was pressing," end quote. The Rambam's lengthened wording, "Shabbat is dechuyah for him," requires explanation. Since he had already written that he had permission to circumcise one of them on Shabbat, it would have been enough to say "and it is a positive mitzvah"; why add that Shabbat is dechuyah for him? Also the word "for him" is extra; he could have said simply that Shabbat is dechuyah. It appears to me that his intent is as follows. Regarding tumah we rule that tumah is dechuyah in the case of the public, and likewise we rule that Shabbat is dechuyah for a sick person, as the Rambam wrote in Hilkhot Shabbat, but with communal offerings on Shabbat, Shabbat is hutrah, as stated in Yoma 46. One must therefore examine milah on Shabbat: is it considered dechuyah or hutrah? From the Rambam's language here it appears that Shabbat is dechuyah in the case of milah, for he wrote "Shabbat is dechuyah for him," and did not write that it is permitted or hutrah for him. It is true that he may be adopting the language of the Gemara, which says that Shabbat was given to be overridden for him, but even in the Gemara itself one can infer this, and it is still more evident from the Rambam's wording. The phrase "for him" implies that he is speaking about the very prohibition of Shabbat, that it is dechuyah, like the Gemara's formulation that tumah is dechuyah in the public. The correct reason is that anything which could be fulfilled on a weekday and has no necessary attachment to Shabbat, but only happens to occur on Shabbat, is only dechuyah and not hutrah. Tumah might not have occurred, and the sick person might not have been dangerously ill on Shabbat; therefore, if tumah or illness occurs on Shabbat, it is only dechuyah. But communal offerings include offerings that belong specifically to Shabbat, or a daily obligation that necessarily includes Shabbat because "tamid" is written, and there it is hutrah. Accordingly, since milah might not have occurred on Shabbat, it is only dechuyah. The author's son noted that our master thereby intended the view of the holy Ramban in Torat HaAdam; see also the work Etz HaChaim of the gaon of Leipnik, Orach Chayyim, where he discusses this. With this the Rambam's wording works well, for in Zevachim 32 Rava concludes that we say "since it was pushed aside, it remains pushed aside," but we do not say "since it became permitted, it remains permitted." Here too, although "since it was pushed aside" is said only for one act and not two acts, that is lechatchilah; but regarding bediavad, and especially where he erred in a mitzvah matter and performed a mitzvah, we properly say that since Shabbat was pushed aside for this prohibition, he is exempt from a chatat. There is something like a proof from Pesachim 98b, regarding those impure by a corpse who forced their way into the Temple courtyard, where it implies that even though lechatchilah it does not help to say "since it was pushed aside" in two places, nevertheless bediavad they are exempt; the same may be said here. This resolves the Lechem Mishneh's difficulty, where he asked that the Rambam's words contradict his rulings regarding offerings. According to what I have written, offerings are different: since offerings are hutrah for the public, and "since it became permitted, it remains permitted" is not said even for one act, all the more not for two acts; therefore he is not exempt on account of having erred in a mitzvah and performed a mitzvah. But with milah, since Shabbat is dechuyah for him, if it were one act it would be permitted even lechatchilah to say "since it was pushed aside, it remains pushed aside." Although these are two acts and we do not say this lechatchilah, it does help bediavad to exempt him from a chatat. The Gemara's challenge from infants to sacrificial limbs, and its answer in another way, follows the view that tumah is hutrah in the public, and according to that view Shabbat too is hutrah, so there is no distinction between milah and offerings; or it follows Abaye in Zevachim 32, who holds that "since it became permitted, it remains permitted" is stronger, and therefore the Gemara challenges by a kal vachomer from sacrificial limbs, forcing a distinction between one who first slaughtered and then circumcised, etc. But for us, where we rule that tumah is dechuyah in the public and Shabbat is likewise dechuyah for a sick person, and consequently milah too is dechuyah, milah is not at all comparable to sacrificial limbs. We also rule like Rava, that we say "since it was pushed aside, it remains pushed aside," but not "since it became permitted, it remains permitted"; therefore the matters are not comparable and the Lechem Mishneh's question is resolved. Thus the Rambam was precise: there are two grounds for leniency, that he had permission to circumcise on Shabbat, and also that Shabbat is dechuyah for him and not hutrah, and also that he performed a positive mitzvah; therefore he is exempt in every case. Examine this well. This appears to me to be the correct intent of the Rambam for now. Perhaps God will grant me another opportunity to clarify it further, and my prayer is fluent in my mouth that our words should be accepted before the Master of all, and that my toil not be in vain, Heaven forbid. Amen.
