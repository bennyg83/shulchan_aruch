[5] If he did not bring the circumcision knife, etc. Olat Shabbos asks why the child should not be brought to the knife, since this would involve no Shabbos prohibition at all because a living person carries himself. There is no difficulty, because regarding a small child who cannot walk we do not apply the principle that a living person carries himself, as Olat Shabbos himself writes in siman 308, seif 41. Regarding moving the knife after circumcision, the Taz in Yoreh Deah siman 266 and above in siman 309 writes at length that it is prohibited, except that one may place it away in the same room so it will not be lost. The Sages permitted the end because of the beginning, lest the mohel refrain from circumcising. The Magen Avraham permits carrying it wherever one wishes as long as it is still in his hand. Once he has put it down, however, it may no longer be moved. According to this, when there is only one mohel and he necessarily must put down the knife in order to perform periah, he may not move it afterward. It is therefore preferable to hand it to another person. A similar rule appears in the Yerushalmi in Perek Lulav HaGazul, where it is permitted afterward to take the lulav from another person's hand and move it. It also appears to me that one may be lenient when there is concern that the knife will be lost. Then, once he has taken it back into his hand, we permit him even to carry it home, as above in siman 308, seif 2, regarding other muktzeh. Moreover, the Rama in Yoreh Deah implies that in all circumstances it may be moved because it was not set aside at twilight, since it was certainly needed that Shabbos. The proofs brought by the Taz and Magen Avraham from a pestle and roasting spit can be rejected, because a circumcision knife is different: it was prepared to be moved and it was known from the previous day that it would certainly be needed. Examine carefully. Nekudot HaKesef there likewise appears lenient in all circumstances. See siman 653. Washing a circumcision knife is prohibited, as stated by Malbushei Yom Tov.
[6] One tells a non-Jew, etc. The Magen Avraham writes to see siman 276, seif 2, where some authorities are lenient even regarding a Torah prohibition. It appears to him that for circumcision one may rely on them if there is no other possibility. This is difficult, because there he writes that the leniency applies specifically when one's body directly benefits from the act, which is not the case here. Nevertheless, I already challenged that distinction there. Even so, it was explained there that we do not rule according to the lenient opinion. The Magen Avraham writes that it is obvious that one may not bring the infant to the synagogue through a courtyard without an eruv even by means of a non-Jew, because the circumcision can be performed at home. Indeed, circumcisions are often performed at home when it is cold. One must say that he is discussing a case in which the circumcision knife is in the infant's home. It appears to me that in places where the custom is lenient, they rely on siman 325, seif 10, which permits instructing a non-Jew to bring beer because one could walk there oneself. Certainly this should apply here, since human dignity is significant and circumcising in the synagogue is honorable. One cannot compare a situation where something is possible to one where it is impossible. The Taz, siman 349, writes that when carrying an infant for circumcision, non-Jews should take the infant out of the house and likewise bring him into the synagogue. Only in the street should Jews pass him from one to another in increments of less than four amot.
