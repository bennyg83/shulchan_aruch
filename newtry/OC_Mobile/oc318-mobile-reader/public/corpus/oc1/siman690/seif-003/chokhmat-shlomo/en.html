Section 3. "He must read all of it, etc." Note: We learn in the Gemara of Megillah, page 18: Rav Chelbo said, etc., the halachah follows the one who says that one must read all of it; and even according to the one who says from "There was a Jewish man," it must be written in its entirety. This is difficult for me: why did it not teach us a greater novelty, that even according to the one who says from "After these things," it must be written in its entirety? For in the mishnah there are three opinions there: the first tanna holds all of it, Rabbi Yehudah holds from "There was a Jewish man," and Rabbi Yose holds from "After these things." If so, if it wanted to teach us a novelty, it should have taught us the novelty that even according to the one who says from "After these things," it must be written in its entirety. One cannot say that according to him, since he is so lenient that it is enough from "After these things," we do not require it to be written in its entirety. For if so, when the Gemara challenges above on the previous page regarding one who was writing it, expounding it, and correcting it, etc., asking, what are the circumstances, etc., and answering that it is where he wrote verse by verse and read it, and then asking, did he fulfill? But Rav Chelbo said, etc., and even according to the one who says from "There was a Jewish man," it must be written in its entirety. What is the challenge? Let it say that it follows Rabbi Yose, who holds from "After these things," and therefore according to him we do not require it to be written in its entirety; thus it is possible to find a case where he was writing it verse by verse and teaching it, and what is the challenge? Rather, certainly according to everyone we require it to be written in its entirety. If so, why did it not teach us that even according to Rabbi Yose, who holds from "After these things," we require it to be written in its entirety? This requires analysis. However, one can say that it teaches us that even if only until "There was a Jewish man" is missing, it is invalid, and all the more so according to the one who says from "After these things," where much is missing, it is invalid. On the contrary, for that opinion this is an even greater novelty. See the Ran and Shulchan Aruch, understand this, and analyze it carefully. From this there will be proof for the opinion of the Ran, that if one omitted a complete matter, even after the fact it is invalid. Therefore one can say well that Rav Chelbo teaches us regarding after the fact, that even after the fact it must be written in its entirety. Therefore it is correct to mention the one who says from "There was a Jewish man," to teach that even if it was missing until "There was a Jewish man," it is invalid. Therefore it did not mention the one who says until "After these things," because in that case it is more obvious that it is invalid if so much is missing. But if we say like the opinion of the Rashba, that after the fact it is valid and Rav Chelbo teaches only the initial law, then regarding the initial law even if one verse is missing it is forbidden initially. Perforce, what he mentioned, even according to the one who says from "There was a Jewish man," means regarding where it is missing until "There was a Jewish man," for even if one verse is missing it is forbidden initially. Perforce, the intent is only that although he is lenient regarding the reading, nevertheless regarding the writing it must be written in its entirety, even without the absence of one verse. If so, the difficulty is strong: it should have taught us a greater novelty, that even according to the one who says from "After these things," it must be written in its entirety, even without the absence of one verse. Why did it not teach us the greater novelty? Perforce, this proves the Ran's opinion as practical law, and according to the Rashba this requires analysis. Analyze this well.
