Reads. See Taz, for the language of the Mechaber is the language of the Rambam, of blessed memory, and in the Tur it is not written this way. See Be'er HaGolah, os 8. The custom is to spread it out entirely before he reads. In Eliyah Rabbah, os 12, he writes to spread it sheet over sheet. It appears that if it is impossible to spread it out completely on the table, spreading sheet over sheet is preferable, like a letter spread out entirely. Eliyah Rabbah wrote further in the name of Shiyarei Keneses HaGedolah that one should be careful that it not drag on the ground, and Pri Chadash wrote similarly. It appears that this is not specifically on the ground; the same applies if it hangs over the table or lectern, for this is also a disgrace to sacred writings, as in siman 323 in the law of one who reads in a book and it rolled over the wall. It appears that he should spread it out before the blessings, so that the rolling should not be an interruption between the blessing and the reading. With a Torah scroll too, he spreads it, sees, and blesses. But the final blessing does not apply to the megillah itself, and with Hashem's help I will explain further in Magen Avraham. What the Mechaber wrote in section 14, that the reader and listener must have intent, is because mitzvos require intent. See Maggid Mishneh, chapter 2 of Megillah, who wrote that perhaps here everyone agrees. See Beis Yosef in 589 regarding shofar, that where he performs an action one can say that intent is not needed, but for reading, intent is needed. However, in Megillah, chapter "one who reads," it challenges from Shema, "if he directed his heart"; see Lechem Mishneh on this. See Magen Avraham in siman 60, os 3, in the name of Radbaz, that regarding a rabbinic mitzvah one can say intent is not needed. One can say that megillah is words of tradition and is like Torah law; see Rosh Hashanah 28 and Pri Chadash here. See Magen Avraham there, os 4, who wrote that the entire passage requires intent because mitzvos require intent. And see Magen Avraham, siman 63, os 8, that the first verse is Torah law and the rest of the passage is rabbinic. If so, it is very simple that for the rest of the passage, which is rabbinic, if he had no intent at all he has fulfilled, since it is rabbinic. However, whenever he intended for something else or intended not to fulfill, it is obvious that even for a rabbinic obligation he has not fulfilled, like that case where he began with wine and concluded with beer, while in the case of matzah, he derives benefit. See Beis Yosef 475 and 589, and above in the laws of Shema and in other places I discussed this at length.
In our novellae. At the beginning of the chapter of one who reads out of order, we wrote that he need not go back from the first verse; rather, he reads verse 2, then 3, then 4. The same applies if he skipped a word, for example, "And it was Achashverosh in the days of"; he need not begin from "And it was," but begins from "in the days of Achashverosh." See there, and this requires analysis.
