And Rav Amram. See Taz. According to Beit Yosef and Shulchan Aruch, there are four explanations of the baraisa. The first is Rashi's explanation, based upon a decree lest some sewing thread remain and later be used as a tzitzis string. According to Rashi, the phrase “all agree” is unrelated to the dispute here, and the Gemara cites the additional teaching only to establish that the halachah follows Rabbi Meir in his decrees, apparently even against the majority; see Beit Yosef. Earlier, on Menachos 40b, the Gemara says that linen with tzitzis is forbidden D'Rabbanan lest the garment tear within three fingerbreadths. Rashi explains that some sewing thread may remain. According to this, the passage must follow the Sages rather than Rabbi Meir, because Rabbi Meir forbids sewing within three fingerbreadths and there would be no reason for such a decree. The Sages allow the sewing and did not decree regarding a passive failure to fulfill a positive commandment. The Sages did not create a safeguard for passive neglect of a positive commandment, as I have written many times. Regarding kilayim, however, they did make a safeguard lest sewing thread remain. The second explanation is Tosafos on Menachos 40b, s.v. “Perhaps.” They challenge Rashi: Rav Huna later, on 41b, requires that the hole be four fingerbreadths distant from the edge. How, then, would he explain the baraisa's distinction between outside three and within three fingerbreadths? Tosafos therefore reject Rashi's understanding that the measurements refer to the width of the garment. They explain that the baraisa refers to a tear along the length of the garment. Length and width are explained in Magen Avraham, siman 11, seif katan 16. If a strip containing tzitzis tore from the garment, but the tear did not approach within three fingerbreadths of the opposite edge that remains attached, Rabbi Meir holds that the remaining connection is still considered joined and the tzitzis that were present at the time of the tear do not become invalid. If less than three fingerbreadths of unbroken fabric remain along the length, the strip is considered detached, and the tzitzis present then become invalid. Even if it is afterward sewn, they remain invalid because of “you shall make,” and not something already made. According to the Sages, even a slight connection is considered joined, and the tzitzis do not become invalid. The text of the additional teaching would then have to be reversed, with Rabbi Meir permitting sewing and the Sages forbidding, otherwise the principle that the halachah follows Rabbi Meir in his decrees would not apply; see Beit Yosef. The phrase “all agree” is then well understood: even if one brings a piece measuring one cubit by one cubit from elsewhere with techeiles attached, it is invalid because of “you shall make,” and not something already made. The same could have been taught regarding a strip that was entirely severed and afterward sewn: the tzitzis present at the time of severance are invalid. The earlier statement on Menachos 40b, “lest his linen garment tear within three,” would apparently follow the Sages who say it may not be sewn. Otherwise the additional teaching is difficult, as Beit Yosef asks. Tosafos there, however, s.v. “Perhaps,” write, “Now our passage follows Rabbi Meir,” implying that their text reads that Rabbi Meir says it may not be sewn. This is difficult, though it may be resolved elsewhere. The third explanation is Rosh's interpretation of Rav Amram, as understood by Beit Yosef. It does not refer to the length of the garment as Tosafos maintain, but to its width, possibly considering both length and width, though not in Tosafos' manner. Like Rashi, it treats within or outside three fingerbreadths as the required corner area. If the tear occurred within three fingerbreadths, the detached piece lacks the minimum corner size. Even if only a minority of the garment tore and most remains connected, sewing afterward does not help, because it is treated as severed. Under this interpretation, the phrase “all agree” has no obvious relation to the dispute, which concerns whether the piece is considered severed, while the one-cubit case is invalid because of “you shall make,” and not something already made, either because it lacks the minimum tallis size or as Taz explains in seif katan 3. According to this view, whether the entire strip was severed or only a minority tore while most remained connected makes no difference. Everything depends upon the width: whether the piece still has the status of a corner. Taz raises a powerful objection. The passage on 40b says, “lest his garment tear within three,” implying a concern of “you shall make,” and not something already made. Yet according to this explanation there is no ordinary issue of something already made. The piece is simply treated as severed, and even tzitzis attached only after sewing would be invalid. One could force an answer by saying that he might correct it by placing an interior patch wider than three fingerbreadths, so that it would no longer be treated as severed, but the matter remains difficult. The fourth explanation is Nimukei Yosef's understanding of Rav Amram. His mention of the corner indicates that he does not follow Tosafos. He nevertheless maintains that only the tzitzis present when the tear occurred are invalid. If the piece is later sewn and new tzitzis are attached, they are valid. The phrase “all agree” is then readily understood: a one-cubit piece brought from elsewhere with tzitzis attached is invalid because of “you shall make,” and not something already made. Taz objects to Rosh's explanation of Rav Amram as understood by Beit Yosef. If sewing cannot help at all, why does the baraisa specify “with techeiles attached”? It should be forbidden even to attach new tzitzis after sewing, because the piece is considered severed. The phrase “even a piece measuring one cubit by one cubit with techeiles attached” implies that without existing techeiles, one may attach tzitzis after sewing even where the piece is smaller than one cubit and smaller than three fingerbreadths. Yet under Beit Yosef's understanding it should remain treated as severed.
In conclusion. Our master Taz found two difficulties in the sacred words of the master who sustains us, Beit Yosef. First, Beit Yosef was lenient where an entire strip tore away, provided that the strip was wider than three fingerbreadths, permitting it to be sewn back with the tzitzis still attached. According to Tosafos this is certainly forbidden. There is no distinction whether the corner came from this garment or another garment; see Magen Avraham, seif katan 3. Rosh may likewise be interpreted straightforwardly in accordance with Tosafos' understanding of Rav Amram. Nimukei Yosef too may be interpreted naturally like Rav Amram in Tosafos. His use of the word “corner” means that if three fingerbreadths remained attached, it still retained the status of a corner of this tallis. Second, Beit Yosef was stringent and held that sewing does not help. Taz says this is incorrect. Sewing helps in every case. When Rosh writes that one may not attach tzitzis at that time, and that all agree one may not bring a one-cubit piece, see Magen Avraham, his wording is not exact. The same law applies to that very strip if it was entirely severed: one may not reattach it with its existing tzitzis, even if it contains the minimum tallis size, according to Taz's view in letter 3, where he questions Darkei Moshe in siman 10. In my humble opinion, we have removed all the pegs from these interpretive paths, and Taz's words shine clearly, as I have written.
