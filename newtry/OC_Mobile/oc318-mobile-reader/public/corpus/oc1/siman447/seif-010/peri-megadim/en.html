[10] But. See Taz. What he wrote, "as written in Shulchan Aruch, se'if 8 below," is no proof for here, because there it is known that it was not used that day, and it speaks of chametz. It appears that he intended Yoreh De'ah 123:5 (the printed 8 is an error), where the Mechaber ruled that even with a Jew, ordinary utensils are presumed not to have been used that day; nevertheless on Pesach we penalize him. It implies that others we do not penalize. For example, if one asked his fellow to make him fat for Pesach for free, and he does not know whether he used a chametz utensil, etc. Regarding what the Rav wrote, that for others one need not be stringent in the smallest amount, although it is not a case of two smallest-amount prohibitions, see Taz 467 and Chok Yaakov. At first glance, an ordinary utensil not used that day is a double doubt: perhaps it was not used today for prohibition, and perhaps it imparts an impaired taste. But with chametz, there are those who say that it reawakens. If so, although we are lenient about a rabbinic smallest-amount prohibition that it does not reawaken, nevertheless it does not leave the realm of doubt; and impaired taste too, one can say, reawakens. If so, the Rav properly wrote that by law it is forbidden. However, there is no logic to combine this toward prohibition, and with Hashem's help it will be explained further in Magen Avraham. See Chok Yaakov 57, that it is permitted for benefit, and this is obvious. The same applies that one may keep it. It is better to sell it on Pesach to a non-Jew, because by keeping it every moment there is concern lest he come to eat it. See Magen Avraham 25, which implies that even if he certainly cooked it in a utensil used that day, it does not forbid another food, and it is also permitted to keep and benefit from it, for it is secondary flavor of permitted matter. But if he cooked it on the eve of Pesach until night in a utensil used that day, see siman 442, and with Hashem's help it will be explained further in Magen Avraham.
