(39) That were salted before Pesach. This applies even when they were salted in a chametz utensil, because salting does not cause a utensil to release absorbed taste. Furthermore, an ordinary utensil is assumed not to have been used that day, and before Pesach all agree that detrimental taste is permitted. For the same reason, even if these foods remained immersed in the utensil for twenty four hours, they are not prohibited. Although prolonged soaking is treated like cooking, the absorbed taste is detrimental. Even if the utensil had been used that day, there is room to be lenient after the fact, because the food acquires the taste only after twenty four hours, by which time the taste absorbed in the utensil has already become detrimental.
(40) And they were not careful with them. They did not inspect the salt to ensure that it contained no crumbs of chametz.
(41) They may be eaten. Even if the salt contained some chametz and the foods absorbed from it, the chametz was nullified before Pesach and does not become significant again.
(42) On Pesach. If they were resting in a chametz utensil in brine produced by the salt, some say that even a short period, long enough for the brine to be placed on a fire and begin to boil, gives it the law of prolonged soaking. Therefore, if the utensil had been used that day for chametz, or the food was heavily salted in the manner used for meat intended for long storage, whatever was within the brine is prohibited. Food above the brine is not included in this rule. Others say that it is prohibited only if it remained in the brine for twenty four hours and was also heavily salted. Where loss is involved, one may rely on the lenient view when the soaking occurred before Pesach.
(43) In water on Pesach. Before Pesach there is no need to be stringent, even initially and even if the utensil had been used that day for chametz, since the water is cold. Even according to those who hold in the preceding seif katan that brine created by salting is treated like prolonged soaking after only a short time, here the food is immersed in water, and the water weakens the strength of the brine. During Pesach itself, however, when even a minute amount is prohibited, one is stringent even after a short soaking, since the sharpness of the salt causes at least a minute amount to be absorbed. Peri Megadim. Magen Avraham inclines to say that salted fish known as herring have exceptionally strong brine that is not weakened by the water. Therefore, even if they soaked before Pesach in a chametz utensil, one should initially refrain from eating them.
(44) One should be stringent. This refers to heavily salted fish known as herring, even when the utensil had not been used that day for chametz. This stringency applies only initially.
(45) From what the utensils release. This implies that if they soaked in a Yom Tov utensil they are permitted, and there is no concern for a crumb of chametz in the salt.
(46) Some disagree and are stringent. This refers back to the beginning of the seif. Their reason is that although the chametz was nullified before Pesach, it becomes significant again on Pesach.
(47) Not to eat cheeses, etc. The reason is that people were not careful to inspect the salt for chametz when the cheeses were made, even if they were pickled in a utensil reserved for Yom Tov.
(48) And dried meat. The same applies to newly salted meat of this kind and to other salted or pickled foods, such as cucumbers, cabbage, and lemons, if the salt was not inspected for chametz when they were prepared. The custom is not to eat them except on the last day of Pesach.
(49) He rinsed it. This means rubbing it by hand and removing anything attached to it. The later authorities write that the custom is also to soak it briefly in a river or in a Pesach utensil with cold water changed three times, but not initially in a chametz utensil.
(50) The meat. This includes dried meat and even newly salted meat that contains much loose salt, since these are normally rinsed, as well as dried fish. Moist salted fish, however, may not be eaten even if one wishes to rinse them, because they are not always normally rinsed and we are concerned that one may forget. For this reason, one might have permitted salmon, which is normally rinsed, and salted fish brought in barrels, known as herring, which are also normally rinsed. Nevertheless, the later authorities write that the custom is not to permit any salted fish during Pesach through rinsing, except on the final day of Pesach. In pressing circumstances or where Yom Tov joy would otherwise be diminished, one may be lenient with these. They further write that pickled foods such as cucumbers, lemons, and similar items cannot be permitted through rinsing, since they are not normally rinsed.
(51) Before Pesach. During Pesach it should not be soaked. After the fact, it should be rinsed even during Pesach.
(52) And intestines. This applies even when they are dry and normally rinsed. Since they are filled internally with finely chopped meat and the salt used upon them was not inspected, rinsing the outside does not help what is inside. This applies even if no fat was placed inside. Likewise, rinsing the outside of cheese does not help, because there may be a crumb of chametz within it.
(53) The dried sausages. This refers to the intestines called tabachia. The later authorities write that if dried meat hung in a room where flour was sifted, rinsing does not help. On the contrary, the rinsing causes the flour to adhere and form very fine particles of dough that cling to the meat. One must therefore take care not to sift Pesach flour near foods, beverages, or Pesach utensils where the dust can reach them.
(54) After the fact one should not be stringent. All the matters in the Rama’s gloss that are subject to a stringent custom apply only initially. If they were already cooked, even without rinsing or soaking, and cannot be kept until after Pesach, they should not be discarded and may be eaten on Pesach. All the more so, they do not prohibit a mixture even when there is less than sixty times against them, since the concern is only a doubt about a minute rabbinic amount. If the sausage contains garlic or another sharp substance, however, it prohibits even a mixture on Pesach in any amount, like actual chametz, because it was cut with a chametz knife. This applies even when the knife was wiped clean and had not been used that day. Some permit, in a case of substantial loss, a mixture containing less than sixty times the sausage if the knife had been wiped clean and had not been used that day. This applies to garlic and onion within the filling. Garlic that is finely chopped and mixed into the salt used for drying meat, so that its aroma enters the meat, does not prohibit the meat. The meat may be eaten on Pesach after soaking and rinsing like other dried meat, provided it was rinsed before Pesach. During Pesach, rinsing does not suffice; the outer layer must be removed. Shaarei Teshuvah.
(55) It is forbidden according to the strict law. It is forbidden for eating, but permitted for benefit.
(56) Unless they were careful. Meaning, it is permitted only if they were careful regarding both of the matters that follow.
(57) At the time it was made, from chametz. We are concerned that crumbs of chametz became mixed into it and remain physically present in the fat. Even if he wishes to strain the fat through a thick cloth before Pesach, it remains prohibited because the pan may have been used that day for chametz.
(58) That the utensils had been used that day. Because of the stringency of chametz, we do not rely on the ordinary presumption that utensils had not been used that day. Even if he now says with certainty that the utensil had not been used that day for chametz and that no crumb of chametz entered the fat, it remains prohibited, since when he rendered the fat he did not have Pesach in mind, and a person does not reliably remember a matter to which he gave no attention. If, however, he cooked it from the outset for Pesach, but the utensil had not been used that day, it may be eaten on Pesach, provided that he poured it into a Pesach utensil before Pesach. Nevertheless, one should not initially render fat in a chametz utensil even when it is known not to have been used that day.
(59) And likewise any item, etc. Meaning, even if it was all prepared before Pesach, it follows the same law stated above regarding fat.
(60) Or preserves and the like. The Taz implies that preserves are tart and sharp, and therefore are prohibited even when it is known that the utensil had not been used that day, since their sharpness improves the absorbed taste. According to him they are prohibited even on the final day of Yom Tov. Several later authorities disagree. Even when the preserve is made from sharp foods such as radish, frying them in honey neutralizes their sharpness. The later authorities write that if people in that place ordinarily cook preserves in utensils set aside for that purpose, using a spoon set aside for stirring them, this is equivalent to certainty that the utensils are clean. They also write that if, when rendering a substance, one intended for some reason to use only a new utensil, this helps even if he did not render it specifically for Pesach.
(61) But on the final day of Yom Tov, etc. Since the concern is rabbinic, we rely upon the ordinary presumption that utensils had not been used that day, as with other prohibitions, and we are also not concerned for crumbs. The later authorities write that if it is known that the utensils had been used that day, one may not be lenient even on the final day of Yom Tov.
(62) One may be lenient. This applies when everything was prepared before Pesach. If it was prepared during Pesach, even when it is known that the utensil had not been used that day, it may not be eaten even on the final day of Yom Tov, as is evident from siman 467 seif 10 in the Taz and Chok Yaakov.
(63) In it. For this reason some permit drinking ordinary mead produced throughout the year. If there is reason to suspect that it was fermented with beer yeast, it is prohibited. See above siman 442, seif katan 25. This concern applies only where beer yeast is ordinarily used. If it was made in kettles used for distilling liquor, it is prohibited in every case, even if other foods were cooked in them several times in the interim. See below siman 451 in Mishnah Berurah seif katan 120 regarding how such a utensil may be kashered for Pesach, and in seif 22 of Biur Halacha, s.v. uveis charoses.
(64) A minute amount of these substances. Several later authorities agree that where substantial loss is involved one may be lenient even if a large quantity became mixed in and there is not sixty times against it. Some are lenient regarding all substances for which there is no concern about actual crumbs, meaning everything except oil, even where substantial loss is not involved.
(65) One should not be stringent to prohibit the mixture. First, some poskim hold that taste released from utensils is nat bar nat, since the chametz imparted taste to the utensil and the utensil imparted taste to the fat or wine, and is permitted before Pesach. Second, it is uncertain whether the utensil had been used that day, and before Pesach all agree that detrimental taste is permitted. These considerations may be relied upon at least not to prohibit a mixture. If it is known that the utensil used for cooking had been used that day for chametz, the later authorities imply that the mixture is prohibited, since the nat bar nat consideration alone is insufficient. Several poskim hold that nat bar nat is prohibited regarding chametz even before Pesach.
(66) Some are stringent regarding using vinegar, etc. Meaning, even if the vinegar was made in a new utensil and was drawn from it with a Pesach utensil, it is nevertheless prohibited.
(67) From which people draw throughout the year. This excludes vinegar kept in a storage cellar, which is permitted.
(68) It contains crumbs of bread. If it was strained before Pesach, it is permitted.
(69) Wine vinegar is not readily available, etc. Therefore, even if people drew from it throughout the year, it is permitted, since other vinegar cannot easily be obtained.
(70) Not to fill, etc. When filling a barrel with wine for Pesach, one should not fill it by means of a chametz utensil, even though ordinary utensils are clean and contain no visible residue, but only absorbed taste. Absorbed taste is not released through cold liquid.
(71) One who is lenient has not lost. If the utensil was scoured and rinsed, all agree that it is permitted, unless the vinegar remained in it for twenty four hours. Even vinegar requires twenty four hours to be considered prolonged soaking, and then it is prohibited even if the utensil had not been used that day, because its sharpness improves the taste. In places where sugar is mixed into wine vinegar, it should not be used on Pesach unless it was strained before Pesach. Where substantial loss is involved, it is permitted after the fact even if it was not strained.
(72) On Pesach. The taste imparted before Pesach was already nullified in sixty, but the wine is forbidden because taste is imparted again during Pesach, when even a minute amount is prohibited. Although this dough is chametz nuksheh, and several poskim hold that chametz nuksheh is nullified in sixty even during Pesach, as stated above at the end of seif katan 14, this case is more severe. Since he places the wine in the barrel intending to leave it there during Pesach, it is like intentionally nullifying a prohibition.
(73) And it is forbidden to drink it. It may nevertheless be retained, since thirty days have passed.
(74) If it was placed there before that. Meaning, the dough used for sealing was applied two months earlier. The Taz rules that thirty days suffice, but Magen Avraham and Chok Yaakov follow the implication of the Rama, that two months are required for the wine to be permitted for drinking.
(75) However, if there is a kezayis, etc. It is reasonable that this applies even when it was placed there more than two months earlier.
(76) In one place. Meaning, the strand of dough is unbroken and, if it were still moist, could be removed as one piece.
(77) He must destroy it. Unless he coated it with clay.
