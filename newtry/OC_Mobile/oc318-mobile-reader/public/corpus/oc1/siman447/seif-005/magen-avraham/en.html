If it remained in the utensil for twenty four hours, it may be considered soaked and therefore cooked, as stated in Yoreh Deah, siman 105. This resolves the ruling of Rav Hai in siman 451. Perhaps, since chametz before its time is still permitted, the rule that soaking is like cooking does not apply. This is also implied by Tur, who writes that cheeses do not expel absorbed flavor. One may reject the proof, because soaking applies only to a liquid food, not to something dry and hard like cheese. It therefore appears that regarding a liquid that remained in the utensil for twenty four hours, we apply the rule that soaking is like cooking. Siman 451, seif 21, concerning beer barrels, appears to imply that there is no concern. See what I wrote there. Bach writes that dried meat may be permitted even when it was salted in a utensil that was ben yomo and remained there for twenty four hours, because once the meat dries, the absorbed flavor is nullified, as stated at the end of seif 5 concerning dough. The custom is to soak it in a river or in a Pesach utensil with cold water, but not initially in a chametz utensil. Salted meat is likewise soaked. One must know, however, that it was not salted in a chametz utensil, meaning one in which hot chametz had been used within twenty four hours, because soaking is like cooking. Know that only the portion lying within the brine is considered soaked, as stated in siman 467, seif 14. Taz in Yoreh Deah, siman 83, Derishah in siman 69, and Be’er Sheva in chapter Kol HaBasar likewise write that since salt does not expel everything absorbed in a utensil, this resembles a dry mixture. Soaking applies only to a clear liquid. Shach, siman 69, writes that even if it remained there twenty four hours, it is permitted, because it becomes considered soaked only after twenty four hours, by which time the utensil is no longer ben yomo. Taz, siman 105, writes similarly. Below, however, in seif 8, it appears that if the pot was ben yomo, the food is forbidden because of soaking. One must therefore say that the deterioration and absorption occur simultaneously. Rabbi Moshe writes similarly in parashas Tzav concerning absorption of nosar. I also found this in Rama’s glosses to Issur VeHeter. Accordingly, it is forbidden only if the food was placed into the utensil immediately after the chametz was removed. Otherwise, the deterioration began before the new absorption. See Yoreh Deah, siman 96, Shach, seif katan 116. Regarding salting, one should be stringent, because if it remained in brine for the time required to boil, the brine draws flavor from the utensil, unlike Shach.

That they are ben yomo. Because of the stringency of chametz, we do not apply the presumption that ordinary utensils are not ben yomo. Even if one says with certainty that it was not ben yomo, it is forbidden, because people do not retain awareness of matters not imposed upon them. Bach and Darkei Moshe imply this. It follows that if it was originally cooked for Pesach in a utensil that was not ben yomo, it may be eaten during Pesach. Although detrimental flavor is initially forbidden because it could have been eaten before Pesach, this is considered an after-the-fact case. See above, seif 2; siman 451, seif 21; Yoreh Deah, siman 94, end of seif 5; and siman 95, seif 2, in the gloss. Yom Tov writes that Hagahos Maimoniyos was stringent specifically with a frying pan, because it is regularly used in that manner throughout the year. Regarding other utensils, even for chametz, ordinary utensils are presumed not to be ben yomo. Knesses HaGedolah. This does not appear correct to me. Tur, Yoreh Deah, siman 122, explains that ordinary utensils are presumed not ben yomo because of a double doubt: perhaps the utensil was not used that day, and even if it was used, perhaps it was used with something that imparts a detrimental flavor. That reasoning applies to other prohibitions. Regarding chametz, however, most uses of pots are with substances that do not impair fat. There is therefore only one doubt, and the food is forbidden. Rashba, however, explains the double doubt differently: perhaps the utensil was not used, and even if it was used, perhaps it was used with permitted food. According to that explanation, the same reasoning applies to chametz. Perhaps it was not used with chametz, but only with water, meat, or the like.
Cooked in chametz utensils. We do not permit this as a permitted flavor twice removed. That principle applies only to something entirely permitted, such as a minute meat flavor that becomes mixed with milk and cannot acquire a new status of meat and milk. Chametz, however, retains its name whether the flavor is slight or substantial. Until now the Torah simply had not forbidden it, and now it becomes like a piece of neveilah. Toras HaBayis, house 4, gate 4; Ran.
One may be lenient. This is because the case involves a permitted flavor twice removed, and because an ordinary utensil is presumed not ben yomo. Taz, siman 442, writes that for this reason some permit drinking ordinary mead. If there is reason to suspect that it was fermented with beer yeast, it is forbidden. See what I wrote in siman 442, seif 5. All the more so, in places where it is cooked throughout the year in cauldrons used for distilled liquor, it is forbidden, because it is a strong and sharp substance that improves mead, as is well known. The fact that the utensil was not ben yomo does not help. There is therefore no remaining doubt. All the more so, one may not buy it from a non-Jew, because beer may have been mixed into it. See Yoreh Deah, siman 114, that yeast need not be suspected unless this is their customary practice. See the gloss there, seif 10. As for prohibiting cauldrons used for distilled liquor, Yoreh Deah, siman 122, seif 3, states that the original sharpness does not make the absorbed flavor favorable. Perhaps he holds that distilled liquor is sharper. The practical law requires study. Perhaps distilled liquor should be compared to idolatrous wine, where a utensil not ben yomo is still forbidden. See what I wrote in siman 451, end of seif 21.
There is no need to be stringent. First, the case involves a permitted flavor twice removed. Second, since there is doubt whether the utensil was ben yomo, we do not establish a prohibition, because in a Rabbinic doubt one rules leniently. Darkei Moshe. This means that the prohibition of any minute amount is Rabbinic. If a large quantity became mixed without sixty times its amount, it would be forbidden because the doubt would concern a Torah prohibition. This also appears from Shulchan Aruch’s wording. Nevertheless, this requires study. After the fact one may rely upon the presumption that an ordinary utensil is not ben yomo together with the rule of a permitted flavor twice removed, as stated in siman 452. On the final day of Yom Tov we are lenient even initially to eat foods cooked in chametz utensils. Why, then, should the mixture be forbidden on the other days? It therefore appears to me that Rama’s statement, “if any amount became mixed,” concerns fat. Fat is certainly forbidden when a large amount became mixed, because crumbs of chametz may be present within it. Regarding cooked wine and similar foods, even if a large amount became mixed, it is permitted.
Where wine vinegar is not common. Consequently, even if some was already taken for the meal, we do not impose a stringency, because another is not readily available.
Not to fill. Darkei Moshe writes in the name of Responsa Mahari Bruna that the established custom in that country is not to drink on Pesach any wine filled within thirty days into a chametz utensil. Strictly according to law, however, there is no prohibition. Regarding vinegar, which rapidly absorbs and expels flavor, one should be more careful. He heard that Maharil forbade drinking such vinegar on Pesach. Nevertheless, he did not see people act stringently. Although vinegar is sharp, the case involves a permitted flavor twice removed, and the utensils’ primary use is only with cold substances. This requires study. Regarding a sharp food, a permitted flavor twice removed is ineffective, as stated in Yoreh Deah, siman 95. Therefore, if wine vinegar remained in a chametz utensil, even one not ben yomo, for the time needed to place it over a fire and bring it to a boil, it is forbidden according to strict law, as stated in Yoreh Deah, siman 105. Although Shach there writes that vinegar also requires twenty four hours, one should not be lenient against Shulchan Aruch. The Mishnah at the end of chapter 1 of Maasros and in chapter 3 of Shabbos compares vinegar to brine, because both are sharp. Although siman 104 states that vinegar also requires twenty four hours, it appears to refer to vinegar that is not very strong. Rashi and Tosafos there imply that the discussion concerns beer vinegar. The same applies to wine vinegar that is not particularly strong; it is not regarded as sharp.
