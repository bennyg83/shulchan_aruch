[5] Permitted. The reason is that the concern of chametz in the salt with which it was salted is nullified in sixty before Pesach and does not reawaken. If so, he should not have written simply to permit, since before this he wrote that some disagree and hold that we say it reawakens. It appears that Shulchan Aruch decided here according to the lenient view because here there are two favorable factors: first, perhaps there was no chametz in the salt at all; and if you say there was, perhaps it was nullified before Pesach according to the lenient authorities. The dissenting view that Rema brings is the Ritz Giyyas, whom the Tur cites, who forbids milk milked by a Jew before Pesach if it is not known that he was careful about chametz. The reason of the Ritz Giyyas is that he holds it reawakens on Pesach, like the dissenting view in se'if 4. The Beis Yosef explicitly brought in the name of the Maggid Mishneh that the Ritz Giyyas holds like those Geonim who say that it reawakens. If so, why did Rema write that the custom is to be stringent, after writing in se'if 4 that the custom follows the first view? One must say the reasoning is the reverse: the reason of the custom to be stringent here is that the salt is visibly on these items, and perhaps actual chametz is present among the salt. Therefore rinsing does not help them.

[5] Three times before Pesach. This means ab initio in a utensil that is not chametz. After the fact, it helps even in a chametz utensil, because cold does not cause release of absorbed taste. Rema mentioned only meat, and it appears that the same applies to fish, that it can be permitted through rinsing like meat, except that I have not seen the practice to permit fish through rinsing. One can give a reason for this from what Semag wrote: nevertheless, the world has the custom not to eat aged cheese or salted fish, perhaps because they are normally eaten without rinsing. But salted meat requires rinsing. This is the end of his language. It appears that this is what he means: since people normally eat fish without rinsing, there is concern that they will eat it without rinsing, unlike meat. Nevertheless, once there was difficulty finding meat, and I permitted rinsing fish on Pesach, because it appears to me that they forbade fish only when they were salted recently. This is not so with salted fish in a barrel, which cannot be eaten without rinsing; this is better than meat. But regarding cheese, it is obvious that rinsing does not help, and it is like intestines. My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote in siman 451 that if after the meat was kashered it was placed in a utensil used for chametz within twenty-four hours, the meat is forbidden for Pesach, because soaking is like cooking. I wrote in Yoreh De'ah, siman 105, that one should not forbid because of soaking like cooking in a prohibited utensil, since after the measure of twenty-four hours, which is the measure of soaking, it is impaired flavor, and impaired flavor is permitted before Pesach. It appears to me that dry meat that was hanging in a room where they sift flour is like actual chametz, and rinsing does not help.
[5] But with melted fat. The Beis Yosef asked: do we not hold that ordinary utensils were not used that day? He wrote at length about this. In my humble opinion, here we do not say so. I already wrote in Yoreh De'ah, siman 122, in the name of Darchei Moshe in the name of Hagahos Maimoniyos Sha'arei Dura, that with a Jew there is a remedy by asking him, and if he does not know, we penalize him. It seems to me that a penalty applies only to that Jew whose pot it is, for he should have known and did not know; but not to another person. This is the end of Darchei Moshe's language. It appears to me that although this reasoning is not agreed upon as halachah, as written in Shulchan Aruch, se'if 8, nevertheless regarding Pesach everyone agrees to this. Every person is accustomed to be careful in every matter needed for Pesach, preparing himself to guard against chametz. Therefore, one who melted fat in any cooking vessel should have warned that the pan certainly not be used that day. Therefore we penalize him by forbidding his cooked food to be eaten on Pesach. This appears correct to me. It appears to me that with preserves, which are sour and sharp, this is forbidden even with other prohibitions even if the utensil was not used that day. Therefore one must be stringent with it even on the last Yom Tov.
[5] But on the last Yom Tov. Since ordinary utensils were not used that day, and they were stringent only as a penalty, they were lenient on the last Yom Tov. The same applies to other rabbinic prohibitions, such as the smallest amount and impaired flavor, as I wrote above in this siman regarding the last Yom Tov, unless it is in the manner I mentioned in se'if 2.
[5] Within two months. In Terumas HaDeshen and in Pesakim, siman 149, he wrote this law in the name of Semag as within thirty days. Rema himself copied this in siman 442, se'if 3, regarding gluing papers, and they share one reason with the case here.
