(Seif katan 5) It is not, etc. “And it is obvious,” etc. His intent is as follows. Pesachim 6b states that Rav Yehudah said in the name of Rav: one who searches for chametz must nullify it. The Gemara asks why he must nullify it at the time of the search; let him nullify it in the fourth or fifth hour. It answers that since this is neither the time when chametz is forbidden nor the time of its elimination, he may forget. The Gemara then asks that he should nullify it in the sixth hour, when he is occupied with burning it. It answers that once chametz is prohibited D’Rabbanan it is no longer in his possession and he cannot nullify it. Rav Giddel said in the name of Rav Chiya bar Yosef that one who betroths a woman from the sixth hour onward, even with hard wheat from Kurdistan, which is chametz nuksheh, need not be concerned for the kiddushin. Rashi explains “from the sixth hour onward” as the beginning of the sixth hour, and the Gemara proves that even then the chametz is not in his possession, for a woman betrothed with it is not betrothed. Rabbeinu Tam proves that Rav Giddel’s statement means from the end of the sixth hour, meaning the beginning of the seventh, when chametz is forbidden according to the Torah. The proof nevertheless stands because although Rav Giddel discusses the beginning of the seventh hour, when ordinary chametz is forbidden according to the Torah, the Kurdistan wheat is chametz nuksheh, which in his view is forbidden only D’Rabbanan. Thus ordinary chametz in the sixth hour, whose leniency arises from the time, is learned from chametz nuksheh at the beginning of the seventh hour, whose leniency arises from its own nature. Maharil follows Rabbeinu Tam. We therefore see that the Gemara compares an item lenient by its nature with another item bearing the same leniency because of the time. Maharil consequently holds that chametz nuksheh on Pesach, although forbidden according to the Torah in accordance with those authorities, carries no kares and is comparable to ordinary chametz from the sixth hour onward on Erev Pesach, which likewise carries no kares because Pesach has not yet begun. Just as ordinary chametz from the sixth hour onward is nullified in sixty because the time does not carry kares, chametz nuksheh on Pesach, which inherently carries no kares, is also nullified in sixty. Maharil’s description of chametz from the sixth hour onward as D’Rabbanan is therefore borrowed language. Since he follows Rabbeinu Tam, “from the sixth hour onward” in Rav Giddel’s statement means the beginning of the seventh hour, when the prohibition is according to the Torah. Maharil’s phrase “from the sixth hour onward, which is D’Rabbanan,” instead refers to the beginning of the sixth hour, because he is commenting on the Gemara’s question, “Let him nullify it in the sixth hour,” which means the beginning of that hour. The answer is that once it is forbidden D’Rabbanan it is no longer in his possession.
And in the writings of Mahari, etc. The principle of lo plug is not applied as stringently as the substance of the decree itself, namely the prohibition in any amount. This is also the meaning of Mahari’s writings there. He must hold that the prohibition in any amount was enacted because of the special severity of chametz, whereas detrimental taste is forbidden on Pesach only because of lo plug. Rashba’s responsum cited by Beit Yosef in this siman also somewhat indicates this, for it explains that detrimental taste is prohibited on Pesach because we are stringent to prohibit even the smallest amount; the same stringency therefore extends to detrimental taste.
Why compare detrimental taste with a minute amount, which contributes favorable taste? See Pri Chadash there. The explanation must be that once the Sages prohibited even a minute amount, although it does not impart taste and the law no longer follows taste, they also prohibited detrimental taste through lo plug. Detrimental taste is indeed less severe than a minute amount because it impairs the food, but since even non-taste, meaning a minute amount, was prohibited, the rule was not divided where the taste is detrimental. He therefore writes that one may distinguish and permit detrimental taste in chametz nuksheh. Although he regards a minute amount of chametz nuksheh as plainly forbidden, contrary to Maharil, that minute amount is the substance of the decree, while the prohibition of detrimental taste in ordinary chametz exists only because of lo plug and was not extended to chametz nuksheh. Tosafot at the beginning of Pesachim proves this specifically according to its final explanation. Tosafot explains that the Sages required searching even though bittul alone suffices because one might come to eat the chametz. Why were they more concerned with chametz than with other prohibited foods that may remain in one’s home? One answer is that people are not accustomed to separating themselves from chametz. Another answer is that the Torah was more stringent with chametz than with other prohibitions by imposing bal yera’eh upon one who retains it, and the Sages therefore also prohibited retaining it after bittul lest one eat it. Tosafot further cites Rashba that even according to the latter reason, although Rabbeinu Tam holds that bal yera’eh does not apply to chametz nuksheh, the Sages required its elimination lest one eat it, because of lo plug. This implies that according to the first reason, that people are not accustomed to separating themselves from chametz, lo plug is unnecessary because chametz nuksheh is itself equal to ordinary chametz in that respect.
Since it is the time of elimination. Meaning, since part of the day, namely the sixth hour, is the time of elimination, throughout that day people are somewhat separated from chametz because they remember that it is forbidden to eat. This resembles the Gemara’s statement regarding Rabbi Yehudah on Pesachim 11a: “He himself is searching for it in order to burn it; will he then eat from it?” Through a mixture it prohibits in any amount. Meaning, if that first mixture then becomes mixed into another substance. Mahari holds that chametz nuksheh and ordinary chametz are equal regarding a secondary mixture.
