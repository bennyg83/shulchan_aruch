During Pesach. Even if there was a small amount of chametz in it, that chametz was already nullified before Pesach. Chametz. This implies that two conditions are required: it was soaked during Pesach, and it was soaked in a chametz utensil. In that case we are concerned for cold absorption from the utensil. Before Pesach, however, even if it was soaked in a chametz utensil, one need not be stringent, because the liquid is cold and the water weakens the power of the brine. Likewise, if it was soaked during Pesach in a Yom Tov utensil, it is plainly permitted, and there is no concern for crumbs of chametz in the salt. This is from the later authorities. If it remained in the utensil for twenty four hours, whether the principle of soaking is like cooking applies, see Magen Avraham and Chok Yaakov. Dry. The same applies to newly salted meat and to other salted and pickled foods, such as cucumbers, sliced cabbage called kompot, and lemons, when the salt was not checked for chametz at the time of preparation. The custom is not to eat them during Pesach, except on the final day of Pesach. See siman 467. The meat. This refers to dried meat, or even newly salted meat that contains much salt that was not checked, where the ordinary practice is to rinse it, and likewise dried fish. Fresh salted fish, however, is not ordinarily permitted initially, because it is not normally rinsed, and we are concerned that one may forget and eat it without rinsing. Therefore, salted lemons should not be eaten. Magen Avraham. Eliyah Zuta writes that perhaps one may be lenient regarding what is called lakshen because it is ordinarily rinsed. Nevertheless, the custom is not to be lenient with any kind of salted fish during Pesach, except on the final Yom Tov. After the fact, in pressing circumstances, or where refraining would diminish the joy of Yom Tov, everything is permitted. This is from the later authorities. Taz likewise writes that on one occasion there was a shortage of meat, and he permitted fish to be rinsed for Pesach. See there. Rinsing means soaking briefly, not merely passing water over it. It should be soaked in a river or in a Pesach utensil with the water changed three times. It should not initially be soaked in a chametz utensil. This is from the later authorities. Pesach. During Pesach itself it should not be soaked. After the fact, one need not be stringent. Maharil writes that if it was not rinsed before Pesach, it may be rinsed even initially on Yom Tov. It may perhaps even be rinsed in a chametz utensil, provided it was not soaked there for twenty four hours. See Chok Yaakov. And regarding intestines, rinsing is ineffective. Rinsing does not help for the filling inside them. The same is true of cheese, for which rinsing is no more effective than for intestines. See Responsa Bach, siman 123. Taz writes that if dried meat was kept in a room where flour was sifted, rinsing does not help. One must be careful not to sift Pesach flour near foods, beverages, or Pesach utensils where flour dust can reach them. Responsa Chut HaShani, siman 55, writes that even dried salted meat upon which garlic was cut into fine pieces with a knife, even where the knife was not wiped clean, and the garlic was placed upon the meat to impart flavor and aroma, the meat may nevertheless be permitted through rinsing, like other dried meat. One need not be stringent. This means that if they were already cooked, they need not be discarded when they cannot be kept until after Pesach. Certainly they do not prohibit a mixture even when there is less than sixty times their volume, because the concern is only a possible trace amount prohibited by Rabbinic law. The same applies in pressing circumstances or where refraining would diminish the joy of Yom Tov, which is treated like an after-the-fact case. If there is garlic in the meat mixture that was cut with a chametz knife, Magen Avraham writes that it is forbidden even after the fact. See there. According to strict law. This means it is forbidden for eating, but permitted for benefit. Chok Yaakov. Not ben yomo. Because of the stringency of chametz, we do not apply the ordinary presumption that a utensil is not ben yomo. Even if he says with certainty that it was not ben yomo, it is forbidden, because people do not ordinarily pay attention to matters not imposed upon them. Bach. This implies that if it was originally cooked for Pesach in a utensil that was not ben yomo, it may be eaten on Pesach. Magen Avraham. It appears to me that it must be poured into a Pesach utensil before Yom Tov. To be lenient with it. This is because it is a permitted flavor twice removed. In addition, on the final day of Yom Tov we apply the presumption that an ordinary utensil is not ben yomo. For this reason some authorities permit drinking the mead used throughout the year. If there is reason to suspect that the mead was fermented with beer yeast, it is forbidden. All the more so in places where it is cooked throughout the year in kettles used for distilled liquor, it is forbidden, because it is a strong and sharp substance. The fact that the utensil is not ben yomo therefore does not help. See above, siman 442, seif katan 11. A confection is sour and sharp, and even regarding other prohibitions it is forbidden when prepared in a utensil that is not ben yomo. It should therefore be treated stringently even on the final day of Yom Tov. Taz. A minute amount. During Pesach one may not be lenient with less than sixty.
