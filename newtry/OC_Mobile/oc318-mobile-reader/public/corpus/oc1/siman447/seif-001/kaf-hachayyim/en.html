1) [Se'if 1] Chametz during Pesach renders a mixture prohibited, etc. Under Torah law, chametz is like other prohibited substances. The Sages were stringent regarding it because its consumption carries the penalty of kares and people are not accustomed to avoiding it throughout the year. Magen Avraham, at the beginning of the siman. Rambam, chapter 15 of Hilchos Ma'achalos Asuros, halachah 9, explains that chametz is a substance that will later become permitted. See there. Beis Yosef cites his words. Rema writes in Responsa Rema, siman 28, that when another basis for leniency exists, it may be possible to rely upon Mordechai, who writes that one may rely upon She'iltos and treat chametz as nullified in sixty like other prohibitions. Responsa Mas'as Binyamin, siman 69, writes likewise. This is also cited by Magen Avraham below in siman 467, subsection 33. In practice, however, their words indicate that this position is relied upon only when many additional grounds for leniency exist. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1; Chok Yosef, subsection 1; Mekor Chaim, subsection 1; Chayei Adam, rule 121, subsection 8.
2) The sacred Zohar states in parashas Ki Seitzei, 282b: 'Those who observe Pesach, when the night of guarding holds sway over them, must all be guarded and protected from even the slightest amount of chametz and leaven, and all their foods and drinks must be guarded. One who guards them from chametz and leaven has the body guarded from the evil inclination below and the soul guarded above. Of such a person it is stated, "Evil shall not dwell with You," for the person's body becomes holy and the person's soul becomes holy of holies. Of the evil inclination it is stated, "No stranger may eat of the holy," and "The stranger who approaches shall die."' These are its words. Ba'er Hetev writes this in the name of the Arizal. At present I have not found it written in the works of the Arizal available to us, except in Mishnas Chassidim, tractate Nissan, chapter 3, subsection 4.
3) There. Whether mixed with its own kind or with another kind, etc. This means both a mixture with another kind, where with other prohibitions the law generally follows whether flavor was imparted, and a mixture with its own kind, where with other prohibitions we measure whether there is sixty times the prohibited substance, as stated at the beginning of Yoreh De'ah, siman 98. See there. Regarding chametz, however, there is no distinction; even the slightest amount prohibits the mixture, including deriving benefit from it.
4) There. Whether with its own kind, etc. A mixture with its own kind includes flour from wheat that became chametz mixed with flour from wheat that did not become chametz. A mixture with another kind includes flour from wheat that became chametz mixed with flour of another grain that did not become chametz, or mixed into a cooked dish, and the like. See above, siman 442, subsection 3.
5) There. Even the slightest amount, etc. This is not necessarily literal; an amount less than one sixtieth is called the slightest amount. Alternatively, it means the slightest amount that improves the mixture and causes it to sell for more than it would previously have sold. Otherwise, the Shulchan Aruch writes below in siman 467, se'if 10, that a cooked chicken in which a split wheat kernel is found during Pesach may be sold, whereas here it states that the slightest amount prohibits benefit. The distinction must therefore be as explained. Peri Chadash. Ma'amar Mordechai there, subsection 16, writes that in the case of the wheat kernel, the Shulchan Aruch relies upon Rosh's opinion that wheat kernels, even when split, are not definite chametz, and therefore rules leniently regarding benefit. See there. See also our words there, with the help of Heaven.
6) There. Even the slightest amount, etc. Whether the substance of the chametz dissolved or whether it remains intact and only its flavor was imparted, the slightest amount prohibits the mixture. Responsa Rashba, simanim 30 and 53. This is also evident from the words of the Shulchan Aruch in this siman and in siman 467. Peri Chadash. Mateh Yehudah likewise agrees that even flavor alone prohibits in the slightest amount. See there. See above, siman 442, subsection 4. Regarding whether it may be sold to a non-Jew after deducting the value of the prohibited substance it contains, see below, subsection 13.
7) There. Even the slightest amount, etc. This applies even to the slightest amount of hardened, inedible chametz, which is rabbinically prohibited. Peri Chadash. Taz, subsection 19, likewise writes that if hardened chametz falls into food, the food may not be eaten during Pesach, but may be retained until after Pesach. See there. Magen Avraham, subsection 5, however, writes in the name of Likkutei Maharil that hardened chametz is nullified in sixty even during Pesach, since it does not carry the penalty of kares. He cites Piskei Mahara'i, siman 186, which prohibits even the slightest amount, challenges that ruling, and concludes that one who rules leniently like Maharil suffers no loss. See there. Nehar Shalom, subsection 2; Erech HaShulchan, subsection 9; Mekor Chaim, subsection 7; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 18; Chayei Adam, rule 121, subsection 13; and Magen HaElef agree. Fruit juice mixed with water, however, prohibits even in the slightest amount although the resulting chametz is hardened chametz. Mekor Chaim, there. See below, subsection 268.
8) There. Even the slightest amount, etc. If some of a food that was itself prohibited by the slightest admixture becomes mixed into another dish, creating two successive minute admixtures, Taz in Yoreh De'ah, siman 92, subsection 16, concludes as follows: if a solid piece became prohibited by the slightest admixture, fell into another pot, and was removed, what it exudes does not prohibit the second pot in the slightest amount, because we do not apply the rule of the slightest amount twice. If, however, one liquid became mixed with another in this manner, it remains prohibited forever by the slightest amount. See there. Peri Chadash agrees with Taz. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1, likewise rules leniently where a solid piece prohibited by the slightest admixture fell into another dish, there is a majority against it, the piece is identifiable, and it was removed. If it cannot be identified, everything is prohibited. See there. Shach in Nekudos HaKesef there, however, challenges Taz's permission of a solid piece prohibited by the slightest admixture that fell into another pot. He writes that all halachic authorities customarily prohibit all the mixtures. See there. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1 in this siman, agrees and writes that the ruling is contrary to Taz. Magen Avraham, siman 467, subsection 13; Chok Yaakov, subsection 5 in this siman and subsection 27 in siman 467; Chok Yosef, subsection 6; and Mateh Yehudah write likewise. Mateh Yehudah writes that this is evident from the words of Maran and Rema, and that this is the custom in Algiers. See there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 16; Chayei Adam, rule 121, subsection 8. Regarding a doubtful prohibition involving the slightest amount, however, Beis Yehudah, page 109, writes that the second mixture is permitted and agrees with this ruling. See there. Beis David, siman 163, likewise writes that if it is uncertain whether any chametz entered the second mixture, since perhaps all the chametz remained in the first mixture, all authorities permit it. See there. Erech HaShulchan, subsection 4, cites this, but writes that Magen Avraham, subsection 26, implies that it is prohibited even where the chametz may have remained in the first mixture. See there.
9) A wheat kernel found in a pot renders prohibited the spoon used to stir the pot. If that spoon was used to stir other pots, all of them are prohibited. Tur, siman 467; Chayei Adam, there. Eliyah Rabbah, at the end of subsection 1 in this siman, likewise writes that a spoon that absorbed the slightest amount renders another mixture prohibited by the slightest amount. Although Eliyah Rabbah maintains that a solid piece prohibited by the slightest admixture does not subsequently prohibit another mixture by the slightest amount, as stated in the preceding subsection, he explains there that the slightest amount cannot spread from a solid piece, whereas it can spread from a spoon, which has no flavor of its own. See there. In other words, when a solid piece that has flavor exudes, it also exudes some of its own substance, producing only the slightest amount of the slightest amount. A spoon, however, has no flavor of its own; when it exudes, it exudes all the minute amount that it absorbed. It therefore renders prohibited the pots subsequently stirred with it.
10) A person cooked fish in a chametz frying pan that had not been used for many days. The spoon used to stir the fish was subsequently used to stir another dish, and some of that second dish was added to a third dish. Responsa Shevus Yaakov, volume 1, siman 21, writes that one may not rule leniently in this case, following Rema below in se'if 10, who prohibits a detrimental absorbed flavor during Pesach. See there. An exception applies when another basis for leniency exists. Here there is an additional basis to permit a detrimental absorbed flavor during Pesach after two months have passed. This implies that even the food cooked directly in the frying pan is permitted after the fact. This is subject to the strict condition that the cooked food not be sharp. See the glosses to Yoreh De'ah, siman 93, and siman 122, se'if 3. From this one may infer that even one who is stringent regarding a pot lid that was not used that day may permit it after two months. Initially, however, with all prohibitions it is forbidden to use utensils that were not used that day without kashering them, even if they were not used for many years, and this is certainly true of chametz. See there. Sha'arei Teshuvah, subsection 2, cites this ruling. Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 75, however, permits it only when twelve months have passed since chametz was cooked in the utensil. Rabbi Naftali Kohen agrees, as stated there in siman 76, and rules likewise in siman 80. See there. This will be further explained below in siman 451, subsection 4. See there.
11) A householder's loaf of chametz that became mixed with loaves of matzah renders all of them prohibited for consumption and benefit, even if it became mixed with one thousand loaves, because it is an item sold by number, as stated at the beginning of Yoreh De'ah, siman 110. It appears to me that they remain prohibited even after Pesach. If the loaf was cut or broken, it is permitted after Pesach. Some maintain that it is permitted after Pesach even while still whole, because every chametz mixture was permitted after Pesach despite having been prohibited by the slightest amount; an item sold by number, which is likewise prohibited by the slightest amount, should be no more stringent. I am stringent in this case, because the Sages treated a mixture containing an item sold by number like the prohibited item itself. Sefer Beis Moed; Birkei Yosef, subsection 2. Peri Chadash in siman 448, se'if 3, likewise rules that it is prohibited even after Pesach. See there. See below, se'if 9, and our words there, with the help of Heaven.
12) There. Even for benefit. It is also forbidden to retain it until after Pesach, as stated below in siman 467, at the end of se'if 10. Shulchan Gavoah, subsection 3.
