(1) "In any amount," etc. Regarding a prohibited item that became prohibited through a mashehu and was then mixed into another cooked food, Taz discusses the matter at length in Yoreh De'ah, siman 92. He concludes: if a piece became prohibited through a mashehu and afterward fell into another mixture and was removed, what it exudes does not prohibit the second mixture through a mashehu. But when liquid became mixed with liquid in this way, it remains prohibited through a mashehu. End quote. Nekudat HaKesef strongly disagrees and writes that this is astonishing, for the halachic decisors all prohibit it. In any event, he argues, the minute prohibited flavor within that piece, since it is mixed together with another substance, should be considered a different species relative to the other pieces, because the other pieces do not contain that mixture; and on Pesach even unlike species are forbidden through a mashehu. In my humble opinion, the words of Taz are correct. Tosafos in Chullin explain the law of a piece becoming neveilah. Rabbeinu Ephraim holds that chatichah na'asit neveilah applies only to meat and milk. Accordingly, in other prohibitions the permitted material does not itself become like the prohibition. If the prohibited flavor does not impart taste into the piece, it does not have the power to spread outward from that piece. The same applies even according to Rabbeinu Tam, who holds that chatichah na'asit neveilah applies to other prohibitions as well: that stringency was said only where the prohibition imparts taste. Where it does not impart taste, such as where the piece contains 60 times the prohibition, the Sages did not say that the permitted portion becomes exactly like the prohibited portion. Accordingly, where the entire prohibition is only because of a mashehu, that mashehu does not have power to spread farther. This appears to me clear in Tosafos. The same is implied by Rashba's novellae, Mishmeret HaBayit, Sefer HaTerumah, and the Rosh. Nekudat HaKesef relied upon Ran at the end of Perek Gid HaNasheh, who writes that although like species are not nullified, we do not find this where another permitted taste is mixed in. He understood this to mean that because another force is mixed in, it becomes unlike species. That is not correct. Ran's intent is that the additional permitted flavor weakens the identity of the original prohibited flavor for purposes of the rule of like species; it does not mean that every piece containing a trace becomes a new unlike species that can transmit a Pesach mashehu indefinitely. Taz also cites a ruling concerning a wheat kernel found in cooked food and a spoon inserted afterward. Some understood from that case that once the kernel was removed, a spoon inserted into the food would not later prohibit another food through a mashehu. I wonder at that reasoning. If so, what do we do every day when a pot contains several pieces of meat and a dairy spoon is inserted, where everything in the pot is combined toward 60, as is explicit in many places? Let us be concerned that perhaps the spoon touched only 1 piece, which absorbed by itself without 60. Rather, certainly all the contents combine. Therefore the answer is as I wrote in Eliyah Zuta: specifically from a food piece, a mashehu does not have power to spread further, because the piece has its own permitted flavor. A spoon, which has no independent flavor of its own, is different; what it absorbed can spread from it. Rama makes a similar distinction in Yoreh De'ah, siman 105, seif 7. Nekudat HaKesef further challenges Taz: why did he specify that the prohibited piece fell into another mixture and was removed? Even if it was not removed, it should not prohibit the other pieces. He concludes that the wording is a scribal error. In my humble opinion, Taz's wording is correct. He means to exclude a case where the prohibited piece is no longer recognizable. In that case, since the piece itself remains prohibited, as Tosafos write, and on Pesach dry food is not nullified even in dry food, the entire mixture becomes prohibited. I proved this at length in Eliyah Zuta from Tosafos and the Rosh, although I also raised a question there concerning the Rosh in Avodah Zarah, Perek HaSocher; here I record only what is necessary for practical halacha. Nekudat HaKesef also sought proof from siman 467, seif 12, where Rama writes that if it became mixed with other pieces one may be lenient because of doubt, which would imply that where the matter is certain the mixture is forbidden. Magen Avraham writes similarly there. This is not a proof. One can say that the case there is where the second cooked food does not contain a majority against the pieces. Alternatively, Rama may not be discussing the food piece at all, but rather a liquid mixture, regarding which we are lenient only because of a rabbinic doubt. Furthermore, even if that were Rama's view, I have already proved from Tosafos and the great halachic decisors that the food piece is permitted in the manner explained above. Taz himself later reinforced his ruling in Orach Chayyim and wrote that it is a peg that cannot be moved. The practical conclusion is therefore to be lenient where a recognizable piece became prohibited through a mashehu and afterward fell into another cooked food that contains a majority against it, provided the prohibited piece is removed. If the piece is not recognizable, everything is forbidden. A spoon, however, that absorbed a mashehu can prohibit another mixture through a mashehu.
