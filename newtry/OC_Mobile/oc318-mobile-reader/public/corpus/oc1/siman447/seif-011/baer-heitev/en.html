[11] That became mixed. The principle here is that a mixture in which there is no biblical prohibition, meaning a case where one would not transgress bal yera'eh and bal yimatzei, as I wrote above at the beginning of siman 442, even though it has a rabbinic prohibition during Pesach, is nevertheless permitted to mix before Pesach in order to keep it. This is not called nullifying a prohibition ab initio, as I wrote above in se'if katan 13. For this reason chametz is called something that has a permitted future. This is unlike mixing it intentionally after Pesach, and certainly during Pesach, which is like nullifying a prohibition ab initio and is forbidden even with a rabbinic prohibition, as explained in Yoreh De'ah, siman 99.
[11] On Pesach. Meaning that it became mixed unintentionally on Pesach in sixty, and he transgressed and kept it even intentionally. It is permitted, for he did not transgress biblically in bal yera'eh, and it is permitted after Pesach in this case even for eating.
[11] In sixty. This is the view of the Tur. But all the poskim wrote that sixty is not required. Therefore it appears to me that one who is lenient loses nothing, even in less than sixty, as long as it became mixed in the majority; it is permitted even for eating. Later authorities. See Sefer Even HaEzer, who reconciles the Tur's view as halachah; see there at length in Sefer Magen Avraham by R. Proops. If the prohibition is something that establishes the mixture and the like, see above, siman 442, se'if katan 11. Beer yeast over which Pesach passed and with which one leavened dough is forbidden; R. Meir of Lublin, siman 121. See Yoreh De'ah, siman 123, se'if 15, in Shach there. Eliyah Zuta wrote: my grandfather the Gaon, of blessed memory, ruled regarding butter in which bread or flour was soaked, and one transgressed and kept it during Pesach, that he should cast the benefit of the bread or flour into the sea or a river and sell the butter to a non-Jew. Chok Yaakov wrote in siman 442 that this refers to where water was mixed with the butter, for otherwise it is fruit juice and does not become chametz; see there. He ruled similarly regarding folded matzah found after Pesach, that he should cast away the fold and also the value of the absorbed forbidden benefit, a perutah's worth, and sell the rest to a non-Jew. But if the matzah had sixty against the fold, he should cast the fold into the sea and the rest is permitted even for eating. The same applies to a wheat kernel found in baked matzah. But with a swollen kernel, all of it is always forbidden for benefit. Chok Yaakov in siman 461 is lenient after Pesach; see there.
[11] The salt. It is permitted even for eating if it became mixed after Pesach. This is unlike where it became mixed before Pesach in less than sixty, where it is forbidden. See Magen Avraham, and see above, siman 442, se'if katan 1.
