Immediately. Taz writes that this applies even before nightfall, following his view in siman 234, seif katan 3. According to what Magen Avraham writes in siman 267, cited there in Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 6, however, one should be stringent during the half hour before night because of the obligation of Shema. According to many authorities, one does not fulfill that obligation through the Shema recited earlier in the synagogue. This is especially true according to our practice of ordinarily praying Maariv at its proper time and Minchah after plag haminchah, as Yad Ephraim writes there. All this applies during the half hour before night. Before that half hour begins, one may recite Kiddush and eat the Shabbos meal on the basis of that Kiddush, because Kiddush may be fulfilled from plag haminchah onward, as explained there in siman 267, seif 2. Nevertheless, Magen Avraham questions how, according to our practice of praying Maariv at night and Minchah after plag haminchah, which indicates that we do not follow Rabbi Yehudah, one can fulfill Kiddush at that earlier time. Although he answers the question there, it appears preferable initially to avoid doing so.
Regarding Taz's statement, were it not for his words, I would say that Tur's use of "immediately" is not intended to urge a person to recite Kiddush as soon as he has personally accepted Shabbos. Rather, Tur means that once the obligation of Kiddush begins, namely after nightfall when the day becomes sanctified, one should perform it immediately and not delay, because the phrase "remember it over wine at its entrance" implies immediately when the day becomes sanctified. Rashi in Pesachim 106 implies this as well. Tur does not mean that a person should hasten to recite Kiddush merely because he accepted Shabbos upon himself. Rashba, cited by Taz, writes only that one may recite Kiddush at that time and may then continue eating after nightfall. Rashba does not say that there is a mitzvah to hasten and recite Kiddush then. Perishah also noticed that Rashba's words provide no proof, and answered that since Kiddush is permitted then, one should automatically perform it early because of the requirement to remember Shabbos over wine at its entrance. This conclusion, however, is not compelled.
A proof may be brought from Berachos 27b, which states that Rabbi prayed the Shabbos prayer on Friday, entered the bathhouse to perspire, then came out and taught us our chapter while it was still day. The Gemara's mention that he taught the chapter apparently demonstrates that his prayer occurred a considerable time before nightfall, since after praying he still had time to enter the bathhouse and teach his students. Since the Gemara does not say that he also recited Kiddush and ate before entering the bathhouse, it appears that he did not recite Kiddush then. We see that there is no requirement to hasten before the obligation has actually begun. One might distinguish between a short time before night and a much earlier time, even though both are after plag haminchah.
No proof can be brought for Taz from Rambam, chapter 29, halachah 11, who writes that a person may recite Kiddush over a cup while it is still day. There his intention is that he is permitted to do so, as is clear from his concluding statement that the same applies to Havdalah. A similar expression appears in chapter 6 of the laws of prayer, halachah 4. See Kesef Mishneh. Rambam does not mean that one should initially do so.
Although Rambam provides no proof for Taz, his wording supports the distinction suggested above between a short time before night and a much earlier time. Rambam concludes that the mitzvah of remembering Shabbos applies both when Shabbos enters and slightly before that time. This implies that even according to those who disagree with Rabbi Yehudah, one may recite Kiddush a short time before night, and that at that point the time of the obligation has begun according to all opinions. It is therefore proper to perform it promptly then. Much earlier, however, even after plag haminchah, one relies only upon Rabbi Yehudah's view, and we do not find that even according to him it is proper to seek out that earlier time. Rabbi therefore entered the bathhouse and taught his chapter first. This also explains Rambam's word "slightly," concerning which Peri Megadim was pressed. This appears to explain Taz's reasoning.
We will now clarify somewhat the basic mitzvah of Kiddush. According to all authorities, sanctifying the day is a positive Torah commandment derived from, "Remember the Shabbos day to sanctify it." Rambam writes in chapter 29 of the laws of Shabbos that one can fulfill the positive commandment merely by verbally recalling the day's praise and sanctity. Reciting Kiddush over a cup is only of Rabbinic origin. This view is also mentioned by several other early authorities. Magen Avraham therefore writes that one fulfills the Torah commandment through the prayer alone, since the sanctity of the day is mentioned in it. He adds a proof from Berachos 33b, where the Gemara asks whether one who recites Havdalah in prayer must repeat it over a cup. The answer compares Havdalah to Kiddush: just as one who sanctifies Shabbos in prayer again recites Kiddush over a cup, one who recites Havdalah in prayer again recites it over a cup.
Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger, however, notes that Ran's discussion of the conflict between purchasing a Chanukah light and a light for one's home proves that Kiddush over wine or bread is itself a Torah obligation. Rashba there also appears to hold this. Rabbi Akiva Eiger further cites Rosh in the chapter Arvei Pesachim, who likewise clearly holds that prayer alone certainly does not fulfill the obligation. Rosh goes even further and maintains that even a complete Kiddush recited away from the place of the meal does not fulfill the Torah obligation. Rabbeinu Yonah holds that a complete Kiddush fulfills the Torah obligation even away from the place of the meal.
One must explain how Rosh, Ran, and Rashba understand the Gemara's statement that although one sanctified Shabbos in prayer, he again sanctifies it over a cup. It may mean that although he mentioned Shabbos in prayer, he must still recite Kiddush over a cup, because the verse says "remember," and the accepted interpretation is to remember Shabbos over wine, or over bread in place of wine. Without this he has not fulfilled Kiddush at all. Similarly, since the Sages enacted Havdalah in a manner parallel to the Torah law of Kiddush, even after reciting Havdalah in prayer, one must repeat it over a cup, and otherwise has not fulfilled Havdalah at all. According to Rabbeinu Yonah, the Gemara likewise proves that prayer alone does not fulfill Havdalah, just as Kiddush in prayer alone does not fulfill the obligation of Kiddush.
Another issue requires attention. Pesachim 117b states in the name of Rav Acha bar Yaakov that one must mention the Exodus from Egypt in Kiddush. Here it is written, "Remember," and there it is written, "so that you shall remember," etc. Although the Exodus must be mentioned every night, there one may mention it at any point during the night, whereas in Kiddush the Torah requires that it be mentioned together with Kiddush. This statement is cited by Rabbeinu Chananel, Rif, and Rosh. How, then, can one fulfill Kiddush through the evening prayer when the Exodus is not mentioned there at all? In the morning prayer, I saw in the gloss Mareh Kohen that he cites from Kol Bo the wording, "a remembrance of the work of creation and of the Exodus from Egypt." Rashbam also writes that the Exodus must be mentioned in prayer and over the cup, but our text of the evening prayer contains no mention of the Exodus. How, then, can Magen Avraham say that one fulfills Kiddush through it?
There is no difficulty regarding Rambam, because when he says one fulfills the mitzvah through verbal remembrance, he means that the Exodus is included, as he states explicitly in Sefer HaMitzvos, mitzvah 31. This also implies that mentioning the Exodus is a Torah requirement and not merely an asmachta, since he includes it in his explanation of the positive commandment. It is nevertheless surprising that Rambam omits it from Mishneh Torah. Magen Avraham and the later authorities who adopted his ruling, however, remain difficult. I heard that Minchas Chinuch also expressed astonishment at Magen Avraham for this reason and therefore ruled against him.
One might defend Magen Avraham by saying either that mentioning the Exodus in Kiddush is only D'Rabbanan and that the verbal analogy is merely an asmachta, especially since the same verbal analogy is needed for the teaching in Shabbos 86b, or that according to Torah law it suffices to have mentioned the Exodus immediately before the prayer. Hashkiveinu is considered a continuation of redemption, as the Sages said, and perhaps the Exodus need not be mentioned within Kiddush itself. This remains strained, because the first three blessings of Shemoneh Esrei intervene. Perhaps one may say that immediately after Hashkiveinu, which is part of redemption, one recites the verse, "The children of Israel shall observe the Shabbos," etc. According to Torah law this may suffice, because it contains praise of Shabbos. Although the sanctity of the day has not yet been mentioned, it will be mentioned immediately after the first three blessings. The mitzvah is not diminished because the first three blessings, which praise Hashem, intervene between the praise of Shabbos and its sanctification. This is only a possible defense of Magen Avraham because of the severity of the difficulty. In practice, however, the matter requires study, since the Gemara may require mention of the Exodus within Kiddush itself, and our prayer text does not contain this.
For all these reasons, we wrote in Mishnah Berurah that this ruling is highly questionable. Derech HaChaim also writes that initially one should defer to the leading authorities who hold that the Torah obligation is not fulfilled through prayer. The further point raised there, that mitzvos require intent, was also raised by Tosafos Shabbos. We further wrote that it is not advisable even to recite Kiddush word for word together with a minor. Since the adult is not being discharged by the minor's Kiddush, a cup must stand before the adult himself. One might argue that it is sufficient for him to rely mentally upon the cup before the child, similar to what Shaarei Teshuvah writes below in siman 296 in the name of Hagahos Yesh Nochalin. There too he does not intend to fulfill through the other's blessing, and the fact that the present reciter is a minor should make no difference. Tosafos Shabbos in this siman, however, implies that if the child has not reached thirteen years, it is ineffective even if the adult recites word for word with him. The responsum of Rabbi Akiva Eiger cited below also indicates that he rejects the entire ruling of Shaarei Teshuvah in the name of Hagahos Yesh Nochalin. We therefore wrote that it is proper to avoid even this method.
Know further that if the child is thirteen years old, although Magen Avraham writes that he is not presumed an adult because we do not know that he has developed two pubic hairs, if the man already prayed, it appears that the youth can discharge him on the basis of a double doubt. Perhaps the youth has developed two hairs, and perhaps the man already fulfilled Kiddush through prayer. Even according to our concern that he may not have intended to fulfill the mitzvah in prayer, together with the other reasons discussed above, the matter still remains at least doubtful.
