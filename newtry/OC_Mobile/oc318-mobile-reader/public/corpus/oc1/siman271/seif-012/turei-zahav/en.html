After he recited kiddush, etc. In the Tur he first brought the words of Rav Amram, who holds as written here, and afterward brought the words of Rashbam, Rabbeinu Tam, and Ri. After all this he wrote that he wonders about the practice of the Rosh, for it requires forcing the statement of Rav Bruna away from its plain meaning. The Beit Yosef wrote to explain what he removed from its plain meaning. It appears to me in this way: in the Gemara, Rav Bruna said that if one washed his hands he should not recite kiddush, and Rabbi Yitzchak said about this that Rav would sometimes have bread beloved to him, etc. This implies that we rule like Rabbi Yitzchak. If so, it is difficult why Rav Amram brought the words of Rav Bruna. Rather, it is simple that he holds Rav Bruna's words were not entirely rejected, but only in part. That is, Rav Bruna holds that if one washed his hands he should not recite kiddush, which by the plain meaning of his words implies any kiddush. According to Rabbi Yitzchak, kiddush is not entirely canceled from him; rather, he recites kiddush over bread, since Rav would sometimes recite kiddush over bread. According to this, we properly rule like Rav Bruna at least that he should not recite kiddush over wine. Thus Rav Bruna's words were not nullified. But according to the other explanations, which hold that initially one should wash his hands first, they must force themselves to say that Rav Bruna follows the reason that there is kiddush not in the place of a meal. According to the plain meaning, his words imply generally in all cases. Why then should we initially follow that view and not follow the explanation of Rav Amram and the Rambam, according to which Rav Bruna's words speak in all cases? Therefore the Tur wondered about the practice of the Rosh, who practiced so, since there is another more spacious explanation. It would have been enough to rely on this explanation after the fact. Thus the Tur means: I wonder about his practice to do so initially, for we must force the statement even if this is done after the fact and remove it at least from its plain meaning; should we then initially remove Rav Bruna's words completely? His wonder was not about the explanation itself, but about the practice to do so initially. It appears to include another wonder in this: it is not proper to establish a practice and uproot the words of Rav Bruna, for he holds it does not help even after the fact; how can we establish always to act initially contrary to his words? This is like diminishing his honor, Heaven forbid. Therefore it appears to me that although he wrote that one should not change, nevertheless one who trembles at the matter and wishes to act according to the first opinion and wash his hands after kiddush, we should not say about him that whoever changes has the lower hand, Heaven forbid. Rather, he can say, I will act according to all views, and who can protest against him? He acts properly, so it appears to me.
