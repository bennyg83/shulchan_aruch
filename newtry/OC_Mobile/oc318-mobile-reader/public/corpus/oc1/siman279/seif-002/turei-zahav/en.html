His words do not appear correct. Beis Yosef explains why use of the lamp itself or its place does not permit moving it here, as it does for other utensils whose primary use is prohibited, as stated in siman 308. He writes: An ordinary utensil whose primary use is prohibited was not set aside at twilight, since it was then permitted to move it for use of the utensil itself or its place. A lamp lit at twilight, however, was then entirely prohibited to move, even for use of the lamp itself or its place. While it was burning, moving it was plainly prohibited in every manner, just as the chapter Notel teaches concerning one who places a stone on a barrel or coins on a cushion: the permitted object becomes a base for the prohibited object. It was therefore set aside for the entire day. This concludes his words. If this is the reason, there is no prohibition according to the author of Sefer HaTerumah’s position in siman 309, who maintains that an object is considered deliberately placed only when one intends it to remain there for all of Shabbos. According to our practice, we intend the lamp to remain on the table only at night and no longer, and we are particular about removing it afterward. The table therefore does not become a base for a prohibited object, as stated in siman 277, seif 3. Its law is consequently like that of something forgotten, and it was not set aside for the entire day; after it goes out, it may be moved for use of the lamp itself or its place. The authorities cited by Beis Yosef who appear to prohibit even use of the lamp itself or its place can be understood as discussing one who placed the lamp where he certainly did not intend to remove it after it went out. Accordingly, it appears to me that there is no disagreement. As Beis Yosef writes, Mordechai and Agudah permit it; they discuss a case in which he certainly intended to take it from there after the night, so it never became a base. See what I wrote regarding this in siman 309.
One who is stringent loses nothing. This expression is puzzling, for one who is stringent certainly loses the use for which he wishes to remove it, though he loses nothing on account of the stringency itself. Mordechai also uses this expression. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that two words are missing and the text should read: ‘One who is stringent should be stringent, and one who is lenient loses nothing.’ I am surprised, however: if so, why does the end of the seif state that muktzeh caused by repulsiveness is permitted? It should suffice to say that it is permitted because it is comparable to a chamber pot. Perforce, although it is repulsive, it nevertheless is not comparable to a chamber pot.
