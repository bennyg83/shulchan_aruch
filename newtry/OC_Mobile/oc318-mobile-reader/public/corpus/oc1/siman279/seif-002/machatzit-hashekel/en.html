Paragraph 2: It is permitted, etc. If so, on Yom Tov, etc., as written in siman 514. Magen Avraham there, paragraph 7, disputes Beis Yosef, who permits placing something around an unlit wax candle before lighting it so that it will go out upon reaching that point, but forbids doing so after it is lit. Magen Avraham permits placing such an impediment even after it is lit. On his view, even at bein hashemashos a wax candle on Yom Tov was not set aside due to the prohibition against consuming its fuel, since one could wrap it with something that prevents further burning, as Ran writes. Thus, a candle lit for ordinary use rather than for the Yom Tov mitzvah lacks the additional rule of being set aside for its mitzvah, and its remainder may be used. A candle lit for the Yom Tov mitzvah, however, is also set aside for its mitzvah and remains forbidden in every circumstance.
It is forbidden to eat it, but permitted to move it. Something set aside for its mitzvah is forbidden only for an act that, if done while the lamp still burned, would nullify the mitzvah. Eating the oil would nullify the lighting, so it was set aside from such use. Moving it would not nullify the mitzvah even while it burned, so it was not set aside from moving. This refers to Yom Tov; on Shabbos it is independently forbidden to move even after it goes out because it is muktzeh due to a prohibition, as Magen Avraham wrote in the preceding paragraph.
See Tosafos, etc. The Mishnah states that one may not place a vessel under a lamp on Shabbos to catch dripping oil, though it may be placed there before Shabbos; nevertheless, one may not derive benefit from the oil dripping on Shabbos. Tosafos infer from 'derive benefit' that moving it is permitted. The Mishnah chose a universally applicable ruling: consuming it is forbidden according to everyone, whereas moving it is forbidden only to Rabbi Yehudah, who holds muktzeh due to a prohibition, but not to Rabbi Shimon. Rabbi Shimon forbids only because it was set aside for its mitzvah, and moving does not nullify the mitzvah. Maharsha explains likewise. Consuming the oil while the lamp burns is forbidden because it nullifies the mitzvah. Indeed, when the Gemara says Rabbi Shimon concedes regarding oil in a lamp because it was set aside for its mitzvah, 'oil in the lamp' cannot be literal, since taking that oil is already biblically forbidden as extinguishing; it includes dripping oil, which involves no extinguishing but remains forbidden while the lamp burns because it was set aside for the mitzvah. This establishes Magen Avraham's point. Magen Avraham concludes that it is forbidden the entire day: Tosafos and the Gemara limit the rule to while it burns only according to Rabbi Shimon, who rejects migo d'iskatzai; according to us, who accept that what was set aside at bein hashemashos remains set aside, it is forbidden all day.
