Paragraph 6: It is permitted, etc. Since it is made to go out, etc. This differs from Beis Yosef's citation of Tashbetz, who holds that a stipulation is ineffective only for the object itself made muktzeh due to a prohibition, but effective where the prohibition arises solely because it became a base. Maggid Mishneh disagrees: even for a base a stipulation is generally ineffective; it works only for a lamp's base because the lamp is expected to go out.
Even with an unstable sukkah, which is expected to fall and resembles a lamp expected to go out, a stipulation is nevertheless ineffective.
Because on Yom Tov we rule like Rabbi Yehudah, etc. Since an unstable sukkah is expected to fall and a person awaits its fall, the unstated intent is like a stipulation; Rabbi Shimon therefore permits it, and an explicit stipulation works even for a sound sukkah. The same applies to a lamp under Rabbi Shimon. Rabbi Yehudah forbids an unstable sukkah despite that expectation, treating even an explicit stipulation as ineffective. Thus on Yom Tov, when we rule like Rabbi Yehudah, a stipulation is ineffective, whereas on Shabbos, when we rule like Rabbi Shimon, it is effective. Magen Avraham objects: regarding muktzeh due to a prohibition, we rule like Rabbi Yehudah even on Shabbos; without a stipulation, an extinguished lamp remains forbidden although one awaits its extinction, so a stipulation should likewise fail. See what I wrote there: a lamp is more certain to go out than an unstable sukkah is to fall, and therefore that greater expectation combined with a stipulation is effective. See siman 518.
