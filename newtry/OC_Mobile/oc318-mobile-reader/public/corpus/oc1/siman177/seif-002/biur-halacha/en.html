Require a blessing both before them and after them - See Magen Avraham, who cites the Tosefta that salted food brought before the meal, with bread brought together with the salted food after the meal, requires a blessing both before and after it. See Machatzis HaShekel for his explanation. The second law, concerning food after the meal, is not applicable in our countries, since everything is considered part of the meal. Regarding the first law as well, according to what Magen Avraham cites from the Rosh in siman 174, that something eaten to stimulate the appetite does not require an after-blessing because it belongs to the meal and Bircas HaMazon exempts it, the matter requires clarification. Rabbi Chaim Sanzer already noted this in his glosses there. Perhaps the case is one in which he does not eat it in order to stimulate the appetite. This requires study.
For we are not accustomed - At large meals, where people commonly withdraw their hands from bread and set the table with various fruits and also drink, some say that even nowadays this law applies and one must recite blessings over them. This is the view of the Tur and Levush. Others say that the law applied specifically in their times, when the tables were removed before Bircas HaMazon, making it appear like another meal. We, however, do not remove the table. Even if they have finished eating and do not intend to eat more bread, as long as Bircas HaMazon has not been recited it is considered part of the meal. This is the view of Bach, Magen Avraham, and Eliyah Rabbah, and Chayei Adam writes that this is the custom. See Eliyah Rabbah, who notes that Kol Bo, Rashba, and Rabbeinu Yonah mention removal of the table. Nevertheless, the other poskim appear to follow the Tur, for Rabbeinu Chananel, as cited in Or Zarua, the Aruch, Rambam, Tosafos Rabbeinu Yehudah, Rosh, Hagahos Maimoniyos, and the other early authorities do not mention removal of the table at all, but only withdrawal from bread. Know further that although the Mechaber rules briefly and without qualification that foods brought after the meal require a blessing, even foods brought because of the meal, and his language implies that this applies to everything in seif 1, even porridge, in my humble opinion this requires study. Many poskim disagree. The Mechaber's ruling follows Tosafos and the Rosh, and Rambam also appears to hold this way. Rashba, however, writes that neither porridge nor meat and fish require a blessing before or after even if brought after the meal, because they themselves are principal meal foods that satisfy. The Eshkol cites two explanations from Rav Hai Gaon. According to the first, the Gemara's rule that foods brought after the meal require blessings before and after applies only to foods not brought because of the meal, such as fruit and the like. Had they been brought during the meal they would have required a blessing before them, and when brought after the meal they require a blessing afterward as well. Foods brought because of the meal, however, require no blessing at all. The Eshkol writes that this is the plain meaning of the sugya and differs from Tosafos and the Rosh. According to the second explanation there, which follows Behag, at least no after-blessing is required for foods brought because of the meal, even when brought after the meal. A blessing beforehand is required because they are not eaten together with bread, but an after-blessing is required only for fruit. See there. Rabbeinu Chananel, as cited in Or Zarua, also appears to hold this way, for he writes that the passage refers to foods not brought because of the meal, such as melons and pomegranates. Since they have withdrawn from bread and these foods do not come to accompany the bread, they require a blessing before and after. The Aruch is cited there likewise. Regarding meat and fish, it is possible that he holds they do require a blessing, unlike Rashba, because their exemption applies only when they accompany bread, which is no longer the case after they have withdrawn from bread. Porridge, however, is a cooked dish and is included among foods brought because of the meal, and therefore even after the meal should require no blessing. It emerges that porridge is subject to a dispute among the authorities, and in a case of doubt concerning blessings we rule leniently. The Mechaber rules without qualification, implying that his ruling applies to everything, as is evident from the Tur and Magen Avraham, though Magen Avraham explains the case as meat and fish, perhaps deliberately. According to what is explained below in siman 208, seif 17, by the later authorities, that one who recited Bircas HaMazon over porridge has fulfilled his obligation, it appears that in our case, if one ate porridge after withdrawing from bread, it is proper to intend to exempt it with Bircas HaMazon.
To withdraw our hands, etc. - If one took the cup in order to recite Bircas HaMazon, which constitutes a complete diversion of attention as explained in siman 179, then even nowadays there would appear to be no clearer termination of the meal than this. If he afterward eats something, he must recite a blessing before and after it, and it is not exempted by Bircas HaMazon. He certainly must recite a blessing beforehand even on foods that are entirely exempt during the meal, for according to most poskim even bread itself requires a blessing once he has taken the cup in order to recite Bircas HaMazon, as explained in siman 179. It would follow that an after-blessing is likewise required. Rabbi Shneur Zalman writes this plainly, and it also appears from Magen Avraham, siman 208, seif katan 24. Maamar Mordechai there, seif katan 35, disagrees. Regarding foods brought because of the meal, it certainly appears proper to rely on his view and not recite a blessing, since in any event some early authorities hold that the Gemara does not refer to foods brought because of the meal, as explained above.
