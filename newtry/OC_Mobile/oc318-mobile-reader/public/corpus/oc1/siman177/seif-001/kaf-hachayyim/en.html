1) [Seif 1] Such as meat and vegetables, etc. This appears to refer specifically to cooked vegetables or vegetables prepared as salad to stimulate the appetite. Raw vegetables, however, such as lettuce, squash, and similar foods that are neither used as an accompaniment to bread nor eaten to stimulate the appetite, have the law of fruit. Beis David, siman 85, writes the same. Although at the end of his discussion he proves from Maharil that raw squash is considered food brought as part of the meal and that Maharil did not recite a blessing over it, in our regions the practice is to serve lettuce, squash, and cucumbers as fruit, and we do recite blessings over them. Accordingly, it appears that even if one ate a cooked dish of lettuce, squash, or cucumbers as an accompaniment to bread, if they are subsequently brought as fruit, he recites a blessing over them. This also appears to be the view of Beis David at the beginning of his discussion. One cannot reject this by arguing that after he filled himself with them, how can he now recite a blessing? This argument is nothing, as is evident from the wording of Rosh regarding fruit, cited by the aforementioned Rav. Understand this. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 1; Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 1; Zechor LeAvraham, under dalet, paragraph 2; Beis Menuchah, paragraph 4. Nevertheless, in Kodshei David, R' David Chazan, of blessed memory, concludes as practical halachah that if one already ate that fruit in a cooked dish with bread, he does not subsequently recite a blessing when eating it as fruit before Birkas HaMazon; see there. R' Zachor LeYitzchak Harari, of blessed memory, cites this in siman 48 and writes that to avoid doubt, one should either leave it until after Birkas HaMazon or bring another fruit with the same blessing, whether Borei Pri HaAdamah or Borei Pri HaEtz, recite the blessing over it, and intend to cover this food as well. He writes that pious men and people of deeds follow this practice; see there. Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 8, and Ben Ish Chai, parashas Naso, paragraph 11, write the same. See below, paragraph 9.
2) Shaarei Teshuvah writes there that in our country, where poor people eat bread in the morning and accompany it with radishes, onions, and raw squash, it appears that if they first eat these as an accompaniment to bread, then even if they afterward eat them without bread, they need not recite a blessing, as with other fruits. It appears that his words are correct specifically regarding raw squash and similar foods that are eaten as dessert. If one designates them as an accompaniment, he must initially eat some of them with bread, in accordance with the dissenting view cited in seif 3. Radishes and onions, however, are not eaten as dessert but specifically to stimulate the appetite. They are therefore considered foods brought as part of the meal, like vegetables and salted foods, and even when beginning to eat them one need not take care to eat them with bread, as stated in this seif. Nevertheless, it appears that one who acts stringently shall be blessed.
3) There: Even when eaten without bread, they do not require a blessing, etc. They are subordinate to the bread, and the primary food covers the subordinate food, as stated at the beginning of siman 212. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1, writes the same.
4) There: Even when eaten without bread, they do not require a blessing, etc. This implies that specifically when one establishes a meal upon bread are these considered foods brought as part of the meal and covered by the blessing over bread. If he eats only a small amount of bread, less than a kezayis, he must recite blessings over them. See Magen Avraham there, who deliberates about this. Therefore, when eating these foods outside a meal, one should take care not to eat bread at all. If he wishes to eat pas habaah bekisnin with them, see above, siman 168, paragraph 99.
5) There: Such as figs and grapes, etc. The same applies to anything brought as dessert. Even if it was before him when he recited HaMotzi, and even if it is cooked fruit, bread does not cover it, since it has no connection to bread. Shelah and Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, write the same; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 2; Beis Menuchah, paragraph 5. This refers to foods that are never customarily brought as part of the meal but are ordinarily eaten throughout the day, even outside mealtime, as Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes there. Fruit-filled pastries, however, such as apple pie or plum-filled pastries and the like, do not require a blessing. One who wishes to act stringently should take another fruit, either raw or cooked, recite Borei Pri HaEtz over it, and then eat those filled pastries as he wishes. Emek Berachah and Shelah; Eliyah Rabbah there. See Magen Avraham, paragraph 3, and our words above at the end of siman 168, paragraph 71.
