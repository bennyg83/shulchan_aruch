Even if he eats, etc. This is the opinion of the Tur. See his wording, where he writes in the name of his father, the Rosh, that there is room for doubt, etc., but when fruit is brought to him in the middle of the meal, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that although the Rosh did not state this explicitly, the Tur understood that since the Rosh wrote that no proof can be brought from there, it follows that he holds the fruit requires a blessing before it. His words are incomprehensible. Since the Rosh wrote only that no proof can be brought, how can his actual opinion be established? It appears to me that the matter is as follows. The Rosh first writes that there is room for doubt. His intention is that when one eats fruit without bread while the meal is principally based on other foods, it is obvious to him, in accordance with Rav Pappa, that a blessing is required before the fruit. Even if he eats some of the fruit with bread, that does not exempt what he eats without bread. His doubt concerns only one whose meal is based on the fruit itself, whether eating some with bread exempts what he eats without bread. He writes that reason supports exemption, because the principal establishment of the meal was upon this fruit. This is a logical conclusion, not an explicit proof. A seeming difficulty can be raised from the Yerushalmi, and therefore the Rosh writes that there is no proof from there. The Yerushalmi states in accordance with Rav Pappa that fruit eaten without bread after the meal requires a blessing both before and after. One could seemingly challenge the Rosh's doubt from the following case: when the meal is based on other foods and fruit is brought to be eaten with bread during the meal, but one eats some without bread, it is obvious to the Rosh that a blessing is required before it. Yet the Yerushalmi could have advised him to eat it during the meal and recite only the blessing before it. Since it did not say so, one might infer that no blessing before it is required, apparently contradicting Rav Pappa. One would then be forced to say that this case is different because he ate some of it with bread, and therefore that exempts even what he eats without bread, contrary to the Rosh's view. The Rosh therefore says that no such proof can be brought, because it is possible that a blessing before the fruit is indeed required. The Yerushalmi did not raise this point because it wished to challenge also that a blessing afterward should be required, which applies only after the meal. The Tur's words, “but when they bring,” are therefore clear and follow the Rosh. One may still ask why the Rosh presented this as a doubt resolved by his own reasoning, when the Yerushalmi seemingly proves it from the fact that Rav Huna did not recite any blessing over the dates he ate, because they were the principal substance of his meal. If a blessing before them were required, he should at least have eaten them during the meal and recited that blessing. It must be that no blessing was required at all. One cannot say that the Rosh initially had only a logical argument and afterward derived the conclusion from the Yerushalmi, because his language does not imply this. Moreover, the Tur cites the doubt and the reasoning, and according to this approach he should have resolved it from the Yerushalmi. It therefore appears that the Rosh was uncertain how the incident in the Yerushalmi occurred. Perhaps Rav Huna did in fact recite a blessing before the dates and omitted only the blessing afterward. The Yerushalmi asked that he should have eaten them after the meal and thereby gained a blessing afterward as well. It answered that the fruit was the principal substance of his meal, meaning: How could I defer the fruit until after the meal merely to gain another blessing, when I needed it as the main part of my eating? According to this possibility, even when the meal is based on fruit, a blessing before it is still required during the meal. Alternatively, perhaps he recited no blessing at all, even before the fruit, and the question concerned both blessings. The answer would then mean that because the fruit was the principal substance of his meal, no blessing was required even before it. The Rosh was therefore compelled to rely on reasoning. If so, why did he not reject the proof simply by saying that perhaps Rav Huna did recite a blessing before it? This is indeed what he meant when he said there is no proof, because the discussion about reciting a blessing afterward can be understood as asking only about the second blessing, while the first may actually have been recited. Everything is thus resolved. The conclusion of the Tur, based on the Rosh, is that when the meal is based on fruit and one eats some with bread and some without bread, what he eats without bread is exempt, whether it is eaten first or last.
And there are those who disagree. This is the opinion of Rabbeinu Yonah. The Beit Yosef writes in his name that when the meal is based on fruit and one first eats it specifically with bread, he is exempt if he later eats it without bread, but not the reverse. If he first eats it without bread, he must recite a blessing. The Beit Yosef inferred this from Rabbeinu Yonah's wording: “If at first he had nothing else with which to eat the bread except these fruits, and he began by eating the bread together with them, and afterward ate them without bread, even if at the end he does not eat any of them with bread, he does not recite a blessing over them. Since he made them the principal accompaniment to his bread from the beginning and began by eating them with bread, he is exempt afterward,” etc. The Beit Yosef inferred that Rabbeinu Yonah's phrase “from the beginning” is exact, and therefore wrote in his name that if he began without bread and only afterward ate them with bread, he must first recite a blessing. This is very difficult. Rabbeinu Yonah afterward cites the Yerushalmi and explains that Rav Huna ate dates after eating bread and did not recite even a blessing before them. He concludes that the reason was that the fruit was the principal substance of his meal, meaning that from the beginning he had established his meal upon them. Although he was now eating them without bread, no blessing was required, because the rule that fruit requires a blessing before it applies only when it was not brought at all to accompany the bread. His words explicitly imply that if he later ate some with bread, then even if he first ate some without bread no blessing is required, since from the beginning he intended to eat them with bread and they were the principal component of the meal. Furthermore, why did Rav Huna not recite any blessing? He should have eaten a little first without bread and thereby become obligated to recite a blessing. There is another difficulty from Rabbeinu Yonah's subsequent words. He writes that this resolves the common question why on the night of Pesach we do not recite the blessing “Who creates the fruit of the earth” over the second dipping as we do over the first. The reason is that from the beginning his intention was to eat the maror, since he knew he was obligated to eat it, and it is therefore considered something that comes because of the meal. For the same reason they recited the blessing “to eat the Pesach offering” over the Pesach offering and did not recite “by Whose word everything came to be,” because it was secondary to the bread eaten beforehand. In the case of maror he does not eat it with bread at all. Even if he later eats bread, the beginning was certainly without bread, for Rabbeinu Yonah says that it is secondary to the bread eaten beforehand, meaning before the blessing over the Pesach offering, and nevertheless that prior intention is effective. All the more so here, where what he later eats with bread should exempt what he initially ate without bread, since from the beginning his intention was to eat it and it forms the principal component of his meal. In fact, it would seem that even if he ate no bread with the fruit at all, he should be exempt, since the fruit is the principal component of the meal and follows the bread eaten earlier, just as with maror and the Pesach offering. Regarding wine as well, Rabbeinu Yonah explicitly writes that by right one should not recite a blessing over it because it follows the bread already eaten, except that wine is important and therefore establishes a blessing for itself. It appears, however, that Rabbeinu Yonah distinguishes those cases because it is evident that the meal is established upon them. Maror and the Pesach offering are Torah obligations, and everyone knows that wine is drunk after eating. This differs from one who bases his meal on fruit, because most people do not establish a meal upon fruit, and some authorities hold that such a person's intention is disregarded in comparison with common practice. Therefore he must make it evident that the fruit is part of the meal by eating some specifically with bread. Rav Huna likewise ate some afterward with bread, even though he first ate some without bread, relying on the bread he had eaten before. Rabbeinu Yonah's phrase, “and began to eat it with bread,” is therefore not exact. He states a greater novelty: beginning with bread alone is enough to exempt what he later eats without bread, even if he subsequently decided not to eat any more with bread. All the more so, if he later eats it with bread, it is evident that his intention remains connected to the bread, and what he ate first without bread follows what he later eats with bread. It is therefore clear that Rabbeinu Yonah does not disagree with the Tur on this point. The rule in this halacha is as follows. Fruit eaten with bread plainly requires no blessing at all. Fruit eaten without bread during the meal merely as dessert plainly requires a blessing before it, because it does not come because of the meal. When one eats some of the fruit with bread, however, distinctions apply. If he first eats it with bread and afterward without bread, he must recite a blessing before the latter unless he intended to eat more of it afterward with bread. Even if it happens that he ultimately does not eat more with bread, we follow his original intention. If he first eats it without bread, he must recite a blessing, because when the meal is based on other foods the fruit is not considered to come because of the meal. The Beit Yosef writes that when fruit is brought during a meal, it is proper to be careful that one's first eating of it should be with bread. This means that he should intend to eat it again afterward with bread, because otherwise he would be obligated to recite a blessing when eating it later without bread. In my humble opinion, this is not satisfactory. First, he may fail to have this intention and later eat the fruit without bread and without a blessing, contrary to the law. Second, he will certainly eat it afterward without bread, since the meal is not based upon the fruit. It therefore appears to me better that he first eat the fruit without bread and recite a blessing, because according to all opinions that eating requires a blessing. Why should he prevent himself from reciting a blessing? The Yerushalmi itself challenged Rav Huna that he should have acted in a way that would obligate him in a blessing. This applies when the meal is based on other foods, as is usually the case. A poor person, however, whose meal is based on fruit, should first eat it with bread and will then not need to recite a blessing over what he later eats without bread. This matter appears clear and illuminated, with God's help.
