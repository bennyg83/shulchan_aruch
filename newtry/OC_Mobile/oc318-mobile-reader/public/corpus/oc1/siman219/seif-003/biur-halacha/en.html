And two of them are Torah scholars - See Peri Megadim, who questions the Mechaber for omitting the view of Tosafos that two scholars are required in addition to the ten, since Rif and Rambam also agree with this approach. In truth, he was mistakenly drawn after the words of Beit Yosef. Perishah already wrote that there is a scribal error in Beit Yosef. On the contrary, Rif supports the view of Rambam, who holds the opposite of Tosafos. Eshkol and Hilchos Raavyah, which has recently been published, likewise record it this way. I found that the commentator to Chiddushei HaRaah already noted this; see there. Fundamentally, there is no difficulty with their position, for it is known that several Rishonim hold that wherever the Gemara does not conclude with a conclusive refutation, the ruling of the Amora is not rejected. Tosafos may follow the view of Rashbam in the chapter Chezkas HaBatim, Bava Basra 52b, s.v. kashya, who does not agree with those Rishonim. I later found that the Gra noted all this. One may also explain our case by saying that Mar Zutra held there is no reason to require two scholars in addition to the ten, for where do we find anywhere in the Torah a requirement of twelve people? Certainly, the two scholars are included among the ten. Know further that the wording of Tosafos as it appears before us is extremely abbreviated. See the later authorities, who struggled to explain it. In Tosafos Rabbeinu Yehudah the wording is much clearer: “Say that there must be ten and two scholars. This remains difficult. Since the matter was not resolved, we act stringently; but if they are unavailable, he should recite the blessing even without two scholars.” It is explicit that these are two distinct points. The Gra, in his explanation, independently reached the same conclusion; see there.
If Torah scholars are not available, etc. - See Biur HaGra, which implies that according to the Mechaber, who first ruled that only ten are required, there is no source for this law. Chemed Moshe likewise questioned it; see there. Nevertheless, in practice the Mechaber’s ruling appears correct. It is possible that even the Gra concedes that the presence of scholars is not indispensable. One who examines Halachos Gedolos will see that it does not quote Mar Zutra’s statement requiring two scholars at all. Perhaps it holds that since Rav Ashi challenged the statement repeatedly, he did not accept it. It may also hold that since earlier Amoraim, such as Rav Yehudah and Abaye, did not mention this requirement, the verse merely describes the ordinary practice of seeking elders so they would bless him. Rambam similarly writes that the listeners say, “May He Who bestowed good upon you,” and so forth, but not that elders are legally required. This parallels Bircas Chasanim according to Rav Nachman, who derives from “Boaz took ten men from the elders,” in Kesubos 7b. Certainly, “elders” there is not literal. He chose them so they would bless him, as the verse itself says that they blessed him. Rav Nachman must explain it this way because he does not accept the alternative derivation that elders were taken to expound the law concerning an Ammonite; examine the passage carefully. The same applies here. The version of Rif before Rabbeinu Yerucham also omitted Mar Zutra’s statement. HaRaah writes in his halachos that Rif did not include it and explains that this is apparently because the Gemara challenges it with “say instead,” and so forth; see there. Shitah Mekubetzes writes similarly, as does the explanation we gave for Halachos Gedolos. Therefore, although we rule that two Torah scholars are required, if scholars are unavailable it is reasonable that their absence does not invalidate the blessing, since Halachos Gedolos and Rif, according to the versions of HaRaah and Rabbeinu Yerucham, did not record this statement at all.
And some say that he has not fulfilled his obligation - See Tur, who proves from Abaye’s expression “he must” that the requirement is only the preferred manner of fulfilling the mitzvah. Halachos Gedolos, however, implies that there is an actual obligation; see there. HaRaah, in his halachos, agrees with Tur. Know that Machatzis HaShekel, at the beginning of the siman, implies that according to the latter opinion, one who recites the blessing without ten has recited a blessing in vain, because the blessing was never instituted without ten. According to him, since the Mechaber did not decide this dispute, one may not recite the blessing without ten. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, however, writes in his novellae that even according to the latter opinion it is not forbidden to recite it without ten as praise and thanksgiving. It is no less than an ordinary person reciting this blessing over another, although he has no obligation at all, as stated in the gloss in seif 4, and certainly the person himself may do so. If ten later become available, however, according to the latter opinion he must repeat the blessing as an obligation. Nevertheless, initially one should be careful not to recite it without ten, even merely as praise and thanksgiving, because if ten later become available he will not be able to repeat it as an obligation, since perhaps the halachah follows the first opinion and he already fulfilled his duty. See there. If, however, he explicitly intended not to fulfill his obligation with the first blessing, it is possible that all agree he may repeat it. Even according to the opinion that mitzvos do not require intent, that rule applies where one had no contrary intent, but not where one explicitly intended not to fulfill the mitzvah. This requires further examination.
