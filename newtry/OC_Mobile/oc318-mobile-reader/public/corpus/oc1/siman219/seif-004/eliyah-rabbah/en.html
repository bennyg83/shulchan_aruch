And he answered Amen, etc. Olat Tamid writes that the listener must also intend to fulfill his obligation, hear the blessing from beginning to end, and the one blessing must intend to discharge the listener, as above in siman 213. At first glance one can distinguish: there the blessings are phrased impersonally, whereas here the one blessing says "Who has bestowed good upon you." Nevertheless, it appears that the listener must intend to fulfill his obligation, as I will explain.
[Levush] Provided that he said, etc. Olat Tamid writes that even in this case he has fulfilled his obligation, and says that Ateret Tzvi wrote otherwise imprecisely. He did not realize that Ateret Tzvi simply copied the Levush. Be'er Sheva implies that Rabbenu Yonah, who gives this ruling, also holds that if the blessing was recited before fewer than ten one has not fulfilled his obligation. According to the opinion above that one does fulfill it after the fact, the same should apply here, and so appear Shulchan Aruch and Magen Avraham. Perhaps the Levush and Ateret Tzvi also mean only that if it is not said before ten, it should be recited without Hashem's Name and kingship, as above. Yet I find difficulty in the Gemara's challenge to Rabbi Yehudah, who said "you have exempted me from giving thanks": Abaye said thanksgiving requires ten, so what is the challenge if Abaye agrees that after the fact one fulfills the obligation? One may answer with difficulty that Rabbi Yehudah should not initially have answered Amen. Although the Gemara later answers that he did answer Amen, perhaps the questioner knew this but did not yet realize that answering Amen is equivalent to personally giving thanks. This requires study. Alternatively, the challenge is that Rabbi Yehudah should not have intended to fulfill his obligation; if he did not intend, then even by answering Amen he would not fulfill it.
