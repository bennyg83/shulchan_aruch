And he answered amen - See Mishnah Berurah. Although elsewhere we rule that hearing is equivalent to responding, even without answering amen, as explained in siman 213, this case is different. The person reciting the blessing is himself not obligated in it and says it only as praise and thanksgiving for his fellow’s deliverance. Therefore, if the rescued person did not answer amen, he has not fulfilled his obligation. This is Tur in the name of Rosh. All this follows the view that one may recite the blessing as praise and thanksgiving for another’s deliverance even though one is not personally obligated. See below in Biur Halachah, s.v. “and this is not,” etc. Shitah Mekubetzes offers another explanation: since the one reciting the blessing does not intend to discharge the rescued person’s obligation, the latter must at least answer amen within the brief interval required for speech after the blessing. See there. According to this reasoning, it would appear that elsewhere too, if one answered amen, the person reciting the blessing need not intend to discharge him. In truth this is not so; see the end of siman 213. Perhaps this case is preferable because it is impossible to intend to discharge him using this wording, as we wrote in Shaar HaTziyun. Rabbi Akiva Eiger gives another reason. The principle that hearing is equivalent to responding cannot apply here, because even if the rescued person himself explicitly recited the blessing in this wording it would not help. The blessing is addressed to the rescued person and is appropriate only for another person thanking Hashem for his fellow’s benefit. He must therefore answer amen to his fellow’s blessing. By doing so, he too at least acknowledges Hashem for the goodness bestowed upon him. Without this, the blessing is entirely ineffective for him. This explanation is very correct.
And this is not a blessing in vain - Magen Avraham agrees and writes that this also appears to be the Mechaber’s view, since he states the ruling without qualification, unlike Beit Yosef, who wrote that one should rebuke a person who recites Bircas HaGomel over his fellow. Eliyah Rabbah, however, writes that Beit Yosef’s view is primary, and that apart from a son or student, others may not recite it. Olas Tamid likewise writes that this is the view of most poskim; see there. This also appears correct. The gloss, which quotes Tur in the name of Rosh, is inherently difficult. Where do we find blessings that one is entirely unrequired to recite, yet is nevertheless permitted to say? Although Shehecheyanu is described as optional, that does not mean entirely optional. Rather, it is not an absolute obligation, but there remains a mitzvah to recite it. Moreover, why should this blessing differ from the blessing over a miracle in siman 218? Throughout that siman the authorities investigate over which events one may recite a blessing and over which one may not, until it is ruled that one may not even bless over a miracle performed for the tribes unless it affected most of Israel. No authority there suggests that even absent an obligation one may recite the blessing because he rejoices over the rescue of thousands of Jews. Necessarily, they reject Tur’s reasoning. Rav Chanan and the Sages recited the blessing over Rav Yehudah only because he was their teacher, as Ramban, Rashba, and HaRaah explain at the beginning of the sugya, because one is likewise obligated to bless over a miracle performed for his teacher. Although HaRaah later offers another reason, that one who rejoices over another must recite Bircas HaGomel, he also writes that one who rejoices over another may recite the blessing over a miracle. In his view, the requirement of a father or teacher applies only when one reaches the place where the miracle occurred, not when seeing the person himself. Since we do not rule this way in siman 218, seif 6, in the gloss, and the place of the miracle requires specifically one’s father, teacher, or most of Israel, we should not rule according to that view for Bircas HaGomel either. It is unreasonable to give Bircas HaGomel greater scope than the blessing “Who performed miracles,” which was instituted specifically over a miracle. It therefore appears that without the relationship of father or teacher, it is improper to recite the blessing, even a father over his son and similar cases. See what Shaarei Teshuvah cites in the name of Birkei Yosef; perhaps his reasoning is as we have written. Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 12, relies upon the gloss regarding a husband reciting it for his wife because one’s wife is like his own body. This too requires study. Just as a husband does not recite the blessing over miracles for his wife, as we wrote in siman 218, the same should apply here. For one’s father or teacher, however, it is reasonable that one must recite Bircas HaGomel, just as one must bless over a miracle performed for them, as in the Gemara’s account of Rav Chana of Baghdad, who blessed over his teacher Rav Yehudah. I saw that Eliyah Rabbah cites Ramban as holding that Bircas HaGomel is weaker: even for one’s father or teacher it is only optional, because the blessing for recovery rests upon the recovered person himself, not upon others. Meaning, this resembles the other ordinary human needs for which a person blesses every morning, such as “Who clothes the naked” and “Who releases the bound.” Could a student or son be required to bless because his teacher or father has clothing? A complete miracle is different, for one’s son or student must bless over it. Although elsewhere we do not find that the Sages permitted blessings where no obligation exists, perhaps because a complete miracle to one’s father or teacher itself creates an obligation, they also permitted Bircas HaGomel for them. Rambam entirely omits the incident of Rav Chana, and his reason requires study.
