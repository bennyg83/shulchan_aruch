The writer said: I saw fit to explain somewhat the laws of sanctity, its accessories, and other matters, and from the true Helper I ask help to explain this properly.
Know. The expressions mentioned in the Talmud and poskim in this matter are these: sanctity, accessories of sanctity, an accessory of an accessory, mitzvah, accessories of mitzvah, and an accessory of an accessory of a mitzvah. I will explain somewhat. Sanctity means Torah, Nevi'im, and Kesuvim; they are matters of sanctity. Gemaras and Midrashim are also included in matters of sanctity, and one may not treat them disgracefully. See Yoreh Deah 288, and in seif 7, note 13. Even printed books have sanctity; see Taz there, 271, note 8, and Magen Avraham 154, note 14, who wondered how people place sacred writings among the pages of books; see there. Eliyah Rabbah, note 9, wrote that according to the Taz there, note 7, where it is not fit for the stricter use, it is preferable to make a lighter use. One can say that this applies to accessories of sanctity, not to sanctity itself, and especially sacred writings, Nevi'im and Kesuvim, may require genizah. See Magen Avraham there, note 9, and with Hashem's help this will be explained below. If they contain names that may not be erased, one can say this is not erasure but merely covering. See responsa Me'il Tzedakah of the Gaon Rabbi Yonah Landsofer, siman 23, from the Gaon Panim Me'iros, regarding a synagogue where they want to plaster lime over the names. According to all, it is permitted. Even according to the Mechaber there, who forbids lime with water because it is erasure, when they bind pages of books with glue one can say this is not erasure but only covering. It still requires study.
There is a difference. There is a difference between Torah and Nevi'im and Kesuvim, for with a Torah scroll, marital relations are forbidden until there is a partition, as explained in siman 240, whereas for other books a vessel within a vessel suffices. In Yoreh Deah 282:8 he wrote that chumashim suffice with a vessel within a vessel, while in Orach Chaim 240 he wrote that chumashim are like a Torah scroll. One must distinguish between whether they are made as a scroll or not. So it also appears from the Beit Yosef, Orach Chaim 240, that Tosafot speaks where they are in scroll form, and that is the case there in Shulchan Aruch, while in Yoreh Deah it speaks where they are not in scroll form. See Beit Yosef there, s.v. when they are not in scroll form, and the halacha follows the Rosh. Nevertheless, in Orach Chaim he was stringent when they are made in scroll form. One must examine this, for in scroll form it implies that it is equal to a Torah scroll in all its laws. Otherwise, from where do we know that chumashim in scroll form require a partition for marital relations, since the statement was made about a house containing a Torah scroll? This proves that it is actually equal, except that one does not read publicly from chumashim because of the honor of the public. This is the view of the Rashba in the responsum brought by the Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah 282, regarding standing before chumashim, that if they are made in scroll form they are like a Torah scroll for all their matters. In Shulchan Aruch there, Rama, seif 2, he wrote that our chumashim do not require standing, and the Levush wrote similarly, because they are not made properly. But in scroll form they are like a Torah scroll. According to this, one also may sell properly made chumashim only to study Torah and marry a woman, and one stands before them. The Rambam's view in Laws of a Torah Scroll 10:1 and 10:2 is not so, for wherever there is one error or one missing letter, it does not have the sanctity of a Torah scroll; see there. You see that in Orach Chaim 240 and Yoreh Deah 282 he ruled not like him. What is marked Rambam means that whenever it is not made properly, he joined the Rambam's view. But if one complete chumash is made properly, its law is like a Torah scroll, and this requires study. In Yoreh Deah 282:19, placing chumashim on a Torah scroll is forbidden, not like the Rashba. This requires study according to the Rashba, for Megillah 27a and siman 134, seif 6, say that a cover for a chumash is forbidden.
And a Torah scroll. A Torah scroll written in other languages, whether the language or the script, does not have the sanctity of a Torah scroll, though it does have the sanctity of other books. See Magen Avraham, siman 334, note 17. Some say that other books, such as Gemaras and Midrashim, were certainly permitted to be written in any language, and regarding a fire one rescues Gemaras and Midrashim. The reason a Megillas Esther is not rescued, since it has no divine names, is that it was written not according to its required manner, for its law is to be in Ashuris script and on parchment. By contrast, Gemaras and Midrashim may be written in any form and language. See Yoreh Deah 282 there, and see Orach Chaim 301, seif 42 and 43; see there. If there are eighty-five letters that can be gathered in a Torah scroll, or if there is a divine name, it has sanctity, as explained in siman 334, seif 12.
And what. What they said regarding the sanctity of the Name, that it is forbidden to erase it: see Shach, Yoreh Deah 276, note 12, that even if it was not written for the sake of sanctity, nevertheless as long as it is not for the need of correction, it is forbidden to erase it. See responsa Me'il Tzedakah, siman 23, in the name of Panim Me'iros, regarding plastering lime over writing in a synagogue, which I brought above. See the Rambam, Laws of the Foundations of the Torah 5:1, in Hagahot Maimoniyot, and halacha 6 there regarding one who has it on his flesh; it implies that even if it was not written for the sake of sanctity, nevertheless it has sanctity. See below, siman 334, seif 21, and in Taz note 16 it implies that if it was not written for idolatry, it has sanctity. The Levush concluded that certainly it was written for idolatry; but those coins where there is doubt whether they intended the Creator, blessed be His Name, have sanctity, even though a Jew did not write it in sanctity and did not intend to sanctify it.
Say now. Matters of sanctity have levels: first, a valid Torah scroll; second, chumashim, meaning one complete book of the Chumash, for if something is missing from it, it does not have the law of the sanctity of chumashim; third, Nevi'im; fourth, Kesuvim. The Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah 282, in the name of the Rashba's responsum, wrote that chumashim are like a Torah scroll in all their matters, except that one does not read from them publicly because of the honor of the public. We already raised difficulties above. The Gemara in Megillah 26a states that chumashim are not like a Torah scroll, and so too in Yoreh Deah 282:19. In the Mishnah there, 26a, they buy scrolls, which according to the Ran means chumashim, showing that they are lighter than a Torah scroll. Rashi explained it as Nevi'im and Kesuvim. It is difficult to say that this speaks when they are not made in scroll form. In Orach Chaim below, 240:7, chumashim in scroll form are like a Torah scroll, implying that they have one law, for otherwise from where do we know to be stringent? In siman 153, seif 1, he ruled that chumashim are lighter, implying even in scroll form, since he wrote without qualification, and this requires study. Furthermore, in Biur he wrote in siman 153, seif 3, that a Torah scroll in which an error was found is like chumashim, although it is one complete chumash and in scroll form, and this requires study from siman 240:6, as I wrote. Regarding Nevi'im and Kesuvim, Yoreh Deah seems to treat them alike in 282:19 in the Rama, while Magen Avraham 153, note 2, considered whether Nevi'im are stricter. This affects many matters. Regarding rescuing from a fire on Shabbos in siman 334, Magen Avraham note 15, written Torah precedes Oral Torah; one can say that the stricter precedes: Torah before chumashim, chumashim before Nevi'im, etc. Also, a Torah written in ink on parchment according to its law certainly precedes one written on paper, etc. Regarding a get, whether it has sanctity: the Magen Avraham in 42, note 6, in the name of the Bach, says it has no sanctity, while Olas Chaim holds it has sanctity.
There is a difference. There are several differences between sanctity itself and accessories of sanctity. First, with sanctity itself, designation is significant: if one processed leather for tefillin, it is forbidden to write mundane writing on it, though from a stricter sanctity to a lighter one is permitted; see Magen Avraham 32, note 10, and siman 42. By contrast, with its accessories, designation is not significant. Second, regarding sanctity itself, a condition does not help at all once sanctity was written in it. Regarding its accessories, a condition helps even while it is in sanctity, as in siman 154, seif 8. But for a degrading use a condition does not help, as in siman 151, Magen Avraham note 14, regarding eating and drinking. See siman 42, Magen Avraham note 6; the reference 153 is a scribal error and it should say 151:11. Third, regarding sanctity itself, if it is sold, wherever it is, it remains in sanctity. Regarding its accessories, one can say that if the seven leaders of the city sold it in the presence of the townspeople, or if it was his own, even though he did not make a condition initially, it becomes profane. In Magen Avraham 153, note 14, he says that accessories of sanctity do not become profane. We objected there from Yoreh Deah 282, from the silver apples in the Tur, where according to the Rosh he renders them profane and the buyer may do with them whatever he wants. Also in siman 153, seif 18, the phrase to render them profane implies the objects themselves. If you say it speaks where he made a condition, then the Rambam would not have forbidden it, and likewise the case of seif 18. This requires study.
And know. The straps of tefillin are accessories of sanctity. But the place of the yud and dalet, according to Rashi, is sanctity itself. See Bach in siman 154. The bayis of the head tefillin can be considered sanctity itself, for the shin is certainly a halacha given to Moshe at Sinai. The hand tefillin bayis is an accessory of sanctity. See what I wrote in siman 154, Taz note 2, at length on this.
There is a difference. There is a difference between accessories of sanctity and accessories of mitzvah. The latter, meaning mitzvah items, may be discarded after their mitzvah; see siman 21. Accessories of sanctity require genizah in a guarded place, and in siman 154, seif 4, the word they may does not mean it is an obligation specifically; see there. Also, the rule that one may not lower from a stricter sanctity to a lighter one applies to sanctity, but with a mitzvah one may lower it. See Shach, Yoreh Deah 259, note 11, in the name of the Mordechai; see there. At first glance, this is subject to dispute: according to the Ran at the beginning of chapter Bnei Ha'ir, that a synagogue has sanctity, it is well understood. But according to the Ramban, that it is a mitzvah, and even so a synagogue may be used to buy an ark and may not be lowered, what can be said? We hold like the Ran, that a synagogue and all that is within it have sanctity; see Magen Avraham, note 28. Even so, it is forbidden to make from a chair benches to sit on, for this has the status of an accessory of sanctity of a Torah scroll, and it is forbidden to lower even within one sanctity from fixed to temporary, as in siman 154, seif 6, from an ark to a chair. But from somewhat temporary to more temporary it is permitted. See responsa Maharam Padua, siman 82, and Magen Avraham 104, note 13.
And know. It is forbidden to use an accessory of sanctity for mundane use even after it has worn out, and it requires genizah. An accessory of an accessory, meaning anything that does not serve the body of the sacred item, though an aron or ark in which a Torah scroll rests, even if it rests in a wrapper, is considered an accessory of sanctity; see Yoreh Deah 282:12 and Rambam, Laws of a Torah Scroll 10:4. Not only does an accessory of an accessory not require genizah, but even while it is fit for use it has no sanctity at all and may be used for ordinary purposes. I derive this from what the Beit Yosef wrote in siman 154, that one may lean on our table because a cloth is spread over it. So it appears from the Levush, siman 154, seif 3, that an accessory of an accessory may be used. Do not challenge me from siman 154, seif 6 in the Rama, where poles are forbidden, the wood used as markers, even though they are an accessory of an accessory, which would show that they have sanctity. On the contrary, from there is a proof, for in the rulings of Mahari, siman 225, he wrote that nevertheless a pole has the sanctity of the synagogue, like benches, etc.; this shows that otherwise it would have no sanctity at all. Therefore those who make a silver or tin cover for the hand tefillin have made only an accessory of an accessory, and it has no sanctity at all. But for the head tefillin it seems to be an accessory of sanctity because of the shin. See what I wrote in siman 154, Taz note 2.
And accessories. Accessories of mitzvah that may be discarded after their mitzvah refers only after they are no longer fit for their mitzvah, not while they are still fit. See siman 21 in the Bach, that a shofar and lulav after Rosh Hashanah, and a sukkah, nevertheless stand for their mitzvah for the next year, and it is forbidden to use them. See Magen Avraham 308, note 12, and Shabbos 36a, where it says a shofar is fit to draw water, which implies that after its mitzvah it is permitted. This requires study. See siman 21 and 666:8, that a degrading use is forbidden even with accessories of mitzvah. See the Ran, Ramban, and Baal HaMaor in Lulav HaGazul regarding that which they would bequeath their lulavim to their children; they asked why they did not make brooms from them. See there.
