Another sanctity equivalent to it. Regarding a Sefer Torah, the Shulchan Aruch rules at the beginning of Yoreh Deah, Hilchos Sefer Torah, that it is forbidden to sell one in order to purchase another, since this does not elevate its sanctity. Although no higher sanctity exists, it is nevertheless forbidden, for this is an unresolved question in the Gemara. Certainly, then, regarding other sacred objects whose sanctity can be elevated, it is forbidden to sell them in order to purchase equivalents. So writes the Ran. Beit Yosef, however, citing Mahari Abuhav, infers from the Tur’s words, “but conversely, to lower them from their sanctity, one may not,” that purchasing an equivalent is permitted. This is the permissive opinion cited here. I cannot understand Mahari Abuhav’s view or why the Shulchan Aruch brings it. If Mahari Abuhav refers also to a Sefer Torah and permits purchasing another Sefer Torah, the matter is an unresolved question, and we rule stringently regarding selling an old one to purchase a new one. Even if he follows the lenient view of Rabbeinu Manoach cited at the beginning of Yoreh Deah, the Tur explicitly rules there that it is forbidden. How, then, can Mahari Abuhav infer from the Tur’s words here that purchasing an equivalent is permitted? The Shulchan Aruch is likewise difficult, for in Yoreh Deah he rules stringently concerning a Sefer Torah, while here he records a dispute. If Mahari Abuhav refers only to other sacred objects and agrees that a Sefer Torah may not be exchanged for an equivalent, and the Shulchan Aruch likewise discusses only other sacred objects, the matter is still difficult, for the Ran argues that all the more so other sacred objects may not be exchanged for equivalents, since their sanctity can be elevated. Moreover, why did Mahari Abuhav infer this from the Tur rather than from the Mishnah itself, which says that sacred books may not be exchanged for a mantle, implying that they may be exchanged for equivalent books? The Gemara sought to resolve the question whether an old Sefer Torah may be sold to buy a new one from the Mishnah’s statement that a Sefer Torah may not be exchanged for sacred books, implying that Torah for Torah is permitted. It rejected the proof by explaining that the Mishnah deals with a completed sale: once it was sold, the proceeds may be used for another Sefer Torah, for otherwise what could be purchased with them? Thus, regarding a Sefer Torah there is a distinction between after the fact and initially. After the fact it is certainly permitted to buy another Sefer Torah, but initially the question remains unresolved. Regarding other sacred objects, however, even after the fact one can purchase something of greater sanctity, and therefore one could infer from the Mishnah’s prohibition only against lowering sanctity that purchasing an equivalent is permitted. Yet the Ran suggests that no inference can be drawn from the Mishnah, because once the Tanna stated regarding a Sefer Torah that only lowering sanctity is forbidden, he used the same formulation for the other objects. The Tur, however, grouped them all together in one statement, “but conversely, to lower them from their sanctity, one may not,” although he rules in Yoreh Deah that a Sefer Torah may not initially be exchanged for an equivalent. If equivalents were also forbidden for the other sacred objects, why did he not state the greater novelty that even an equivalent is forbidden? One may therefore say that because, for the other sacred objects, only lowering sanctity is forbidden, he included a Sefer Torah with them and relied on his ruling in Yoreh Deah that a Sefer Torah may not even be exchanged for an equivalent. This correctly explains Mahari Abuhav’s second difficulty. The first remains, for if exchanging other sacred objects for equivalents is more lenient than exchanging a Sefer Torah, how could the Gemara attempt to prove from the Sefer Torah clause that purchasing a new one is permitted? Perhaps it is forbidden, and the Mishnah uses the language of lowering sanctity only because of the other objects. This requires study. Nowadays we see many people sell sacred books and buy others with the proceeds. It appears that when a person initially purchases a book, his intention is to retain it only as long as he needs it, and if a better edition becomes available he will sell this one and buy the better one. This is effective even without an explicit stipulation. A similar principle appears elsewhere, including siman 154, seif 8, in the gloss.
