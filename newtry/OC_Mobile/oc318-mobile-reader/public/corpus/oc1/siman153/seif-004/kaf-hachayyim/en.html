(18) [Se'if 4] Whether it is permitted to buy, etc. Some forbid and some permit. Ran infers from the Gemara's question about a Torah scroll, which has no higher purchase available, that for lesser sacred objects with possible ascent it is plainly forbidden to sell and buy an equal object. Mahari Abuhav reads Tur's phrase 'but the reverse, to lower their sanctity, is forbidden' as objecting only to descent; buying an equal object is permitted, and he found this in Rabbeinu Yonah's name. Bach says this permission is only after sale; initially all agree one may not sell merely to buy an equal object, as Ran states. The Gemara's unresolved question asks whether a Torah scroll, with no possible ascent, may be sold initially; we rule stringently. Bach's silence on Shulchan Aruch implies he reads its dispute as after-the-fact only, while initial sale is forbidden by all, like Yoreh De'ah 270. Although Beis Yosef's Ran excerpt sounds initial, Ran on Rif implies that even after sale other objects' proceeds must ascend where possible, unlike a Torah scroll; Kesef Mishneh reads Rambam, Hilchos Tefillah 11:14, likewise. Eliyah Rabbah, Birkei Yosef, Maamar Mordechai, Nehar Shalom, and Yad Aharon defend this reading and answer Taz and Magen Avraham. Erech HaShulchan says Bedek HaBayis 154 explicitly shows Rabbeinu Yonah and Tur permit even initially, but concludes that even after sale ascent is required, following Rabbi Ephraim, Baal HaMaor, Ran, and Rashba. Chida retracts his Birkei Yosef agreement that Maran limits the dispute to after-the-fact, because Bedek HaBayis shows Maran understood Rabbeinu Yonah as permitting initially. He suggests Maran may accept that view only after the fact. Practically, even if Maran understood an initial dispute, the views can be reconciled like Bach: all forbid initially, while they dispute after sale; Meiri also implies this. Most poskim hold the dispute is after sale and initial sale is universally forbidden, and most read Maran likewise. Moreover, when Maran gives a stringent 'some say' followed by a lenient 'some say,' we rule like the latter but initially heed the former. Therefore, one may not initially sell a sacred object to buy its equal. After sale, if possible buy something more sacred. If ascent is impossible; for example, they need an equal object and not the higher one, or already purchased an equal one; they may rely on the permitting view. See below, siman 154, paragraph 73.
(19) Taz observes that nowadays many sell sacred books and buy others with the proceeds. Presumably at initial purchase each person implicitly intends to retain the book only while needed and sell it if a finer one becomes available; such implicit conditions are recognized elsewhere, including siman 154:8, gloss. The reason appears to be that sale to buy a more halachically valid or more beautiful book is permitted, as Kol Yaakov on Yoreh De'ah 270:2 states. The proceeds should be entrusted to another until purchase to prevent delay. Eliyah Rabbah's rationale that an individual's books may be sold under se'if 10 is not accepted, as explained there. See below, paragraph 88.
