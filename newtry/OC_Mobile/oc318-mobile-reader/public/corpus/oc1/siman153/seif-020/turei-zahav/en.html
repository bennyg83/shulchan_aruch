The community cannot take possession of it. Beit Yosef, in the name of Maharik, siman 70, explains that no presumption applies to a Sefer Torah that from the outset was made on condition that the public read from it and that it remain constantly in the synagogue until the owner recalls it. Even witnesses do not suffice to say that it belongs to the community, because they are interested parties, and removing their interest does not help unless the community has another Sefer Torah as accurate and valid as this one. Responsa Maharshal, siman 16, discusses one who placed a Sefer Torah in the ark many years earlier. It remained there and had sacred mantles belonging to the community, and for years in the donor's presence no one heard him say, “It is mine and I did not give it absolutely.” It was, however, called by his name, as is customary for wealthy people, each of whom purchases a Sefer Torah and places it in the ark, while everyone knows which Sefer Torah is his. Maharshal ruled that if the donor himself later seeks to take it back, he is not believed without proof, because the presumption is that he consecrated it, and he is the one seeking to remove it and therefore bears the burden of proof. For this reason, I have seen and heard that when people donate a Sefer Torah to a synagogue, they stipulate publicly that the gift is not absolute. One who failed to stipulate caused his own loss. He further writes that an earlier case had occurred in which the owner took back the Sefer Torah, but that is no proof, for perhaps it was done unlawfully. My grandfather, Rabbi Yitzchak son of Betzalel, of blessed memory, agreed with this ruling. It appears that this disputes the Shulchan Aruch's ruling here, which follows Maharik and holds that the congregation is not regarded as possessing it. The Shulchan Aruch's view is more reasonable, because once it is known that the Sefer Torah originally belonged to the individual, how can the congregation retain it on the basis of doubt and remove it from his certain prior ownership? The fact that it had communal mantles proves nothing, for the custom is to exchange mantles from one Sefer Torah to another, whether privately owned or communal.
