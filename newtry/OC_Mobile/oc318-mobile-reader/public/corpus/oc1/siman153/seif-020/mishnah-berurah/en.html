The public cannot. We do not say that he certainly transferred it absolutely to hekdesh. This is not comparable to the silver sacred vessels customarily brought to the synagogue, mentioned in seif 18, where the owners cannot remove them for non-sacred use. Here, by a Torah scroll, it is different, for this is the way people make a Torah scroll, on this condition, to place it in the synagogue so that the public will read from it, and so that it remain there until the day the owners decide to take it to their home. Even if it was dressed in covers belonging to the public and he did not object, we do not say that because of this he presumably already removed his hand from it, for the constant custom is to switch covers from one Torah scroll to another, whether privately owned or communal. However, several later authorities wrote that here too, regarding a Torah scroll, it speaks where we know clearly that at first he did not place it there absolutely, but only lent it to the synagogue for the public to read from it. It teaches that we do not say that since he left it there a long time, he presumably already transferred it absolutely to hekdesh. But if he placed it there without specification, we say that he presumably consecrated it forever, like the law of sacred vessels in seif 18. See the Magen Avraham, seif katan 22, who wrote that the common custom today is to sell a Torah scroll even if it was initially given to the synagogue to read from it, and he wrote that since they practiced this way, it is as though they stipulated explicitly from the beginning that the sanctity of the public should not take effect upon them. According to this, it appears that all agree that one should not be stringent in this after the fact, but initially it is certainly good to stipulate explicitly that he is not giving it absolutely.
To retain it. Even if there are witnesses from that city that it was given to the public, they are not effective, for they are interested parties in their testimony. Even if they want to remove themselves from it, it does not help unless they have another Torah scroll that is as precise and valid as this one. A settlement had no communal Torah scroll and read from an individual's Torah scroll. Afterward the public bought a Torah scroll, and the individual claimed that he has a chazakah that they should read specifically from his Torah scroll. This is not a chazakah, for it has no claim with it, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 149, in the gloss, see there. Nevertheless, one should not diminish the honor of the individual's Torah scroll; rather, they should read each Shabbos from a different Torah scroll.
