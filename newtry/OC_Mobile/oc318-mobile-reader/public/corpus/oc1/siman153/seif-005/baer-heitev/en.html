We may change. The Kessef Mishneh asked: do we not hold that designation alone is not significant? See there. The Magen Avraham answered that the reason it is forbidden to change the money is that the townspeople gave the money only with the intention that they build a synagogue, and it is forbidden to change it without their consent. What is written in seif 8, that designation alone is not significant, concerns a case where the community bought it with unconsecrated money that they collected without specification. The same applies to new bricks; one may change them, as the Kenesses HaGedolah writes. It is obvious that the seven leaders of the city, in the presence of the townspeople, may change the money, end quote of the Magen Avraham. The Taz wrote that certainly what is written that designation alone is not significant is not relevant here, for in anything to which a vow of sanctity applies, he is obligated to fulfill it because of his vow; if he comes to fulfill it and designated something for that purpose, he may change it, for designation alone is not significant, and fulfill it in another manner according to what he vowed. Therefore, in the case of one who built an unspecified house, where it is written in seif 8 that it is not sanctified until they use it, nevertheless he has not fulfilled his vow and must fulfill it by making it into a synagogue and praying in it. It is only that as long as he has not prayed in it, it has no sanctity. So too, regarding new bricks, the matter is exactly so: he must fulfill his vow and make from them a synagogue, but before the act they have no sanctity. Yet the one who vowed must fulfill his vow. What is written here, that one may not change the money except to a more severe sanctity, is not because there is sanctity in the money itself, for certainly designation alone is not significant. Rather, if they wish to fulfill their vow, having collected for a synagogue, by making some other mitzvah from the money and to say that they have thereby discharged their obligation, we do not say this; rather, they have not fulfilled their obligation until they spend it specifically for a synagogue. If they wish to spend this money for another matter and place other money in its stead for the synagogue, it is certainly permitted, for there is no sanctity in the body of the money collected; only that they have not fulfilled the mitzvah that is incumbent upon them until they build a synagogue. This is explicit in this siman, seif 13, that where they collected money one may spend it for a mitzvah, implying even for a mitzvah of lesser sanctity. That is where he spends the money as a loan until he repays the money. All this applies only to something that has only designation alone. But with something in which there has already been an act of sanctity, or where the thing itself has sanctity, it is forbidden even for a mitzvah matter. Therefore, with old bricks that had already been in a synagogue, it is forbidden to take from them as a loan and put others in their place. This is the language of the Taz; see there. The Re’em, part 1, siman 53, wrote that collecting the money is considered an act and not mere designation, and so wrote the Bach; see there. See Shach, Yoreh Deah, siman 259. The Magen Avraham challenged him; see there. The Yad Aharon resolves it; see there. See Eliyah Rabbah, s.k. 18, for what he answers regarding the Magen Avraham’s astonishment at Maharam Lublin and at the Mishnah Berurah.
As they wish. Even for a mundane purpose. The reason is that the townspeople transferred their intent to the money. See Magen Avraham, Taz, and Bach. The Taz writes that this is specifically for a mitzvah purpose, and public needs are also considered a mitzvah purpose. See Neeman Shmuel, siman 108.
Wood and stones. The Taz wrote: the words of the Rama require study, for since the Shulchan Aruch wrote that the money has sanctity upon it, who can say that the sanctity departed through buying the wood? Also, what is written here, where they brought wood from their houses for the need of building a synagogue, that there is a distinction whether it came into the treasurer’s hand or not, is difficult. Does coming into the treasurer’s hand remove it from the category that designation alone is not significant? Regarding the essence of the matter, we have already explained that there is no consecration at all in the body of the money wherever it is within the rule that designation alone is not significant, as in the case where they collected money. Rather, the obligation upon him is because of his vow, to make specifically this thing and not something of lesser sanctity. If so, even if it did not come into the treasurer’s hand, end quote; see there. See Magen Avraham.
