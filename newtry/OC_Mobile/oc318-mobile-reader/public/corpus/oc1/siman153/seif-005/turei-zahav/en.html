They may change the surplus, etc. Rashi explains that they may lower it, because it has not yet come into actual use for the more stringent sanctity. This implies that it must at least be spent for a sacred purpose, but may be used even for a lesser sanctity. This also appears to be the intention of the Tur, Rambam, and Shulchan Aruch, who write at the end that it may be changed even to a lesser sanctity. Proof is that here they use the expression “they may change,” whereas in seif 7, when the seven trustees consented, the Shulchan Aruch writes that they may spend the money for whatever they wish, meaning even for a secular purpose. Here, where a mitzvah purpose is required, all communal needs are included within mitzvah purposes. See below for proof. The Kesef Mishneh asks in the name of Ramach: Why may they not change the money? This should be comparable to spinning thread for a garment, which all agree does not create sanctity. Even regarding bricks, the beginning of the chapter states that they have no sanctity; all the more so collected money. He therefore suggests explaining the passage about collected money differently, or ruling that it is not halachah. Beit Yosef later asks regarding a house built without designation: Just as money collected for building a synagogue acquires synagogue sanctity, why do donated wood and stones not acquire sanctity, so that they may be changed only to a higher sanctity? He answers that one may distinguish between materials delivered to the treasurer and those not delivered, interpreting the rule that new bricks lack sanctity as applying only before delivery. He admits that this is forced. My father-in-law, the Bach, called this true Torah, but I am astonished. There is no difficulty. The principle that designation alone is ineffective is not relevant to the donor’s vow. Whenever a person vows an object for a sacred purpose, he must fulfill his vow. If he designated a particular object but has not yet used it, he may substitute another because designation alone is ineffective, but he must still fulfill the vow in another way. Thus, one who built a house for a synagogue has not sanctified the building until prayer is held there, but he has not discharged his vow and must use it as a synagogue. The same applies to new bricks: he must fulfill his vow and use them for a synagogue, but before actual use they possess no inherent sanctity. The same applies to one who wove a garment for the dead, or even spun thread for such a garment. The garment itself is not prohibited for benefit, but the person who vowed must fulfill his vow and provide a garment, whichever one he chooses. The rule here that collected money may be changed only to a higher sanctity does not mean that the money itself has become sacred, for designation alone is ineffective. Rather, if the contributors wish to fulfill their obligation to build a synagogue by spending this money on another mitzvah, they have not fulfilled their obligation. They discharge it only by using it for the synagogue. If they wish to lend this money temporarily for another purpose and later replace it with other money for the synagogue, that is certainly permitted, because the collected coins themselves possess no sanctity. Their obligation remains until they build the synagogue. This is explicit in seif 13, where collected money may be used temporarily for a mitzvah until it is replenished. This applies only where there has been mere designation. Once an object has actually served a sacred purpose, sanctity inheres in the object, and it may not be lent even for another mitzvah with a replacement supplied. Therefore, old bricks that were part of a synagogue may not be lent and replaced by others. Proof that the obligation arises from the vow appears in Sanhedrin 48. Rava holds that designation alone is ineffective. The Gemara challenges him from the law that surplus money collected for a deceased person belongs to the heirs. It answers that the deceased suffers humiliation from the collection and waives that humiliation so that the surplus should go to his heirs. The question and answer concern only the surplus. The amount needed for the burial clearly belongs to the deceased. Why is it not mere designation? Necessarily, it must be given because of the vow made on his behalf. Mordechai likewise discusses one who vowed land for a synagogue but the priests prevented construction and the congregation wished to change its purpose. Maharam rules that although they may not change it without Reuven’s consent, Reuven likewise may not retract. It is no less than saying, “I will give to so-and-so,” which becomes a vow where that was his intention, and all the more so here, where poor people are involved. If they cannot build now, they must wait in case permission is later granted. The same applies to money collected for a synagogue. They are obligated by their vow and may not use it to discharge a lesser sacred obligation, although the money itself is not sacred. Beit Yosef himself later writes that new bricks do not revert to the donor; they may be lent, but remain communal property, like the land discussed by Maharam. This is the foundation of all the rules stated here. One difficulty remains. In Sanhedrin the surplus belongs to the heirs only because of the deceased’s waiver. Otherwise, the heirs would not receive it. Why, then, must the surplus here be spent at least for a lesser sanctity, as Rashi and the poskim imply? Why may it not be used for a secular purpose? It appears that when people collect money for a Sefer Torah or synagogue, they ordinarily intend from the outset that any surplus should be used for another mitzvah, since they removed it from their possession for a mitzvah. When they collect for a deceased person, however, their initial intent is that the surplus should go to the heirs, because the deceased suffers humiliation from the collection only if the surplus is not given to them. One might ask about Rashi’s explanation that the surplus must at least be used for a lesser sanctity. Megillah 27 states that Rava permits use of a surplus after collection. Abaye challenges him from a Baraisa: “When does this apply? When they did not stipulate; if they stipulated, it may even be used for hiring a messenger to the governor.” If this concerns money remaining after a sale, why should a stipulation help? It must concern money remaining after collection, implying that without a stipulation it may not be used. Why is this a difficulty? Perhaps a stipulation is needed only for a secular purpose, while no stipulation is needed for a lesser sanctity. It appears that the questioner regarded hiring a messenger for communal needs as a mitzvah purpose, since communal needs may be attended to on Shabbos.
And if they purchased with part of this money, etc. I do not understand Rama’s intent in this gloss. It derives from Beit Yosef’s discussion of a house built as a synagogue. Beit Yosef writes that this applies when they bring their own wood and stones and build with them. If, however, they collected money to build a synagogue, sanctity rests upon the money, and when they purchase wood and stones with it, the sanctity of the money transfers to those materials, which may then be changed only to a higher sanctity. He infers this from the rule that if money was collected and a surplus remained, the surplus may be used, implying that before the intended object was purchased the money may not be used otherwise. According to his view, this is obvious. If the money is sacred, purchasing wood with it plainly does not remove the sanctity; rather, the object is brought closer to actual sacred use. Beit Yosef needed to state this only to distinguish materials brought from one’s own home. But what does Rama’s gloss add here? Once the Shulchan Aruch has written that sanctity rests upon the money, who would imagine that purchasing wood removes it? Moreover, one who builds a house expressly as a synagogue certainly bought the wood for that purpose, yet it does not become sacred before prayer is held there. Rama also distinguishes between materials delivered to the treasurer and those not delivered. What relevance does delivery have? Does coming into the treasurer’s possession transform designation into actual use? Beit Yosef himself later withdrew this distinction and wrote, “It appears more correct,” etc. The Gemara’s challenge from the Baraisa concerning hiring a messenger also proves the point. If delivery to a treasurer created the prohibition, the Gemara could have answered that the Baraisa concerns money delivered to the treasurer. Clearly, delivery makes no difference. Although a distinction based on delivery appears in Yoreh Deah, beginning of siman 259, that concerns money given to a charity treasurer, who may not lend it because doing so may cause loss to the poor. Here, however, the issue is whether mere designation is effective, and delivery to the treasurer does not change that. As explained above, no inherent sanctity rests upon money that is merely designated. The obligation is to fulfill the vow by using it for the specified purpose and not for a lesser one. This is true even before delivery to a treasurer. Rama’s position therefore requires study.
