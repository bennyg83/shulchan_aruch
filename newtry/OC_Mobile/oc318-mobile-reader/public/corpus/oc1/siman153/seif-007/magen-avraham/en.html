All this. Meaning, that a village synagogue may be sold specifically when they have another, meaning that they have now built another. But if they had two synagogues previously, it is forbidden to sell one, for even demolishing one is forbidden, as written at the beginning of siman 152. This also clearly implies regarding a synagogue of a large city, that even if they built another, it is forbidden to sell the first, for that is what the discussion refers to. The reason is that they do not have power to remove its sanctity. But demolishing it is permitted, for it remains in its sanctity. Nevertheless, the wood, stones, and ground remain in their sanctity, and it is forbidden to use them and also to sell them, for perhaps there is one person at the end of the world who is not agreeable that their sanctity be removed. So it also implies in Rabbeinu Yerucham, nesiv 3, part 5: bricks or plaster of a synagogue may be sold as one sells a synagogue, and their law is exactly to go out to non-sacred status like the law of a synagogue, as written above. So too it implies in the Rambam, that they are exactly similar; see there. In the Mishnah Berurah he permitted using the stones even of a large city synagogue, for what do distant people care about the stones once they have another synagogue in which to pray? The Mishnah Berurah wrote in siman 33 that in a place where many people from elsewhere are not commonly found, it is called a village, even though many passersby are commonly found from time to time, since they are not fixed in the city but merely pass through casually. It is called a village. However, any place where many people from elsewhere are commonly found and come to pray there, such as a place where merchants gather regularly for commerce, or a place where there is a great scholar to whom many who need him and his Torah gather, is called a large city, for presumably they built it with the entire world in mind. But if it is clear to us that they built it only with the townspeople in mind, and others did not assist them, it is called a village. Although from the language of the Rosh and Rabbeinu Yerucham it implies that whenever many come to pray there, presumably the townspeople consecrated it with them in mind and we do not require that the townspeople’s intent at the time of construction be for the many, nevertheless from the language of Tosafot, the Ramban, and the Ran, it is explicit that the matter depends on the time of construction. Perhaps the Rosh and Rabbeinu Yerucham also hold this way, only they were brief in their language. End quote. I did not understand this, for according to everyone, even if they built without specification, we say that they built it with the whole world in mind.

There is no overreaching in it. This refers to land, and we do not say that he is like an agent, whose sale is void even by any amount. Ibid. It implies that with movable items, the law of overreaching applies.
To a lighter sanctity. It is a textual error, and this is how it should read: that it is forbidden to change it, it requires announcement. So it appears in the Darkei Moshe and in the responsum of the Rashba. For example, one instructed that this house should belong to the poor so that they eat its fruits, and instructed that the community not evade this. If so, the public cannot give it as a gift to another. The same applies that they may not sell without announcement, for whenever they did not announce it, we view it as though they cheapened it somewhat, and they are not permitted. Also, every consecrated item like this, the public may not change because of theft from the poor. Only with a charity fund, soup kitchen, and the like are they permitted, for if they need, they will collect more to meet their needs. The same applies to a synagogue lamp, where the synagogue has sufficient funds besides this; they may change it. But when the public does not give more to the poor except their fixed allotment, and one person consecrated land for its fruits to add to their allotment, it is obvious that the public is not permitted to change it. If so, whenever they did not announce it, it is an error in an explicit Mishnah. See Yoreh Deah, siman 256, seif 4.
