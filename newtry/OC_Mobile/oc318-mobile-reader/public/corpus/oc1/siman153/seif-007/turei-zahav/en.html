They may spend, etc. This means even for a secular purpose. The Shulchan Aruch therefore does not use the expression “they may change,” as it did above concerning collected surplus. Here, because the seven trustees stipulated, the money becomes entirely non-sacred. Accordingly, “even for hiring a messenger to the governor” is not a mitzvah purpose at all but a secular communal need, unlike what the questioner initially assumed, as discussed above.
Even if they built it from their own funds. They built it with the intention that it serve everyone, and the entire public acquired rights in it. If it is known that it was made only for a limited group, they may sell it even in a large city, as the Gemara states regarding the synagogue of the coppersmiths. Even a city synagogue is prohibited from being sold only when the sale would cause it to cease being a synagogue. If it will remain a synagogue, it may be transferred even from public ownership to an individual, following the Sages against Rabbi Meir in the chapter Bnei Ha'ir, 27a.
Unless they made it dependent upon the discretion of an individual. Beit Yosef cites Mahari Chaviv’s question: If this condition was made when the building was constructed, why say that it was dependent upon one individual? Even if they retained authority for themselves, they could sell it. He offers lengthy answers. In my view, there is no difficulty. The case is not one in which they explicitly said that the individual may do whatever he wishes, which would obviously be effective. Rather, he was the person who supervised and directed the construction and acted as he saw fit. Tosafos similarly explains Rav Ashi’s statement concerning Mata Mechasya, “It was built subject to my discretion,” to mean that the contributions were entrusted to him so he could act as he wished. This conduct reveals that he had authority to sell, because if he did so it was certainly because he perceived a benefit, and he was empowered to act. This rationale does not apply when many people jointly supervise and direct the construction.
It may be sold without public announcement. It may even be given as a gift, as stated in the chapter Bnei Ha'ir. By contrast, when they lack authority to change its use and sell without announcement, the sale may be voided like a beis din sale conducted without announcement. So states Rashba, siman 217. Rama discusses two separate matters. Regarding land, he says that no public announcement is required here, whereas movable property never requires announcement. He then says that the law of price fraud does not apply, referring to movable property, since price fraud never applies to land.
