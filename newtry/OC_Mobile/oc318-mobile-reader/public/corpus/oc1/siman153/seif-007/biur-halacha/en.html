That is when the townspeople sold it without the consent of their leaders. It appears that specifically if there are leaders in the city, then we say the townspeople do not have full authority over this sale. But if leaders were not appointed in the city, their sale is fully effective, like the seven leaders of the city in the presence of the townspeople. So it also appears from the Rashba's novellae to Megillah. See there.
The same applies if the seven leaders of the city sold, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, that appointment as leaders is not with fewer than seven. It is obvious that all seven must be present at the sale. What the Baer Heitev wrote, that it is not necessarily all seven, means that when the seven disagree, we follow the majority. By strict law, this too is specifically where the townspeople stipulated at the time they appointed them that they should conduct themselves this way. Otherwise, it is explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 12, seif 18, siman 13, seif 7, and siman 18, seif 1 in the Rama, that we follow the majority only in beis din, but not among communal appointees. See there. However, it is implied in Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 116, that in several regions they had the practice to follow the majority in this, for if we wait until everyone agrees, no matter will ever be completed. See there, and it is brought in Pischei Teshuvah, Choshen Mishpat, siman 163, seif katan 1. Know further that it is stated in the Yerushalmi that three from a synagogue are like the synagogue. This means a synagogue of private individuals, such as craftsmen and the like; if they chose three of their members to oversee their communal affairs, they are equivalent to all the members of the synagogue regarding sale, and they do not require seven leaders as with those appointed over the needs of an entire city, where specifically seven are required, as above. This is from the Ran and other poskim. The authority of these three over their synagogue is like the authority of seven over the synagogue of the townspeople.
But if they agreed, etc. From the Mechaber's plain wording it implies that the sanctity is automatically removed from the money through this sale, and even if they did not explicitly stipulate at the time of sale that the money should become mundane, they may later change it to mundane use. This is Peri Megadim in Mishbetzos Zahav; see there, for he wrote that this law is not clear to him. Afterward I found in Ritva on the sugya of they sold and there was extra that he wrote this in his second answer.
They may spend the money. The Mishnah Berurah wrote in responsum 33, and this is the substance of his words: even the seven leaders of the city in the presence of the townspeople do not have enough power to remove sanctity into mundane status except specifically in such a case where they sold it, for its sanctity takes effect at least at the time of the act upon the money, and then the sanctity is removed even from the money, whose sanctity is weaker. But they do not have power to remove sanctity for nothing. Therefore, if they demolished a synagogue, or a synagogue fell, the stones still remain in their sanctity, and even if the seven leaders of the city, in the presence of all the townspeople, agreed to take them out to mundane use, it does not help, unless they first built another synagogue and then demolished the old one, for then the sanctity takes effect on the new synagogue and the old one becomes mundane. The Magen Avraham brings this later in seif katan 27. However, this law is not clear, for it is proven only according to the view of the Ran, as the Peri Megadim and other later authorities wrote. The proof brought by the Magen Avraham from the Tosefta can be rejected, as written in Bigdei Yesha; see the commentary Minchas Bikkurim. But according to the Ramban and those who support him, it implies that the seven leaders in the presence of the townspeople do have sufficient power even without a sale, and whenever they agreed that they no longer need it, it automatically becomes mundane and it is permitted to use its wood and stones even for mundane use. See there in Magen Avraham, seif katan 27, where this law of the Mishnah Berurah was also not clear to him. This requires study.
And if the townspeople explicitly accepted upon themselves, etc. See Mishnah Berurah. Seemingly this is difficult: why should the power of an individual whom they appointed for this be greater than if they themselves sold it, where it is forbidden to lower the money from its sanctity? Perhaps here it speaks where the townspeople together with their leaders appointed that person, as in Peri Megadim, Mishbetzos Zahav, letter 7.
And whenever the seven leaders of the city, etc. This gloss belongs after the words above, for whatever they wish.
But of large towns, etc. Responsa Masas Binyamin, siman 33, wrote that in lands where the communities' practice is to establish leaders and officials over themselves, and they have full authority in all communal affairs, there is no distinction between villages and large towns. In every respect the leaders have the power to sell a synagogue even to drink beer in it, and they may also take the money out to mundane use, even if the townspeople object. This applies specifically when they built it from their own funds and no outside money was mixed into it, as Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 37 and Nahar Shalom. I did not copy it inside, because in our lands the officials do not have this power, as is understood, and some later authorities also question this; see Eliyah Rabbah.
Who come there, etc. Meaning, they also certainly made it with the intent of everyone. Magen Avraham wrote in the name of Mabit that they may sell a cemetery.
Unless they made it dependent on the will of an individual, etc. This does not mean that they explicitly stipulated that he may do whatever he wants. Rather, when they made it dependent on an individual to build as he wishes, we say that presumably they gave him permission also to sell according to his word. This is the Taz, who brought a proof from the words of Tosafot regarding Rav Ashi. Eliyah Rabbah and Be'er Heitev, however, did not copy the Taz's words at all, implying that this law was not clear to them. Afterward I found in Sefer Chemed Moshe that he also wrote that not every individual is equal in this regard without explicit stipulation, unless he is as important as Rav Ashi in his generation. Certainly an ordinary treasurer, even if he is involved and appointed over their building, is not someone upon whose intention and will everyone gives. See there. Therefore I did not copy this law of the Taz in the Mishnah Berurah.
With the agreement of the community. See what I wrote in Mishnah Berurah in the name of the Magen Avraham. According to this, it implies that if the sale was not in the presence of the townspeople, the sale does not take effect at all, for the community that authorized him did not intend this manner. But from the Beur HaGra it implies that its law is like that of the seven leaders without the presence of the townspeople in villages, where their sale is effective, though afterward the money must be elevated in sanctity.
An individual who built, etc., its law is like that of a communal synagogue, etc. The Rav was vague and did not explain what case is being discussed here, whether in a village or even in a large town. In Or Zarua, the source of this law, it is clear that it speaks both in a large town and in a village, and nevertheless they may sell it according to the community itself, or at most together with the donor, as in the end of the seif. However, Or Zarua wrote this because he follows his own reasoning there, that the advantage of a large town is that presumably outsiders contributed to the building expenses. Therefore in this case, where it was an individual synagogue and he consecrated it, even if he consecrated it to residents of a large town, the world at large did not acquire it, and they have no share in it, only the townspeople themselves, or also the donor has power in it if he reserved some right for himself, as at the end. Therefore it may be sold. But according to us, who maintain the view of those poskim that in a large town, even if they built it themselves without outside assistance, everyone has a share in it, for presumably they consecrated it also with the intent of everyone, then perhaps the same applies here: when the individual consecrated a synagogue to the townspeople, if they are residents of a large town, perhaps he consecrated it also with the intent of everyone, and then neither the intent of the donor nor of the community is effective in it, like the law of a synagogue of large towns. I found that Sefer Olas Tamid wrote this truly according to our ruling. See there. This is also somewhat implied by the flow of Responsa Maharash Duran, siman 7. See there. But according to this, the Rama was far too brief here. It is more reasonable to say that the Rama was intentionally vague, and that in every case it can be sold here. For if they said regarding a synagogue built by many that they consecrate it for everyone, they did not say this regarding an individual who gives a gift to a known community without any reservation, that he did not intend to transfer it to them completely but reserved power also for everyone, for that is not reasonable at all. Nor can one say that once it came into the hands of the townspeople it automatically became sanctified also for everyone, because presumably they consent to this, for Responsa Masas Binyamin already wrote that this is said specifically at the time of building, since presumably they built with that intent. But if it is clear to us that they did not intend this at all at the time of building, we do not say that it becomes sanctified automatically after the building, even though many people from the world at large come into it to pray, as we copied in Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 33. The same applies in our case. This also appears to be the tendency of the Beur HaGra's wording in his commentary, seif katan 24 and 25, to one who examines it. Nevertheless, this requires study.
And all this, etc. This refers to villages, as we explained in Mishnah Berurah. But regarding large towns, even if they built another one first, they are forbidden to sell the first, as the Magen Avraham wrote. However, all this is only according to his view; see there. But according to the Taz and those who support him, whom I brought in Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 41, there is no proof for this.
It is forbidden to sell it. See Magen Avraham, that to purchase with this a higher sanctity is permitted, specifically in villages, as Eliyah Rabbah wrote. He wrote similarly later in seif 13, seif katan 33. See below in Biur Halacha what we will write there regarding this. It appears that this is specifically in a case where there is no concern for negligence, for otherwise it stands to reason that it is forbidden, lest afterward he not purchase the sacred item, as the Magen Avraham wrote regarding buying another one with its money. Although regarding buying a higher sanctity he did not mention this, one may say that he stated it in the first case and the same applies in the latter case.
To change it, etc. This derives from a responsum of the Rashba. Seemingly, according to the Magen Avraham's view that we do not read the text as saying to a lower sanctity, its meaning is specifically when it is forbidden to change it at all, even to a higher sanctity, such as where the one who consecrated it commanded so; then it requires public announcement. But if they have permission to change it to a higher sanctity, such as through the seven leaders without the presence of the townspeople, as above, then it is considered theirs and does not require announcement. This is in fact proven to be the Rashba's intent, for he makes the reason depend on the fact that he could give it as a gift, and he himself holds in his novellae to the chapter Bnei Ha'ir that for a gift, the seven leaders alone suffice. This is also the view of the Ramban. However, it is difficult, because according to this, following the Magen Avraham's own ruling later in seif katan 26 that a gift requires specifically the seven leaders in the presence of the townspeople, it follows that a sale by the seven leaders without the presence of the townspeople should require announcement. Perhaps the Magen Avraham relied mainly on the reason of the Ran, which he copied in his name in seif katan 18. His wording there is: we found announcement only in a matter imposed upon beis din to do, such as when beis din sells in order to collect for others, etc. But regarding something that we do by our own will and do not need beis din for, announcement was not mentioned. This clearly implies that the seven leaders alone also do not require announcement, for they are the townspeople themselves and their essence, selling what is theirs. So it is also implied by the wording of the Tur, brought by the Rama in Choshen Mishpat, siman 103, seif 1, in the gloss, who wrote that since it is sold against his will by beis din, appraisal and announcement are required. See there. In truth, it is explained in the Ran and other early authorities there that the seven leaders alone are at least no weaker than all the townspeople without the seven leaders. If so, if we say that through the seven leaders alone it requires announcement because they may not change it to a lower sanctity, then if all the townspeople sold it without the seven leaders it should also require announcement, for they too may not change it to a lower sanctity. That is not reasonable at all, that townspeople selling what is theirs require announcement. Rather, it must be that this does not depend on that at all. Although the Sages were stringent regarding sacred items not to lower them or their money, and decreed that their power is not strong enough to remove them to mundane status unless the townspeople agree in the presence of their heads and important men, nevertheless this has no bearing on requiring those who sell what is theirs, in order to elevate it in sanctity, to make an announcement. Similarly, the same applies to the seven leaders, who are the main representatives of the townspeople: although the Sages decreed that they may not remove sanctity unless all the townspeople stand there at the time of removal, this is not proof that they did not rely on them regarding the sale price. It further stands to reason that the Rashba also agrees to the Ran's reason, but did not need it according to his view regarding gifts, since he holds that even the seven leaders alone suffice. I have elaborated on all this because I saw that Eliyah Rabbah challenges the Magen Avraham and presses to maintain our version and to be stringent where one may not change it to a lower sanctity, requiring announcement, and he presses the words of the Rashba and Ran. In my humble opinion, the main ruling is like the Magen Avraham, as we have written.
