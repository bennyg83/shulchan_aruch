Seif katan 5: But there is, etc. Meaning, when a synagogue was sold without the townspeople being present, it is presumed that they do not wish their mitzvah to be entirely forgotten and that the synagogue’s sanctity should transfer to the proceeds. The sale proceeds may therefore not be changed to another use. Regarding money merely collected, however, there is obviously no inherent sanctity. The prohibition against changing it is because the townspeople contributed only for the synagogue. Magen Avraham then asks from money collected for a deceased person, which is only designation, yet we do not explain the restriction there merely by saying that the people gave only for the deceased. He answers that the reason here is likewise comparable to the deceased. There, although it is only designation, changing the use is prohibited because it is degrading once money was collected specifically for the deceased. So too, when townspeople gave money specifically for a synagogue and it was collected for that purpose, changing its use is degrading. This does not apply to an individual donor, since there was no public collection and therefore no degradation; the fact that he gave it for a synagogue does not by itself prevent him from changing it. The same applies when they purchase materials with communal funds that had previously been collected without designation for a synagogue. Seif 8, concerning a synagogue that was built but not yet used for prayer, deals with such a case: designation alone is ineffective. The same applies to new bricks donated by an individual, or to bricks acquired with public funds originally collected for another purpose and only afterward designated for a synagogue.
