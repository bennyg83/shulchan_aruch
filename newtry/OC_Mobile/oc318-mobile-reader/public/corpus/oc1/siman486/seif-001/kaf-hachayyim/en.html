1) [Se'if 1] Regarding the measure of an olive-sized quantity, some say it is approximately half an egg. Beis Yosef writes this in the name of Tosafos, Chullin 103b, and this also appears in Tosafos, Eruvin 80b. Piskei Tosafos, Chullin, subsection 272; Mordechai, chapter Arvei Pesachim, order of Pesach; Terumas HaDeshen, siman 139; Pesakim U'Kesavim, siman 26; Derashos Maharil, order of the Haggadah; and Mahari Weil, siman 123, write likewise. The Shulchan Aruch presents this as the opinion of some authorities because it is not universally accepted. Tosafos there cite an opinion that an olive-sized quantity is less than one third of an egg. This is also explained by Magen Avraham, Peri Chadash, and the other later authorities. The Shulchan Aruch adopts approximately half an egg as a stringency in order to satisfy all opinions. Kisei Eliyahu, subsection 1, likewise writes that in practice one should not be lenient regarding an obligation, such as an olive-sized quantity of matzah or maror, or the quantity requiring an after-blessing, unless one eats specifically half an egg. See there. Eshel Avraham writes likewise. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1, writes that regarding maror, which is rabbinic, and every other rabbinic requirement whose measure is an olive-sized quantity, one may rely upon the authorities who define it as slightly less than one third of an egg. Regarding matzah, which is required under Torah law, and every other Torah requirement, one should be stringent like the first opinion. Regarding an after-blessing and Birkas HaMazon whose minimum is an olive-sized quantity, the principle that a doubtful blessing is ruled leniently applies, and one should not recite the blessing until eating approximately half an egg. Initially, one should avoid entering a doubtful blessing situation. For food not requiring an olive-sized quantity, one should eat either approximately half an egg or considerably less than one third of an egg. One must likewise be careful with other laws where a larger measure creates leniency and a smaller measure creates stringency, such as eliminating chametz. These are his words. His statement that one may rely upon the lenient opinion regarding rabbinic maror appears to apply specifically to determining after the fact that one fulfilled the obligation when one lacks half an egg or cannot eat more than one third of an egg. Regarding the blessing over eating maror, however, one should not recite it over less than half an egg, because the established principle is that a doubtful blessing is ruled leniently. Since many authorities maintain that an olive-sized quantity is approximately half an egg, how could one recite, 'Who sanctified us,' and so forth, over less? We already wrote above in siman 168, subsection 46, that according to Rambam and several authorities, an egg is measured as eighteen Egyptian dirhams. Regarding foods, including an olive-sized quantity of matzah on the first night of Pesach, an olive-sized quantity of maror, and an olive-sized quantity requiring an after-blessing, all are measured by weight based upon an egg of eighteen dirhams. An olive-sized quantity, half an egg, is nine dirhams. See there, where we wrote this in the name of several authorities. This is the custom, both to fulfill the mitzvah according to all opinions and because of doubtful blessings.
