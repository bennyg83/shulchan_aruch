Seif 1: The measure of a kezayis, etc. This depends upon the disagreement between Tosafos and Rambam concerning eruv. Rambam rules in chapter 1 of Hilchos Eruvin, halachah 9, that the measure for an eruv is two meals, equal to eighteen dried figs, which are approximately six eggs. This follows Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah in Eruvin 82b and the Gemara’s conclusion there that half is deducted for the shopkeeper. His proof is the anonymous Mishnah at the end of Peah, which follows Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah. It teaches that one may not give a poor person less than a loaf purchased for a pundeyon when four seah of wheat sell for a sela. That loaf constitutes two meals, as the Mishnah there later states concerning one who possesses food for two meals.
Tosafos in Eruvin 82b, in the passage beginning “incidentally,” rule like Rabbi Shimon. They bring proof because the Gemara’s discussion elsewhere follows his view, saying that an egg sized amount conveys impurity, etc. This proof is not conclusive. Rambam explains that the egg mentioned elsewhere regarding impurity means an egg without its shell, and that the measures are not mathematically identical, as stated in Eruvin 83a.
Accordingly, Tosafos’ ruling like Rabbi Shimon appears difficult, since it conflicts with the anonymous Mishnah in Peah, which follows Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah. It seems, however, that the Mishnah in Peah provides no proof for Rambam. On the contrary, it supports Tosafos. The Mishnah says that Rabbi Shimon gives “two thirds,” rather than stating explicitly that the loaf is five and one third eggs. The reason is that Rabbi Shimon’s measure of two thirds is not the universal measure of two meals. Rather, the entire loaf is the Torah measure concerning a house afflicted with tzaraas, because the verse says, “one who eats in the house,” requiring the measure of an eating. Half the loaf is one meal. This is the pras stated everywhere, meaning half of a loaf that consists of two meals. Regarding eruv, however, all the authorities intended to be lenient. Rabbi Shimon therefore says that two thirds of that loaf suffice for two meals. Rashi writes similarly there, 82b, in the passage beginning “half of it.”
This explains the continuation, “half of it for a house afflicted with tzaraas.” One should not ask why the Mishnah failed to give the measure explicitly. It tells us that the principal measure of the loaf is half of it for the afflicted house. This also explains the order of the Mishnah in Kelim, chapter 17, mishnah 11, which lists Rabbi Shimon before Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah. Here it was necessary to mention “half of it,” teaching that the primary measure of the loaf concerns the afflicted house and that Rabbi Shimon was lenient only for eruv. Therefore Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah is listed before Rabbi Shimon in Peah, so that one not mistakenly think the clause “half of it” applies only to Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah if he had been listed last. In Kelim, however, the dispute is quoted incidentally, so it was unnecessary to repeat the clause concerning half of it, and Rabbi Shimon is listed first beside Rabbi Yehudah, his disputant.
This resolves the ruling of Tur and Shulchan Aruch, siman 409, who write that two meals equal approximately eight eggs, an apparently unsupported ruling. They follow Rabbi Yehoshua at the beginning of Maseches Parah, who says that the pras mentioned regarding one who eats in an afflicted house is one third of a kav. He agrees with Rabbi Shimon that half the loaf is one meal regarding the afflicted house, but Rabbi Shimon was lenient for eruv and required only two thirds. Rabbi Yehoshua maintains that the pras always means half that loaf. Since Rabbi Yehoshua states his opinion without qualification and we do not find that he adopts the leniency regarding eruv, Tur and Shulchan Aruch understand the measure as eight eggs.
At first glance one might explain the discussion in Eruvin differently. The Gemara asks that one statement of Rabbi Chisda contradicts another, and answers by distinguishing between a case where the householder provides wood and one where he does not. Why not say that Rabbi Chisda retracted his first answer, deduct one third, because of the Gemara’s question that according to one view nine measures remain, and therefore answered instead to deduct one half? What new principle is taught by the two answers, that when wood is supplied only one third is deducted, and when it is not supplied, etc.?
The explanation is that Rabbi Chisda initially answered, “deduct one third,” because he was troubled by the contradiction between the two Mishnayos at the end of Peah, just as the Jerusalem Talmud asks. There it says: Here you say that one may not give less to the poor at the threshing floor, while elsewhere you say that one may not give less to a poor traveler. Since the loaf purchased for a pundeyon equals half a kav of wheat, when grain requiring no baking expense is distributed, one should give more. Rabbi Huna answers: Deduct one third for expenses. The loaf purchased for a pundeyon is made from half a kav minus the one third earned by the shopkeeper for the expense of baking. Since half a kav of wheat sells for a pundeyon, the shopkeeper deducts his expense when selling the loaf. The half kav of grain distributed at the threshing floor is therefore actually one third more than the measure of the loaf purchased for a pundeyon.
This is precisely Rabbi Chisda’s first answer. He did not answer to deduct one half because he rules like Rabbi Shimon and interprets the anonymous Mishnah in Peah according to Rabbi Shimon. Deducting one third leaves two thirds of half a kav, namely eight eggs, exactly Rabbi Shimon’s measure. Rabbi Chisda was later still troubled by the baraisa concerning the neighboring measures, which could not be resolved by deducting one third, as the Gemara asks, “According to one authority there are still nine,” etc. He then answered to deduct one half.
The Gemara’s sequence is thus clear. Initially it attempted to resolve the baraisa with Rabbi Chisda’s first answer. Because of the remaining difficulty, he gave the second answer, deduct one half. The Gemara then properly asks that Rabbi Chisda’s two answers contradict one another, and resolves that one case concerns where wood is provided, while the other does not. When food is given to a poor person, the cost of wood need not be calculated, and the loaf purchased for a pundeyon is measured in a case where the householder supplies the wood, so the shopkeeper deducts only one third. Where he does not supply the wood, a larger deduction is made.
Accordingly, the anonymous Mishnah in Peah follows Rabbi Shimon, and Rabbi Chisda agrees with him. Rabbi Huna in the Jerusalem Talmud likewise agrees, since he answers to deduct one third in order to establish the anonymous Mishnah like Rabbi Shimon. Tosafos therefore rule correctly like Rabbi Shimon. Rambam’s proof from the Gemara’s phrase, “Who are the Sages? Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah,” which suggests that the halachah follows him, is not decisive for Tosafos, because the anonymous Mishnah in Peah follows Rabbi Shimon, and the halachah follows an anonymous Mishnah.
A difficulty nevertheless remains from Eruvin 82b, which teaches that two meals equal eighteen dried figs. According to Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah, a dried fig is one third of an egg, and Rambam rules accordingly. The difficulty is even greater according to Tosafos, who rule like Rabbi Shimon, since a kezayis is smaller than a dried fig, as stated in Shabbos 91a. Yoma 80a states that the throat cannot hold more than an egg, while Kerisos 14a states that it cannot hold more than two olive sized amounts. See Magen Avraham and Tosafos there, in the passage beginning “incidentally.” Because of this, Tosafos write that regarding eruv we rule like Rabbi Meir. The Gemara likewise asks, “According to whom did your father act? According to Rabbi Meir.” See there.
Rabbi Meir’s precise measure is not stated. The Gemara says that it equals two loaves of the type made in Nehar Pafis. It is possible, according to Tosafos, that the measure of Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehudah is twelve eggs. A dried fig would then equal two thirds of an egg, and a kezayis, which is smaller than a dried fig, would equal half an egg. All the passages would then be reconciled.
Rambam’s view remains difficult, since he explicitly writes that a dried fig equals one third of an egg. It appears that Rambam’s ruling that six eggs equal eighteen dried figs, as the Gemara states, refers to eggs with their shells. A dried fig is one third of an egg with its shell. The statement in Yoma that the throat cannot hold more than an egg refers to an egg without its shell, as Eruvin says, “like it without its shell.” When the Gemara says that an olive is half an egg, it means half an egg without its shell. These are effectively the same measure, although they are not mathematically exact.
Rambam therefore rules regarding eruv that two meals equal six eggs. According to this, a kezayis is less than one third of an egg with its shell. Regarding the measure of a kezayis, however, he writes that it is half an egg, meaning without its shell. Shulchan Aruch likewise follows Rambam regarding eruv, while regarding Pesach it writes that a kezayis is half an egg. According to what I have explained, the words of Shulchan Aruch are correct both according to Tosafos, following Rabbi Meir, and according to Rambam.

And with vegetables, etc. Tosafos, Yoma 79a, in the passage beginning “a date,” cite the Jerusalem Talmud that an empty cavity must be compressed. This is similar to the Mishnah in Uktzin, chapter 2, concerning onion leaves when they contain a cavity, and to spongy bread. See there. The same passage discusses veal.
Menachos 54a, however, concludes that swollen veal is included only by Rabbinic law, and Rambam rules this way in chapter 4 of Hilchos Avos HaTumah and chapter 14 of Hilchos Maachalos Asuros. At first glance, Rabbah’s answer there appears intended only to prevent a difficulty against Reish Lakish. Once Reish Lakish is rejected for another reason, there is no need to force the explanation that the law is only Rabbinic, nor to say that rejection does not apply to prohibitions. At the beginning of HaMatznia, the Gemara leaves unresolved whether rejection applies. Although Kesef Mishneh in the name of Maharik gives a forced explanation, and this also resolves Tosafos’ question in Menachos, several passages appear to indicate that rejection does apply, including the beginning of chapter 3 of Taharos concerning gravy, etc.
Nevertheless, Rash and Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura write in Taharos, chapter 3, mishnah 4, that there is no rejection, and they understand the discussion straightforwardly. Ramban and Rashba at the beginning of HaMatznia likewise struggle to answer Tosafos’ question in Menachos and do not reject that ruling from halachah. I therefore cannot be lenient against them in practice.
