Se’if katan 1: “A merchant, etc.” He is particular about his professional reputation. The example should read: “such as pomegranate wine.”
For adulteration is objectionable even to non-Jews. Pomegranate wine is drunk medicinally, and if vinegar is mixed into it, it does not produce its medicinal effect.
“Which were spun not for their sake.” He cannot avoid the difficulty by means of this distinction, for this matter does not depend on professional expertise, and the non-Jew can also sell the strings to other non-Jews. See what is written about this in Responsa Chacham Tzvi, at the end of siman 39.
“Therefore it appears to me, etc., because it is not the usual practice of non-Jews, etc.” That is, this reason combines with the fact that he is a merchant and therefore presumably will not damage his professional reputation. Although the concern for his reputation is not very strong here, as the Magen Avraham wrote above, when it is combined with the fact that non-Jews do not ordinarily make such items, even this lesser professional concern is sufficient.
“And the proof he cites, etc.” The text should read: “from tzitzit to pomegranate wine.” He means that a proof is brought (as cited in Beit Yosef, Yoreh De’ah, siman 114) that regarding pomegranate wine we rely on the presumption that a craftsman will not damage his professional reputation, because the Talmud states that one may purchase a fringed tallit from a merchant. According to the Magen Avraham, however, what proof can be brought from a tallit? That case is different because it also has the rationale that non-Jews do not ordinarily make such an item.
“But only to learn that we rely, etc.” The presumption that a craftsman will not damage his professional reputation is a strong presumption. Consequently, regarding a tallit, where only a slight professional concern is needed because it combines with the fact that non-Jews do not ordinarily make such an item, it is effective. Regarding pomegranate wine, where there is a substantial incentive to adulterate it, the presumption concerning the craftsman’s reputation is nevertheless sufficient by itself, as stated in Avodah Zarah 39a.
“Chaviyot” is an acronym for: a piece of fish, meat, wine, and techelet.
According to one explanation in the Rashba, the view he cites as “another opinion” is Rashi’s view. Rav mentioned techelet and did not state the greater novelty of even white strings, because according to Rav’s own view white strings need not be spun lishmah.
“In truth, white strings do not require a seal.” Specifically regarding techelet there is concern that it may be kela ilan, whose color resembles techelet but which is invalid. Therefore two seals are required.
“But according to us, etc.” This requires explanation. In Avodah Zarah, Shmuel states that the acronym “beit” identifies items requiring two seals: meat, wine, and techelet. He omits a piece of fish, which is included under meat. According to Shmuel, who holds that spinning lishmah is required, as stated in Sukkah 9a and Menachot 42a, one should ask why he mentioned techelet rather than teaching the greater novelty that even white strings require two seals. It is strained to say that he mentioned techelet only because Rav did, for we see that he departed from Rav’s formulation by omitting a piece of fish, since it is automatically included under meat. All the more should he have departed from Rav and mentioned white strings, which would have practical halachic significance. One could answer that the Magen Avraham could in any case have answered as the Beit Yosef writes in response to Mahari’a: the prohibition against buying a tallit from a non-Jew who is not a craftsman applied specifically in the era of techelet, because one might suspect that it is kela ilan. Since genuine techelet is expensive, we are concerned that the non-Jew may lie. According to us, when we have only white strings, one may buy them even from a non-Jew who is not a craftsman, since there is not such a significant price difference between strings spun lishmah and those not spun lishmah. The Beit Yosef writes the contrary: since techelet can be tested, one might have thought that the non-Jew would not falsify it; nevertheless, we are concerned. All the more so regarding white strings, which cannot be tested to determine whether they were spun lishmah. The Magen Avraham could thus have answered his question similarly: Rav mentioned techelet as the greater novelty. Even techelet, which can be tested, is not sufficiently protected by one seal, because we are concerned that the non-Jew may nevertheless exert himself and falsify it; all the more so white strings, which cannot be tested for lishmah, require two seals. The Magen Avraham’s actual answer, however, is that according to Rav, white strings do not require two seals because he holds that spinning lishmah is unnecessary. According to Shmuel, techelet may indeed have been mentioned as the greater novelty, as explained. Eshel Avraham may be understood this way, and the matter is straightforward. Regarding the suggestion that the non-Jew benefits from the exchange, etc.: we are not concerned that a non-Jew will cause a Jew to transgress when the non-Jew derives no benefit. What benefit would he obtain from making the exchange solely to anger the Jew? According to the non-Jew’s mistaken view, the Jew receives no punishment for the matter because the non-Jew believes there is no prohibition; he merely wishes to anger the Jew because the Jew’s desire was not fulfilled. Since the Jew does not know that the exchange occurred, this does not meaningfully anger him. Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 39, writes likewise.
“And it is possible that if they are twisted, etc.” See above, siman 11. There the dispute among the halachic authorities is cited concerning one who finds cut and twisted threads: may we assume they are certainly tzitzit, since a person would not exert himself so much merely to use them in sewing a garment, or are we concerned that they were not spun lishmah and were made for a garment? Here, however, when purchasing cut and twisted threads from a merchant, it is possible that everyone agrees they are permitted, because the presumption that a craftsman will not damage his professional reputation can be added. Although we do not rely on that presumption by itself, as the Magen Avraham wrote above, it combines with the fact that non-Jews do not ordinarily make tzitzit for a garment. With cut and twisted threads, there is also the additional presumption that a person would not exert himself so much merely for a garment. Therefore, even the view that does not rely on that presumption alone when such strings are found may concede that, when they are purchased from a merchant, the combined presumptions may be relied upon. Alternatively, the statement may refer to the immediately preceding point: in the case of twisted strings, they may be permitted even with only one seal.
