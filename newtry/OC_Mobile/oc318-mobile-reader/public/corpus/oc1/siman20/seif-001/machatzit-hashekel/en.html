“But according to us,” etc. This needs explanation: there in Avodah Zarah we say that Shmuel said that the acronym “beit” requires two seals, namely meat, wine, and tekheles, and he omits a cut of fish, which is included in meat. If so, according to Shmuel, who holds that spinning lishmah is required, as in Sukkah 9 and Menachot 42, one should ask why he mentioned tekheles; he should have stated the greater novelty, even white strings. It is forced to say he mentioned it only because Rav did, for we see that he changed from Rav and omitted the fish-cut, since it is automatically learned from meat; all the more should he have changed from Rav and mentioned white strings, where there is practical halachic relevance. One could answer somewhat that the Magen Avraham could in any case have answered as the Beit Yosef wrote against Mahari’a: the rule not to buy a tallis from a non-Jew who is not a tradesman applies specifically in the time of tekheles, because one might say it is kela ilan; since tekheles is expensive, we fear the non-Jew may lie. But according to us, when we have only white, even from a non-Jew who is not a tradesman one may buy, since there is not such a price difference between spun lishmah and not lishmah. The Beit Yosef wrote the opposite: since tekheles can be checked, one should have said he will not falsify it, and nevertheless we are concerned; all the more with white, where one cannot check whether it was spun lishmah. If so, the Magen Avraham could likewise have answered his question: Rav mentioned tekheles as the greater novelty, that even tekheles, which can be checked, is still not enough with one seal, because we fear he may still trouble himself and falsify it; all the more white, which cannot be checked for lishmah, requires two seals. But the Magen Avraham’s true answer is that according to Rav, white does not need two seals, since he holds that spinning lishmah is not required. According to Shmuel, however, one may indeed say that tekheles was mentioned as the novelty, as above. And one can say, in the Eshel Avraham, that this is simple. If one can say that he benefits from the exchange, etc.: we are not concerned that a non-Jew will make a Jew stumble where the non-Jew derives no benefit, for what benefit does he get from the exchange if he does it only to anger the Jew? According to the non-Jew’s mistaken view, the Jew does not receive punishment for the matter, since by the non-Jew’s belief there is no prohibition; he is only acting to anger the Jew by frustrating his will. Since the Jew does not know that he exchanged them, this is not so much “to anger.” So wrote the responsum of the Chacham Tzvi, siman 39.
