4) [Seif 2] What is a private domain? A place enclosed by partitions, etc. This means four partitions, or three partitions with a sidepost or crossbeam on the fourth side. It is then a complete private domain within which carrying is permitted even rabbinically. If it has only three partitions and no sidepost or crossbeam on the fourth, Rambam, chapter 14, halachah 4, considers it a karmelis. Other authorities consider it a private domain, and one who throws from a public domain into it is liable, as explained in Beis Yosef; see there. See the following paragraph and below at the beginning of siman 363.
5) There: A place enclosed by partitions, etc. The authorities imply that two partitions and a sidepost form a private domain by Torah law, as stated in Eruvin 12 that a sidepost functions as a partition. Rosh, chapter 1 of Sukkah, writes the same. Four double posts at the four corners, like the boards surrounding wells, also form a private domain by Torah law, as stated in Eruvin 20. Each double post must be ten tefachim high and two amos wide, one amah extending in each direction, and no more than thirteen and one third amos may separate adjacent posts. Machatzis HaShekel. A sloped mound that rises ten tefachim within a horizontal distance of four amos is a private domain. If it rises ten tefachim over five amos, its use is easy and it is a public domain when located there. If one planted a pole ten tefachim high in a public domain with a basket four tefachim wide at its top, we do not extend the basket's partitions downward, because animals pass beneath them and they are not valid partitions. The basket itself, however, if ten tefachim high and four wide, is a private domain, as corrected by Tosefes Shabbos and Machatzis HaShekel, although the space beneath remains public domain. If a pillar is ten tefachim high and four wide at its top but not four wide at its base, the space beneath is not a private domain even if animals cannot pass there, because they do not ordinarily rub against it or pass under its slight incline. Only its top is a private domain. Tosafos, Shabbos 99; Magen Avraham, paragraph 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav. If the base is three tefachim wide, the space beneath is also a private domain. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 5; Biurei HaGra, based on Tosafos, Shabbos 102; see there. This applies when the top is only four tefachim wide. If the top extends three tefachim beyond the base, the space beneath is not a private domain, because animals can pass beneath the partition, invalidating it.
6) There: Partitions ten tefachim high, etc. This excludes partitions less than ten tefachim high. Even if the enclosed area is very wide, it is not a private domain. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 2.
7) There: High partitions, etc. When the breached portions equal the standing portions, it is permitted, as stated below in siman 362, seif 9; see there.
8) There: And it contains four tefachim, etc. The law transmitted to Moshe at Sinai teaches that a smaller area is not an independent domain, while an area four tefachim or more is a private domain. Levush.
9) There: And it contains four tefachim, etc. Taz, paragraph 2, is uncertain whether the thickness of the partitions joins the four tefachim. Bach and Magen Avraham, paragraph 1, consider it obvious that it does not. Mor U'Ketziah rejects their view and writes that the early authorities explicitly state that if the partitions are sturdy enough to be used by laying a board upon them, they join; see there. Machazik Berachah, paragraph 1, cites him. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3, writes the same and holds this is Shulchan Aruch's view. The implication in seif 19 that they do not join refers to weak partitions; see there. Chemed Moshe, paragraph 1, however, writes that they join only regarding the surface above: if one places an object upon it and uses it, the surface is a private domain. To render the interior itself a private domain, making one liable for carrying from a public domain into it or the reverse, the enclosed space must certainly measure four by four tefachim excluding the partition thickness; see there. Even HaOzer, Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 3, and Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 2, write the same. This is the view of the later authorities. See below, paragraph 11.
10) There: Or more. It may extend even many mil when enclosed for residential use. If not enclosed for residential use, it is a private domain by Torah law but rabbinically a karmelis when larger than beis sasayim, as explained below in siman 346, seif 3, and at the beginning of siman 358; see there.
11) There: Likewise, a trench ten tefachim deep, etc. Even when situated in a public domain, it is a private domain if it has this measure, as stated below in seif 11. If less than ten tefachim deep, it is a karmelis. If ten deep but not four wide, it is an exempt place. The same applies to a mound: if less than ten high, it is a karmelis, and if not four wide, it is an exempt place, as explained below in seifim 14 and 19; see there.
12) There: Likewise, a mound ten tefachim high, etc. On all four sides we apply the principle of extending the face of the partition upward to its top. Its top is thereby enclosed on four sides with an interior measuring four tefachim. Beis Yosef. This principle is a law transmitted to Moshe at Sinai. Levush.
13) There, gloss: Some say these require four by four tefachim together with their diagonal, etc. They interpret every requirement of four amos to mean the diagonal of four square amos, which is five amos and three fifths, based on the accepted measure that the diagonal of a square amah is one amah and two fifths. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 2. Levush omits this gloss. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 4, writes that he properly omitted it, because Rashi in Eruvin 21 explains that four amos together with their diagonal is required only in siman 349 regarding carrying in a public domain, where corners cannot be assigned. Here four amos alone suffice. He writes that several authorities imply the same; see there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, Kuntres Acharon, paragraph 1, and Biurei HaGra rule likewise.
