In order to eat from it, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, which states that a healthy animal may not be slaughtered unless it is needed on Yom Tov itself. This applies even if one needs only a kezayis, because obtaining a kezayis of meat is impossible without slaughtering. Consequently, one may also skin the animal completely, because otherwise he may refrain from slaughtering. Peri Megadim writes likewise in Eshel Avraham. One should not object that we rule that a Torah prohibition may not be permitted merely because otherwise he may refrain from slaughtering, as Magen Avraham writes in siman 497, seif katan 18, while skinning is a full melachah. Seemingly, only enough skinning to remove the kezayis beneath it should have been permitted, and no more, since skinning the amount needed to make an amulet is classified as a melachah on Shabbos and incurs liability, as Rambam states in chapter 11 of Hilchos Shabbos, halachah 5. The answer is that on Yom Tov this does not involve a Torah prohibition because of the principle of ho'il: if many guests arrived and needed to eat, it would certainly be permitted to skin the animal for all of them. Consequently, even now there is no Torah prohibition, but only a D'Rabbanan prohibition, which was permitted because otherwise he may refrain from slaughtering. According to this, completely skinning the animal is permitted only when sufficient time remains before nightfall to feed at least a kezayis of roasted meat to each potential guest. If he wishes to slaughter shortly before evening merely to eat a kezayis and there is insufficient time to roast and feed meat to many guests, he may skin only as much as he needs for the meat he will take.
Some say that it may not be skinned at all. This wording apparently implies that one may not even skin a small portion in order to inspect the lung. Magen Avraham, seif katan 12, also appears to say this. It seems, however, that this applies specifically when insufficient time remains in the day to inspect the lung, and therefore one may not skin it at all. If time remains, and he slaughters because he fears that the animal may die before nightfall, it is certainly more proper to inspect the lung so that the meat will be properly permitted should guests arrive. The stringency applies only to skinning the entire animal, because the Sages did not permit this when he presently has no guests who need the meat. Magen Avraham in seif katan 13 connects the rule that the animal need not be inspected with the fact that it may not be skinned. This could imply that even where time remains, it may not be skinned and inspected if he does not presently intend to eat the meat. Nevertheless, in my humble opinion the ruling is as I have written. In addition, many authorities disagree with this gloss, as I wrote in Shaar HaTziyun. At minimum, there is certainly no reason to be stringent in this case.
