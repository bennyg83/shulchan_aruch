A firstborn nowadays, etc. The reason for the prohibition of examining blemishes on Yom Tov: the Rambam wrote in chapter 2 that it is because of blemishes that arose on Yom Tov, upon which one may not slaughter by the law of muktzeh, for he had no intention upon them from erev Yom Tov; and for that reason they forbade even regarding those that arose before Yom Tov, whenever a scholar had not seen them from the evening. And the Maggid Mishneh wrote that there is one who gives the reason that he may sometimes come to move what is not fit for him, that is to say, if he finds that the blemish is not a [valid] blemish, the animal is not fit, and it is impossible that he will not move it; and there is one who wrote that it appears like repairing; end quote. And do not object to the Maggid Mishneh's first reason that if so all rulings by a scholar on Yom Tov would be forbidden, lest it be forbidden and he move in a forbidden way: that is not difficult, for specifically regarding a firstborn we say so, since it never had a presumption of permissibility; and the proof is that if it was born with its blemish, it is permitted after the fact if the scholar sees it on Yom Tov, because it never had a presumption of prohibition; and that we do not permit examining it l'chatchila in that case is because it did not clearly have a presumption of permissibility so long as the scholar had not seen it; which is not so regarding other rulings, where from the outset there was a presumption of permissibility. And it appears at first glance that sometimes there is a prohibition of ruling in a matter that was forbidden from the start, for example an extra limb that they show to a scholar whether it is a prohibition or not; but it seems that this too is not comparable to a firstborn, for regarding a firstborn the prohibition was clear before the blemish arose, which is not so here, where the prohibition was never clear, only a doubt. But one may learn this from a firstborn: if there was a clear prohibition, for example a moist prohibition that fell into moist permissible [food] in less than sixty, or dry into dry without a majority of the permissible, and afterward more permissible was added and the prohibition was nullified: this one may not rule on Yom Tov, for it is exactly like a firstborn, and a scholar may not rule on this on Yom Tov, as it appears to me. And Maharshal wrote in chapter Ein Tzadin that it is permitted for a scholar to examine the firstborn and be silent and not permit it, even if he has concluded in his heart that it is a [valid] blemish, for matters of the heart are not matters regarding the prohibitions of Shabbos and Yom Tov; end quote.
