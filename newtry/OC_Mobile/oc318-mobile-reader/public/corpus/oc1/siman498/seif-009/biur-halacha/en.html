But if it was born with its blemish. If it was born unblemished and acquired a blemish that same day, Pri Chadash writes that it is also permitted, according to our ruling in siman 308 that there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos. Here too, it was fit at bein hashemashos by virtue of its mother. Although it became prohibited upon birth, it became fit again when it acquired a blemish. See there. The language of the Gemara, however, does not imply this. Peri Megadim and Chemed Moshe likewise express hesitation based on Rashi's wording. See there. I found that Meiri addressed why the Gemara had to speak of an animal born with a blemish, since it could have spoken of one that acquired a blemish that day. He answers that perhaps once it was born unblemished, it was completely set aside, because its becoming fit again is not a development one would reasonably anticipate, as it is uncommon. This is unlike sunrise upon dried fruit that became wet from rain, which is common. In such a case we say that since it was fit at bein hashemashos, when it is later set aside in a manner that a person could reasonably anticipate would be reversed, it is permitted. The same applies to an animal born with its blemish, because even at the time it was set aside there was a circumstance that made its becoming fit again reasonably foreseeable, since one sees the blemish and it merely remains to show it to a sage to determine whether it is temporary or permanent. This appears clear to me. These are his words. He thus rules explicitly contrary to Pri Chadash.

He can investigate it. See Taz, who gives several reasons for the rule that blemishes may not be examined. He concludes that if a prohibited liquid became mixed with permitted liquid and there was not sixty times its amount, or dry prohibited food became mixed with permitted dry food and there was no majority, and afterward more permitted food was added until there was sixty or a majority, a sage may not rule on the mixture on Yom Tov, just as in the case of a firstborn animal. See there. It appears that he wrote this only according to Maggid Mishneh's reason, that the concern is moving muktzeh without need. Maamar Mordechai and the GraZ in Kuntres Acharon write likewise. It is unclear, however, what muktzeh exists in prohibited food mixed with permitted food. At bein hashemashos it was at least fit for dogs, and something prepared for dogs is considered prepared for people, as explained above in seif 5. If the mixture occurred on Shabbos and more permitted food was then added, it was fit for people at bein hashemashos, and there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos. I found that Nehar Shalom raises part of this point. See there, where he also questions the comparison itself: how do we know to derive this case from a firstborn animal? Perhaps muktzeh involving a living creature is more stringent. Maamar Mordechai also questions the Taz's basic ruling even according to Maggid Mishneh's reason. See there. Certainly, according to the reason we adopted in Mishnah Berurah for the rule that blemishes may not be examined, there is no basis at all to compare any other halachic ruling to a firstborn animal. Machatzis HaShekel likewise writes that according to Terumas HaDeshen's reason, the Taz's ruling has no basis. I also found that Bigdei Yesha straightforwardly rejects the Taz's ruling from practical halachah, giving several reasons. See there.
