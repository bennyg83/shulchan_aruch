If they arrived on Yom Tov. This is Rambam's wording, and Ra'ah writes likewise in his novellae. This implies that if they arrived on Erev Yom Tov, their classification as animals that graze in the wild is removed, and they are presumed prepared. It is obvious that this applies only if he knew of them at bein hashemashos. I likewise found in the conclusion of the Halachos of Yom Tov in Behag: “An animal that came from the grazing area and entered the techum on Erev Yom Tov, but he did not know of it, and it reached a Jew on Yom Tov, or alternatively, it came for non-Jews, there is no concern for the prohibition of techum, because we say that an item brought for one Jew is permitted to another Jew, and all the more so an item brought for a non-Jew. The only concern is the prohibition of muktzeh,” etc. See there for the lengthy discussion. Regarding what he writes, “or alternatively, it came for non-Jews,” he must hold like the opinion that an item belonging to a non-Jew also requires preparation. This is actually a dispute in the Yerushalmi, as Rashba and Ra'ah cite. See Pri Chadash, siman 497. It is explicit in Behag that if the animal entered the techum on Erev Yom Tov, it can retain the classification of an animal that grazes in the wild only if he was unaware of it. This clearly proves that if he knew of it, even though he did not bring it himself, it is ordinarily considered prepared like the other animals in his home, because he has it in mind should he need it on Yom Tov. Meiri, however, writes explicitly that even if they arrived on Erev Yom Tov, he presumably did not have them in mind unless he expressly designated them beforehand. See there. The practical halachah requires study. See Beis Meir, who discusses this detail at length.
Therefore, etc. “If it is known,” etc. The wording is very difficult. The requirement that it specifically be known has no connection to what was written previously. Moreover, what does it mean afterward by saying, “even if they came for a Jew,” when the earlier passage also addressed that case? Pri Chadash already raised this difficulty. In Avodas HaKodesh, the source of this ruling, the wording is much clearer: “Muktzeh is only from the words of the Sages, and in a case of doubt we rule leniently. Therefore, if a non-Jew brought an animal into a Jewish meat market, although it was brought for Jews, if it is known that it spent the night,” etc. This is a separate matter and has no connection to domestic and wild animals. He also refers to the prohibition of techum as muktzeh. The wording as I emended it in the main text appears correct. Since the preceding matter written in Shulchan Aruch, “unless they came for a Jew,” concerned domestic and wild animals, while the words “therefore” begin a different matter, it returned and clarified, “even if they came for a Jew.”
