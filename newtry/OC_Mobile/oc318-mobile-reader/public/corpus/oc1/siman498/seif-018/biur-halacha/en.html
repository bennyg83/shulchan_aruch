And in the evening, if its mark remains, etc. Many later authorities write that even if one wishes to place a little earth in a vessel and receive the blood in it, this is also improper on Yom Tov. These include Shach in Yoreh Deah, siman 28, seif katan 10, in the name of Derishah, Aderes Eliyahu there citing several later authorities, Rabbi Akiva Eiger's novellae there, and Magen Avraham. Without earth, blood may not be received in a vessel because this is the idolatrous practice of receiving it for sprinkling before an idol, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 11. Nevertheless, even placing earth in the vessel is improper on Yom Tov, because people may say that since placing earth was permitted, the animal must certainly have been a wild animal and the earth was placed there for kisui hadam.
In a corner, etc. See Shabbos 121b, which implies that only refuse in a courtyard's garbage heap is not treated as a repulsive item, but elsewhere in the courtyard no distinction is made between the center and a corner. It must therefore be that blood is not considered a repulsive item. If so, it should also be forbidden in the center. This requires a little study.
Even the blood of a domesticated animal. See Mishnah Berurah. Magen Avraham questions this from the Gemara on page 8. The Gemara asks, “If so, the same should apply on a weekday,” and answers that on a weekday people will say that he needs the earth to clean his courtyard. Since the act is prohibited on Yom Tov, this shows that on Yom Tov we do not attribute it to cleaning his courtyard, and it proves that cleaning one's courtyard is forbidden on Yom Tov. Magen Avraham writes that it is difficult to say that the Gemara concerns one who slaughtered in a corner, for if so the same should apply on a weekday. Eliyah Rabbah, Maamar Mordechai, and Nehar Shalom agree with Magen Avraham's first answer, that the Gemara concerns a corner. They answer his objection by explaining that on a weekday a person certainly normally cleans every corner of his courtyard, whereas on Yom Tov, if the filth is not in a place where people walk, one may not clean it. Their answer is not entirely simple, as I shall explain below. In any event, the unqualified discussion of the Gemara proves that one may not initially slaughter a koy on Yom Tov under any circumstances, even in the middle of the courtyard where all household members walk. Although in such a place covering the blood does not create concern, because observers will say that he is cleaning his courtyard, it is still forbidden. This is also implied by the gloss, which says, “If he slaughtered it,” etc. The reason must be that the Sages did not wish to distinguish among cases regarding initial slaughter. The intent of the gloss is also unclear. If he slaughtered in the middle of the courtyard, may he cover the blood even solely to fulfill the mitzvah of kisui hadam, because there is no concern regarding observers? Or perhaps covering for the purpose of the mitzvah is forbidden in every case, because the Sages did not distinguish in their decree based on where the slaughter took place, just as they forbade initially slaughtering in every case. The gloss may have come to permit covering only when he fears becoming soiled while walking, in which case he may cover to prevent his clothing from becoming dirty. Magen Avraham inclines to this in his concluding words. Yet the gloss's phrase, “even the blood of a domesticated animal,” implies that all the more so the blood of a koy may be covered, since two factors are present, the mitzvah of kisui hadam and cleaning the courtyard. This can nevertheless be rejected. When his intention is to clean the courtyard because he is concerned that his utensils or clothing will become soiled, he may cover the blood and also intend to fulfill the mitzvah of kisui hadam. If he would not have been concerned about the filth and his principal intention is only the mitzvah, it is forbidden, even though observers will say that he is cleaning his courtyard, because he himself knows that his sole intention is covering the blood. This appears from the Gemara's statement, “If he comes to consult,” etc. See Rashi there. I also saw that Machatzis HaShekel derives proof for this from that discussion. Know further that in my humble opinion he should not cover the blood of a koy even when he wishes to clean the courtyard unless he also intends to fulfill the mitzvah of kisui hadam. Otherwise, he actively nullifies the mitzvah according to the principle that mitzvos require intent. If he did not cover it at all, the mark might remain visible until evening, when he could cover it. Magen Avraham's statement that the case is one in which he intends to clean his courtyard also means that he should additionally intend to fulfill the mitzvah of kisui hadam. Know also that Gra explains the gloss as follows: Riaz understands that the Gemara concerns earth that is not fully prepared, such as where a spade was inserted into it, or the like. See there. Ra'ah in Bedek HaBayis, bayis 1, shaar 5, also holds this view. This implies that according to our accepted ruling following the authorities who understand the Gemara to concern even fully prepared earth, we do not accept this gloss.
