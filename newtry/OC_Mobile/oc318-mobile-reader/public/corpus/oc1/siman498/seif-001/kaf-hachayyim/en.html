1) [Se'if 1] A knife is not shown to a scholar, etc. This follows the practice in the era of the Sages of the Talmud, when an animal was not slaughtered with a knife until the knife was shown to a scholar to determine whether it was fit for slaughter and free of a nick. Levush. Nowadays, however, the custom throughout the Jewish Diaspora is not to trust butchers. Designated people are appointed over slaughter and inspection, and the Sages waived their honor for them because these appointees are diligent and careful. Consequently, inspection by a scholar was entirely discontinued, even for one who slaughters for his own household, as the Tur writes in Yoreh Deah, siman 18, in the name of his father, the Rosh. Shulchan Aruch rules accordingly there, se'if 17. See there. This is the meaning of Rama's statement here in the gloss, 'Nowadays, when every shochet, etc.' It follows the aforementioned position of the Rosh. Nevertheless, the inspection must be performed with great deliberation and with fear of Heaven upon one's face, as the later authorities there wrote in their explicit admonitions regarding this matter. This is not the place to elaborate.
2) There. A knife is not shown to a scholar, etc. This applies even if the butcher already inspected it himself on erev Yom Tov and found it satisfactory. Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 1.
3) There. A knife is not shown to a scholar, etc. After the fact, if he showed it to the scholar and it was found satisfactory, he may slaughter with it. Mishnah Berurah, subsection 3.
4) There. A knife is not shown to a scholar, etc. If, however, a Torah scholar recently studied the laws of shechitah, his teacher gave him full authority and permission to slaughter, and he sharpened and prepared the knife on erev Yom Tov, but when he came to slaughter on Yom Tov he became uncertain about it, he may certainly show his knife to his teacher in such a case. Shulchan Gavoah, subsection 2. It appears that this does not apply only to his teacher; the same applies to anyone greater or more expert than he.
5) Shulchan Gavoah further writes there that at times something resembling a nick appears on a knife, and the shochet smooths it against the palm of his hand with saliva, as he would ordinarily do against a sharpening stone. This appearance comes upon the knife because he slaughtered with it on erev Yom Tov and some moisture from the blood or saliva remained upon it. When the night passes, the blood or saliva dries and appears like a nick. When he smooths the knife with saliva against the palm of his hand, that blood softens and comes off, after which he can determine whether it is truly a nick. Smoothing in this manner is permitted. The prohibition against sharpening applies only to its sharpening stone, but sharpening against wood, earthenware, or a stone is permitted, as stated below, siman 509, se'if 2. It is certainly permitted in this case. These are his words.
6) Regarding the rubbing practiced by shochtim, in which they rub the knife against the hand or arm when a nick is found after slaughter and declare the animal valid if the nick disappears, see Chochmas Adam, rule 3, subsection 11. He rules very stringently that this is forbidden and writes that the gaon Beis Meir and the people of his city agreed. See there. The gaon Rabbi David Chazan likewise writes in Toras Zevach, siman 89, subsection 43, in the name of Bris Avraham, siman 12, who rules that it is forbidden. Rabbi David Chazan himself writes, however, that the custom is to declare it valid, provided the rubbing is done gently and not forcefully. See there. Zivchei Tzedek on Yoreh Deah, siman 18, subsection 19, cites this and writes that the custom in the city of Baghdad, may God protect it, is to permit it. See there. This has practical application in a place where the permissive custom is followed and such a case occurs on Yom Tov. It is permitted to rub the knife for the reason given by Shulchan Gavoah, since this is done only against the hand. Moreover, it is needed for food preparation, to render the animal or bird valid for Yom Tov.
7) There. Lest it be nicked, etc. Rambam, chapter 4, writes this. There are, however, other explanations, which Beis Yosef cites. Rashi's explanation implies that only a butcher who sells meat in a shop may not show his knife on Yom Tov, because the matter becomes public, but any other person may do so. According to Halachos Gedolos, the concern is that he might go beyond the techum. According to Razah, this resembles inspecting a firstborn animal for a blemish, but inspecting the knife for oneself is permitted. See there. Maggid Mishneh writes there that Rambam's explanation appears primary. Beis Yosef also cites him and therefore rules accordingly in Shulchan Aruch. Ramban, in Milchamos, likewise mentions Rambam's explanation and says that it is the most correct of all the explanations. Bach cites him. Nevertheless, Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, subsection 1, writes that in practice one should be concerned for all the reasons. A knife is therefore not shown to a scholar even by one who is not a butcher and not an am haaretz. See there. This is also the view of Erech HaShulchan, subsection 1. Pri Chadash, however, agrees with Rambam's reason as written by Shulchan Aruch. Levush likewise follows Shulchan Aruch, as do Chemed Moshe, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1; and Mikraei Kodesh, siman 43, subsection 1. See the following subsection.
8) There. And he will go and sharpen it on a sharpening stone. According to this reason, the prohibition against showing the knife applies only to an am haaretz, but there is no concern regarding anyone who is not an am haaretz. Bach. We have already written in the preceding subsection that according to the other explanations it is forbidden even for one who is not an am haaretz. See there. Their view should be taken into account initially.
9) There. And he will go and sharpen it, etc. If a knife became nicked on Yom Tov, whether one may tell a non-Jew to sharpen it requires further study. In pressing circumstances and in a case of great need, it appears that this is permitted. Eshel Avraham, subsection 1; Mikraei Kodesh, siman 43, Likutei Rima, subsection 2; Biur Halachah.
10) There. And a scholar who inspected a knife, etc. Levush explains that he inspected it on erev Yom Tov. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1, however, writes that this requires further study, because Beitzah 28b clearly indicates that he may inspect it even on Yom Tov. He writes that this is also evident from Beis Yosef according to all the explanations. See there. Pri Chadash likewise writes that initially a scholar may inspect it for himself. Chemed Moshe, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 4, write likewise. See Pri Chadash there, who inclines to say that initially a scholar may inspect a knife for himself with the intention of lending it to others. See there. Chemed Moshe there, however, writes that after a scholar has inspected knives for himself, he may lend them to others. This is also implied by the wording of Shulchan Aruch.
11) There. And a scholar who inspected a knife, etc. Similarly, one scholar may show it to another scholar, for he knows to take care not to sharpen it. Magen Avraham, subsection 1; Chemed Moshe there; Shulchan Aruch HaRav there; Mikraei Kodesh, siman 43, subsection 1. This follows Rambam's reason, which Shulchan Aruch adopts. See above, subsection 8.
12) There. He may lend it to others. This means to an am haaretz. There is no concern that the borrower might go and sharpen it, because it is already sharpened. Since he knows the laws of shechitah, although he is not a scholar, he may go and slaughter. Maaseh Rokeach on Rambam, at the beginning of the work, page 4c, in the name of Mahari ben Chachmon.
13) Regarding the inspection after slaughter, Levush writes that some forbid it and some permit it, without deciding between them. Rabbi Be'er Sheva, however, in the ruling after the indices, decides in accordance with those who are stringent, and this is the custom. Damesek Eliezer on Chullin, page 44, writes likewise. Mahari Weil also writes in his derashos that since the meat must be rendered valid, this is needed for food preparation. Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, subsection 2. Magen Avraham, subsection 2, likewise writes that anyone may inspect the knife after slaughter, unlike Levush, who was uncertain about this. Pri Chadash; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 3; Kisei Eliyahu, subsection 1; Eshel Avraham, subsection 2; Chemed Moshe, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 3; Chayei Adam, rule 89, subsection 2; Mikraei Kodesh there, subsection 2; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 7, write likewise. See Yoreh Deah, siman 18, se'if 12.
14) It is proper to slaughter before Yom Tov whatever will be needed for Yom Tov. Derashos Mahari Weil there; Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah there; Olas Shabbos, subsection 1.
15) There, in the gloss. Nowadays, when every shochet inspects the knife himself. The reason is as written above, subsection 1. Furthermore, reliance is placed upon the authorization he possesses, as written in Yoreh Deah, siman 1, se'if 1, in the gloss. See there.
16) There, in the gloss. He should inspect his knife on erev Yom Tov, etc. He should not return the knife to a hard sheath. If the edge or sides of the knife touch the hardness of the sheath, it is no longer considered an inspected knife, as Tevuos Shor writes in Yoreh Deah, siman 18, subsection 14. Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger; Mikraei Kodesh there, Likutei Rima, subsection 1; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 6.
17) There, in the gloss. He should inspect his knife on erev Yom Tov, etc. It is proper for every butcher to have two or three knives inspected on erev Yom Tov. Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, subsection 1; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2; Shulchan Gavoah, subsection 4; Likutei Rima there; Mishnah Berurah there.
18) There, in the gloss. He should inspect his knife on erev Yom Tov, etc. He should conceal it in a place where it will not become nicked, and slaughter with it on Yom Tov or lend it to others. Levush. This implies that one may slaughter with it in reliance upon the inspection performed on erev Yom Tov. Darkei Moshe, subsection 1, writes likewise. Although on a weekday it is forbidden to slaughter without first inspecting the knife in reliance upon inspecting it after slaughter, as written in Yoreh Deah, siman 18, nevertheless, on Yom Tov it is permitted because no other course is possible. These are his words. Chayei Adam, rule 89, subsection 2, likewise writes that one relies upon this presumption and slaughters without inspection, specifically when it is a knife whose established nature is not to become nicked by itself. These are his words. Mateh Ephraim, siman 685, se'if 23, however, writes that when one comes to slaughter on Yom Tov he should nevertheless inspect the knife immediately before slaughter, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 18. There is then no concern that he might find it unsatisfactory and sharpen it. Since he inspected it on erev Yom Tov and concealed it, it will presumably be found satisfactory. His inspection is merely to address the remote possibility that it became nicked. We do not have so great a concern that he might find it unsatisfactory and also forget that it is Yom Tov and sharpen it. When he did not inspect it on erev Yom Tov, however, this concern does apply. See there. Zivchei Ratzon writes likewise, as do Likutei Rima there and Tosafos Chaim on Chayei Adam there, subsection 4. If he did not designate it on erev Yom Tov and inspect it thoroughly, he should not perform its initial inspection on Yom Tov except in pressing circumstances, as stated afterward.
19) There, in the gloss. And not on Yom Tov, etc. If the shochet appointed for the community did not inspect the knife on erev Yom Tov, he may inspect it on Yom Tov. There is no concern that he might find it nicked and sharpen it. This concern was stated only in earlier times, when the butchers themselves performed the slaughter. Now that designated people are appointed, they themselves stand in the place of the scholar, and regarding a scholar himself we do not suspect that he might sharpen it. Knesses HaGedolah on Yoreh Deah, siman 18. See above, subsection 1.
20) There, in the gloss. And it is a pressing circumstance, etc. For example, he cannot borrow from someone else a knife that was inspected on erev Yom Tov, and he needs meat in honor of Yom Tov.
