[1] Over spices, etc. Olat Tamid rules in the name of Taz on Yoreh Deah siman 108 that one may recite Havdalah over pepper. He did not see Nekudot HaKesef, who disagrees with Taz and proves from Rashi that pepper is measured according to the amount needed to season a small egg, whereas concerning fragrant spices the Mishnah teaches any amount, showing that pepper is not included among fragrances. Taz's proof from Beit Yosef in the name of Rambam is rejected there on the ground that pepper is not included among seasonings and is not termed fragrance. In my Eliyah Rabbah on Yoreh Deah I objected that, with due respect, they misunderstood Beit Yosef. He says only that garlic and onions are also included among seasonings and that they differ between raw and cooked. Pepper is plainly a seasoning, except that there is no distinction between cooked and raw. They also did not see Rambam himself, Peirush HaMishnayot to Orlah 2:10, who explicitly warns against thinking that only aromatic mixtures such as ginger and pepper are called seasonings. Rather, anything used to season a cooked dish, including garlic, onions, oil, vinegar, and wine, is called a seasoning when used for that purpose. Bartenura explains similarly. Thus pepper and ginger are plainly called seasonings. On this basis I wrote there that one should likewise not recite a fragrance blessing over ginger, since Rambam calls it a seasoning. I later found Piskei Tosafot stating that one does not bless over pepper and should not smell ginger; if one smells ginger, no blessing is recited. This implies that pepper may even initially be smelled without a blessing, whereas one should initially avoid smelling ginger. The reason given there is concern for Rashba, cited in Shulchan Aruch Yoreh Deah siman 108, that Havdalah may be recited over ginger. In my humble opinion there is no proof from there, because Rashba may be referring to other fragrances, as in Shulchan Aruch here, seif 3, concerning sacks filled with spices. Nekudot HaKesef there also implies this. On reconsideration, although I established against Taz that pepper and ginger are called seasonings, the practical law may still permit Havdalah and a blessing over them. Perhaps they are called seasonings only when placed into a pot, as Rambam's words imply, but when intended for smelling they are called fragrances. One may also distinguish between crushed material, which is called fragrance, and whole material. This would also reject Nekudot HaKesef's proof. Yet since the great authorities did not make this distinction, I cannot be lenient in a doubtful blessing. It also appears to me proper to be stringent regarding vows. Magen Avraham writes that he does not understand the doubt, since some hold that Havdalah may even be recited over warm bread, as Beit Yosef records. This is no objection, for pepper is worse because its odor is very sharp, as Piskei Tosafot writes.
[2] [Levush] It cannot be restored, etc. Therefore, even one who is fasting recites the blessing over spices, because the additional soul enters on Friday night and does not depart because of a daytime fast until Motzaei Shabbos (Shiyurei Knesset HaGedolah). This is unlike Yom Kippur, siman 624, seif 3.
