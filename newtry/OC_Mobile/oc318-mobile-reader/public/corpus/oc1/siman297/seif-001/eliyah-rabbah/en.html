[1] Over the spices, etc. Olas Tamid rules in the name of Taz to Yoreh Deah, siman 108, that Havdalah may be recited over pepper. He did not see Nekudos HaKesef, who disputes Taz and proves from Rashi’s explanation that the measure for pepper is enough to season a light egg, whereas regarding fragrant spices the Mishnah teaches that any amount suffices. This indicates that pepper is not included among fragrant spices. Nekudos HaKesef also rejects Taz’s proof from Beit Yosef in the name of Rambam, arguing that pepper is not included among seasonings and is not described as a fragrance.

In Eliyah Rabbah to Yoreh Deah I objected, with due respect, that they did not understand Beit Yosef. He means only that garlic and onions are also included among seasonings and that a distinction applies to them between raw and cooked. Pepper is certainly a seasoning, except that no distinction applies between cooked and raw, since it is always called a seasoning. They also did not consult Rambam’s own commentary to Orlah 2:10, where he cautions that one should not think only aromatic compounds such as ginger and pepper are called seasonings. Rather, anything used to season a dish, including garlic, onions, oil, vinegar, and wine, is called a seasoning when used for that purpose. Bartenura explains similarly. It is therefore explicit that pepper and ginger are unquestionably called seasonings.

On this basis I wrote there that one should likewise not recite a fragrance blessing over ginger, since Rambam calls it a seasoning. I later found in Piskei Tosafos: “One does not recite a blessing over pepper, and one should not smell ginger; if one does smell it, no blessing is recited.” His wording implies that pepper may even be smelled initially without a blessing, whereas ginger should not be smelled initially. The reason appears to be concern for Rashba’s opinion, cited in Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah, siman 108, that Havdalah may be recited over ginger. In my humble opinion, there is no proof from there, for that statement may refer not to ginger but to other spices, as in seif 3 here concerning sacks filled with spices. Nekudos HaKesef there also implies this.

After further consideration, although I established that pepper and ginger are called seasonings, contrary to Taz, the practical law may still be that Havdalah and a fragrance blessing may be recited over them. Perhaps they are called seasonings only when placed into a pot, as Rambam’s wording implies, but when one intends to smell them they are considered fragrant spices. It is also possible to distinguish between crushed pepper, which may be considered fragrant, and whole pepper. This would also rebut the proof of Nekudos HaKesef. Nevertheless, since the great authorities did not make these distinctions, I cannot rule leniently where a blessing may be in doubt. It also appears proper to rule stringently regarding vows. Magen Avraham writes that he does not understand the doubt, since some authorities permit Havdalah even over warm bread, as cited in Beit Yosef. This is no objection, because pepper is inferior in this respect, since its odor is extremely sharp, as Piskei Tosafos writes.
