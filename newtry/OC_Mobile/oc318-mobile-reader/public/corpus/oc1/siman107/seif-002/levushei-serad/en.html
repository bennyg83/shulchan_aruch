There—from doubt, automatically through innovation it is permitted. And that which one stops even in the middle of a blessing refers to one who cannot innovate. And that which nedavah is permitted in every manner for an individual—this is the statement of R' Yochanan, "Would that..." And that which in the manner of obligation it is forbidden—this is the statement of one who stops even in the middle of a blessing: since he began with intent of obligation and does not know how to innovate, he may not finish as nedavah. What Beit Yosef challenged—that Rif should have brought the statement of R' Yochanan regarding individual vs. congregation. Beit Yosef holds that for an individual in obligation even innovation does not help, like the statement of one who stops even in the middle of a blessing; and it seems to Beit Yosef even if he could innovate. And regarding entering the synagogue, Beit Yosef holds he prays as nedavah; and if he were not in the synagogue he would not need innovation—but since he is with the congregation in the synagogue he needs innovation; thus Beit Yosef. Therefore he challenged: since the law of the individual is from R' Yochanan's statement and there is a distinction between whether the individual prays alone or with the congregation—then regarding what Rif wrote, that R' Yochanan applies to the individual but the congregation may not pray, whether as obligation or nedavah—he should have brought this statement to inform that R' Yochanan has another distinction between individual and congregation, namely that one praying alone need not innovate, but when praying with the congregation one needs innovation. From this difficulty Beit Yosef proved that an individual in nedavah needs innovation even when praying alone. On this the Taz answered: in truth innovation helps for an individual in the manner of obligation, unlike Beit Yosef. Also, when entering the synagogue he has no permission as nedavah, because he appears to pray with them for obligation; therefore he has permission only to innovate, since innovation helps also for obligation. Thus Rif need not rely at all on Beit Yosef's difficulty—for R' Yochanan deals with nedavah and Rav Yehuda with obligation; in obligation there is no distinction at all between one praying alone or with the congregation—both are permitted through innovation and not without it. And what the Ramban wrote—that specifically with the congregation one needs innovation but not as an individual—means because he can pray nedavah; but as obligation there is no distinction between individual and congregation. And if because in nedavah an individual need not innovate when praying alone but when praying with the congregation needs innovation because it is forbidden to pray nedavah with the congregation—this is not considered a distinction between individual and congregation at all, for the reason is only maris ayin, that he appears to pray for obligation, as the Taz wrote later—understand.
