1) [Seif 1] If one is uncertain whether he prayed, he returns and prays. Although in other rabbinic matters we rule leniently in a case of doubt, prayer is a plea for mercy, so we act stringently and he may pray again, for it would be desirable that a person pray and seek mercy all day. Levush, seif 3. Although Levush, who gives this reason, holds that prayer is rabbinic, as stated above in siman 106, letter 7, the same rationale applies even according to those who hold that prayer is a Torah obligation. As explained there, even according to that view the number of daily prayers is rabbinic. By Torah law 1 prayer a day is sufficient and the other prayers are rabbinic. Yet here the ruling is stated without distinction: if one is uncertain whether he prayed, he repeats the prayer, whether Shacharit, Minchah, or Maariv. Even if he already prayed another prayer and thereby fulfilled the Torah obligation, if he is uncertain about this prayer he repeats it. Why, when a rabbinic doubt is normally treated leniently? The reason must be that prayer is mercy and therefore we act stringently, as Levush explains. Maamar Mordechai, letter 3, writes likewise and rejects Olat Tamid, letter 2. 2) There, he returns and prays. He should stipulate: if I did not pray, this is my obligatory prayer; if I did pray, this is a voluntary prayer. Beit Yosef in the name of Rashba; Levush; Olat Tamid; Magen Avraham, seif katan 1; Ateret Zekenim, letter 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, letter 1. Eshel Avraham, letter 2, writes that if he is uncertain whether he prayed and the time has passed, he should make it up with the adjoining prayer and make this stipulation. It appears that according to our practice, under which we do not offer voluntary prayers, as will be explained below in seif 4, he should not do this. It is sufficient that within the proper time one who is uncertain may pray conditionally; after the time has passed we should not add to that leniency. See the next letter and letter 20 below. Moreover, Eshel Avraham himself, in siman 108, letter 2, again expresses doubt and does not resolve the matter. Therefore what we have written is primary. 3) There, he returns and prays. The wording implies an obligation to repeat the prayer. Rashba writes so in Responsa, siman 91, and Lechem Mishneh, chapter 10 of the Laws of Prayer, law 6, understands Rabbeinu Yonah this way. In Chiddushei Rashba, however, he cites a Gaon, as brought by Beit Yosef, whose words imply that it is not an obligation but a permission: if he wishes, he may repeat the prayer. Pri Chadash, letter 1, agrees, as does Maharshal in Chochmat Shlomo on Tosafos, Berachos 21a. Even according to those who hold that one is obligated to repeat, Pri Megadim in Eshel Avraham, letter 1, writes that for Maariv one is not obligated to repeat, because although Maariv was accepted as obligatory, that applies to certainty, not doubt. Likewise, regarding Shabbos and Yom Tov prayers, even Shacharit and Minchah, one does not repeat because of doubt, since one cannot stipulate that if he already prayed the repetition should be voluntary, as voluntary prayer is not offered on Shabbos or Yom Tov. See there, and see below, letter 8, and siman 124, letter 54. 4) There, and he need not introduce anything new. There is no greater innovation than the fact that he is praying because of doubt, and it may be that the entire prayer is obligatory because perhaps he did not pray previously. Beit Yosef; Levush, seif 3; Shulchan Aruch HaRav there. Some authorities nevertheless hold that even when repeating because of doubt, he should introduce some new request. Bach writes so according to Rav Hai Gaon. It is proper to take his words into account, because there is no concern in adding such a request; even in an obligatory prayer one may add a personal request, as stated at the beginning of siman 119. Chesed LaAlafim, letter 1.
