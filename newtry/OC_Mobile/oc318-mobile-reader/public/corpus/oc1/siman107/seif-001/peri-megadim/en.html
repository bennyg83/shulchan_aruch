If. See Taz, who discusses this at length. There appear to be three approaches: that of Rabbeinu Yitzchak, author of Tosafos; that of the Rif; and that of Rav Hai Gaon. All agree that Rabbi Yochanan’s statement and the two statements of Rav Yehudah in the name of Shmuel are accepted law, as in Berachos 21a. Rabbeinu Yitzchak holds that when one certainly prayed, a voluntary prayer without a new element is forbidden, but a prayer framed as obligatory is permitted when it contains a new element. Therefore, when one is uncertain whether he prayed, he must pray as an obligation, and the uncertainty itself supplies the new element. Rabbi Yochanan’s statement, “Would that a person pray all day,” must consequently be interpreted as the Rosh does in his responsum. Rav Yehudah’s ruling that one who began and then remembered must stop need not be forced into a case where he first thought he had not prayed and then remembered that he had. It may refer to one who was uncertain, began as an obligation, and then remembered. He stops when he cannot introduce a new element; otherwise he may complete the prayer with such an addition. The Rif, by contrast, permits a voluntary prayer even when one certainly prayed and adds nothing new. This is Rabbi Yochanan’s statement, without the Rosh’s qualification. He also permits an obligatory prayer with a new element. Thus, when the Gemara says that one who finds the congregation praying may pray again, the novelty is that he is thereby considered to pray with the congregation. A voluntary prayer is otherwise permitted only when it contains a new element, and an obligatory prayer is likewise permitted with such an addition. Rav Hai Gaon also explains Rabbi Yochanan as referring to one who certainly prayed, but only when he adds something new. A voluntary prayer without a new element is forbidden, and in his view even an obligatory prayer may not be repeated merely by adding a new element. Taz writes that the wording, “If he can introduce a new element so that his prayer will be supplication,” supports this approach, but that wording is not in our Gemara; it is the Rif’s text. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 4, and what I wrote there. The underlying dispute is whether voluntary prayer follows the model of an olah, which may be offered voluntarily, or the model of the daily offerings, which cannot be voluntary. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak, a new element permits even an obligatory repetition. According to Rav Hai, the new element merely identifies the prayer as voluntary, but does not permit a repeated obligation. I have not found an explicit statement of Rav Hai that an obligatory prayer remains forbidden even with a new element. See simanim 50 and 80.
