If he is in doubt — See in Chayei Adam, who wrote that today this law is not practiced, according to what is explained below in seif 4, that he must know himself and estimate in his mind that he will be able to have proper intention; and it is known that in our time even one in a thousand will not be found [able to do so], and therefore he cannot make a condition. And it does not seem correct to me that the words of the Rashba are agreed upon by all, for the view of the Raavad [brought in the responsum of the Rashba] is that he is obligated to return and pray; and even per the Rashba one may distinguish from that which is taught in seif 4, that there it speaks of one who prays plainly as a voluntary prayer—there we apply to him well, "Why do I need your many sacrifices?" which suffices with the constant offerings; and similarly with the three fixed prayers. But regarding this one who wishes to pray in order to fulfill his doubt, his law is like other prayers, that lekhatchilah he must strive to have intention, and bedieved it suffices if he did not have intention only in the first blessing; and see in Biur HaGra in seif 4. And similarly the language of the Shulchan Aruch implies to one who examines, that this law of seif 4 is not at all related to the doubt whether one prayed explained in seif 1, but is a separate matter. And similarly in all the early and later decisors that I have seen, no hint is found that today this law should change. And also the Chida, the Gra, and Shulchan Shlomo copied the Shulchan Aruch as halacha.

He returns and prays — See in Pri Megadim, who wrote that per the Rashba in his responsum he is obligated to return and pray and make a condition; and according to his innovations there is no obligation upon him, only that it is proper to return, for would that a person pray all day. And in Sefer Maamar Mordechai he proved from the words of the responsum itself that there is no obligation upon him; only that the Rashba wrote that since the Sages permitted him to return and pray, and there is no concern of "idle words" if he does not return, behold he is like one who shows about himself that he is not concerned for prayer; and therefore he must return and offer voluntarily. He also removed from him the difficulty of the Peri Chadash; see there that his words are correct.

And he need not innovate anything — Whether he prays now alone or with the congregation; and there is also no difference whether the first time was alone or with the congregation (Olat Tamid).

And by means of innovation he returns and prays, etc. — And there is no difference whether he prays this prayer with the congregation or alone; so too in the Tur. And see in Beit Yosef that if he prayed and innovates something in it and did not intend it for the sake of obligation, and also not for the sake of voluntary prayer, it is also fine. And see in Rambam, chapter 10 of Laws of Prayer, halacha 6, who wrote regarding one who was in doubt whether he prayed, etc., with the intent that it is voluntary; and certainly—whether his intent is through innovation as Darkei Moshe explained, or the intent of the Rambam that doubt is like innovation—in any case it is proved from this that certainly by innovation as well it only helps when intending for voluntary prayer, and not when praying plainly.

With the intent that he had not prayed, etc. — And when he began conditionally out of doubt and remembered in the middle, see in Mishnah Berurah what he wrote in the name of the Levush that he must innovate; and the Aruch HaShulchan wrote that this is advice for the times. And in Sefer Maamar Mordechai he wrote that the Levush speaks from the law, for the reason that that which we say "you have no greater innovation than this" is because what he prays from the power of doubt—that is his innovation; and whenever he remembers in the middle that he already prayed, the matter is revealed retroactively that his prayer is a voluntary prayer and requires innovation like any voluntary prayer; and as above.

He stops even in the middle, etc. — See the reason in Mishnah Berurah. And even in Maariv, for we hold it is optional—he stops, since now we have rendered them as obligatory; presumably he began it with the intent of obligation (Magen Avraham).
