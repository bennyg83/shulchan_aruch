(1) Whether he prayed. Regarding Krias Shema and its blessings when he is uncertain, see above, siman 67.
(2) He returns and prays. He stipulates: If I have not prayed, let this count as my obligation; and if I have prayed, let it count as a voluntary prayer. Although every uncertainty concerning a Rabbinic law is ordinarily treated leniently, here he returns and prays because “would that a person pray all day” as a voluntary prayer, provided he introduces a new element, as explained below. When he repeats the prayer because of uncertainty, he need not introduce a new element, because there is no greater new element than resolving his uncertainty. Derech HaChaim writes that with regard to Maariv, when one is uncertain he need not repeat the Amidah, because the Jewish people accepted it as obligatory only in a definite case, not in a case of uncertainty. The Peri Megadim, however, implies only that there is no absolute obligation according to any view. See there. Nevertheless, ideally it is proper to repeat Maariv as well, because even according to the view that Maariv is optional, it is still a mitzvah ideally, and resolving his uncertainty is therefore considered a new element in Maariv as well. This also appears to me from the Rambam’s wording in chapter 10 of Hilchos Tefillah. See there. As the best course, he should introduce a new element when repeating it.
(3) He does not return, etc. The prayers were instituted corresponding to the daily offerings, and this would be like offering two daily offerings as obligatory offerings, which is forbidden because of bal tosif. Even if he first prayed alone and now wishes to pray with the congregation, it is nevertheless forbidden.
(4) And prays voluntarily. Meaning, specifically as a voluntary prayer. To pray it as an obligation is forbidden even if he introduces a new element. A new element is required in a voluntary prayer because it makes evident from within the prayer that it is supplication rather than an obligation. Without a new element it appears that he is offering it as an obligation. See Beit Yosef, who explains the Tur’s view that the same applies if one prays neither as an obligation nor with explicit intent that it be voluntary, provided he introduces a new element. Through the new element it is evident that the prayer is supplication. See Biur Halachah.
(5) The Mussaf prayer. Meaning, even the Mussaf of Rosh Chodesh and Chol HaMoed. Since he says, “and the Mussaf offerings according to their law,” and those offerings are brought only from communal funds, an individual cannot donate them. The later authorities write that according to this, if one is uncertain whether he prayed Mussaf, and likewise on Shabbos and Yom Tov regarding all the prayers, he cannot repeat them, because he would have to stipulate as explained in seif katan 2, and in these cases he cannot make such a stipulation. See below, siman 124, seif 1, in Biur Halachah.
(6) At all. Because vows and voluntary offerings are not brought on those days.
(7) Even if he can, etc. Presumably he began the prayer definitely intending it as an obligation, because he thought he had not yet prayed, and introducing a new element does not help. Therefore, even if he now wishes to complete it as a voluntary prayer, this is ineffective, because he already began it as an obligation, and an offering cannot be half obligatory and half voluntary. All this applies where he began on the assumption that he had not prayed. If, however, he was uncertain whether he had prayed and began conditionally, as explained in seif katan 2, and then remembered during the prayer that he had in fact prayed, it appears that he need not introduce a new element and may complete it as a voluntary prayer. Since he began properly on account of uncertainty, and uncertainty itself is considered a new element, no further new element is needed. The Levush implies that he completes it by introducing a new element, perhaps as the preferable course.
