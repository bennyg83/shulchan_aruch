Seif katan 4, A new element, etc. “I was asked,” etc., “his prayer is not supplication.” The Gemara concludes: What is meant by fixed? Rabbi Yaakov bar Idi said in the name of Rabbi Oshaya: Any prayer that seems to him like a burden. The Sages say: Any prayer that he does not recite in a supplicatory manner. This is the ruling above in siman 98, seif 3. Rabbah and Rav Yosef say: Any prayer in which he cannot introduce a new element. Rabbi Zeira said: I am able to introduce a new element, etc.
Furthermore, according to this, what did Rabbi Zeira mean, etc. Meaning, if so, Heaven forbid, Rabbi Zeira never prayed properly throughout his life, because his prayer was fixed.
The Rif’s version, after stating that a voluntary prayer is permitted only if one can introduce a new element, concludes with the reason, “so that his prayer should be supplication.” This differs from the Rambam’s formulation cited in Shulchan Aruch, that the purpose is to indicate that it is voluntary.
This implies that he maintains, etc. Meaning, even without this, one could ask according to the view that a voluntary prayer requires a new element: Why is that necessary? According to Rabbah and Rav Yosef, even a fixed obligatory prayer requires a new element. One must therefore say that this view does not follow Rabbah and Rav Yosef, but Rabbi Yaakov and the Sages, who interpret the Mishnah’s statement that one who makes his prayer fixed in another sense, as explained above. According to them, an obligatory prayer requires no new element, while a voluntary prayer does. But according to the Rif’s wording, he necessarily follows Rabbah and Rav Yosef, since he says that without a new element the prayer is not supplication. The difficulty then returns: According to Rabbah and Rav Yosef, even an obligatory prayer requires a new element, so why was it necessary to teach specifically that a voluntary prayer requires one? One must therefore say that Rabbah and Rav Yosef interpret the Mishnah, “One who makes his prayer fixed,” as follows: A voluntary prayer requires a new element because it must be supplication, and if he introduces nothing new it is not supplication. An obligatory prayer, however, need not have the status of supplication and therefore requires no new element. Thus the Mishnah means that one who makes his prayer fixed, meaning that he introduces nothing new, has not made it supplication. The practical consequence is that he may not pray in this manner voluntarily, because a voluntary prayer must be supplication.
And regarding this as well he said, etc. In truth, he did not pray voluntarily.
Alternatively, this is what it means, etc. Meaning, Rabbah and Rav Yosef may indeed be discussing an obligatory prayer. Their statement that one can introduce a new element does not mean that a new matter actually occurred to him and that he introduces it. Rather, his mind must be so clear that, had a new matter occurred to him, he would have been able to introduce it. If his mind is not sufficiently clear, he should not pray, because his prayer would be fixed. Rabbi Zeira therefore said, “I am able,” meaning that his mind was clear, and had a new matter occurred to him he could have introduced it. For that reason his prayer was supplication. Nevertheless, even had a new matter occurred to him, he would not rely on introducing it, lest he become confused. Although his mind was clear enough that he could do so, why should he enter even a remote doubt that he might become confused? The Magen Avraham nevertheless agrees that a voluntary prayer requires an actual new element, meaning where a new matter did occur to him. If nothing new occurred to him, he should not pray voluntarily.
