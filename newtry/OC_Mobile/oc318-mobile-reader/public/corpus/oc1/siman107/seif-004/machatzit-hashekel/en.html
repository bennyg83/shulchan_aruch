(Siman kaf dalet.) "Something new occurred to him..."—I was asked... "his prayer is not supplications." The Gemara concludes: What is "fixed"? R' Yaakov bar Idi said in the name of R' Eliezer: anyone whose prayer is like a burden; and the Rabbis said: anyone who does not say it in the language of supplications—and so we rule above in siman 98, seif 3. Rabbah and R' Yitzchak said: anyone who cannot innovate something in it. R' Zeira said: I can innovate...

And further, according to this, what is R' Zeira saying...? Meaning: if so, he never prayed, heaven forbid, all his days as established—for his prayer was fixed.

(ג) And the version of the Rif on that which we say—a prayer of nedavah specifically requires that one can innovate something in it—the Rif concludes the reason: so that his prayer be supplications. And not as the Rambam wrote in Shulchan Aruch, in order to inform that it is nedavah.

"It implies that he holds..."—meaning: in any case this is difficult for one who says one must innovate something in a prayer of nedavah; we must say that even a fixed prayer requires innovation according to Rabbah and R' Yitzchak—therefore one must say he does not hold like Rabbah and R' Yitzchak but like R' Yaakov and the Rabbis, who explain the mishnah "one who makes his prayer fixed" as something else, as above; and according to them, in a fixed prayer one need not innovate. But according to what the Rif wrote, he must hold like Rabbah and R' Yitzchak—that the reason is that if he does not innovate it is not supplications. According to this, it is difficult—for even a fixed prayer he would need to innovate according to Rabbah and R' Yitzchak. If so, why need we be informed regarding a prayer of nedavah that one must innovate? Rather Rabbah and R' Yitzchak explain the mishnah "one who makes his prayer fixed" thus: they hold that a prayer of nedavah requires innovation, for a prayer of nedavah must be supplications; and if he did not innovate in it, it is not supplications—unlike a fixed prayer, where it goes without saying it is supplications; therefore one need not innovate in it. If so, this is what the mishnah means: "One who makes his prayer fixed"—meaning one who does not innovate in it—his prayer is not supplications. And the practical difference is that one may not pray thus as nedavah, for it requires supplications.

And on this too he says...—and in truth he did not pray nedavah.

Alternatively, this is what it means...—Rabbah and R' Yitzchak always deal with a fixed prayer; and that which they said "one who can innovate something in it" does not mean that something actually occurred to him and he innovates that thing in it, but rather that his mind must be so clear that if something had occurred to him he could innovate in it; but if his mind is not so clear he may not pray, for it is fixed. Therefore R' Zeira said: I can...—meaning my mind is clear, and if something had occurred to me I could innovate; and therefore my prayer is supplications. In any case, even if something had occurred to me, why would I rely on innovating lest I be distracted—even though my mind is clear and I could innovate, what do I gain by entering the house of doubt, lest I be distracted even from a distant doubt? Nevertheless Magen Avraham agrees that for nedavah one must actually innovate—meaning if something occurred to him; but if nothing occurred to him he should not pray nedavah.
