1) [Seif 1] If some of them can see, etc. If a glass partition separates two groups and the members can see one another through it, do they combine for Zimun? Petach HaDevir, paragraph 1, writes that this depends upon the dispute between Rashba and Orchos Chaim cited by Beis Yosef at the end of siman 298 regarding the blessing over a flame after Shabbos when one sees the flame through a glass case. The conclusion from the halachic authorities is that this constitutes seeing, and they therefore combine, provided both groups clearly hear the words of the person leading the Zimun. If, however, the two groups see one another by means of a mirror, called espejo in the vernacular, they do not combine; see there. Ruach Chaim and Sedei Chemed in Asifas Dinim, section zayin, paragraph 6, cite this; see there. It appears that since the early authorities dispute this, and since the unqualified wording of the Shulchan Aruch appears to indicate that Maran, of blessed memory, rules like Orchos Chaim that the flame must be removed from the lantern before reciting the blessing, as he does not cite Rashba's distinction in the Shulchan Aruch, and since one may also distinguish between seeing a flame and seeing for the purpose of combining, one should not rely upon seeing through a glass enclosure to combine except for a Zimun without mentioning the Name. See further regarding combining above in siman 55 and our words there, with the help of Heaven.
2) There: If some of them can see, etc. Regarding two or three householders who go out into a courtyard in the summer and each eats at his own table, separate from the others, see Kesones Yosef, siman 7, who concludes that they do not combine for Zimun; see there. Zechor LeAvraham, under zayin, cites this and writes that this applies specifically when they did not initially sit with the intention of joining together. If they initially sat with this intention, they certainly combine even though they do not eat at one table; see there. See Ben Ish Chai, parashas Korach, paragraph 3, and below, paragraph 6.
3) There: If some of them can see, etc. If all the wedding guests could not eat in one house with the groom and divided into groups, they all recite the wedding blessings even if they ate in houses that do not open toward the place where the groom eats. It is not necessary to say this when an attendant connects them; even if no attendant connects them, since those in the other houses began eating when the wedding guests began, they are all considered one group for the wedding blessings, because they eat from the meal prepared for the wedding. Tur in the name of Rosh. Hagahos Maimoniyos on chapter 2 of Hilchos Berachos, however, writes in the name of Sefer Mitzvos Gadol that Ritzba ruled to recite the wedding blessings in houses opening toward the house where the wedding meal is held, provided that an attendant connects them, even though the two groups can neither see one another nor hear the person reciting the blessing. Beis Yosef cites this and writes that as practical halachah it appears proper to rely upon Rosh, of blessed memory. The Shulchan Aruch rules accordingly in Even HaEzer, siman 62, seif 11, and Levush likewise rules this way. Levush's words imply that they combine specifically for the wedding blessings, but not for Zimun, for which seeing or an attendant is required as with other groups; see there. Perishah, paragraph 4, Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 4, and Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 3, write the same. See below, paragraph 10.
3) There: They combine for Zimun. This means that they may combine and make one Zimun together, but they certainly may divide and each group make its own Zimun. Bach; Yeshuos Yaakov.
