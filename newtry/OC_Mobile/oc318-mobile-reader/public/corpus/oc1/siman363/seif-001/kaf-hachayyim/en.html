1) [Se'if 1] A place that has three partitions, etc. The Tur writes that under Torah law it is a complete private domain. Beis Yosef explains that the Tur does not mean that one who throws an object into it from a public domain is liable, but that it is permitted to carry throughout it, for this is the status of a karmelis under Torah law, in accordance with Rambam in chapters 14 and 17. Others disagree and maintain that a place with three partitions is a complete private domain, such that one who throws an object into it from a public domain is liable. Maggid Mishneh explains in chapter 17 that their dispute depends upon the interpretation of the discussions in Eruvin 11 and 94. See there. Bach, however, writes that the Tur disagrees with Rambam and means that one who throws into it from a public domain is also liable. Taz, subsection 1, writes likewise, but distinguishes between a place open to a public domain where the public presses through, in which case one who throws into it from a public domain is not liable, and a place where the public does not press through, which is a complete private domain and one is liable. See there. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1, cites him and writes that Bach and Magen Avraham imply that one is liable in every circumstance. Chemed Moshe, subsection 1, likewise writes that the unqualified wording of the Tur implies liability in every circumstance. See there. Nehar Shalom writes likewise. Regarding the principal ruling, the opinions of Rashi on Eruvin 12b, Tosafos in several places, Raavad in his strictures, Rosh in chapter 1 of Sukkah, Rashba in his novellae and Avodas HaKodesh, and Ritva are that three partitions, or two partitions and a lechi, form a complete private domain under Torah law. See above, siman 345, subsection 5, and siman 346, subsection 3, and examine the matter carefully.
2) There. The Sages prohibited carrying in it. This means more than four amos, as a decree on account of a public domain, which it resembles. Levush; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1.
3) There. The Sages prohibited carrying in it. This applies whether it is open to a public domain or to a karmelis. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, there.
4) There. The Sages prohibited, etc. Since it requires rectification only by Rabbinic law, they permitted it through even a minimal rectification. Tur; Levush.
5) There. Until one makes some form of rectification, etc. A tzuras hapesach is effective under Torah law to make it a complete private domain, as stated by Tosafos on Eruvin 6a, beginning with the words 'Rav,' and there on 22a, beginning with the words 'But did Rabbi Yochanan not say,' and by Rosh in chapter 1 of Sukkah, siman 34. A korah, however, is effective only by Rabbinic law, as stated by Tosafos on Eruvin 86a, beginning with the word 'Korah.' See also Tosafos there on 5b, beginning with the words 'Whose breach,' who write that it is a tradition from Sinai that the width of a lechi and korah is effective, while a courtyard requires a four-handbreadth strip. See the gloss of Chok Nosson there, who questions this, and Maharsha there regarding his explanation. See also Rosh there, chapter 8, siman 7, and Ritva on folio 86, who write that a korah four handbreadths wide constitutes a partition under Torah law, while one narrower than four handbreadths is effective only by Rabbinic law as a visual marker. Orach Chaim, subsection 1. See above, siman 362, subsection 88.
6) A lechi functions as a partition under Torah law, whereas a korah functions as a visual marker. Eruvin 12 states: If an alleyway was rendered permitted by a lechi, one who throws an object into it is liable; if it was rendered permitted by a korah, one is exempt. According to Rambam, this refers to an alleyway with three partitions; according to the other authorities, it refers to one with two partitions. A practical difference concerns disqualification from testimony or the status of an apostate through desecrating Shabbos: one who desecrates Shabbos in violation of Torah law is deemed an apostate with respect to the entire Torah. However, some say that nowadays we have no public domain; see siman 345, Taz, subsection 6. Mishbetzos Zahav, subsection 1. See our words there in se'if 7. Another practical difference concerns whether the Shabbos desecration violated a Torah or Rabbinic prohibition with respect to the required rectification. See the end of siman 334.
