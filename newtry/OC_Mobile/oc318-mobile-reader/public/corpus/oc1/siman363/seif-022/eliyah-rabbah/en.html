[23] And some say that it must, etc. So write the Shulchan Aruch and Beit Yosef. In my humble opinion, this 'some say' does not actually disagree with the first view. If the two beams together are one handbreadth wide, it is permitted whenever the gap between them is less than three handbreadths. If together they are only half a handbreadth wide, however, the gap may be only one handbreadth, so that a half-brick one and one-half handbreadths wide could rest upon them with one finger-width on each beam. Although a beam ordinarily needs only one handbreadth, here the two beams themselves lack that width and the gap between them is a handbreadth, so both are needed. This appears to me to be the meaning of the Tur and Rabbi Yonatan, and it is proven by Avodat HaKodesh, page 5. This is clear. The Bach and later authorities did not address this. The Bach's stringency that the two beams must together be a full handbreadth and actually touch one another is not implied by the authorities, and Nachalat Tzvi disagrees with him.
[24] Within one handbreadth. The Magen Avraham writes that even if the two beams together are four handbreadths wide, a four-handbreadth gap between them does not help, because a half-brick cannot rest upon them, unlike seif 14 where it could rest but the beams lack strength. It appears to me that his text contains an error and should read: if the air between them is more than one handbreadth, it does not help. Examine carefully. In any event, I have already written to be lenient whenever the two beams together are one handbreadth wide and the gap between them is less than three handbreadths.
[25] And some invalidate, etc. We follow the stringent view, as stated by the Bach.
