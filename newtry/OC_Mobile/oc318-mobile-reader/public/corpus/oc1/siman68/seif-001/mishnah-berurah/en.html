(1) There is no prohibition: The statement in Berachos 11 that, where the Sages said to lengthen, one may not shorten, and where they said to shorten, one may not lengthen, applies only to long blessings that begin and conclude with “Blessed.” One may not shorten them by omitting the opening or conclusion, and conversely, one may not lengthen short blessings, because doing so changes the formula established by the Sages. Regarding the rest of a blessing’s wording, however, the Sages did not prescribe an exact number of words. Had they done so, they would have had to formulate every blessing in counted words and transmit the precise text of each blessing, which we do not find. Rambam writes in the first chapter of Hilchos Krias Shema: “The general rule is that anyone who changes the formula established by the Sages for blessings is in error and must repeat the blessing according to its prescribed formula.” Kesef Mishneh, in the first chapter of Hilchos Berachos, explains that this applies specifically where one opened with a blessing where no opening was instituted, concluded where no conclusion was prescribed, omitted the opening of a long blessing with Hashem’s Name and kingship, or failed to conclude with Hashem’s Name. If, however, he altered the wording and did not recite the exact text, but used another formulation expressing the subject of the blessing, he has fulfilled his obligation after the fact even if he omitted several words, provided it contained mention of Hashem’s Name, His kingship, and the subject of the blessing. This excludes words that the Sages specifically identified as indispensable, such as mention of the covenant and Torah in Bircas HaMazon, Mashiv HaRuach, and similar matters. All this is cited by Magen Avraham in simanim 64 and 187. Biur HaGra writes that Rambam retracted this position in the first chapter of Hilchos Berachos and maintains that even in a long blessing, if one did not complete its conclusion properly, he nevertheless fulfilled his obligation after the fact, in accordance with the first opinion cited in siman 187, seif 1; see there.
(2) As long as the congregation: The same applies even if both he and the congregation have already finished the liturgical poems and the blessing, while he is standing between one section and another; he is likewise forbidden to interrupt.
(3) For as a result: Meaning, when the general public sees him looking into a book, they too may come to speak. Mere mental contemplation of Torah matters may perhaps be treated leniently; see Peri Chadash. However, while the chazzan is reciting Kaddish, it is forbidden to contemplate other matters, because one must concentrate carefully when answering Kaddish.
(4) A person should not: Rabbi Chaim Vital wrote in the name of the Arizal that he would not recite liturgical poems and hymns except those composed by the early authorities, such as Rabbi Elazar HaKalir, which were composed in accordance with the true Kabbalistic tradition. He likewise did not recite Yigdal. Nevertheless, Rabbi Chaim Vital’s son testified concerning his father that when he served as chazzan for the congregation on the Days of Awe, he recited all the confessions and all the liturgical poems. A wise person should hear this and derive wisdom and moral instruction from it, namely, not to change established customs. Shelah elaborates in the name of the Geonim that it is a mitzvah to recite liturgical poems, and that when Rabbi Elazar composed “And the creatures that are square beneath the Throne,” fire blazed around him; see there. However, with respect to customs practiced concerning the essential forms of prayer, all agree that one may not change from the custom of one’s locale, such as changing from Nusach Ashkenaz to Nusach Sefard or the reverse, and similarly in other cases. There are twelve gates in Heaven corresponding to the twelve tribes, and each tribe has its own gate and custom. As a matter of law, Ashkenazim who pray with Sephardim, or the reverse, fulfill their obligation of prayer. All this concerns only customs practiced in the essential forms of prayer. Anything stated in the Gemara, or in the rulings of the poskim derived from the Gemara, applies equally to everyone, and no person has authority to follow a divergent custom.
(5) From the congregation: See Magen Avraham, who writes that on Pesach, when the chazzanim greatly prolong the melodies, it is preferable to recite the liturgical poem before beginning the blessing of Yotzer Or. Most of the poem is unrelated to the subject of the blessing and would be considered an interruption if the pause lasted long enough to complete the entire blessing. Ideally, it is proper to take this into account, as explained above in siman 65 in Mishnah Berurah; see there. If one forgot to recite the liturgical poem until after completing the blessing, he may not recite it, for it is no better than Al HaNissim on Chanukah and Purim.
(6) With them: When praying alone, one should not recite any liturgical poem in the middle of a blessing, as stated by the later authorities. Incidentally, it is appropriate to mention here an error made by many ordinary people on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. While the chazzan chants, at the beginning of the blessing of Yotzer Or, the liturgical poem beginning “He spoke of lights from darkness, and it came to be,” the congregation afterward begins immediately with “Melech” or “Selach Na,” without beginning the blessing from its opening. They should be warned about this.
