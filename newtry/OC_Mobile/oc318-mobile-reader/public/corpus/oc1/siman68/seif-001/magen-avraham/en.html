The Arizal would not recite liturgical poems and hymns except those composed by the early authorities, such as Rabbi Elazar HaKalir, which were composed in accordance with the true Kabbalistic tradition. However, customs practiced concerning the essential forms of prayer should not be changed from the custom of one’s locale, for there are twelve gates in Heaven corresponding to the twelve tribes, and each tribe has its own gate and custom. Matters mentioned in the Gemara, however, apply equally to everyone. This is stated in Shaar HaKavanos. The Yerushalmi states: “Although we sent you the order of the prayers, do not deviate from the custom of your ancestors.” Sefer Chassidim, siman 256, likewise writes that even the verses recited in connection with the liturgical poems should be chanted with the melody used for the kerovos. Shibolei HaLeket elaborates in the name of the Geonim that it is a mitzvah to recite liturgical poems, and when Rabbi Elazar composed “And the creatures that are square beneath the Throne,” fire blazed around him. A person should not separate himself, etc. Maharam of Rothenburg likewise writes in responsum 87, but states that his custom was to recite the liturgical poem and afterward begin the blessing of Yotzer Or, so as not to interrupt in the middle of the blessing. When praying alone, one should not recite any liturgical poem in the middle of the blessing. Nevertheless, at the end of siman 54 the Rama writes that it is forbidden to interrupt between Yishtabach and Yotzer. When the poem is recited in the middle of the blessing, it can at least be considered relevant to the blessing; see siman 119, seif 1, in the gloss. Yet on Pesach, when the chazzanim greatly prolong the melodies, it is preferable to recite the liturgical poem before beginning the blessing, since most of it is not relevant to the subject of the blessing. It also appears to me that if one forgot to recite the liturgical poem until after completing the blessing, he may not recite it, for it is no better than Al HaNissim on Chanukah and Purim; see siman 294. Furthermore, when it is recited within the blessing it can be considered relevant to that blessing, but between one blessing and another it is forbidden to interrupt.
