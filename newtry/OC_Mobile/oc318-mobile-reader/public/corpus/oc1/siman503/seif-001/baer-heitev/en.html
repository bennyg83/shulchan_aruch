She fills. Even after she has placed the pot on the fire, she is permitted to add meat, because the dish is better prepared when there is much there (Tur, Beis Yosef, Yam shel Shlomo). It is implied that even if it is in a small pot, it is permitted to put it into a large pot and add upon it so that it be fatter; and see this se'if. The Tur wrote that it is permitted to say explicitly, "For the second day of Yom Tov I want this leftover," since in any case there is benefit on Yom Tov from the greater quantity; and in Yam shel Shlomo he ruled that one should be stringent not to say so explicitly, but only in a nonspecific way; and so it seems in the Bach. And the world practices a leniency, cooking in the morning for the need of the night; and in any case one must be careful to eat a little of it. And Beis Yosef wrote that even if one eats only from the dish that is with the meat, or from the gravy, it is permitted. And the Bach wrote that specifically when one needs one piece, then it is permitted to fill the pot; but if the main cooking is for the need of the night, and in the morning one eats a little of it so that it not be forbidden for him, that is circumvention, unlike many who err in this prohibition; see there. And Magen Avraham wrote: Nevertheless it appears to me that since the custom is to be lenient, cooking in the morning and tasting something of it, one should not forbid it for them; but to prepare from Yom Tov to a weekday in such a manner is forbidden; see there. And so wrote the Taz, whose wording is: A woman may cook many kinds of dishes on the first day of Yom Tov even though she knows for certain that they will leave over one kind, so long as they taste from it even an olive-bulk, since she cooks everything before eating. And in this it appears that it is forbidden to say explicitly that one kind will be for the second day of Yom Tov; and even the Tur who permits, as I wrote, that is in one pot, to add because of the benefit of the greater quantity; but here, where the permission is only because it is before eating and one will taste from it an olive-bulk, if he says explicitly that it is for the need of the second day of Yom Tov, the tasting from it does not avail him; end quote; see there where he elaborates further, and concludes: Nevertheless it appears as the practical law that since in any case there is a prohibition if one says explicitly that it will be for the second night, therefore one must be careful to cook before Yom Tov what is needed for the second night. And after the fact it appears that if he circumvented by eating an olive-bulk even after eating, it is permitted after the fact, for circumvention by eating an olive-bulk does not have the status of circumvention so as to forbid him more than an intentional [transgression]; and so it is nearby here, where the Shulchan Aruch wrote, "However, if he transgressed and cooked, it is permitted," and that is with circumvention by an olive-bulk. Later authorities, and not like the Bach. The Mordechai wrote in chapter 2 of Beitzah that Rabbeinu Yoel permitted baking on Yom Tov for the need of a bris milah that fell on the next day, and it was their festival day so that they could not bake if not on Yom Tov, and one oven was not enough, and he permitted baking twice; see there. And Hagahos Maimoniyos disagrees with this and wrote that this is called circumvention and is forbidden; see there. And Magen Avraham wrote: One can say that specifically regarding bread he was stringent, because each loaf needs effort and preparation on its own; but regarding a pot, which is one effort, it is permitted; see there.
