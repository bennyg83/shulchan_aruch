In vain. See Baer Heitev, who cites Taz that one should inspect the hole at the corner. Taz explains that this area is also prone to deterioration. I do not know why Baer Heitev omitted Magen Avraham, who writes the opposite because the threads there are not ordinarily expected to break. See there. It is clear from this that if the thread breaks within the hole before the knot, the tzitzis are invalid. Responsa Sheilas Yaavetz, siman 33, likewise rules that even if only one thread breaks inside the corner hole, producing two half threads while all the other threads remain whole, the tzitzis are invalid. He explains that the broken thread is no longer suspended from the garment. See there. Magen Avraham writes that in pressing circumstances one may rely upon the established presumption that the threads remain valid, provided he knows that they were whole the previous day. It appears that he means circumstances in which he cannot delay to inspect them, such as the case cited in the name of Taz where he is called to the Torah while standing with his tallis in hand and has no time to inspect it because of human dignity, or another comparable situation. Beit Yaakov, cited in Baer Heitev, writes that one traveling on the road who fears he will have no other tallis should this one prove invalid may rely upon the presumption, since strictly according to law he is not obligated to inspect it. This does not appear correct. What difference is there between being on the road and being at home? Inspection protects him from wearing a tallis without valid tzitzis and from reciting a blessing in vain. Since the matter can be clarified beforehand, he accepted this inspection upon himself and made it obligatory. The fact that he may have no other tallis if this one is invalid changes nothing. In either event he cannot wear an invalid tallis. Furthermore, while traveling there is no concern for human dignity, since an observer will assume that the tallis was lost or became invalid during travel and that another cannot be obtained on the road. A genuinely pressing circumstance is different, because the obligation was never accepted where it is not readily possible to clarify the matter. Beit Yaakov further writes that if one failed to inspect the tallis and later found it damaged, while it had meanwhile remained in a protected place so that the damage certainly occurred earlier, he is considered inadvertent rather than compelled. In my humble opinion, this is also incorrect. Were it permitted to bless and wrap oneself without inspection, then since he acted permissibly, the later discovery that it was invalid would render him compelled. The proof cited from Tosafos in Beitzah is no proof at all, because there Tosafos explains that he should not have hurried so much, which is inapplicable here.
