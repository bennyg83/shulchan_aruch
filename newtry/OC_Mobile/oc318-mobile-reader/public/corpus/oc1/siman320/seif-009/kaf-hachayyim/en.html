9) There, gloss: In a place where people customarily squeeze a particular fruit, etc. The plain meaning of Rema's words is that the prohibition applies only to the residents of that place, not worldwide. The source of this law, however, is Beis Yosef, who explains that if we know that in any place a fruit is squeezed for its juice, it may not be squeezed anywhere, as we find concerning pomegranates. The gloss must therefore mean that if there is a place where people customarily squeeze it, its law is like that of mulberries and pomegranates and it is forbidden everywhere. The phrase 'in a place' is imprecise. This interpretation is compelled by Rema's conclusion that its law is like mulberries and pomegranates: just as those are forbidden everywhere, the same must apply to other fruits. This is contrary to Magen Avraham, paragraph 1, who writes that Rema disputes Maran Beis Yosef and holds that the prohibition applies only to the inhabitants of the place where the fruit is squeezed. Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 2, writes that the primary view is Beis Yosef's and that Rema agrees; see there. Chemed Moshe, paragraph 3, likewise challenges Magen Avraham at length and concludes that Beis Yosef's view is primary and that Rema agrees, as necessarily follows from his comparison to mulberries and pomegranates; see there. Pears, called birnen in German and angas in Arabic, may not be squeezed even according to Magen Avraham, because they are customarily squeezed for beverages in Ashkenazic lands; see there. Apples likewise may not be squeezed, because in Ashkenazic lands many barrels are filled with apple juice, as Chayei Adam, klal 14, paragraph 3, writes. All this follows the authorities who permit squeezing other fruits. We already wrote in the preceding paragraph, however, that one should initially account for the prohibiting view and not squeeze any fruit anywhere for the purpose of obtaining a beverage; see there.
