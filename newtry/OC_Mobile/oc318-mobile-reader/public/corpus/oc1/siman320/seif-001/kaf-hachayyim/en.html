1) [Seif 1] Olives and grapes may not be squeezed. By Torah law, one is liable only for pressing olives and grapes. Shabbos 145a; Rambam, chapter 8, halachah 10; Magen Avraham at the beginning of the siman. One is liable even for squeezing them by hand into a bowl; the law does not apply only to pressing them with a utensil, as Rambam writes explicitly there; see there. Eshel Avraham at the beginning of the siman. One is not liable unless the liquid squeezed measures the volume of a dried fig. Rambam there. See below, siman 368, seif 3, regarding this measure.
2) There: Olives and grapes may not be squeezed. This constitutes extracting, for which one is liable as a derivative of threshing. Rambam, chapter 21, halachah 12; Levush; Olas Shabbos, paragraph 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1. One who squeezes raisins is likewise liable for threshing. Chayei Adam, klal 14, paragraph 2. Squeezing them into food is permitted, however, as stated below in seif 4.
3) There: Olives and grapes may not be squeezed. By Torah law, one may squeeze other fruits to extract their juices, because no fruit juice has the status of a beverage except that produced from olives and grapes. The juices of other fruits retain the status of food, and separating food from food does not constitute extracting. Shulchan Aruch HaRav there. The Sages nevertheless forbade squeezing certain fruits because some people squeeze them for their juice, as explained below.
4) There: If the liquid emerged on its own, it is forbidden, etc. This is a rabbinic decree lest one squeeze the fruit initially. Levush. The liquid remains forbidden until after Shabbos. So write the later authorities, and in my view this is obvious.
5) There: Even if they were intended only for eating. It is not only when their purpose was unspecified that the liquid is obviously forbidden. Even if one gathered them solely for eating, their juice is nevertheless forbidden. Since most olives and grapes are intended for pressing, he has this possibility in mind, is pleased with the juice, and might come to squeeze them by hand. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 3.
6) There: Mulberries and pomegranates may not be squeezed. Some people who possess many mulberries or pomegranates squeeze them for juice as they do olives and grapes. If other people had such quantities, they too would ordinarily squeeze them for juice. Therefore, one who squeezes them for juice causes his intent to give them rabbinic beverage status, and we do not say that his view is disregarded against that of everyone else. Shulchan Aruch HaRav there.
7) There: If they are intended for juice, it is forbidden. The same is forbidden when their purpose is unspecified, because unspecified fruit is considered intended for juice. This is implied at the beginning of the chapter Chavis. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 5, and Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah write the same, as does Meiri, contrary to Korban Nesanel.
8) There: All other fruits may be squeezed. Beis Yosef cites a dispute. Rif, Rambam, Rosh, and Tur imply that all other fruits may initially be squeezed even for their juice. Rashi, Tosafos, and Hagahos Semak permit squeezing only to sweeten the fruit, but forbid squeezing for its juice; see there. The Shulchan Aruch's unqualified words imply that squeezing even for the juice is permitted. Bach, however, writes that one should not rule permissively concerning squeezing any fruit for its juice, contrary to the unqualified ruling in Shulchan Aruch and the gloss that follows it. He writes that the universal practice is to forbid squeezing every fruit for its juice. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1, cites him and writes that where this practice exists it certainly remains binding. Nevertheless, when one squeezes fruit only to dip food into the juice, it is permitted because the juice is not considered a beverage, as Beis Yosef writes in the name of Shibolei HaLeket and Rosh; see there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, at the end of paragraph 1, likewise writes that the first view is primary, but where the stringent practice exists one should not deviate. Taz, paragraph 1, distinguishes that even according to the Shulchan Aruch, who permits squeezing other fruits, this applies only without a specific intention, whereas squeezing them specifically to extract juice is forbidden. Chemed Moshe, Nehar Shalom, and Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 1, have already challenged this distinction; see there. In practice, although the first view, that of the Shulchan Aruch and those who agree, is primary and only a place with a stringent practice must preserve it, one should initially account for the prohibiting view. Chayei Adam, klal 14, paragraph 3; Ben Ish Chai, Parashas Yisro, paragraph 3.
9) There, gloss: In a place where people customarily squeeze a particular fruit, etc. The plain meaning of Rema's words is that the prohibition applies only to the residents of that place, not worldwide. The source of this law, however, is Beis Yosef, who explains that if we know that in any place a fruit is squeezed for its juice, it may not be squeezed anywhere, as we find concerning pomegranates. The gloss must therefore mean that if there is a place where people customarily squeeze it, its law is like that of mulberries and pomegranates and it is forbidden everywhere. The phrase 'in a place' is imprecise. This interpretation is compelled by Rema's conclusion that its law is like mulberries and pomegranates: just as those are forbidden everywhere, the same must apply to other fruits. This is contrary to Magen Avraham, paragraph 1, who writes that Rema disputes Maran Beis Yosef and holds that the prohibition applies only to the inhabitants of the place where the fruit is squeezed. Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 2, writes that the primary view is Beis Yosef's and that Rema agrees; see there. Chemed Moshe, paragraph 3, likewise challenges Magen Avraham at length and concludes that Beis Yosef's view is primary and that Rema agrees, as necessarily follows from his comparison to mulberries and pomegranates; see there. Pears, called birnen in German and angas in Arabic, may not be squeezed even according to Magen Avraham, because they are customarily squeezed for beverages in Ashkenazic lands; see there. Apples likewise may not be squeezed, because in Ashkenazic lands many barrels are filled with apple juice, as Chayei Adam, klal 14, paragraph 3, writes. All this follows the authorities who permit squeezing other fruits. We already wrote in the preceding paragraph, however, that one should initially account for the prohibiting view and not squeeze any fruit anywhere for the purpose of obtaining a beverage; see there.
10) There, gloss: If it is done only for medicinal purposes, there is no concern. This implies that if a fruit is normally squeezed as a beverage, it may not be squeezed even though this person wants it for medicinal purposes.
11) There, gloss: But it is permitted to suck, etc. Although nursing directly from an animal is forbidden, as stated in siman 328, seif 33, this is because it is the ordinary manner. Here it is unusual, so the Sages did not decree against it. Beis Yosef in the name of Shibolei HaLeket; Magen Avraham, paragraph 2; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 4. Taz, paragraph 2, writes that Shibolei HaLeket contains a scribal error, but Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2, correctly notes that he was imprecise, because its words refer to nursing from an animal. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 6, likewise challenges him; see there.
12) There, gloss: Certainly with other things. Beis Yosef writes in the name of Shibolei HaLeket that sucking meat or bread, or sucking juice from fruits or grapes with one's mouth, does not resemble squeezing at all. In the name of Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 21, however, he writes that one should take care not to squeeze meat that has absorbed broth for the purpose of obtaining the liquid. One who places meat in broth, or soaks bread in wine, brings it to his mouth, and sucks out the liquid acts impermissibly, and there is concern that he may be liable to bring a sin offering. Certainly one should not suck grapes or similar fruit with his mouth. These are its words. Rema therefore writes in the gloss that some forbid sucking with the mouth. Maran's view in Beis Yosef appears to follow those who permit, as Birkei Yosef writes in Shiyurei Berachah, paragraph 1. Bach writes in the name of Maharshal that a distinction should be made. Broth absorbed in meat or wine absorbed in bread already possessed beverage status, so sucking it is close to incurring liability to bring a sin offering and is forbidden. Liquid that emerges from grapes before it possessed beverage status, however, such as when one sucks grapes in his mouth and discards the seeds, does not constitute extracting. This is the ordinary manner of eating grapes and is legally permitted, although one who is stringent will receive a blessing. Bach writes that this is the accepted ruling: meat and bread are forbidden, while grapes are permitted. Magen Avraham, paragraph 4, however, writes that Rema implies that other things are more lenient than grapes and that Rashi's commentary also implies this; see there. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 9, likewise is stringent specifically regarding olives and grapes, and this is the view of Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3; see there. Since this matter is disputed, one should take care not to suck meat or bread that absorbed liquid, nor olives or grapes. Other fruits appear subject to leniency.
13) There, gloss: Some forbid sucking with the mouth, etc. It appears that this applies specifically when one places part of the fruit in his mouth, leaves part outside, and sucks, because this is the manner of sucking. If he places the entire fruit in his mouth and sucks it, all opinions permit it because this is the manner of eating.
14) There, gloss: Some forbid sucking with the mouth, etc. Nevertheless, fried foods that have absorbed much fat may be squeezed, because they were food initially and remain food now. Chayei Adam, klal 14, paragraph 6. Zichru Toras Moshe writes the same.
