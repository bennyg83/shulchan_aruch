They are accustomed to go out, etc.—see Mishna Berurah in the name of Eliyah Rabbah that even opposite another woman it is permitted, and so it appears from the conclusion of all the poskim, and not like Chasam Sofer who was strict about this in siman 36.

Outside their braids—and those women who come from countries where their custom is not to uncover outside their braids, to a place where their custom is to uncover, are permitted to uncover if they do not intend to return [Magen Avraham in the name of R' Moshe Alsheikh]. Regarding reciting Keriat Shema opposite them, Shulchan Shelomo wrote that it seems permitted even if they intend to return [reason: there is no arousal]. And if they came from a place where their custom is to uncover to a place where their custom is not to uncover, they may not uncover even if they intend to return [reason: men's arousal], and it is forbidden to recite Keriat Shema opposite them even if they intend to return. And if one man comes from a place where their custom is to uncover to a place where their custom is not to uncover, it is forbidden to recite Keriat Shema opposite them in all cases; and if he comes from a place where his custom is not to uncover to a place where his custom is to uncover, it is permitted if he does not intend to return. See Mishna Berurah seif kaf-dalet what we wrote there.
Regarding the main prohibition of uncovering a woman's hair, Magen Avraham wrote in the name of Tosafos in Ketubos that only in the market is it forbidden, but in a courtyard where people are not found they are permitted to walk with uncovered head [and what was written in Magen Avraham in the name of Acharonim 115 is not proven at all to one who examines]. But in the Zohar, Parshat Naso, he was very strict that no hair of a woman should be seen, and so one should conduct—so far Magen Avraham. And know that this matter does not apply at all to the law of "hair of a woman is ervah" stated in this seif; and even in her house and in her room it is forbidden for all authorities to recite Keriat Shema opposite her, since their custom is to cover in the market there is arousal in it and it is ervah—so it is plain to one who examines, and likewise Yad Ephraim. However, even the main law regarding uncovering hair that he brought in the name of Tosafos is not clear, for Semag wrote in the name of the Jerusalem Talmud to forbid, and so is Tur's view there, and Bach wrote there that such is Rambam's view, and our Gemara also explains thus—that in actual uncovering, even in her room, it is forbidden. And Shulchan Shelomo in siman 115 wrote that per our custom it is dat yehudit in actual uncovering even in her courtyard and room, and even if you say she will not leave for this without a ketubah, nevertheless there is a prohibition. And likewise in responsum of Chasam Sofer siman 36, that since our forefathers' fathers already accepted upon themselves in every place we heard Israel is dispersed to forbid this—if so it is a complete law that they accepted this prohibiting view, and as Magen Avraham wrote siman 551 seif kaf-zayin—and see there where he extended greatly on this and wrote at the end that in our lands where the custom spread per the Zohar it is an absolute prohibition, and one should be concerned for "ravitzat Elokim" stated in the Zohar [and brought in Mishna Berurah], and whoever wants blessing should distance himself from it—so far.
All this regarding her courtyard and room; but to walk in the market and public courtyard, for all authorities—even if she walks with only a scarf to cover her hair—it is considered violating dat yehudit until she walks with a radid on top like all women, as explained there in siman 115. And know further that women who were accustomed to walk with their head hair loose—how very much the woman standing for immersion must watch carefully that none of her hairs go outside, even something of one hair, for otherwise she remains a full niddah and is liable for karet. And if she immerses on Friday night when she cannot move the candle and watch carefully for this, she should wrap her head in a thin thing (called tul) that is full of holes so water enters them and there is no chatzitzah—and even though this is a very simple matter for anyone who knows religion and law [see Yoreh De'ah siman 198 seif 40], nevertheless because of the severity of the matter, which touches on karet prohibition, I did not refrain from informing this here.
