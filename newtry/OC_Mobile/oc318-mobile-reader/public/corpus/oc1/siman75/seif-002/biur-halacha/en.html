Outside their binding. Women who come from countries where it is not customary to expose hair outside its binding to a place where it is customary to expose it may expose it if they do not intend to return. Magen Avraham in the name of Maharam Alshakar. Regarding reciting Krias Shema opposite it, Shulchan Shlomo writes that it appears permitted even if they intend to return, because it does not cause improper thought. If they come from a place where it is customary to expose it to a place where it is not customary, they may not expose it even if they intend to return, because it causes improper thought among the men there. It is prohibited to recite Krias Shema opposite them even if they intend to return. If one man comes from a place where it is customary to expose it to a place where it is not customary, he may never recite Krias Shema opposite it. If he comes from a place where it is not customary to expose it to a place where it is customary, he may do so if he does not intend to return. See what we wrote in Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 14.
Regarding the essential prohibition against a woman uncovering her hair, Magen Avraham writes in the name of Tosafos in Kesubos that it is prohibited specifically in the marketplace, but in a courtyard where men are not commonly present women may go with uncovered heads. What is cited in Magen Avraham from Even HaEzer 115 is not at all proven there upon examination. The Zohar in parashas Naso, however, is exceedingly stringent that no hair of a woman be seen, and it is proper to conduct oneself accordingly. These are Magen Avraham's words. Know that this matter is entirely separate from the law that a woman's hair is ervah stated in this seif. Even in her house and private room, all agree that it is prohibited to recite opposite it, since she ordinarily covers it in the marketplace, it causes improper thought and is ervah. This is obvious to one who examines the matter, and Yad Ephraim writes likewise. Even the basic ruling concerning uncovering hair that Magen Avraham cites in the name of Tosafos is not clear. Semag writes in the name of the Yerushalmi that it is prohibited, and this is also the view of Tur there. Bach writes there that Rambam agrees, and he interprets our Gemara this way as well, that actual uncovering is prohibited even in her private room. Beis Shmuel in siman 115 writes that according to our custom, actual uncovering is a violation of Jewish practice even in her courtyard and private room. Even should one say that she does not thereby forfeit her kesubah without warning, there is nevertheless a prohibition. Chasam Sofer, responsum 36, writes likewise. Since our ancestors in every place where we have heard that Jews are dispersed accepted this prohibition, it has the force of a complete law because they accepted the stringent opinion, as Magen Avraham writes in siman 551, seif katan 7. See there, where he discusses this at length and concludes that in our countries, where the practice has spread in accordance with the Zohar, it is an absolute prohibition, and one must fear the curse mentioned in the Zohar, which is cited in Mishnah Berurah. One who desires blessing should distance himself from this.
All this concerns her courtyard and private room. In the marketplace or a public courtyard, however, all agree that even if she wears only a kerchief to cover her hair, she is considered to violate Jewish practice until she wears an outer covering over it like other women. This is explained there in siman 115. Know further that women who are accustomed to wear their head hair uncovered must be exceedingly careful when immersing that none of their hair protrudes, even the slightest amount of a single hair. Otherwise she remains fully a niddah, and relations with her incur kares. If she immerses on Friday night, when she cannot carry a lamp to inspect carefully, she should wrap her head in a thin perforated material, called tulle, so that water enters through the openings and it does not constitute an interposition. Although this is exceedingly elementary to anyone who knows the religious law, see Yoreh Deah, siman 198, seif 40, because of the severity of the matter and its bearing on a prohibition punishable by kares, I have not refrained from recording it here.
