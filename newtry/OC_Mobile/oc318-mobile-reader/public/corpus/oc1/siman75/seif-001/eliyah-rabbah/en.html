[1] Handbreadth exposed, etc. — implies for another woman prohibition only at handbreadth; though Rama wrote some say less than handbreadth is nakedness for another woman — Bach wrote Rama also holds we do not establish this. Rama concludes from Rosh: handbreadth on woman is nakedness even for another woman — only she herself may read naked. From 'handbreadth is nakedness' — less than handbreadth not nakedness. Not precise — Rosh specified handbreadth; Rama innovates woman-to-woman nakedness. I think Rama disputes 'some say' on another woman less than handbreadth; 'from Rosh' means handbreadth applies woman-to-woman too. 'Some say' is Ba'al Halakhot Gedolot — no proof: he wrote even wife if exposes handbreadth forbidden — obviously another woman at handbreadth; less than handbreadth no prohibition even for another. Resolves Olat Tamid. Ba'al Halakhot Gedolot: thin garment appearing like skin — forbidden; learned from seif 5 on sackcloth. No difference maiden/married. Be'er Sheva 119 on voice:
[2] That she ordinarily covers, etc. This includes even her lower leg, but the face, hands, and feet are permitted. Bach's wording is: “In towns where people go barefoot, those parts are always ordinarily exposed.” Lechem Chamudos writes that it appears her arms are not included in “hands,” unless she is in a place where they are accustomed to expose them. I saw in Rokeach, siman 324: “A handbreadth of her flesh or her exposed arms is prohibited.” Levush writes that this applies specifically to a man, but the woman herself may recite even when entirely naked if her face is pressed to the ground. Malbushei Yom Tov writes: Why did he specify a man? The same should apply to a woman, for the exclusion applies only to herself. This is also the wording of the Rosh, “specifically with another,” etc. Perhaps Levush holds that one woman is not prohibited by another woman, as Rashba writes in Beit Yosef. It is difficult, however, why he would be lenient where Rosh is stringent. In my humble opinion, Levush understands the Rosh's phrase, “specifically with another,” not as referring to one woman with another woman, but as excluding herself. Alternatively, he used “another” to teach that even with another woman it is prohibited only when a handbreadth is exposed. Examine this carefully. Or perhaps there is a scribal error in the Rosh and it should read, “specifically for others,” meaning her husband or another man. I found this version in Sefer Zikaron by the Gaon Rabbi Yosef HaKohen. Anyone who examines his work will see that he follows the Rosh, and he copied the entire discussion there from the Rosh. See there. Hagahos Semak, siman 64, writes likewise, and this is explicit in the rulings of the Rosh and in Rabbeinu Yerucham, a student of the Rosh, folio 25. Examine this carefully. This explains why Tur and all the great authorities cited above did not record the novel ruling that one woman is prohibited by another woman. Rather, all agree that it is permitted. A further proof appears to me from Beit Yosef, who writes concerning the Rosh's wording, “Rashba writes likewise.” This is difficult, because according to Rashba one woman is permitted with another woman. Evidently the Rosh also permits it. This supports what I wrote above in explaining Rama. Examine this carefully. I saw in Maadanei Melech, folio 26, that he writes regarding Rashba: “His words imply that a woman is not prohibited by seeing another woman's ervah, but the Rosh's words appear correct, for just as a man may not see another man's ervah, and we indeed find a prohibition against women engaging in intimate conduct with one another.” I am astonished. Certainly, regarding seeing the actual ervah, one woman is also prohibited by another woman, for it is no better than her own ervah, as Levush implies. The permission concerns only other ordinarily covered parts. Regarding those parts, one man is likewise permitted with another man. I have also explained that the Rosh never intended to prohibit one woman with another woman. This is clear as practical halachah.
[3] Even if she is his wife, etc. Even her lower leg, which is ordinarily exposed on a man, is ervah on a woman. Rashba and Beit Yosef. Taz, however, writes that the lower leg is prohibited even in the case of one's wife and even when less than a handbreadth is exposed, because it is more private than other limbs.
