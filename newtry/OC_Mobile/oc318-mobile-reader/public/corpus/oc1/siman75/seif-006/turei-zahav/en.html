Or he closed his eyes. Beit Yosef writes this, but it is astonishing to me. First, the language of the Gemara does not imply this. It states there: What is the reason? Because the verse says, “And no naked matter shall be seen in you,” and here it is visible. It does not say, “and he sees it.” We learn that since it is visible opposite him, it is prohibited even though he does not see it. Furthermore, regarding excrement as well, the matter is said to depend on sight, namely that when it is before him he must distance himself the full range of vision, as stated in siman 79. Nevertheless, it is prohibited even for a blind person and at night, since it is possible to see it. The Gemara's language of sight concerning excrement, where it says that when it is not in another domain it is prohibited within the range of vision, cannot be more stringent than the verse's language, “And no naked matter shall be seen in you.” Both use the language of sight. I am further troubled by the Tosefta in Terumos, cited in the gloss at the beginning of Chullin, that a naked person may not separate terumah. Likewise, regarding shechitah, we rule at the beginning of Yoreh Deah, siman 1, that a naked person may not slaughter. According to Beit Yosef, it should be permitted if he closes his eyes while reciting the blessing. One cannot answer that the reason is that his heart sees the ervah, because then it would remain difficult according to the authority who permits when the heart sees the ervah. How would he explain this Tosefta? Beit Yosef adduces proof from Rambam, who permits turning one's face. That case is different, because when he turns his face the ervah is not opposite him and is not visible to him. The Torah was particular only when the ervah is opposite him, as Rambam writes that it is prohibited to recite opposite it. One cannot say that regarding terumah and shechitah he should likewise turn his face and recite the blessing, because he himself remains with exposed ervah. It is prohibited to recite because of “And no naked matter shall be seen in you,” meaning that no naked matter may be upon him. This differs from Beit Yosef's interpretation, according to which everything depends on his actual seeing and its visibility is irrelevant. Beit Yosef further adduces proof from Rashba, cited in Beit Yosef, siman 74, concerning the Gemara's question regarding one bathing in clear water: “But his heart sees the ervah.” The Gemara does not ask regarding his eyes, because his eyes are outside the water and he looks outward and does not see the ervah. This is no proof at all. We have already said that it is prohibited only when ervah is opposite him, not when it is below him. If one stood at the edge of a pit with ervah inside the pit, there would be no prohibition against reciting Krias Shema. Although he is standing in clear water, the ervah is considered covered relative to other people. The fact that he can look downward and see his own ervah does not make it “opposite him.” It therefore appears that the law follows Rokeach, cited by Beit Yosef, who prohibits even if he closes his eyes. I saw that my father-in-law, of blessed memory, also rules stringently. The turning of the face that was permitted means turning his entire body and standing in another direction. It is comparable to siman 73, where one sleeping with his wife may recite by turning his face. There, merely turning the face cannot suffice while he is lying down. Here too, it specifically means turning his entire body.
