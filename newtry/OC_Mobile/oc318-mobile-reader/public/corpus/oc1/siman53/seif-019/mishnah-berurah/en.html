(49) From being shaliach tzibbur — because he will not be able to say "God of our fathers"; but when he prays by himself he says "God of the fathers of Israel."

(50) And their words were rejected — for he can also say "God of our fathers," for Abraham is called "father of a multitude of nations" as written "for father of a multitude," etc., because he taught the whole world faith in Hashem. Shaliach tzibbur who is a mamzer depends on the dispute whether a mamzer is fit to write tefillin [so it is written in Shiurei Knesset HaGedolah]; and according to this, for Magen Avraham in siman 39 who wrote in the name of Derishah that one permitted to write tefillin is permitted to be shaliach tzibbur. However, Birkei Yosef in Yoreh De'ah 481 concludes he should not be shaliach tzibbur, and tefillin he wrote are valid.

(51) Can obstruct — at the time of appointment; and the reason is that prayers were instituted in place of the sacrifices, and every Israelite has a share in the sacrifices—and who can offer his sacrifice against his will unless he has already agreed to him? Then a single individual cannot [remove him], and even half the congregation can transfer him even if they wish to give him his wages from their own—since he did not act improperly. But the majority of the congregation can transfer him if they give his wages from their own [Avraham Zvi]. And now the authority to transfer him is only in the hands of the seven good men of the city or those selected, each place according to its regulation [Pri Megadim].

(52) "I do not want" — and specifically when there is another shaliach tzibbur whom the congregation can hire with his wages; but if he is not available, or is available but comes to add to the wages, the congregation can say to him that we have no ability to add, but he should add from his own; and even if he clarifies his claim that this second one is superior to the first against the addition of wages, nevertheless the congregation can say we have no such ability in our hands to add to the wages.

(53) "So-and-so shall be cantor" — the same applies to all appointments: one can protest even against a sage who disseminates Torah [head of a yeshiva]; and specifically when he wishes to accept another. But if he protests that he does not want there to be a Torah disseminator in the congregation, obviously his protest counts for nothing, and even the minority can compel the majority. And even when wishing to appoint another—if the one the majority agree upon is preferable to this one, he also cannot protest. And possibly even the minority can compel the majority to hire a superior one [Knesset HaGedolah]. Later authorities wrote that this ruling of Shulchan Arukh is specifically in their days; but today it is known that, with God's help, many hold disputes without reason or sense and not for Heaven's sake—if they had to ask each and every individual regarding appointments, whether shaliach tzibbur or Torah disseminator or av beit din in the city and the like, they would never agree; therefore they follow the majority of tax payers, even disqualified relatives among them [Magen Avraham]. And now the custom is to follow the seven good men of the city or those selected from the congregation regarding this, each place according to its custom [Pri Megadim]. And all this is so that disputes should not multiply, and they do not heed an individual. Magen Avraham wrote another reason: the law of individual obstruction regarding shaliach tzibbur does not apply today—specifically in their time when the shaliach tzibbur discharged the multitude with the eighteen [blessings] in his prayer, then an individual could obstruct, for his agent is not made against his will; not so now when everyone is expert and the shaliach tzibbur is only for piyyutim—even though he says Kaddish and Barechu, there is not such strictness.

(54) Initially — and if they accepted him for a set time and the time was completed, it is not called that they agreed to him initially—for now there is a new acceptance as though he were not there yet [Taz]. And see what we wrote at the end of the previous section. Reuven, who was a hired shaliach tzibbur for a year, completed his year and began to pray for a number of days of the new year; and some people protested against him saying the year they hired him for had ended and they no longer want him. The shaliach tzibbur claimed that since he had already begun to pray in the Days of Awe which are of the new year and they did not refuse him, that being hired for a year—part of it is like all of it—and they should pay him from their own. Maharitz ruled in responsum that the law is with the shaliach tzibbur. See Leket HaKemach 14, 1a.

(55) The good men of the city — meaning he proposed before them some matter for which he does not agree to him—e.g., that his traits are not upright in his eyes, and the like—and not a claim of disqualification, for in this all agree an individual cannot protest so long as he has not clarified, for there is concern for his honor.

(56) His enemy — meaning it is known in truth from before that they hate one another; therefore he can protest against him, for his agent for prayer is not made against his will. But if he merely says he hates him—that is not of such consequence.

(57) "Previously, etc." — But if one becomes an enemy of the sheliach tzibbur after they had agreed to him, then an individual may not protest; rather, the sheliach tzibbur is obligated to state explicitly that he will remove jealousy from his heart and fulfill their obligation through his prayer. Also, if he intends not to fulfill the enemy, his friends too would not fulfill their obligation; therefore cantors must be careful about this. [Pri Megadim] wrote in Darkei Moshe in the name of Eliyahu Zuta: a sheliach tzibbur need not flatter the congregation in divine matters and must rebuke them; but regarding his own affairs he should overlook slights and be beloved to the congregation, as below.

(58) "Enemy of the sheliach tzibbur" — meaning: since he is an enemy of the sheliach tzibbur, presumably the sheliach tzibbur also does not love him, for "as water reflects a face" (Proverbs 27:19), etc., and perhaps the sheliach tzibbur who reads in the rebuke portion will intend it against his face — and there is concern of danger for him; therefore he ruled that for this reason, even if they called him, he should not go up. Keneset Hagedolah wrote it is better that he go up than that he enter punishment for belittling the honor of the Torah; and the Gra agreed; and so wrote Shaarei Ephraim, as follows: he must be beloved to the congregation and he must love them. If it happened that the reader hates one of the congregation, the sexton is obligated not to order this one to read the rebuke portion, because it is dangerous. If the reader knows that someone hates him and fears they may call him, he should go out person by person from the synagogue until another is called, and then enter afterward; if he did not go out and they called him, he should go up, since he intends for the honor of the Torah — a mitzvah observer, they will not know anything bad. It is forbidden for the reader to think at the time of his reading about a curse toward any person; see there. Pitchei Shaarim wrote that even per Magen Avraham he goes up after the fact if they called him.
