14) [Se'if 4] A shaliach tzibbur must be fit, etc. Scripture says, 'She raised her voice against Me; therefore I hated her.' Mar Zutra bar Tuvyah said in Rav's name (and some say Rabbi Chama said in Rabbi Elazar's name) this refers to an unfit shaliach tzibbur who descends before the lectern. Taanis 16b; Tur. Regarding the verse's feminine language, Kisei Shlomo on Taanis, page 116b, writes: It is known that every prayer is called rachamim. The shaliach tzibbur must arouse mercy, which comes from the right, the masculine side, as those familiar with the mystical wisdom know. An unfit shaliach tzibbur not only fails to arouse the masculine side, but even transforms the attribute of mercy into the attribute of judgment, which comes from the feminine side. The verse therefore uses the feminine, 'She raised,' etc. See there for his ethical rebuke of an unfit shaliach tzibbur. Machazik Berachah 1; Shalmei Tzibbur, page 71c; Shaarei Teshuvah 6. See also below, paragraph 16.
14) There: A shaliach tzibbur must be fit, etc. Abudraham explains why the shaliach tzibbur is customarily called a chazzan: the Aramaic translation of 'he saw' is 'chaza,' because he must see how he reads. Eliyah Rabbah 5.
15) There: A shaliach tzibbur must be fit, etc. See siman 579:1: for a fast, additional qualifications of this kind are required, all according to the circumstances. Magen Avraham 6. See siman 581:1: on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur he should be married and thirty years old, resembling the Kohen Gadol, who had a wife; see there. This implies that throughout the rest of the year he may serve even if unmarried. Nevertheless, a married man takes precedence over a bachelor, even if the latter's beard is full, including during the rest of the year. Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham 6. See the next paragraph.
16) There: What constitutes this? He should be free of transgressions. Zohar, Vayikra, states: Rabbi Yitzchak said, 'If the anointed kohen shall sin'; this refers to the kohen below, who was appointed for the service and in whom a sin was found; 'bringing guilt upon the people.' Woe to those who rely upon him. Similarly, if a shaliach tzibbur sins and a transgression is found in him because the people were not found meritorious before the Holy One, blessed is He, woe to those who rely upon him. Sefer Chassidim writes that regarding shaliach tzibburs who are not complete in Torah and mitzvos yet descend before the lectern, it is said, 'Your advocates rebelled against Me.' Solel Boneh 1; Shalmei Tzibbur, page 72a. Rama writes in explanation of 'Show me your appearance': First correct your deeds, and the Divine image will rest upon your face as it did before you sinned. Afterward, 'let me hear your voice' in prayer and supplication, for then 'your voice is pleasant' before Me 'and your appearance is lovely.' The reason is that if a person merits some spark of the sanctity of the Shechinah, his prayer is accepted. He does not merit this unless complete in all 613 mitzvos, as Tikkunim explains concerning 'Any man in whom there is a blemish shall not approach.' Therefore, if any sin is found in him, woe to those who rely upon him. Solel Boneh, ibid. The shaliach tzibbur must consequently take great care not to interrupt with speech between the silent Amidah and its repetition. Kesher Gudal 18:21 and Siddur Beis Yaakov 10 write in the name of the Arizal's disciples that even for an individual, speaking between the silent Amidah and the repetition is a grave sin. All the more does this apply to the shaliach tzibbur, as Pischei HaDevir 111:3 and Kaf HaChaim by Rav Chaim Palagi 15:52 write; see there. How could he commit a prohibition and then serve as shaliach tzibbur, repeating the prayer before Hashem on the people's behalf? Does he not fear the verse, 'She raised her voice against Me; therefore I hated her'? This will be further explained in our comments to siman 124:1, with G-d's help.
17) There: No bad reputation should have emerged concerning him, even in his youth. Re'em, Responsa 1:86, writes that Rambam disagrees with Baal HaTurim. Rambam holds that an ordinary shaliach tzibbur (not one serving during a fast) need not have an unblemished past. If he now possesses good deeds, then even if a bad reputation emerged in his youth, and even if he worshipped idolatry, once he repented he is fit to be a permanent shaliach tzibbur. This is the primary ruling. Rashach 2:107; Rabbi Aharon Sasson 157-158; Mahara di Boton, handwritten responsum 3; and Maharshal, Responsa 20, rule likewise. Maharshal writes that all these qualifications are required specifically on fasts and during maamados. On other days, even for a permanent appointment, anyone who is not wicked, is acceptable to the people, has a pleasant voice, and is accustomed to read may be appointed shaliach tzibbur; see there. Rashdam, Orach Chaim 32 and Yoreh Deah 113, however, writes that Rambam does not disagree with Baal HaTurim; even if he does, Baal HaTurim's view is primary. Mishpat Tzedek 1:18 writes that perhaps even according to Rashdam, one who became an Ishmaelite and then fully repented may serve as shaliach tzibbur, although the question posed to him did not concern an apostate; see there. Raavad 13 writes that this is also evident from Rashdam; see there. Knesses HaGedolah cites this in Hagahos HaTur and Shiyurei Hagahos Beis Yosef 2. Magen Avraham 8 writes that if he committed a transgression under compulsion, everyone agrees he is permitted. Shalmei Tzibbur, page 73c, rules likewise. Yad Aharon, Hagahos Beis Yosef, however, questions Rashdam's aforementioned words and concludes: For an ordinary shaliach tzibbur we do not require an unblemished past. Anyone whom we see has repented is called fit. This is the primary ruling, and most later authorities rule accordingly. Pri Chadash 25; Birkei Yosef 2 in the name of the later authorities, who writes that he also found this explicit in the handwritten Responsa of the great master Mahari ibn Migash 97; Maamar Mordechai 1; and Shalmei Tzibbur, page 73b. Olas Tamid 5 likewise concludes this, adding that if the congregation decrees a fast, then even if it is not presently an established public fast, such a person should not serve; all the more so on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. Eliyah Rabbah 6 rules likewise, but concludes that during the rest of the year too it is proper to be stringent in accordance with Shulchan Aruch and Levush.
18) Olas Tamid further writes that even Shulchan Aruch's stringency (that no bad reputation should have emerged in one's youth) applies only to appointing him initially. We need not be stringent so as to remove or dismiss him after he has become established as shaliach tzibbur. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, writes likewise in the name of Raanach 2:41, as do Magen Avraham 7; Eliyah Rabbah 6; Shalmei Tzibbur, page 73c; and Shulchan Aruch HaRav 6.
19) Once he has already been removed, his present status is that of an initial appointment. Raanach 2:57. Rashdam, Orach Chaim 32, writes that if the entire congregation agreed to remove him and did so, and they now wish to appoint him again, even a minority of the congregation may object. Mishpat Tzedek 2:77 writes that this applies specifically when the congregation agreed to remove him and he remained out of office for many days or years. If the congregation did not agree to remove him and he did not remain out for days or years, the minority cannot object. Knesses HaGedolah, ibid.; Magen Avraham, ibid.; Eliyah Rabbah 29; Shalmei Tzibbur, ibid.; Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham 7; and Chayei Adam 29:11.
20) There: He should be humble and acceptable to the people. This means humble and agreeable toward others, etc., for anyone with whom people are pleased is pleasing to the Omnipresent. Eliyah Rabbah 7. A shaliach tzibbur who insulted an individual member of the congregation in the synagogue and before beis din must ask that individual for forgiveness in the presence of those before whom he sinned. Maharash Chayun in Mishpatim Yesharim 14; Yad Aharon, second edition, Hagahos HaTur 1.
21) There: Acceptable to the people. It is proper for the shaliach tzibbur to enter the synagogue first and leave last. Maharam Mintz, Responsa 81; Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur; Magen Avraham 7; Eliyah Rabbah 18; Solel Boneh 11; Shalmei Tzibbur, page 71b; Shulchan Aruch HaRav 7; and Chayei Adam 29:8.
22) There: Acceptable to the people. The chazzan must take care that if a dispute or quarrel arises between two householders, he remains passive and takes no side, whether in speech or deed, openly or secretly. All the more so, if a non-Jew has a dispute with a householder, he must take no side, even if that non-Jew is his relative by marriage or other relative. If he himself has a quarrel or enmity with an individual or with one member of the congregation, he must submit the matter to the Rav or the congregation for compromise and do whatever they deem proper. If the deficiency or obstruction comes from the opposing party, he is blameless; what more can he do? Eliyah Rabbah 7 in the name of Responsa Maharam Mintz; see there.
23) The poskim write that among the qualities required of a shaliach tzibbur is that he forgo his claims against others, as the Talmud states in Taanis 25b: Rabbi Eliezer descended before the lectern and was not answered. Rabbi Akiva descended, said Avinu Malkeinu, and was answered. A heavenly voice declared: It is not because this one is greater than the other, but because this one forgoes his claims and the other does not. Solel Boneh 2; Shalmei Tzibbur, page 71b. He must also not be a flatterer. Sotah 41b states: Rabbi Elazar said that anyone who possesses flattery brings wrath to the world, as it is written, 'Those who flatter in heart incur wrath.' Moreover, his prayer is not heard, as it says, 'They do not cry out when He binds them.' It is also written, 'For what is the hope of a flatterer when he gains,' and, 'Will God hear his cry when distress comes upon him?' Mahari Abuhav already writes that one category of flatterers consists of those who perform good deeds so that people will honor them. This filth ruins all of a person's service. Some people pray with a pleasant voice while thinking during prayer, 'How pleasant my voice is, and how many people listening to me derive pleasure from it!' Shalmei Tzibbur, ibid.; Solel Boneh 5. See Shaar HaKedushah by Maharchu, part 2, gate 4, which writes that one who publicizes his good deeds before people not only receives no reward for them, but is also punished; see there. I cited his words in my sacred work Beis Yaakov, discourse on the Torah, Vayikra; see there. See also what I wrote concerning this in my sacred work Edus BeYaakov at the beginning of chapter 2 of Avos.
24) There: He has a pleasant melody and voice. Pesikta expounds, 'Honor Hashem with your wealth'; read it as 'with your throat.' If your voice is pleasant, stand and honor Hashem with your voice. If a person is unfit and they nevertheless allow him to pray because his voice is pleasant, the Holy One, blessed is He, does not accept his prayer, as it is written, 'She raised her voice against Me; therefore I hated her.' Ateres Zekeinim 2. See below, paragraph 54.
25) There: Accustomed to reading Torah, etc. The verses of prayer should be ordered before him. Rashi, second chapter of Taanis, 16a at the end; Beis Yosef; Levush; and Shulchan Aruch HaRav 4. It also appears to me that one unaccustomed to reading falls under, 'One who turns his ear from hearing Torah; even his prayer is an abomination.' Eliyah Rabbah 8. It further appears to me that the Torah records performance of the mitzvos, while Neviim and Kesuvim contain ethical rebuke. Through them he will become humble and diligent in mitzvos, and his prayer will be favorably accepted. See above, paragraph 16.
