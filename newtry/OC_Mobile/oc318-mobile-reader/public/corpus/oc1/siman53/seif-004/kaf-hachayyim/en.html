14) [Seif 4] "A shaliach tzibbur must be fit," etc. Scripture states, "She raised her voice against Me; therefore I hated her." Mar Zutra bar Tuvyah said in the name of Rav, and some say R' Chama said in the name of R' Elazar, that this refers to an unfit shaliach tzibbur who descends before the lectern. Taanit 16b; Tur. Regarding the feminine wording of the verse, Kisei Shlomo explains that every prayer is called mercy, and the shaliach tzibbur must arouse the attribute of mercy, associated with the right side and the masculine aspect. An unfit shaliach tzibbur not only fails to arouse that side, but can transform mercy into judgment, associated with the feminine aspect; therefore Scripture uses the feminine form. See there for his ethical rebuke of an unfit shaliach tzibbur. Machazik Berachah, letter 1; Shalmei Tzibbur, page 71c; Shaarei Teshuvah, letter 6. See also below, letter 16. 14) There, "A shaliach tzibbur must be fit," etc. Abudraham explains why the shaliach tzibbur is customarily called a chazzan: the Aramaic translation of "he saw" is "chaza," for he must see how he reads. Eliyah Rabbah, letter 5. 15) There, "A shaliach tzibbur must be fit," etc. See siman 579, seif 1, where additional qualifications are required for a public fast, each matter according to its circumstances. Magen Avraham, seif katan 6. See siman 581, seif 1, where on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur he should be married and 30 years old, analogous to the Kohen Gadol, who had a wife. This implies that during the rest of the year an unmarried person may serve. Nevertheless, a married man takes precedence over a bachelor even if the latter has a full beard, including during the rest of the year. Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, letter 6. 16) There, "What constitutes this? He should be free of transgressions." Zohar, Vayikra, states that if the kohen appointed for service is found to have sinned, woe to those who rely upon him. The same applies to a shaliach tzibbur in whom sin is found when the congregation is not meritorious before Hashem; woe to those who rely upon him. Sefer Chasidim writes that shaliach tzibburs who are not complete in Torah and mitzvot yet descend before the lectern are included in the verse, "Your advocates rebelled against Me." Solel Boneh, letter 1; Shalmei Tzibbur, page 72a. Rama explains the verse, "Show me your appearance; let me hear your voice," to mean: first correct your deeds so that the Divine image rests upon your face as before sin, and afterward let Me hear your voice in prayer and supplication, for then your voice is pleasant and your appearance lovely. A person's prayer is accepted when he merits a spark of the sanctity of the Shechinah, and he merits this through completeness in the mitzvot. Therefore a shaliach tzibbur must be exceedingly careful not to speak between the silent Amidah and the repetition. The disciples of the Arizal write that even for an individual this is a grave sin; all the more so for the shaliach tzibbur. How can he perform a prohibited act and then stand to repeat the prayer before Hashem on behalf of the congregation? This will be explained further in siman 124, seif 1. 17) There, "No bad reputation should have emerged concerning him, even in his youth." Re'em, responsum 1:86, writes that Rambam disagrees with Baal HaTurim and holds that an ordinary shaliach tzibbur, unlike one serving on a fast, need not have an unblemished past. If he now performs good deeds, then even if a bad reputation emerged in his youth, and even if he served idolatry, once he returned in repentance he is fit to serve permanently. Rashach, Rabbi Aharon Sasson, Mahara di Boton, and Maharshal rule similarly, maintaining that the full list of qualifications applies specifically on fasts and during maamados. On ordinary days, even for a permanent appointment, anyone who is not wicked, is acceptable to the congregation, has a pleasant voice, and is accustomed to read may be appointed. Rashdam disagrees and maintains that Rambam does not reject Baal HaTurim, or that Baal HaTurim's view is primary. Several later authorities nevertheless conclude that for an ordinary shaliach tzibbur an unblemished youth is not indispensable and that sincere repentance restores fitness. If the transgression was committed under compulsion, everyone agrees he is permitted. For a communal fast, and all the more so Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, one should be stringent. Eliyah Rabbah adds that even during the year it is proper initially to satisfy the stringent view of Shulchan Aruch and Levush. 18) Olat Tamid further writes that even the Mechaber's stringency regarding a bad reputation in youth applies only to an initial appointment. Once a person has become established as shaliach tzibbur, one need not remove him on that account. Knesset HaGedolah in the name of Raanach; Magen Avraham; Eliyah Rabbah; Shalmei Tzibbur; Shulchan Aruch HaRav. 19) Once he has actually been removed, a later reappointment is treated as a new appointment. Raanach. Rashdam writes that if the entire congregation agreed to remove him and did so, and they later wish to restore him, even a minority may object. Mishpat Tzedek limits this to a case where the congregation actually agreed upon his removal and he remained removed for a considerable period. Otherwise the minority cannot object. 20) There, "He should be humble and acceptable to the people." Meaning humble and agreeable toward others, for one with whom people are pleased is also pleasing before Heaven. Eliyah Rabbah. A shaliach tzibbur who insulted an individual member of the congregation in the synagogue and before beit din must ask that person for forgiveness in the presence of those before whom the insult was made. 21) There, "Acceptable to the people." It is proper for the shaliach tzibbur to enter the synagogue first and leave last. Maharam Mintz; Knesset HaGedolah; Magen Avraham; Eliyah Rabbah; Solel Boneh; Shalmei Tzibbur; Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Chayei Adam. 22) There, "Acceptable to the people." The chazzan should avoid taking sides in disputes between members of the congregation, in speech or action, openly or secretly. All the more so, where a non-Jew disputes with a Jewish householder, he should not take sides merely because the non-Jew is his relative by marriage or otherwise. If the chazzan himself has a dispute or enmity with someone, he should submit the matter to the Rav or the congregation for compromise and accept what they deem proper. If the obstruction comes from the opposing party, he has done what is required of him. 23) The halachic authorities write that among the qualities required of a shaliach tzibbur is that he forgo his claims against others. Taanit 25b relates that R' Eliezer descended before the lectern and was not answered; R' Akiva descended, said Avinu Malkeinu, and was answered. A heavenly voice declared that this was not because one was greater than the other, but because one yielded concerning his personal claims and the other did not. He must likewise not be a flatterer. Sotah 41b states that flattery brings wrath and prevents prayer from being heard. Mahari Abuhav writes that one category of flatterers consists of people who perform good deeds in order to receive honor from others. This corrupts a person's service. A person may pray with a pleasant voice while inwardly admiring his own voice and the pleasure others derive from hearing him; this too is spiritually corrupt. Shaar HaKedushah writes that one who publicizes his good deeds receives no reward for them and may even be punished. 24) There, "He has a pleasant melody and voice." Pesikta expounds, "Honor Hashem with your wealth," as though it said, "with your throat": if your voice is pleasant, stand and honor Hashem with your voice. But if a person is unfit and is nevertheless appointed only because of the pleasantness of his voice, Hashem does not accept his prayer, as indicated by the verse, "She raised her voice against Me; therefore I hated her." Ateret Zekenim. See below, letter 54. 25) There, "Accustomed to reading Torah," etc. The verses of prayer should be properly arranged before him. Rashi, Taanit 16a; Beit Yosef; Levush; Shulchan Aruch HaRav. One who is not accustomed to read may fall under, "One who turns his ear from hearing Torah, even his prayer is an abomination." Torah teaches performance of the mitzvot, and Neviim and Ketuvim contain ethical rebuke; through them he becomes humble and diligent in mitzvot and his prayer is favorably accepted.
