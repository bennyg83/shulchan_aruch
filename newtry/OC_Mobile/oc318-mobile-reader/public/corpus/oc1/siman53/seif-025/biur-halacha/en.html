If witnesses came against him. See Mishnah Berurah regarding an unceasing rumor. See also Biur HaGra, who cites Gittin 89a and Kesubos 36b. This refers to the Gemara's statement there that if a rumor circulated in the city that a woman had committed adultery, we do not take it into account. At first glance, this implies that even an unceasing rumor is disregarded, for otherwise the Gemara's question there would arise; see there. In Kol Bo, from which this ruling is derived, the text says “valid witnesses,” which apparently implies that an unceasing rumor alone is certainly insufficient. In Responsa Chasam Sofer, siman 11, he cites these words of Kol Bo and concludes that even where there is an unceasing rumor, if the entire congregation is satisfied to retain him as chazzan, one need not protest against them. If, however, one member objects and says, “I believe this unceasing rumor,” he may object and have him removed. He further writes that at times one removes him because of an unceasing rumor even when no one objects, such as where the rumor is that the indecent act was committed before witnesses who subsequently traveled overseas. Although the witnesses are not before us, since there is a rumor that witnesses exist elsewhere, and the rumor initially emerged through valid witnesses, he is removed from his position. But where the accusation did not originate from witnesses, and the rumor initially emerged through a slave, maidservant, or women, he is not removed unless some individuals object to him, as stated above. An unceasing rumor is defined as one concerning which the entire city spoke for a day and a half, where he has no enemies in the city of whom we can say that they spread the rumor, as stated in Yevamos 25a. Responsa Maharik, siman 188, implies that it is called an unceasing rumor only where the public agrees upon it and it does not cease, for we may presume that such a rumor did not arise without cause. But if we know that only one person initially spread the rumor and it then circulated throughout the city, it is not termed an unceasing rumor. Possibly the same applies here. I later found that Chasam Sofer, Orach Chayim, siman 25, implies this; see there. Yet there is seemingly a major difficulty: how can any unceasing rumor suffice to remove a chazzan, when he thereby also suffers a monetary loss, as is evident from Yam Shel Shlomo, Chullin, at the end of the first chapter; see there. With regard to an ordinary monetary loss, it is obvious and clear that no rumor is effective, as is implied by the unqualified language of the Gemara in Gittin 89a at the beginning of the page, and likewise by the statements there concerning a rumor that one consecrated or rendered ownerless his property; see there. Even a compelling presumption is ineffective to extract money, as implied in Choshen Mishpat, siman 408, and all the more so a mere rumor. Perhaps the reason is that Gittin 89a states that we disregard a rumor that a woman committed adultery because it is possible that people merely saw her acting immodestly, and this gave rise to the rumor. Regarding a chazzan, however, people presumably would not initially wish to appoint a person who had acted with such immodesty that an unceasing rumor of adultery arose concerning him. He was obligated to guard himself against coming to such a situation. Where several signs of immorality are observed in him, it is obvious that he must be warned, as Rama writes regarding a chazzan who uses obscene language.
