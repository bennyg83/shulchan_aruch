For his father. Meaning that his father died and he wishes to pray in the manner of mourners, for prayer is a greater mitzvah than saying Kaddish.
That the congregation wants. The reason is that specifically Kaddish he can say for his father, and the congregation has no authority to push him away. But he cannot become the congregation's agent to pray against their will. In this respect, it is also like the law of a shaliach tzibbur, where an individual does not object, so as not to increase dispute, but we follow the majority or those appointed for this, as we wrote above in the name of the later authorities. There are places that have an ordinance in the synagogue not to allow a mourner to pray before the amud throughout the week, but only their regular shaliach tzibbur; the ordinance should not be set aside. It was made because of ignoramuses who do not know and understand the blessings and prayers and say them incorrectly, causing disgrace, and therefore they enacted even regarding those who know that they should not pray, so that they not come to quarrel. However, in a place where there is no fixed ordinance and custom, it is a mitzvah for the congregation to allow the mourner to pray, for it gives satisfaction to the deceased and saves him from the judgment of Gehinnom. Nevertheless, certainly where he cannot articulate the letters and the like, he should not pray before the amud. The Magen Avraham wrote: if there is a mourner and a mohel, the mourner is pushed aside from prayer because of the mohel. A day on which one's father or mother died, which is called yahrzeit, pushes aside the mohel and the mourner. All this is where the congregation is not particular about it, but the congregation has the right to choose for themselves whomever they want.
