One should not recite a blessing, etc. Tur begins by discussing removal without qualification and concludes, 'when he removes them, even at night.' His meaning appears to be as follows. One might initially say that the scholars of Eretz Yisrael recited 'Who commanded us to observe His statutes' as thanksgiving to Hashem for having already fulfilled the mitzvah, similar to Birkas HaMazon after a meal. If so, we too should recite this blessing when removing tefillin after the morning prayer. Tur therefore explains that this is not so. The scholars of Eretz Yisrael held that nighttime is not a time for tefillin, etc., and recited their blessing close to sunset. Since we reject that view, we do not recite a blessing even when removing them at night.
No blessing whatsoever. This means not only that one does not recite 'Who commanded us to observe His statutes,' which the scholars of Eretz Yisrael recited because they held that nighttime is not a time for tefillin and the verse says, 'You shall observe this statute.' Plainly, we do not recite it because we rule that nighttime is a time for tefillin. Even when removing tefillin on erev Shabbos at twilight, however, when according to us as well he must remove them because the verse calls tefillin 'a sign' while Shabbos and Yom Tov require no such sign because they are themselves a sign, one might have thought he should recite a different blessing; Tur teaches that he does not. Tur explains that whenever removal of tefillin is not derived from 'You shall observe,' no blessing is required. Beit Yosef asks: Why should the identity of the verse matter? He could recite a blessing reflecting 'It shall be a sign for you.' Beit Yosef gives two answers. First, that verse does not create a prohibition; it merely says there is no need to wear tefillin then. This is difficult, because there truly is a prohibition. Rosh cites Baal HaIttur as forbidding tefillin on Shabbos and Yom Tov based on 'You shall observe,' then disagrees and derives the prohibition from 'It shall be a sign for you.' Thus, they disagree about the source, not the existence of the prohibition. Beit Yosef's second answer is that we recite only a blessing found in the Talmud; 'Who commanded us to observe His statutes.' Once that blessing is rejected, no blessing is recited. Still, why did the Talmudic Sages not institute another blessing? It appears that we thank Hashem in a blessing only for sanctifying us through a mitzvah He gave us and explicitly commanded us to perform; under the Western scholars' view, 'You shall observe.' According to us, however, the prohibition results from the arrival of Shabbos sanctity, which displaces the sanctity of tefillin. We already bless over the sanctity of Shabbos, and the other sanctity consequently departs.
