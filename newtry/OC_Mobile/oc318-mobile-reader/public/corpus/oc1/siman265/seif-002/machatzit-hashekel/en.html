Seif katan 2, "one may not place," etc., because it is possible to shake it off. See siman 277, seif 3, where it is explained that if one forgot a wax candle on a table, he may shake the table and the candle will fall. It is written there that even if the candle is lit, he may shake the table, provided that he does not intend to extinguish it. This is not comparable to oil, which cannot be shaken away without loss, because the oil would have to be shaken onto the ground and would be ruined. If he shook it into another vessel, he would instead render that second vessel unusable on Shabbos. "According to the view in siman 309, seif 4," etc. That view holds that the permission to shake off muktzeh applies specifically where the forbidden item was forgotten on the permitted object, not where it was deliberately placed there before Shabbos. Where it was deliberately placed, the permitted object itself becomes a basis for the prohibited item and may not be moved. Nevertheless, when he placed it there with the intention that it not remain there for the entire Shabbos, it can be treated like a forgotten item and may be shaken off. "And so writes Ran," etc. The wording is not precise if it implies that Ran forbids for the same reason as the first opinion in siman 309. Their reasons differ. According to that first opinion, the prohibition is that the vessel became a basis for a prohibited item. According to Ran, the prohibition is that one may not render a vessel unusable even for part of Shabbos. Beit Yosef's words, "and so writes Ran," refer only to the practical ruling that Ran too prohibits. "It requires study, for everyone agrees that if he placed it there on Shabbos it is permitted," this is the correct text. Beit Yosef sought to prohibit for 2 reasons: first, according to the first opinion in siman 309, because deliberate placement creates a basis; second, according to Ran, because it is forbidden to render a vessel unusable even for part of Shabbos. Magen Avraham rejects the first reason: where the prohibited item was not upon the vessel at bein hashmashot, even that opinion does not treat the vessel as a basis. "This is an explicit Gemara," meaning Shabbos 154b. Rav Huna says that where one's animal was carrying glass vessels, the Gemara establishes the case as small bloodletters' horns, which are repulsive and muktzeh. They may not be removed directly by hand. Leaving them on the animal causes pain to the animal, while loosening the sacks and allowing them to fall would break them and cause monetary loss. Therefore one brings cushions and pillows, places them underneath, loosens the ropes, and allows the sacks to fall onto the cushions so that they do not break. The Gemara asks that this renders the cushions unusable, and answers that the case involves small bundles that can be shaken off the cushions. Large bundles are forbidden because their weight prevents shaking them off. If deliberate placement during Shabbos itself made the cushions a basis for the entire Shabbos, even the small bundles should be forbidden, because after placing them on the cushions he could not shake them off. The Gemara's permission therefore proves that an item placed there during Shabbos does not create the status of a basis in this manner. "Rather Ran," etc., still holds that it is forbidden to render a vessel unusable even for part of Shabbos, except in a case of substantial loss. Thus Magen Avraham rejects Beit Yosef's first reason but leaves the second reason in place. The discussion concerning sparks is different. Sparks extinguish immediately after falling into the vessel, so the vessel is unavailable only for an extremely brief moment and immediately becomes usable again. The Gemara therefore permits placing a vessel beneath sparks. By contrast, where a candle or another muktzeh item rests on the vessel for a meaningful period, the vessel is unavailable for that interval. One must also distinguish between a case in which the prohibited item can be shaken off at once and a case in which it cannot. Where it can be shaken off, the vessel does not remain unusable and there is greater room for leniency. Where the item cannot be removed until later, the prohibition of rendering the vessel unusable applies. The principle is therefore that an object does not become a basis merely because muktzeh was newly placed upon it during Shabbos, but one may still violate the separate prohibition of making a permitted vessel unavailable for use. In a case of substantial monetary loss, the authorities permit more, as explained in the sources cited by Magen Avraham.
