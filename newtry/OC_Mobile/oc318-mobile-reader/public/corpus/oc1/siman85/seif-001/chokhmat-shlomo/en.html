Se'if 1. A person should not walk through filthy alleyways, etc. Gloss: After the fact, if he violated this and recited, it appears that he must recite again, as written above in Shulchan Aruch, siman 76, se'if 5, and in my work Sefer HaChayim. I later found this in Pri Megadim, who explains that the reason is that it is a mitzvah performed through a transgression. I have now seen that one may explain in this manner the statement of the Talmud in the third chapter of Berachos, page 24b: If he did not stop, what is said of him? Rabbi Meyasha, son of the son of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi, said: Scripture says of him, “I too gave them statutes that were not good,” etc. Rabbi Elazar said: “Woe to those who draw iniquity with cords of falsehood.” Rabbi Adda, etc., derived it from here: “For he has despised the word of the Omnipresent.” To understand what they disagree about and what practical difference follows, it appears as follows. There is a dispute concerning a mitzvah performed through a transgression. Some hold that it is completely valid, while others hold that it is invalid. That dispute concerns a Torah mitzvah, but when the mitzvah is rabbinic, a mitzvah performed through a transgression is valid. We have already written in several places that according to this, the same applies where the transgression is rabbinic: a mitzvah performed through that transgression is valid. Others hold that even a rabbinic mitzvah performed through a transgression is invalid. It appears that these authorities dispute this very point. The authority who derives the ruling from the verse, “I too gave them statutes that were not good and ordinances by which they shall not live,” holds that a mitzvah performed through a transgression is valid. Although a transgression was committed, and it is explained in tractate Sotah that a transgression extinguishes a mitzvah, so that he receives no reward for the mitzvah, conversely he is also not punished, because the mitzvah extinguishes the transgression. The two offset one another. Therefore, the verse says, “I too gave them statutes that were not good and ordinances by which they shall not live”: they receive no reward for them, but they also receive no punishment. The other authority holds that a mitzvah performed through a transgression is invalid. Since there is no mitzvah, only the transgression remains, and he is punished for it. It is known that the reward of a transgression is another transgression and that one transgression leads to another; this is the way of the evil inclination, which continually increases its burden, as stated in the Midrash. Consequently, a mitzvah performed through a transgression subsequently leads to a bare transgression. Transgressions themselves are not compared to ropes, because they are like spiders' threads and do not form a rope. Mitzvot, however, are strong and are compared to ropes. If one performs a mitzvah without a transgression attached, these are cords of truth. If one performs what appears to be a mitzvah but in truth contains a transgression and is invalid, these are cords of falsehood. Therefore the verse says, “Woe to those who draw iniquity with cords of falsehood”: through a mitzvah performed through a transgression, which appears to be a mitzvah but is not one, they afterward draw actual iniquity. This proves that a mitzvah performed through a transgression is invalid. Otherwise, they would at least deserve no punishment, and an actual transgression should not be drawn from it. This authority therefore holds that even if the prohibition against reciting in a filthy place is only rabbinic, the recitation is invalid because it is a mitzvah performed through a transgression; he holds that this concern applies even to a rabbinic transgression, and likewise to a rabbinic mitzvah. The remaining authority holds that a mitzvah performed through a rabbinic transgression is valid. If recitation in a filthy place were forbidden only rabbinically, one would therefore fulfill his obligation, although he violated the prohibition. However, the verse says, “For he has despised the word of the Omnipresent,” making this an explicit Torah transgression. A mitzvah performed through a Torah transgression is invalid, and therefore he does not fulfill his obligation. But if the transgression were rabbinic, he would fulfill it; likewise, a rabbinic mitzvah performed through a transgression is valid. Thus there are three distinct opinions, and the passage is well explained. Examine this carefully.
