(4) Even to think. This applies even when no person is present there.
(5) Words of Torah. It is likewise forbidden in a privy to study the grammatical weights of sacred Hebrew nouns and verbs when they can be understood only through Scripture, because he will come to think about verses. It is obvious that a person may reflect in a privy upon his own lowliness and that ultimately he will entirely become dust, worms, and decay, and that arrogance is therefore unbecoming.
(6) It is forbidden. He should think there about his household accounts and expenses so that he not come to think words of Torah. On Shabbos he should think about attractive buildings and designs.
(7) In a privy. Even within four amos of the privy, beginning from the point where the odor ends, it is still forbidden to think words of Torah. See above, siman 79, Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 3. Tosafos Yerushalayim writes in the name of the Yerushalmi that in a tannery it is forbidden to pray or mention any sacred matter once the tanning process has begun and there is a foul odor. Before it has begun, it is permitted. It is obvious that when it is forbidden, the prohibition applies even to the craftsman himself, who is accustomed to the odor and does not notice it, provided that ordinary people are distressed by that foul odor, as stated above in siman 79:1.
(8) And in a bathhouse. This is because it contains abundant filth and is repulsive, making it comparable to excrement and a privy. The reason thought is forbidden in all these places is that the Torah requires, 'Your camp shall be holy,' and this condition is absent. Beit Yosef writes that one whose learning is fluent in his mouth and who involuntarily thinks Torah in a privy or bathhouse is permitted. Some say that since his thought was involuntary, he may even articulate the words with his lips, and this appears to follow from Zevachim 102b. Birkei Yosef, however, concludes that speech is forbidden in every circumstance. The proof from the Gemara can be rejected, as written by Bircas HaZevach and Tzon Kodashim; see there. The statement that involuntary thought is permitted means only that if he thought involuntarily, he committed no prohibition. Initially, however, he is obligated to repel the thought, and speech is certainly forbidden. Yeshuos Yaakov likewise rules that speech is forbidden.
(9) In the sacred tongue. It is a pious practice to be stringent.
(10) Faithful. The Raavad forbids the designation Merciful. Bach writes that one should be stringent, and Peri Chadash rules likewise. In another language, however, all agree that it is permitted because that word is not used exclusively for the Holy One, blessed be He. Other words of Torah, and certainly the Divine Names that may not be erased, may not be said there even in another language, such as 'Gott' in German or 'Boga' in Polish and Russian and similar terms. Although such a name has no sanctity in the letters in which it is written and may be erased, mentioning it in a filthy place is nevertheless degrading. This is comparable to mentioning the word peace, which may also be erased, yet because the Holy One, blessed be He, is called by it, it may not be mentioned there when one intends the Divine designation, as written in siman 84. This is certainly true here, since for several laws these foreign-language terms have the status of Divine Names in sacred Hebrew, including oaths, mentioning Hashem's Name in vain, and cursing another person with the Name.
(11) They may be said. This is because people are also sometimes described by these terms, as in the expressions gracious, merciful, and righteous. This applies only when one says the word alone. If he says, 'May the Merciful One have mercy upon you,' it may be forbidden, like inquiring about another's welfare. (Peri Megadim.)
(12) That may not be erased. This is not precise, for the word peace is also forbidden to mention there, as explained above in siman 84.
(13) To prevent a prohibited matter. The same applies if a sinful thought enters his mind. He may think words of Torah there, because this is comparable to preventing a prohibition, since Torah protects a person from improper thoughts.
(14) And concerning sacred matters. Meaning, he may even tell the person in the language of a ruling that it is forbidden to act that way. He need not carefully phrase it merely as, 'Do not do that,' without the language of a ruling. Some are stringent and maintain that one may not speak in wording that clearly appears to be Torah. For example, if he sees people on Erev Pesach shaving after midday, he should say, 'One does not shave,' because this is not phrased as a formal ruling even though the prohibition is automatically understood. He should not say, 'It is forbidden to shave.' It appears to me that if a brief statement will not be heeded, he may explain to them the severity of the prohibition in all its details in order to separate them from it. Magen Avraham writes in the name of the Ran that even when there is no need to prevent a prohibition, one may tell another, 'Do such and such for me,' even if this automatically conveys that the act is permitted, provided that he does not state it in the language of a ruling.
(15) And in a place where it is permitted. For example, in the middle room of a bathhouse, as explained above, or where the person himself is not fully clean, such as when his hands touched normally covered parts of his body. If his hands or other parts of his body are actually soiled, even thought is forbidden. (Peri Megadim.)
(16) To decide a ruling. This is comparable to thought, since he considers the reason in his mind. See above, siman 47:4, in Mishnah Berurah and Biur Halachah.
