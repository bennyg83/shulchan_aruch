Faithful. The Raavad forbids saying "Rachum." The Bach wrote that one should be stringent, and so ruled Peri Chadash. However, in a foreign language everyone agrees it is permitted. Specifically "shalom" or "Rachum ve-Chanun" may be mentioned in a foreign language, but other divine names that may not be erased are forbidden to mention even in a foreign language. Likewise, other matters of the holy tongue are forbidden to say in a mundane language, as Ateret Zekenim and Olat Tamid write. The forbidden. The same applies if a sinful thought enters his heart: he may think there of words of Torah, for this is to separate himself from prohibition, as Sefer Chasidim and Magen Avraham write. Beit Yosef wrote: one whose learning is fluent in his mouth in the privy or bathhouse and who thinks of it under compulsion is permitted. Perishah explained that since he is compelled in his thought, he may even articulate it with his lips, and so it also implies in Zevachim 102b.
Sacred. Like the case where Rabbi Meir said to him, "One may not anoint on Shabbat." It would have sufficed for him to say, "Do not anoint," or, "I do not want you to anoint," yet he said it in the language of instruction. So it appears. The Ran wrote that even without separating someone from prohibition, it is permitted to tell one's fellow, "Do such and such for me," even though it automatically constitutes instruction, for he said to him, "Take in a second vessel and place," even though we derive from it that warming in this manner is not cooking.
