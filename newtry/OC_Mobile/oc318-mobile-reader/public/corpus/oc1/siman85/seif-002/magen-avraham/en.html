In the sacred tongue. It is a pious practice to be stringent. (Sefer Chasidim, siman 954.) Shabbos 150 states that one may think words of Torah opposite nakedness, for the verse says, 'No nakedness of a matter shall be seen in you,' implying that speech is forbidden but thought is permitted. See the Rosh and Rabbeinu Yonah on the Mishnah concerning one who experienced a seminal emission, etc. One must therefore say that the prohibition against thinking in a bathhouse is because its filth is abundant, as written in siman 45. Nevertheless, Yoreh Deah 1:10 implies that when one is naked and another person recites a blessing, the naked person may not intend to hear the blessing and fulfill his obligation through it. The principle that hearing is equivalent to answering cannot apply, because he is forbidden to answer. See the Taz and Shach there.

To prevent a prohibited matter. The same applies if thoughts of a woman enter his mind. He may think words of Torah there, because this is considered preventing a prohibition. (Sefer Chasidim.)
And concerning sacred matters. Kesef Mishneh writes that this is like the incident in which Rabbi Meir told someone, 'One may not anoint on Shabbos.' It would have sufficed to say, 'Do not anoint,' or, 'I do not want you to anoint,' yet he nevertheless spoke in the language of a ruling. The Ran writes that even where it is not necessary to prevent a prohibition, one may tell another, 'Do such and such for me,' although this automatically conveys a ruling. Thus he said, 'Take it in a secondary vessel and pour it,' even though we derive from this that merely warming is not considered cooking.
