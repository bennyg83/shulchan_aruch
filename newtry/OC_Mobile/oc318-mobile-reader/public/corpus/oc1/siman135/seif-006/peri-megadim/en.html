However. See Taz. In my humble opinion, our master intended a particular interpretation, as I shall explain. One who studies Rashi to Gittin 59b, s.v. nitpardah hachavilah, finds two versions, and each may be understood in three ways. The plain sense of the first version is stringent: a levi is not called at all, whether first, second, third, fourth, or later. Although a Yisrael may receive the first aliyah in place of a kohen, a levi possesses distinct importance; if this became habitual, people would say that his importance derives from being a kohen. The second version is entirely lenient: a Yisrael is called first and a levi second, while the case in Kesubos concerns people of equal stature. See Tosafos, s.v. nitpardah. This is the Rosh’s view according to Bach. When the Rosh writes that the levi should not be called “in his place,” Bach explains that this means in the place of the kohen. The Rosh’s proof from Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi establishes only that where they are equal, the levi specifically is called first; this cannot be proved from Kesubos. Taz, however, regarded Bach’s insertion of “meaning” as forced and understood “in his place” literally, meaning the second position, thereby deriving two stringencies. According to the first version, the levi is not called at all, in any position. According to the second version, he is not called in his normal second position, but may be called first, whichever they choose. Alternatively, a Yisrael is called first and no levi after him, or a levi is called first. The passage in Kesubos proves nothing, and the proof comes only from Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi. What the Rosh writes concerning equality is somewhat difficult, for precedence over a kohen is not directly relevant here. According to this understanding, the second version prohibits the levi only in his regular position, but permits him third or fourth. A third explanation is that according to the first version he is never called third or fourth, and this also includes a case where a great Yisrael is present, for the levi is nevertheless called first. According to the second version, the levi may be called second when a great Yisrael was called first, while Kesubos refers to equals. This accords with the written tradition cited by Beit Yosef, and is also the view of Magen Avraham, seif katan 10. Taz understood the Rosh as containing the two stringencies of the first and second explanations. He therefore raised three objections: first, Rama should have written “some say,” since he follows the Rosh and Tur; second, according to the second version the levi may be called third or fourth, as indicated by the phrase “in his place”; third, the ruling should have specified that where they are equal the levi must be called first according to the Rosh. Taz therefore explains that Rama rules stringently like the first version, that the levi is not called third or fourth, and even if a great Yisrael is present, the levi does not receive an aliyah, lest people say that he is a kohen. But where they are equal, Rama relies on the lenient second version and the levi specifically is called first. The word “may” means that such a case can occur, and then the levi precedes. If the Yisrael is greater, the levi is not called first, nor is he called third or fourth, unlike the Rosh. Rama therefore did not write “some say.” Magen Avraham holds similarly, as will be explained there, with Hashem’s help. This is not the place to elaborate further.
