He should awaken at dawn — It is very proper to be careful, before each of the three prayers, to say the acknowledgments and “May it be Your will” formulas explained in Yerushalmi Tefillas HaShachar: for Shacharis—Modeh Ani before You, living and enduring Lord, who brought me from darkness to light; for Minchah—Modeh Ani … just as You privileged me to see [the sun] when it was in the east, so You privileged me to see it in the west; for Maariv—May it be Your will … just as I was in darkness and You brought me to light, so bring me from darkness to light.

It is a great principle in the Torah … — Whoever wishes to fulfill “I have set the Lord before me” properly should hasten to fulfill what we cite from Sefer HaChinuch; because of their preciousness many great authorities copied them into their works. Thus HaChinuch [in his introduction and in mitzvos 25, 26, 17, 18, 432, 387]: Six mitzvos whose obligation is constant never cease from a person even a single moment all his days; every moment he thinks of them he fulfills a positive command, and reward for mitzvos has no limit. They are:
(a) To believe there is one God in the world who brought all beings into being; by His will and desire is everything that is now, was, and will be forever; who took us from Egypt and gave us the Torah—this is the positive mitzvah of “I am the Lord your God who brought you out …”—meaning: know and believe there is a God who supervises the world, since “I brought you out of Egypt.”
(b) Not to believe in any power besides Him—“You shall have no other gods before Me”; even if one admits God rules all but imagines He entrusted the world’s governance to an angel or star—he admits idolatry and violates “no other gods”; rather he must believe the Holy One blessed be He Himself in His glory supervises all worlds and no creature can do anything against His will—hence He is called “God of gods.”
(c) To declare His unity—“Hear O Israel, the Lord is our God, the Lord is One”: Hear, Israel, and know that the Lord who brings everything into being by His will, who supervises all worlds, is our God—the Lord is One without any partnership.
(d) To love God—“You shall love the Lord your God …” How does love come? Through Torah, as Sifrei explains: “You shall love”—I do not know how to love the Omnipresent; Scripture adds “These words … shall be upon your heart”—through them you recognize Him who spoke and the world came to be; through contemplating Torah one recognizes God’s greatness, incomparable and endless, and love settles necessarily in the heart. The mitzvah is that one should direct all ambition and thought toward love of God and assess in his heart that all worldly wealth, children, and honor are as nothing compared to His love; he should toil constantly to acquire Torah wisdom to gain knowledge of Him. One who fixes his mind on physical matters and vanities of the world not for Heaven’s sake but for pleasure and honor negates this positive mitzvah and its penalty is severe.
(e) To have fear of Heaven always before one so as not to sin—hence “The Lord your God you shall fear”; if sin presents itself one must rouse his spirit and consider at that moment that God supervises all human deeds even in darkness and repays evil accordingly—“Can a man hide in secret places …?” (Jeremiah 23:24).
(f) Not to stray after the imagination of the heart and what the eyes see—“You shall not stray after your hearts …”; the Sages said “after your hearts” is heresy, “after your eyes” is licentiousness; heresy includes all foreign thoughts contrary to Torah; licentiousness includes pursuing worldly lusts without intending any good purpose—not eating or acting for health and divine service but solely to increase bodily pleasure.
All this I gathered briefly from his pleasant wording—they are eye-salve; happy is one who fulfills them properly. See Chayei Adam, general rule 1, where he also elaborates.

And he should not be ashamed … — See Mishnah Berurah citing Beis Yosef. Know: Beis Yosef speaks only of a mitzvah one performs for himself when people mock him—then he need not heed their mockery nor quarrel with them. But if he stands where heretics oppose Torah and wish to enact city ordinances by which they turn the public from God’s will, and he opens with peace and is not heard—Beis Yosef said nothing about such a case; it is a mitzvah to hate them, quarrel with them, and thwart their counsel in every way possible. David said, “Do I not hate Your enemies, O Lord? … I hate them with utmost hatred” (Psalms 139:21–22).
