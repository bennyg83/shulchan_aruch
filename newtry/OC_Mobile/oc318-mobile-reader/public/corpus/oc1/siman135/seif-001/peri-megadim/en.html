I have, in my lowliness, several thoughts here. I said: I shall speak briefly; I shall converse, and it will bring me relief.
Behold, in Gittin 59b Rabbi Chiya bar Abba derives the law from “and you shall sanctify him” (Vayikra 21:8), and so rule the poskim. See Magen Avraham, siman 201, seif katan 4, who explains at length that this is a Torah law. Rambam likewise counts as positive commandment 32 the obligation to honor the descendants of Aharon and give them precedence in every sacred matter, as it is stated, “and you shall sanctify him.” Rashi on the Torah cites the words “he shall be holy to you,” meaning that one sanctifies him even against his will. See also Re’em and the Perishah here, who explain that Rashi quoted the end of the verse, while the law is derived from “and you shall sanctify him.” Rav Mattanah derives it from “and he gave it to the kohanim, the sons of Levi, and to all the elders of Israel” (Devarim 31:9), and Rabbi Yitzchak, etc. Four practical differences may be suggested between these derivations. First, the verse “the kohanim, the sons of Levi” may establish precedence only for Krias HaTorah, but not for other matters, whereas “and you shall sanctify him” applies to every sacred matter. Sacred matters include giving him the better portion from maaser ani and tzedakah. See Tosafos there, s.v. velitol. As for the Gemara’s reference to a meal, I do not know what sanctity is involved, unless it refers to a seudas mitzvah among scholars. Second, from “and he gave it to the kohanim, the sons of Levi,” one might derive that a kohen precedes a levi and a levi precedes a Yisrael, since that is the order of the verse. However, in the Yerushalmi on this passage, Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai derives only that the kohanim, the sons of Levi, precede the elders of Israel; he does not derive precedence of a levi over a Yisrael from the apparent redundancy of “the sons of Levi.” It would therefore follow that a levi has no precedence over a Yisrael. See the commentary there in Tosafos. Third, a blemished kohen may be included in the verse “the kohanim, the sons of Levi,” just as “speak to the kohanim” includes even blemished kohanim. By contrast, from “for he offers the bread of your God,” one might say that a blemished kohen is not included in the positive commandment. Yet “offers” may also include eating. See Rambam, Hilchos Klei HaMikdash 4:1-2, and his count of positive commandment 32, to honor the descendants of Aharon. Fourth, if the law is derived from “the kohanim, the sons of Levi,” followed by “all the elders of Israel,” it appears that a kohen precedes even if he is an am haaretz and the other is an elderly talmid chacham, since the verse is stated without qualification. By contrast, from “and you shall sanctify him, for he is holy,” one may say that the sanctity of Torah is greater than the sanctity of kehunah, and the positive commandment applies only where they are equal. See the commentary there and Rambam’s commentary to the Mishnah in Gittin, where he explains that “and you shall sanctify him” applies where they are equal, but a talmid chacham takes precedence because the sanctity of Torah is greater. Yet Rambam writes in Hilchos Klei HaMikdash 4:2 that every person must honor him, which implies even a talmid chacham greater than he. This can be resolved. Magen Avraham assumes as established in siman 128, seif 40, that a kohen who marries a woman forbidden to him does not receive the first aliyah, while a Yisrael does. This may be because one may suspend a positive commandment through inaction, assuming this is considered inaction, or because he has profaned his sanctity and, as a wicked person, is no longer holy. According to this, regarding a kohen guilty of other sins, if another kohen is present, the other certainly precedes him. But if no other kohen is present, perhaps he still precedes a Yisrael by virtue of “and you shall sanctify him.” Yet I find this difficult: if a talmid chacham precedes a kohen because the sanctity of Torah is greater, then all the more so a righteous Yisrael should precede a wicked kohen, since the Yisrael possesses greater sanctity. If one answers that this is indeed so, then explain the ruling in siman 128, seif 40, which implies that this applies specifically to transgressions of kehunah. This requires study.
Behold, in Gittin there, Abaye answers that the teaching is necessary according to the one who derives the law from “and he gave it to the kohanim.” For if it were derived from “and you shall sanctify him,” one could say that the Mishnah speaks where they are unequal, meaning that the kohen is not as great a talmid chacham as the Yisrael, so the Yisrael may precede him, and the enactment was made only because of peaceful relations. Alternatively, the Mishnah may refer to a blemished kohen. Nevertheless, Abaye states that we have a tradition that when no kohen is present, the bundle is separated. This means that the levi should not be called in the middle position. Even so, there remains a mitzvah to give the levi precedence over a Yisrael. With Hashem’s help, I will explain this in Taz and Magen Avraham, seifim katanim 6 and 10. From Even HaEzer, siman 3, where one is believed regarding Torah reading, see Chelkas Mechokek and Beis Shmuel, it follows that there is no prohibition so long as there is no degradation of kehunah: the person states that he is a kohen, and the one calling him believes him. There is therefore no violation of a positive commandment. See Magen Avraham, seifim katanim 6 and 20, where this will be explained, with Hashem’s help.
If there are ten kohanim and one of them separated from the group, and by law we silence him from claiming kehunah only D’Rabbanan, they may call a Yisrael first. The verse states, “and you shall sanctify him, for he offers the bread of your God”; since D’Rabbanan he may not offer, there is no obligation to sanctify him. This is certainly so regarding one who may be the child of a woman whose husband returned after nine months, where paternity is doubtful. By contrast, according to the verse “and he gave it to the kohanim and to all the elders of Israel,” one might say that a Torah-level doubt must be treated stringently.
