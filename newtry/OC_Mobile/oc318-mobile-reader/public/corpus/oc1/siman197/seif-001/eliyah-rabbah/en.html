[Levush] They are not satiated, etc. The Perishah discusses the Levush at length and says, with respect, that he was imprecise. The matter does not depend on appetite, because even a person who is very full may desire a fragrant, pleasant food. Rather, it depends specifically on whether he still has halachic permission to eat, such as before saying 'Let us bless.' When the Rif and Shulchan Aruch say 'able to eat,' they mean legally permitted to eat. I found, however, that the Semag, folio 110, writes explicitly that we estimate whether they ate so much that even if delicacies and beloved foods were brought they would still be able to eat them; if so, the newcomer may join. This clearly supports the Levush. Baal Halachot Gedolot also implies this, and this is surely the meaning of the Rif and Shulchan Aruch. Note this well.
[Levush] Above siman 193, etc. Malbushei Yom Tov writes that there is no such obligation there. On the contrary, below in siman 479 it appears that specifically on Pesach night one must seek three people. He says the Rosh in Three Who Ate merely writes that if a third arrives, the first two must give him food, so there is no contradiction with the Rosh in Arvei Pesachim, who holds that even on Pesach night the custom is not to seek a third. I do not understand his objection to the Levush. There is no doubt that where the text cites siman 192 it should read siman 193, because seif 1 there explicitly states like the Levush that one must seek zimun. The objection from siman 479 also seems answerable. There the novelty is that one should seek zimun specifically in order to answer Hodu, with a practical consequence that the group should not disperse after Birkat HaMazon. Alternatively, even one who would not otherwise need to recite Birkat HaMazon should seek a zimun to answer Hodu. One can also say the Levush means there is a mitzvah to seek three. Maadanei Melech indeed expresses surprise that in Arvei Pesachim the Rosh proves there is no mitzvah to seek three except on Pesach night, and even there concludes that this was not the custom. Yet Malbushei Yom Tov himself resolves that question by distinguishing a case where a third person has already come, in which case the two must return and give him food so he can join them. Another resolution is that on Pesach night even one person eating alone must seek a zimun, whereas during the rest of the year the obligation to seek a third applies only when two are already eating together, as above in siman 193. Regarding the principal law I am uncertain. Berachot 47, where Shmuel says that if certain delicacies and young birds were brought he could still eat, seems to imply that it is insufficient for only one of the two to remain able to eat. Perhaps both are required. If so, even if one said 'Let us bless' or washed mayim acharonim and the other still intends to eat, a third person cannot join them. Some poskim, however, somewhat imply that if even one still intends to eat, the third can join. According to that, Shmuel merely stated the actual facts of his case. This requires study.
They said 'Let us bless,' etc. The same applies if they took the cup in hand for Birkat HaMazon even without saying 'Let us bless,' as above in siman 179, seif 3. Yet it is difficult why the Shulchan Aruch and Levush rule here without qualification, while in siman 179, seif 1, they write that according to Rabbenu Yonah and the Ran one may continue eating without a new blessing even after saying 'Let us bless.' If so, here too a third person should be able to join.
