Above, siman 193, etc. Malbushei Yom Tov writes that there is no obligation stated there at all. On the contrary, below in siman 479 it is implied that only on Pesach night is one obligated to seek three people, whereas Rosh in the chapter Three Who Ate states that if a third person comes, one must give him food. He therefore finds no contradiction in Rosh, because in Arvei Pesachim Rosh rules that even on Pesach night we do not customarily seek three people. I do not understand his objection to Levush. There is no doubt that instead of siman 192 the text should read siman 193, because seif 1 there explicitly states, like Levush, that one is obligated to seek a zimun. His objection from siman 479 is likewise unfounded. There the novelty is that there is a mitzvah to seek a zimun in order to answer Hodu. A practical difference is that they should not separate after Bircas HaMazon. Alternatively, even if he was not otherwise required to recite Bircas HaMazon, he should nevertheless seek a zimun in order to answer Hodu. One may further say that Levush means that there is a mitzvah to seek three people. In truth, Maadanei Melech there writes: It is astonishing, because in Arvei Pesachim Rosh proves that there is no mitzvah to seek three people except on Pesach night, and even on Pesach night he concludes that this was not the custom. Nevertheless, we see that Malbushei Yom Tov resolves this difficulty by holding that if a third person comes to them, they are obligated to seek that he eat and join their zimun. It further appears to me that the main difficulty of Maadanei Melech against Rosh and his objection to Levush can be resolved as follows. On Pesach night, even if only one person is eating, he must seek a zimun. During the rest of the year, the obligation exists only when two are eating, as stated above in siman 193. Regarding the basic law, I am uncertain. Berachos 47 appears to indicate from Shmuel’s statement, “If they had brought me mushrooms and young pigeons to eat,” that one participant is insufficient and both must still be eating. Accordingly, if one of the two said, “Let us recite Bircas HaMazon,” or washed his hands, while the other still intends to eat, a third person cannot join them. Some poskim, however, imply that even when only one still intends to eat, the newcomer may join. According to that view, Shmuel merely described the actual circumstances. The matter requires study.
