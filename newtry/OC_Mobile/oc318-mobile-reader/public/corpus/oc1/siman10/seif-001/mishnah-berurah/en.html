That does not have, etc. This applies whether it lacked corners when it was first made, or whether afterward he cut off one corner and made it round.
It is exempt. This is explicit in the verse, as it is written, "upon the four corners of your garment," and we expound in the Sifrei: four, and not three. More than four is included from the words "with which you cover yourself." We cannot say the reverse, for four is included in five, but four is not included in three. This does not apply only to a five-cornered garment, rather the same law applies to one with six or seven corners.
It is obligated. There are those who exempt it, and therefore one should not make a five-cornered garment initially. The Pri Megadim writes that even according to those who exempt it, our small taleisim, which have a neck opening, are nevertheless mostly closed. If, however, it happens that most of it is open, he should make the collar, as tailors do, somewhat rounded.
On four. If he made five, he violates the prohibition of adding. If he cut off one of them, it is valid, provided that the four corners that are farthest from one another remain with tzitzis, as explained below.
That are farther from one another. For it is logical to place them on those corners that are most recognizable among the rest. Some say that this is required according to the Torah, and one should be stringent when possible. However, if it was already made on the nearer corner and it is not possible to repair it, such as when the time for prayer has arrived, he may rely on the lenient authorities and recite the blessing.
