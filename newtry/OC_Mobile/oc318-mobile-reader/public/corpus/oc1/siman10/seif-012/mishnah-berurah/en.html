Even though they have, etc. — The reason: see in Beit Yosef.

Since they are not, etc. — Behold, Beit Yosef wrote: one should examine our garments, which have two corners near the neck and two near the ground—why do we not affix tzitzit to them? He answered with several answers, and Darchei Moshe rejected them and said this is the reason for the custom: since the mitzvah of tzitzit is lechatchila that one have two in front and two behind, and our garments have all four corners in front and it is impossible to wear them otherwise, they are not obligated in tzitzit. This is what he meant by "since their corners are not fashioned," etc. A garment called a rek, to which is attached above a square piece of cloth resting on the shoulders from behind, called a collar—it is plainly exempt, for on the upper part there are no corners except the collar corners resting slightly behind, and these are not corners since the two behind are not aligned opposite those in front. Until here Darchei Moshe. Magen Avraham wrote that since this ruling is weak among the Geonim and they found no proper reason—for even Darchei Moshe's reason may be doubted, as Pri Megadim wrote—therefore a God-fearing person should round one corner, as Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the elders; and so Pri Megadim and other later authorities. Especially today, regarding garments newly made open in back—the garment called a kapote—often most of it is open; according to Darchei Moshe it is obligated in tzitzit, since the two behind align opposite the two in front. Therefore one who wishes to exempt himself must round at least one of the four corners, and it is exempt from tzitzit, as above in seif 9; and see Biur Halachah. Better still to round two more corners so only three remain, for now it has six.

Two behind them — From this wording stems an error by some who fold one part of the tallit very much until it is thin and covers nothing except the neck, intending that two tzitzit be literally behind on their shoulders. This is not ideal, for then there is no bodily covering, only head covering. Better is the custom of the Byzantine Jews, where the hand separates at the middle of the tallit so that on both sides one tzitzit is in front and part behind; for the sides too are called his front and back, and therefore the hand's separation suffices.
