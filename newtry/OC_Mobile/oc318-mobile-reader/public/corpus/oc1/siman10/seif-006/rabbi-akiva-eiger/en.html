In my humble opinion, Tosafos in Sanhedrin indicate the following. Since they were forced by the discussion in Sukkah to say that adding is rabbinically prohibited, they evidently hold that regarding the principle 'each stands independently,' one cannot distinguish between the prohibition of bal tosif and diminishing the mitzvah, unlike the view in Sukkah. Rather, these concepts are linked: diminishing the mitzvah follows from violating bal tosif. They were therefore forced to say that the prohibition is rabbinic. One can say that Rambam agrees and additionally holds that since the bal tosif prohibition is rabbinic, the addition also diminishes the mitzvah rabbinically, for every rabbinic enactment was fashioned in the pattern of Torah law. Rambam derived this from our ruling that adding tzitzis to existing tzitzis falls under ta'aseh v'lo min ha'asui. It follows necessarily that before the first tzitzis were cut away, the new set was invalid, and that invalidity was rabbinic. This also appears from Ran, Sukkah, chapter 3, page 248, who writes in Rambam's view that since each component stands independently, there is no Torah-level bal tosif but only a rabbinic prohibition. Therefore, when the addition is for the mitzvah's beautification, the Sages did not decree; see there. Yet according to our text Rambam writes there, 'If his intent was to add or subtract, it is invalid.' It follows that even after the fact it is rabbinically invalid. Indeed, it appears almost necessary to say this in Tosafos' view. If a rabbinic transgression did not diminish the mitzvah, then Menachos' objection from adding tzitzis to existing tzitzis, which constitutes ta'aseh v'lo min ha'asui, could only concern a case in which he later cuts off the first set. Since the second set was unnecessary when made, it afterward becomes valid through that which was already made. Beit Yosef initially understands it this way. If so, Rava's statement is difficult: 'At present this person violates bal tosif; his act is not an act.' According to Rashi, because of bal tosif it is not called an act at all, and cutting the first set becomes the act of making. According to Beit Yosef's explanation of Rambam, because of bal tosif it is a fully effective act. Either way, the difficulty remains that by Torah law there is no bal tosif, because each stands independently. The second set was therefore made while invalid, and after the first set is cut the second should be invalid by Torah law because of ta'aseh v'lo min ha'asui. How could a rabbinic bal tosif prohibition remove its Torah-level invalidity? We must conclude that whenever bal tosif exists, it diminishes the mitzvah; rabbinic bal tosif diminishes it rabbinically. By Torah law, then, when one adds tzitzis to existing tzitzis, even if he later cuts away the first set there is no ta'aseh v'lo min ha'asui, because both sets were made validly. The Gemara's principal challenge concerns only the rabbinic level: before the first set was cut away, the second was rabbinically invalid, and cutting the first set made it valid; this should constitute rabbinic ta'aseh v'lo min ha'asui. Rava's answer is therefore sound from either direction: 'At present he violates bal tosif; his act is not an act.' At the Torah level, there is no ta'aseh v'lo min ha'asui in any event. At the rabbinic level, because the bal tosif transgression prevents it from being an act, there is no ta'aseh v'lo min ha'asui, following either Rashi's or Rambam's explanation. Rambam's words are thus founded upon golden bases.
