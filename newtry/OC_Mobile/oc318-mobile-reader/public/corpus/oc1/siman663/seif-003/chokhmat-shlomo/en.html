Seif 3. We take out two sifrei Torah; in one we read 'See, You say to me,' and the maftir reads the offerings of the doubtful day, etc. Note: Seemingly this is difficult for me from tractate Rosh Hashanah [daf 30a]: What error did the Leviim make with the song? Here they explained that they did not say any song at all; R. Zeira said that they said the weekday song. Seemingly this is difficult: why there did they not say both the weekday song and the Yom Tov song because of doubt? Why did they say only the weekday song? It is especially more difficult according to the explanation that they did not say any song at all, for this implies that we are more concerned for a doubtful change than for a doubtful omission, and even than for a definite omission. After all, if they say no song at all, the song is certainly lacking; but if they would say the weekday song or the Yom Tov song, it would be only a doubtful change. Nevertheless, they were more concerned for a doubtful change than for a definite omission. If so, why do we read the entire doubtful day and not worry about a change? Certainly it is only either the second day or the third day, and if so, when reading the offerings that do not belong to that day, it is a definite change, yet we are not concerned for it. One cannot say that since we read both because of doubt, it is not considered a change; for if so, there too they should have said both because of doubt, the weekday song and the Yom Tov song. Seemingly one could distinguish between a matter that stands to be clarified and one that does not. Where it does not stand to be clarified immediately afterward, then if they say both, it will become clear that they certainly said one of them improperly, and this is a disgrace to the Leviim and to the offering. Therefore we prefer that they not say anything at all rather than say something improper, or possibly improper. However, this satisfies me only to resolve the explanation there that they said nothing at all. But according to R. Zeira, who says that they said the weekday song, then in the end it will become clear that they said it improperly; this is difficult, for they should have said both. In truth, this itself is not difficult, for one may say that they were not concerned for a doubtful impropriety, even though the matter would be clarified afterward; but if they would say both and it would become clear that they certainly said one of them unlawfully, then we are indeed concerned for that clarification. Yet it remains difficult: according to R. Zeira, why did they say the weekday song and not say the Yom Tov song and the weekday song? Since we, when we are in doubt about the doubtful day, pray the Yom Tov prayer and observe Yom Tov because of doubt, and likewise there they treated the day as holy from the beginning of the day because of doubt, why did they treat it as Yom Tov for every matter because of doubt, while for the song they said the weekday song? They should have said the Rosh Hashanah song because of doubt. And if afterward it would become clear that they said it improperly, that is also true if they said the weekday song. One cannot say that the weekday song is more frequent, for that applies only regarding precedence; but to reject the other entirely, we do not say that the more frequent is preferable. See what I wrote about this in my new work, siman 425, and see Tosafos in chapter 2 of Shabbos and Taz, siman 681; see there and understand. Perhaps they did this as a recognizable sign, out of concern lest the witnesses not come until tomorrow, and tomorrow would be holy. If people would hear that they had said the Yom Tov song, they would think that today is certainly Yom Tov and would treat tomorrow as weekday. Therefore they made a recognizable sign by saying the weekday song on this day. Still, this requires investigation. Now, based on the above, it is proven from the explanation there that we are more concerned for a doubtful change than for a doubtful omission, even more than for a definite omission. Presumably the halachah is so, for those who explained it there are the many against R. Zeira. Furthermore, even R. Zeira disagrees only because he holds that we are more concerned for a definite omission than for a doubtful change; but with regard to a doubtful omission, he agrees that one should be more concerned for a doubtful change than for a doubtful omission. It would appear from this to resolve what the Mordechai asked in Megillah: why do we not read the Megillah on the fifteenth because of doubt, since we are not expert in the fixing of the month? According to the above this is understandable. If we read the Megillah on the fifteenth, it is a doubtful change, for perhaps it is not Purim, and it is a doubtful change. But if today is Purim and we do not read the Megillah on it, it is a doubtful omission. One should be more concerned for a doubtful change than for a doubtful omission; therefore there is no obligation of Megillah reading on it. This is not comparable to our observing Yom Tov for two days because of doubt and praying the Yom Tov prayer. First, since we expound 'you,' even if mistaken, 'you,' even if misled, therefore when we make two days because of doubt, it is truly considered Yom Tov, and it is not a doubtful change, but truly Yom Tov. Furthermore, there the main part of Yom Tov is the prohibition of doing labor, while the prayer is secondary: since it is Yom Tov, it is proper to pray the Yom Tov prayer on it. If so, the prohibition of labor is not a doubtful change, since it is by sitting and not acting, and anything not done by an active deed is not included in 'change.' Since it is forbidden in labor, and especially since this is a Torah doubt and it is forbidden in labor in any event, once it is a day forbidden in labor it has the law of Yom Tov, and it is applicable to pray the Yom Tov prayer and recite Kiddush according to the law of Yom Tov. But with Megillah the main matter is only the reading of the Megillah, the feast and rejoicing, and sending portions, and all of this is through active deeds. It is also not included among the appointed festivals, and it is not applicable to say regarding it 'you,' even if mistaken. Therefore we are more concerned there for doubtful change than for doubtful omission, and therefore we do not read the Megillah on the fifteenth because of doubt. This is understandable; examine it carefully. See proof for the above reasoning, that on Yom Tov the main matter is the prohibition of doing labor, from tractate Rosh Hashanah 32, where R. Akiva said: Why was 'Shabbason' not said? Because Scripture opened first with resting from labor. Rashi explained: it comes to warn about labor, which is the main matter, and this is the proper way to expound, etc. End quote. If so, it is proven that even relative to the prayer of Malchiyos and Zichronos, which is from the Torah, labor is the main matter; all the more so relative to rabbinic prayer, labor is the main matter. Therefore the prayer follows from it automatically. But with Megillah it is the reverse: the reading is the main matter. Therefore it is impossible to enact reading it because of doubt, as appears correct in my humble opinion. Examine this carefully.
