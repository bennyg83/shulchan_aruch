It is permitted in every circumstance. This means even if he gives him no payment, for otherwise what novelty would this teach? This requires study, for he nevertheless performs labor on the Jew's behalf by carrying the letter from one domain to another and transporting it four cubits in the public domain, as stated in siman 325, seif 15. Although nowadays we do not have a biblical public domain, it should still be forbidden, since the prohibition of boundaries is likewise rabbinic. This also appears from the Gemara's statement concerning Rabbi Yose that no document in his handwriting was ever found in a gentile's possession, lest the gentile carry it on Shabbos. This implies that even when he goes on his own it is forbidden. Moreover, in siman 266 it was permitted to give one's purse to a gentile only because of potential loss, lest the Jew otherwise carry it four cubits; absent such loss it would be forbidden even though the gentile travels on his own. It therefore appears to me that 'in every circumstance' means even where there is no established postal service in the city. If the addressee is not found at home, the gentile will not pursue him, because the Jew did not send him for that purpose. This is why Rabbi Yisrael Isserlein concludes that this applies only where the gentile does not know the recipient. If he knows him, there is concern that he may lengthen his route on the Jew's behalf, meaning that if he does not find the man at home he may travel farther to deliver it into his hand. But to send it with him on Friday when there is not enough time to arrive before nightfall is plainly forbidden, since he transports it on the Jew's behalf on Shabbos. All the more so is it forbidden to give it to him on Shabbos, as stated in siman 246, seif 2. I subsequently saw that Raavan explains an established postal service in the city to mean that the messenger lives in the city to which he is carrying the letter. It is permitted to send it with him before Shabbos because he must travel there in any event and is not the Jew's agent. His teachers explained it differently. His words nevertheless imply that there must at least be enough time to reach the city to which it is sent, for the dispute of Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel concerns that case.
