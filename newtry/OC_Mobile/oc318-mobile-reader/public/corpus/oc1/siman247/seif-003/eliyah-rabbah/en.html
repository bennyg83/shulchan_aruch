[3] [Leush:] Rather, it depends upon the Jew, etc. The Levush understood that the distinction between an established postal station and one that is not established is that, in the latter case, the non-Jew is engaged on behalf of the Jew. This is not convincing. Rather, the reason is explicit in all the halachic authorities: where the station is established, it is sufficient that the messenger can reach the house nearest the city wall; but where there is no established postmaster, meaning that he travels for commerce only occasionally, even reaching his own house is insufficient, because we are concerned that the Jew will have to pursue him and that he will not leave the letter at home. It appears proper to be lenient in accordance with the permissive authorities, since the Raavan permits it in every case. The Magen Avraham writes that although one may give other utensils to a non-Jew whenever he can complete the work before Shabbos, even without a fixed price, as stated in siman 252, here the Jew's handwriting is recognizable, etc. Accordingly, a letter written by a non-Jew would be permitted. End quote. His words are surprising, for the cases are not comparable. There the work can be completed before Shabbos, whereas here, if the postal station is not established, the Jew may need to pursue the messenger on Shabbos. This is also implied by the Magen Avraham himself in siman 252, seif katan 8. His statement that the Agudah likewise writes this is imprecise, because according to the Agudah, other utensils are permitted without a fixed price even where the work cannot be completed before Shabbos. His further statement that Sefer HaTerumah and the Mordechai likewise rule this has already been addressed at the beginning of this siman, where I explained their meaning. Nevertheless, as a matter of practical law, I wrote above that one may be lenient regarding a letter written by a non-Jew, utensils, and money, but for a different reason.
