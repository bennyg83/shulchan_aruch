Ibid. “Although other utensils are permitted,” etc. See what I wrote above concerning this. At first glance this requires study, for what is the difficulty? Here, from the outset, we are concerned that he may be unable to finish during the day. If afterward he did not do so and performs the task on Shabbos, that does not concern us, since he acts on his own account. It is strained to say that the Magen Avraham's difficulty is that there too, from the outset, we should fear that some circumstance may arise preventing him from finishing during the day, forcing him to work on Shabbos. For whence do we know to be lenient? Perhaps sending a letter is different because it is common and ordinary for the addressee occasionally to leave his home for business, whereas with other labor, since a substantial part of the day remains and he can finish the work, we do not fear that some unforeseen event will prevent him from doing so and cause him to perform it on Shabbos. Indeed, the Magen Avraham himself writes in siman 252 in accordance with this distinction, stating that sending a letter is as though the Jew told him to go on Shabbos. I subsequently saw that Eliyah Rabbah noticed this point. After further consideration, however, it appears to me that the Magen Avraham's words are correct. I will first explain that sending such a letter involves two distinct commissions: first, sending the messenger to a particular place; and second, instructing him to deliver it into the hands of a particular person. As to the mission to the place, if the place is near enough for him to arrive before Shabbos, there is sufficient time during the day. But if it is distant and he cannot arrive before Shabbos, then even though the non-Jew is free to travel on whichever day he chooses, and no particular arrival date was fixed such that we could say the Jew expressly instructed him to go on Shabbos, nevertheless the ordinary understanding is that, since he hired him to bring the letter to that place and it is impossible to arrive before Shabbos, it is as though he told him to travel every day, including Shabbos. By contrast, with other labor, when he merely tells him to perform a task without specifying when, the Jew does not care on which day it is done. The Magen Avraham in siman 252 discusses labor that cannot be completed before Shabbos and compares it to sending a messenger on Wednesday or Thursday where he cannot reach the destination before Shabbos. He distinguishes that, in the case of a mission, since the Jew told him to go to a particular place, it is as though he told him to travel every day, even on Shabbos. Here, however, the discussion concerns a case in which the messenger can reach the place before Shabbos. Thus, no concern arises from the commission to travel to the place. The only concern is the second commission, that he was instructed to deliver the letter to a particular person, who may not be at home, causing the messenger to follow him. To this the Magen Avraham objects: even if he does not find the person, he has no obligation to follow him on Shabbos, for he can do so after Shabbos; and if he does follow him on Shabbos, he acts on his own account. This is comparable to other forms of labor, where the Jew does not care when the task is performed. What difference is there between telling him to perform a particular task and telling him to deliver a letter to a particular person, when the Jew did not insist on any particular day? It should therefore be permitted even without a fixed price, and even if he ultimately performs it on Shabbos, the Jew should not be concerned, since the non-Jew does not act pursuant to the Jew's instructions but on his own account. The reason it is forbidden when insufficient time remains during the day, even though the Jew does not care when the task is done and it could be done after Shabbos, is that when it is given on Friday and cannot be completed that day, it appears as though the Jew gave it to him to perform on Shabbos. When sufficient time remains, however, it does not appear that he gave it to him for Shabbos; therefore, even if the non-Jew does not perform it that day but instead performs it on Shabbos, it is permitted, because he acts on his own account. The same should apply when the Jew sends a letter for delivery to a particular person and the messenger goes after that person on Shabbos. He is not thereby acting pursuant to the Jew's instruction. Since the Jew sent him to a particular place and he could have reached that place before Shabbos, it does not appear as though the Jew instructed him to act on Shabbos. This is the Magen Avraham's question: Why should we fear that he will not find the person at home and will need to follow him on Shabbos? Accordingly, the prohibition here results from the Jew's instruction to deliver the letter to that particular person. The Magen Avraham therefore asks that other utensils are permitted, because the Jew does not care when the work is done. He answers that here the Jew's handwriting is carried and is recognizable, etc. As to what I asked, that even where a fixed price was set it should be forbidden because the Jew's handwriting is recognizable, and we must therefore say that upon investigation people will learn that a fixed price was set: similarly, upon investigation they would learn that the Jew handed it over during the day with sufficient time for it to arrive. This may be answered as follows. Certainly, it is not realistic that people will investigate and ascertain the precise circumstances. Thus, even if they know when the Jew handed it over, how would they know that he instructed the messenger to take it to a nearby place that could be reached before Shabbos, but the intended recipient was not found there? They may instead assume that the sender knew the recipient had left his original residence for the present location and deliberately sent it there, and they will say that he gave it to the messenger intending that he transport it on Shabbos. A further answer is that the Maggid Mishneh's statement that upon investigation they will discover the facts applies only to the very nature of the non-Jew's work for the Jew. People are not foolish enough to suppose that the non-Jew works for the Jew without compensation. They understand that he does so for some consideration, and when they inquire what compensation he receives for his labor, they will discover that he works under a sharecropping arrangement, or the like. Here too, with respect to fixing the price, it is reasonable that they will learn the truth. But there is no reason they would think to investigate when the Jew delivered the letter to the non-Jew. When they see the non-Jew carrying it on Shabbos, they will assume that the Jew gave it to him with that intention.
