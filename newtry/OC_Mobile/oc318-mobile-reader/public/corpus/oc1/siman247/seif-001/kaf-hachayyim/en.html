1) [Seif 1] A person may send a letter, etc. A letter is specified to teach a greater novelty: although the Jewish person's handwriting is recognizable in the non-Jew's possession, it is permitted when a fixed fee was set. Bach; Olas Tamid, paragraph 1. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1, writes that a fixed fee is required for all types of labor, as stated in siman 252. A letter is specified to teach that if there is no established postal service, it is forbidden to send it even on Sunday; see there. Specifically a letter may not be sent even on Sunday when no fixed fee was set and there is no established postal service, because the Jewish person's writing is recognizable. Other utensils and articles that are not recognizable, however, may be sent even on Friday and even without a fixed fee, provided they can reach there while it is still Friday. Eshel Avraham, paragraphs 1 and 2; see there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 6, writes the same. See Eliyah Rabbah, paragraphs 1 and 3, and our words above in siman 246, paragraph 20.
2) There: A person may send a letter, etc. Similarly, one may send other articles and utensils with a non-Jew even shortly before nightfall on Friday, provided the non-Jew leaves the entrance of the Jewish person's house while it is still daytime, if the Jewish person fixed his compensation for the journey. The non-Jew then acts for himself in order to receive the fixed compensation and does not appear to be the Jewish person's agent. The Jewish person must not tell him to travel on Shabbos, because instructing a non-Jew is rabbinically forbidden even when the instruction is given before Shabbos. Nor may he tell him, 'See that you arrive there on Sunday,' or Monday, and the like, if he knows that the non-Jew cannot arrive there unless he also travels on Shabbos, for this is equivalent to telling him, 'Go on Shabbos.' Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1. See below, paragraph 7.
3) There: A person may send a letter, etc. It appears that if a sick person became gravely ill on Friday, and his brother or another relative is in a village, and toward evening close to the onset of Shabbos his condition worsened and he told the person managing his household to send for his brother because he wanted him present, which would put his mind at ease, or so that he could instruct him regarding his children and the like, it is permitted to send him a letter. This applies even in a Jewish person's handwriting and even when no fee was fixed. Although it is certain that the non-Jew will not reach the village before Shabbos, one may nevertheless be lenient so that the sick person's mind will not become unsettled, since this involves danger to life. Maran permits even more than this below, in siman 306, seif 9, on Shabbos itself; see there. Petach HaDevir, paragraph 1. See our words there, with the help of Heaven.
4) During a time of plague, epidemic, or dangerous illness, when the public is in distress, I permitted writing a letter shortly before nightfall on Friday and sending it through a non-Jew even on Shabbos. This is especially so for the needs of a sick person, in order to learn how others are faring. In all such cases one may be lenient, and this is obvious. One may even have a non-Jew write on Shabbos itself, for everything depends upon the circumstances of the time. Ruach Chaim.
5) There: Provided that he fixed a fee, etc. In that case, the non-Jew exerts himself for his own benefit and not for the Jewish person. Taz, paragraph 1.
6) There: He must not tell him to travel on Shabbos. If the non-Jew says on his own that he wishes to travel on Shabbos, however, the Jewish person need not object. Beis Yosef; Olas Tamid, paragraph 1; Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 1.
