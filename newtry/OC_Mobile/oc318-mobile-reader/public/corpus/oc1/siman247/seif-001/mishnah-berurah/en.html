A letter. The same rule applies to every form of labor: when one gives the task to a gentile and there is not enough time to finish it before Shabbos, one must stipulate a fixed payment for the work. The gentile then acts for his own benefit rather than for the Jew, as stated below in siman 252, seif 2. A letter is mentioned to teach that if no fixed fee was stipulated and there is no established postal service in the city, it is forbidden, as the Mechaber concludes, for the reason explained below. Other utensils, objects, or money sent with a gentile on Friday are governed by the general rules of labor. Therefore, even without a fixed fee, if there is enough time that day to reach the recipient's location, it is permitted, and we do not fear that the recipient may not be at home and that the gentile will have to search for him on Shabbos.
Toward nightfall. This means while the sun is still shining. The gentile must also leave the entrance of the Jew's house before Shabbos begins, as ruled by the Rambam.
Provided that, and so forth. If the gentile independently says that he will travel on Shabbos, there is no concern, because the Jew did not instruct him to do so and the gentile acts for his own benefit in order to earn his payment.
That he travel on Shabbos. Likewise, one may not tell him, 'See that you are there on Sunday,' or on Monday, and the like, where it is known that he cannot arrive unless he travels on Shabbos. This is considered as though the Jew explicitly told him to go on Shabbos, as ruled by the later authorities.
There is no established postal service, and so forth. This means that the postal official is not permanently present in the destination city but sometimes travels elsewhere. It is forbidden because the gentile may not find him and may have to pursue him on Shabbos. The Sages were especially stringent here because the gentile carries a document in the Jew's handwriting, making the Jew's involvement recognizable, and people may say that the Jew gave it to him on Shabbos to carry. If the letter is written in a gentile's script, however, it may be transported even on Friday without a fixed fee, provided the messenger can reach the first house inside the city wall before nightfall, just as we permitted above when sending other utensils and objects through a gentile.
It is forbidden to send. The Olat Shabbos writes that all this concerns sending the letter through a gentile to the postal official. When a person sends a letter with him directly to a friend, however, there is no concern that if the friend is absent the gentile will pursue him on Shabbos. It is therefore permitted even on Friday and without a fixed fee, provided he can reach there before nightfall. This also accords with the view of Rabbi Avraham of Prague cited by the Bach, who discusses the matter at length and rules leniently. The Taz and Magen Avraham maintain that even when sending a letter directly to one's friend this concern remains. The recently published commentary of Rabbeinu Chananel clearly supports the Olat Shabbos. Nevertheless, if the Jew tells the gentile to go there and, if the person is absent, to deliver it to members of his household, all agree that it is permitted even on Friday and without a fixed fee, provided he can reach the first house inside the wall of the city where the person lives before nightfall.
Even from Sunday. If the journey is short, such as one day's travel, it appears that even if he sends it on Monday or Tuesday it is permitted, for even if the recipient is not at home the messenger has ample time to seek him out, as written by the Magen Avraham.
If there is an established postal service. This means that the official appointed for this purpose is permanently stationed there. This includes what is now called a postmaster, commonly found in large places, who remains at his station at set times to receive letters and forward them to those to whom they belong.
The house nearest the wall. This means the destination city, namely the first house inside its wall.
Some permit, and so forth. Their reasoning is that since the Jew did not explicitly tell him to travel on Shabbos, it is forbidden only close to Shabbos, when he appears to be the Jew's agent for that purpose. Instructing a gentile is rabbinically forbidden even when the Jew tells him before Shabbos to perform labor on Shabbos. But when it is several days before Shabbos, and a fixed fee was stipulated, it does not appear as though the Jew told him to perform labor on Shabbos. Therefore, according to the Rosh, it is forbidden only on Friday.
Even if there is no established postal service. According to this view it is permitted even when it is known that he cannot arrive before Shabbos, because when he was sent several days before Shabbos he does not appear to be the Jew's agent, as explained above.
