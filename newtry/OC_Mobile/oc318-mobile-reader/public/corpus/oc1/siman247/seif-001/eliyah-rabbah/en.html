[1] A letter, etc. The Magen Avraham writes: The same applies to every type of labor, for which a fixed price is required, as stated in siman 252. The text mentions a letter only to teach that where there is no established postal station it is forbidden. This appears correct to me. End quote. He overlooked the explanation of the Maharshal, who asks concerning the Semag: Why was it necessary to give the reason that it is more recognizable that a Jew wrote the letter delivered by the non-Jew? He could simply have said that the non-Jew appears to be the Jew's agent, as in the case of hides given to a tanner. The Maharshal answers that tanning hides is itself a primary category of labor, whereas carrying a letter is not so clearly a labor, especially today when we have no full-fledged public domain. It might therefore have been appropriate to permit it even without fixing a price, and the Semag accordingly explains that the prohibition is because the Jew's authorship is more recognizable. End quote. According to this, it was necessary to mention a letter in order to teach that a fixed price is required; the same would consequently apply to a letter written by a non-Jew, or to utensils or money, when no price was fixed. In truth, however, I find the Maharshal's question difficult. Perhaps the Semag agrees with what the Agudah writes in the first chapter of Shabbos: This applies specifically to a letter, because a Jew's handwriting is recognizable, but other work, such as giving hides for tanning, does not require a fixed price. End quote. If so, the Semag indeed needed the reason that the handwriting is especially recognizable. I subsequently examined Tosafos, the Ran, and all the halachic authorities, who explain the Mishnah concerning hides as referring to a fixed-price arrangement; this is also explicit in Sefer HaTerumah, even though it records the Semag's reason. The Bach's distinction between contracting and fixing a price is incorrect, as is evident from the Beit Yosef in siman 244 and Sefer HaTerumah in siman 252. Likewise, where it is customary to pay a known fee to a launderer, as is our practice, this is considered a fixed price. This is the meaning of the Hagahos Mordechai cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 252; examine this carefully. As for the Agudah's statement that the Mishnah refers to a case where no price was fixed, it appears to me that he understood the Mordechai's wording, "This applies specifically to a letter written by a Jew, etc., but if he fixed payment for him, etc.," in that manner. I have no doubt that the printed text is corrupt, as anyone who examines it will see, and its statements are also repetitive. Rather, the Mordechai's intent is as I and the Maharshal explained the Semag; examine this carefully.
[2] Provided that, etc. The non-Jew must also leave the Jew's house carrying it before nightfall. This is from the Rambam, chapter 7. End quote from Malbushei Yom Tov. The Rambam's wording is, "before Shabbos," and Sefer Adam VeChavah, page 82, states that the sun has not yet set, meaning that it is still shining. The Magen Avraham writes that one may not say to him, "See that it arrives there on Sunday," or "on Monday," and the like, where it is known that he cannot arrive there unless he travels on Shabbos. End quote. See siman 307, seif katan 9.
[3] [Leush:] Rather, it depends upon the Jew, etc. The Levush understood that the distinction between an established postal station and one that is not established is that, in the latter case, the non-Jew is engaged on behalf of the Jew. This is not convincing. Rather, the reason is explicit in all the halachic authorities: where the station is established, it is sufficient that the messenger can reach the house nearest the city wall; but where there is no established postmaster, meaning that he travels for commerce only occasionally, even reaching his own house is insufficient, because we are concerned that the Jew will have to pursue him and that he will not leave the letter at home. It appears proper to be lenient in accordance with the permissive authorities, since the Raavan permits it in every case. The Magen Avraham writes that although one may give other utensils to a non-Jew whenever he can complete the work before Shabbos, even without a fixed price, as stated in siman 252, here the Jew's handwriting is recognizable, etc. Accordingly, a letter written by a non-Jew would be permitted. End quote. His words are surprising, for the cases are not comparable. There the work can be completed before Shabbos, whereas here, if the postal station is not established, the Jew may need to pursue the messenger on Shabbos. This is also implied by the Magen Avraham himself in siman 252, seif katan 8. His statement that the Agudah likewise writes this is imprecise, because according to the Agudah, other utensils are permitted without a fixed price even where the work cannot be completed before Shabbos. His further statement that Sefer HaTerumah and the Mordechai likewise rule this has already been addressed at the beginning of this siman, where I explained their meaning. Nevertheless, as a matter of practical law, I wrote above that one may be lenient regarding a letter written by a non-Jew, utensils, and money, but for a different reason.
To the adjacent house. Meaning the first house in the city to which it was sent.
[Levush] Perhaps, etc. Rambam writes this. Nachalat Tzvi asks at length: if so, how could Beit Shammai require enough time for the messenger to reach the non-Jew's house when the sender may not know where that house is? He therefore rules that even if the house is known in the city, reaching the adjacent house suffices because the messenger could leave it there. He did not examine Kesef Mishneh, who answers that Beit Shammai indeed means until the location of the house is known; if it is unknown, we stringently require enough time to reach the farthest house, while Beit Hillel is lenient in the uncertainty. I also saw in Sefer Adam VeChavah that it says the non-Jew lives in the house adjacent to the city wall. This is itself difficult, because then what is the dispute with Beit Shammai? Perhaps he means that in uncertainty we assume his house is nearby. In any event, from all this we learn that where his actual house is known, there must be enough time to reach it.
