[1] A letter, etc. The Magen Avraham writes: The same applies to every type of labor, for which a fixed price is required, as stated in siman 252. The text mentions a letter only to teach that where there is no established postal station it is forbidden. This appears correct to me. End quote. He overlooked the explanation of the Maharshal, who asks concerning the Semag: Why was it necessary to give the reason that it is more recognizable that a Jew wrote the letter delivered by the non-Jew? He could simply have said that the non-Jew appears to be the Jew's agent, as in the case of hides given to a tanner. The Maharshal answers that tanning hides is itself a primary category of labor, whereas carrying a letter is not so clearly a labor, especially today when we have no full-fledged public domain. It might therefore have been appropriate to permit it even without fixing a price, and the Semag accordingly explains that the prohibition is because the Jew's authorship is more recognizable. End quote. According to this, it was necessary to mention a letter in order to teach that a fixed price is required; the same would consequently apply to a letter written by a non-Jew, or to utensils or money, when no price was fixed. In truth, however, I find the Maharshal's question difficult. Perhaps the Semag agrees with what the Agudah writes in the first chapter of Shabbos: This applies specifically to a letter, because a Jew's handwriting is recognizable, but other work, such as giving hides for tanning, does not require a fixed price. End quote. If so, the Semag indeed needed the reason that the handwriting is especially recognizable. I subsequently examined Tosafos, the Ran, and all the halachic authorities, who explain the Mishnah concerning hides as referring to a fixed-price arrangement; this is also explicit in Sefer HaTerumah, even though it records the Semag's reason. The Bach's distinction between contracting and fixing a price is incorrect, as is evident from the Beit Yosef in siman 244 and Sefer HaTerumah in siman 252. Likewise, where it is customary to pay a known fee to a launderer, as is our practice, this is considered a fixed price. This is the meaning of the Hagahos Mordechai cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 252; examine this carefully. As for the Agudah's statement that the Mishnah refers to a case where no price was fixed, it appears to me that he understood the Mordechai's wording, "This applies specifically to a letter written by a Jew, etc., but if he fixed payment for him, etc.," in that manner. I have no doubt that the printed text is corrupt, as anyone who examines it will see, and its statements are also repetitive. Rather, the Mordechai's intent is as I and the Maharshal explained the Semag; examine this carefully.
