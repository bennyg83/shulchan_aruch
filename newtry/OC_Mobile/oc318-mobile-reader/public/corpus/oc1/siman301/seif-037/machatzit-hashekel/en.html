Tosafos on Shabbos 61b explains Rav Pappa's discussion. If a physician wrote three amulets for three people and each amulet cured three times, it is obvious that both the physician and the amulets are established as expert. Rav Pappa asks about three amulets written for one person, each curing him once. The individual amulets are not expert; is the physician expert, or should all three cures be attributed to that patient's favorable fortune in responding to written amulets? Rashi says that when one amulet cures the same person three times, the amulet is expert, because it is more reasonable to attribute the cures to a correctly prepared amulet than to the patient's fortune. When there are three different letters, however, the possibilities are either the physician or the patient. Rashba asks about the obvious case: How can the third amulet become expert? As soon as it cures the third patient once, the physician has already become expert, so its next two cures might be due to the physician rather than the amulet. Tosafos answers that the two statuses of expertise take effect together. This answer assumes that Rav Pappa's unresolved question is decided to mean that three letters curing one patient do not establish the physician, because the cures are attributed to the patient. Maharsha explains that Tosafos's answer works only under that assumption. If Rav Pappa's question were resolved in favor of the physician's expertise, the third amulet could never become expert, since the physician's status would precede it. Later authorities ask: We treat Rav Pappa's unresolved question stringently and do not deem the physician expert. In the obvious case, then, how can we call the third amulet expert, since perhaps the other side of Rav Pappa's question is correct and the physician became expert first? To treat the physician as expert would itself be a stringency here. The answer is that if the physician were certainly expert, Maharsha's point would be correct. Where the matter remains doubtful, however, the third amulet may still become expert. In the obvious case, at least one of them; the physician or the amulet; is certainly expert, and when the two possibilities are evenly balanced we treat both as expert. Rav Pappa's question differs because there it is possible that neither the physician nor any amulet is expert, for all the cures may depend upon the patient's fortune; we therefore rule stringently. Although Rashi indicates that a cure is generally more attributable to the amulet than to the physician, the physician has the countervailing advantage that his expertise may have begun earlier, together with the possibility that Rav Pappa's question should be resolved in his favor. The considerations balance, so both are deemed expert. Maharsha too would disagree only if Rav Pappa's question were certainly resolved in favor of the physician; while it remains doubtful, both may be expert.
