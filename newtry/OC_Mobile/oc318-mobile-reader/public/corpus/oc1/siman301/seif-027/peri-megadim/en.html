Hat. See Taz, who discusses Shabbos 138b and Eruvin 102b. Each interpretation there yields both a leniency and a stringency. According to Rashi, the leniency is that when the hat is firmly secured with a strap, or when it is not normal for the wearer to go bareheaded, it is permitted even if the brim forms a tefach of overhang. For this reason, extending a cloak a tefach beyond one's head is permitted. The rationale is that making a temporary tent is rabbinic and, in a case of this sort, since the covering is upon his head, it is not treated as a tent; alternatively, there is no tent without partitions. See Taz, siman 315, letter 8. If, however, the cloak extends from his head with a tefach-wide roof and hangs in front of him, it constitutes a genuine temporary tent with roof and partitions, so the first rationale must be relied upon. Rashi's stringency is that where the hat is not secured it is forbidden because it may fall and he may carry it 4 amot in a public domain, even if it bends, even if there is no tefach, and even if it was already on his head before Shabbos. According to Rabbeinu Chananel and Rabbeinu Tam, however, when there is no tefach the matter is permitted and we are not concerned at all that it may fall and be carried. Bach explains likewise. Their stringency is that where there is a tefach of stiff brim it may be forbidden as a temporary tent even when firmly secured. The Mechaber in seif 40 follows Rabbeinu Chananel, Rabbeinu Tam, and Rambam, chapter 22 of the Laws of Shabbos, law 14. The Rif's text contains the word 'rather,' and it is possible that Rambam does not disagree with his teacher. The discussion can be read as follows: a sayana is a felt hat. If its brim is a tefach it is forbidden; if it is less than a tefach it is permitted. The novelty in the latter ruling is that we do not fear that it will fall and be carried, even though felt does not sit perfectly on the head. The Gemara then retracts that assumption and concludes that concern for falling does exist, while the distinction concerns whether the brim is sufficiently stiff and roof-like to constitute a temporary tent. This also accords with Rambam's wording that the brim must be extremely stiff like a roof. Accordingly, there is no contradiction in the Mechaber: although in seif 40 he follows the view that the prohibition depends upon a tefach-wide roof, in seif 41 he nevertheless records and rules like the opinion that we fear the hat may fall and be carried 4 amot in a public domain. Taz distinguishes between our Britel and a Kapelush. In a Britel it is not defined which part serves as a tent and which as a head covering, and the wearer intends only a head covering, so it is permitted according to Rabbeinu Chananel. A Kapelush, or what is called a Hut, whose brim extends a tefach around the head and is very stiff, may be forbidden according to Rabbeinu Tam. Where such hats are folded into a triangular shield shape, it is possible that their width is less than a tefach. Even if they project a tefach from the head, both length and a tefach of width are required; this will be discussed in siman 315. A Britel is nevertheless forbidden outside an eruv according to Rashi, because it may fall and one may carry it 4 amot in a public domain or karmelit; see siman 303, seif 18. Nachalat Tzvi writes that in a karmelit, which is a rabbinic doubt, one may rely on Rabbeinu Tam and Rabbeinu Chananel. According to the Mechaber, however, who incorporates both concerns, and because we frequently find a decree lest an item fall and be carried 4 amot in a public domain, Taz's ruling appears correct that outside an eruv it should be prohibited. It requires study what people rely upon to be lenient. The Gemara did not distinguish between an obvious brim such as that of a Hut or Kapelush and our less distinct Britel, perhaps because the form of sayana itself had a recognizable brim. Taz's statement that the Mechaber was lenient in seif 41 where one went out wearing it before Shabbos applies likewise where there is no tefach or the brim bends; he mentions 1 of the alternatives. Regarding the pantines and the ruling in seif 7 that a minor may not go out wearing a shoe too large for him, although it is not normal to walk barefoot, one can answer that because the oversized shoe is difficult to walk in, there is greater concern that it will fall and he will carry it 4 amot in a public domain, as explained above.
