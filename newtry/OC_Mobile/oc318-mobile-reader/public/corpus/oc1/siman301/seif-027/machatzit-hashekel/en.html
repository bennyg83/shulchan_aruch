Tosefes Shabbos, se'if katan 62, asks whether an amulet possesses sanctity. He brings proof from an expert amulet, which may be worn outside even though one may need to remove it before entering a bathroom and then come to carry it. The Gemara answers that since it heals, it is an ornament even when carried in the hand; Rashi likewise writes that one who carries it four amos is not liable. This appears difficult from Rabbi Oshaya's statement that permission applies only when he does not hold it in his hand and carry it four amos. Rashi explains that it is an ornament only when worn. Tosafos asks that perhaps holding it in one's hand is forbidden only because of appearances, like tying an amulet to a ring or bracelet. Tosafos answers that 'he does not hold it in his hand' includes even carrying it concealed in his fist, where there is no concern for appearances; it must therefore be a Torah prohibition. We see that Tosafos initially compared an amulet held in the hand to an ornament worn, because the amulet heals even while held.
Nevertheless, Tosafos compared it to an amulet tied to a ring or bracelet and did not distinguish those cases on the ground that they provide a stronger basis for suspicion. Although Tosafos ultimately proves that carrying an amulet in the hand is prohibited by Torah law, their initial comparison remains valid: An ornament held in the hand is at least rabbinically forbidden because of appearances. Every ornament should therefore be forbidden when carried in the hand. The later authorities' objection to this has no basis.
The Yerushalmi relates that Rabban Gamliel walked in a courtyard with a gold key in his hand, and his colleagues rebuked him because it was an ornament. It then gives two explanations: 'It was made for this and for that,' and 'The same applies to a man and a woman.' The first means that an object made both as an ornament and for practical use, such as a key, is forbidden to both men and women. The second means that an ornament made for both men and women is forbidden to a man as well: Since women are ostentatious and may remove it to show it, the Sages made no distinction and prohibited it for men too. Both interpretations are accepted, as stated in se'ifim 9 and 11. This implies that a purely male ornament having no practical use is permitted even in the hand. Magen Avraham therefore writes that 'in his hand' is not literal. Yet the Yerushalmi plainly proves that an ornament may be forbidden in the hand. If carrying an ornament in the hand were permitted, why would they rebuke him because he might remove it to display it? The ordinary cases of women's ornaments would not prove this, because those objects may be ornaments only when worn, whereas a cane or gold key is ornamental specifically when held. The Yerushalmi therefore establishes that even such an object is forbidden.
Although the prohibition of a hand-held ornament is itself only because of appearances, this does not preclude a further decree lest he remove it and carry it. The Sages sometimes enact a safeguard upon a safeguard when the first safeguard would otherwise fail; see Magen Avraham 348:4. Moreover, under the explanation that the object was made both as an ornament and for use, the cane belongs to both categories. Even if a pure ornament may be held just as it may be worn, an object made for both ornament and use is forbidden even when worn, as stated in se'if 11.
