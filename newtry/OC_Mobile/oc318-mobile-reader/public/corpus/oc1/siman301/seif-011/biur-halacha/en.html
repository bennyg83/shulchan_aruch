Some permit one made of silver, etc.; but if the key, etc.; and some write, etc. This wording implies that an iron or copper key fixed and attached at the end of the belt, as the later authorities explain, is less favorable than a silver key: One who permits this certainly permits a silver key, even if attached in the middle of the belt, as Taz explains. Taz himself appears to hold that the attached iron key is more favorable, so even one who is stringent there agrees here, since it becomes a single body with the belt.
To permit in this case. Regarding carrying a watch outside the eruv inside one's pocket, see Mishnah Berurah: It is more obvious than an egg in kutach that an object hidden in one's pocket cannot be an ornament. Chayei Adam 56 and Zachor Toras Moshe write likewise. Some later authorities call a watch an ornament at the end of siman 308; I copied their words in Mishnah Berurah there. That concerns only whether it may be handled, regarding which some are stringent even there, as explained. Even if one hangs the watch from a chain around his neck and leaves it visible to all, it still appears forbidden. Its primary manufacture is for use, and everyone who ordinarily wears it intends to use it to know the time while walking; it only incidentally adorns him as well. Proof is that when it breaks and no longer runs, people do not ordinarily wear it. Since he takes it out for use, even if it was initially made as an ornament too, Yerushalmi, Bameh Isha 3, demonstrates that he is liable to a sin-offering. It says: 'If it was made for both purposes' - a ring made both as a seal and an ornament - 'if he took it out to seal with it, he is liable; if as an ornament, he is exempt.' Even if he expressly takes the watch out only as an ornament, it is still forbidden. According to the first view in se'if 11 it is certainly forbidden, because an observer will say that he took it out for use. Even the second view permits only an object recognizably made as an ornament: People do not ordinarily make a key from silver or gold, as Levush writes. A watch is different. There is also concern that he may remove and show it. Although the poskim permit a man's ornament because a man does not ordinarily show it to another, people commonly show one another the time on a watch and look at it themselves. In doing so he may take it into his hand and carry it four amos. See below, se'if katan 29. Even if a true ornamental gold necklace is also around his neck, the watch is not subordinate to it, though the necklace is more valuable. The poskim rule similarly concerning a knife in a sheath and spectacles in a case: Even if the sheath or case is silver, the knife and spectacles are not subordinate, because spectacles are not made for the case; the case is made for the spectacles, as Beis Yosef writes. Likewise, the watch is not made for the chain; the chain is made for the watch. See Rashba's responsum cited by Beis Yosef and Shiltei HaGibborim on Mordechai. Taz writes that according to the permissive view in se'if 11, an iron key hanging from a silver chain is permitted because it is subordinate and considered an ornament. Tosefes Shabbos already challenges him for this same reason; see there. Even Taz would plainly agree in our case. There he holds that the key is entirely insignificant relative to the silver chain, like the case at the beginning of se'if 38. A watch, however, is itself highly significant. It is no less significant than the silk threads in se'if 38, which are not subordinate, and all the more so here. I have discussed this at length to reject the opinion of one great authority who wrote leniently in passing without giving any reason or proof. It is especially astonishing that he himself writes that the later authorities, including Chacham Tzvi, prohibited it, yet disagrees based on unsupported reasoning. The conclusion is that there is no basis for leniency in any form. In particular, several poskim hold that even nowadays a true public domain exists, as explained below in siman 345 in Mishnah Berurah and Biur Halachah. There is therefore concern of liability to a sin-offering, since this is its ordinary mode of carrying. I found that Kitzur Shulchan Aruch also rules as we do, prohibiting it even with a gold necklace, and reports that the gaon Mahara Heller ruled likewise.
