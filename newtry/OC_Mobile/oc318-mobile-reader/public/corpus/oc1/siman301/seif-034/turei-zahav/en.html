With a folded scarf, etc. This requires explanation: why is folded permitted here more than by a tallis, where one is liable for a folded one? The Beit Yosef wrote that Rabbeinu Yerucham wrote that it is permitted to go out with a scarf wrapped like a cloak. It implies from his words that if it is not wrapped it is forbidden, yet it is difficult, for the statement that a person may go out with a scarf folded on his shoulder implies that even though he is not wrapped in it, it is permitted. It is not comparable to a tallis, for a scarf is different, since that is its way of wearing; and so appears from the Rambam, chapter 19, who wrote that one who goes out with a folded tallis placed on his shoulder is liable, but he may go out with a scarf on his shoulder. This implies that in the manner forbidden by a tallis, it is permitted with a scarf. I do not know the reason why a scarf should truly be permitted more than a tallis; if there is a difference in their form, the poskim should have written the difference, as the Tur wrote regarding a sakhnis. It appears to me that in this sugya there is an explanation. The Gemara says that Rabbi Yochanan said: one who goes out on Shabbos with a folded tallis placed on his shoulder is liable. Rashi explained: after it was hanging on his head, he lifted its edges onto his shoulders, and he is liable because this is not the manner of clothing. It is taught similarly, etc., and merchants go out with a scarf on their shoulders, etc. Later it says that bath attendants may bring women’s garments, provided that his head and most of his body are covered by them; a sakhnis must have its ends tied below the shoulders. It seems to me that Rashi was compelled to explain ‘folded tallis’ as after he had placed it on his head, etc., and not simply that he took a folded tallis and placed it on his shoulder. He also had to mention that after he placed it on his head, which is extra wording. It seems that the question bothered him: why is a tallis forbidden while a scarf is permitted? Therefore he explained that in truth, if he found the tallis folded and placed it on his shoulder in a manner of wrapping, it is permitted. What is forbidden is when he first placed it as it was across the full width of the tallis, and that is not the manner of clothing, so he is liable. But if he did not do so, and instead took a scarf already folded and wrapped it on his shoulder, it is permitted, for that is the way a scarf is worn while folded. The same is true of a tallis. Therefore Rashi explained that the prohibition of a folded tallis does not speak of placing it while it was folded, for if so it would be difficult from the folded scarf. Rather, it speaks where he first places it on his head and afterward lifts its edges and folds them on his shoulder. But if he places it while it is folded and wraps himself with it on his shoulder, it is indeed permitted. There is no distinction between scarf and tallis in this regard: in both, it is permitted when folded and wrapped with it, and forbidden when he places it on his head and then lifts and folds it over his shoulders. The Tur’s view follows Rashi, as is explicit in his explanation of a folded tallis, and afterward he writes the permission regarding a folded scarf, for any garment with which one wraps himself is permitted even if folded. There is no distinction between tallis and scarf; he merely used the language of the Gemara in each case. There in the Gemara Rabbi Yochanan used the language of tallis for prohibition, and the beraisa used scarf for permission. Afterward he wrote the law of bath attendants, which is not a garment at all unless his head and most of his body are covered; and likewise a sakhnis if its two ends are not tied below, for in those two cases folding is not relevant, only that they are not called garments. The Gemara also says that it is forbidden to make a gutter when going out to the public domain, and the Tur included it in what he wrote about gathering its two ends, etc. But the Rambam in chapter 19 wrote: one who wraps himself in his tallis and folds it on this side and that side, etc., as copied here in seif 31, which is the law of a gutter. Afterward he wrote: one who goes out with a folded tallis placed on his shoulder is liable, but he may go out with a scarf on his shoulder even though no thread is tied to his finger; and any scarf that does not cover his head and most of him is forbidden to go out with; if it was a short sakhnis that is not wide, he ties its two ends below the shoulders and it is found like a belt and it is permitted to go out with it. It must be said that the permitted scarf does not speak of folded, for he wrote that it must cover his head and most of him, and this matter was said in the Gemara regarding bath attendants, where it does not speak of folding at all, as the Tur wrote. Rather, the law of the scarf according to the Rambam is exactly the law of bath attendants and likewise does not speak of folding. Therefore the Rambam did not mention a folded scarf as he did by a tallis. The Beit Yosef wrote that it speaks in the very manner in which a tallis is forbidden, but with a scarf it is permitted; we have already shown that this is not so. Rather, it seems to me that this is the main difference between them: the folded tallis that is forbidden is not explained like Rashi, but simply, that he takes a folded tallis and places it on his shoulder; although it is in a manner of wrapping, it is forbidden. If so, I might have thought that anything that does not cover his whole body is also not a garment even when it is not folded; therefore he wrote that with a scarf on his shoulder that extends somewhat downward, it is permitted, but in any event it must cover his head and most of him, which is the case of bath attendants mentioned in the Gemara. The Beit Yosef wrote that it need not actually cover, but only have the measure to cover, and this is very surprising, for the Gemara says explicitly ‘provided that his head and most of him are covered by it,’ and it did not say only the measure to cover. Certainly the Rambam also holds this. He mentioned ‘on his shoulder’ not to exclude the rest of the body somewhat afterward, namely most of the body. According to both Rashi and the Rambam, permission for a scarf is only specifically in a manner of wrapping, and so wrote the Beit Yosef in the name of Rabbeinu Yerucham, that it is permitted to go out with a scarf wrapped like a cloak. The Beit Yosef wrote on this that it implies that if it is not wrapped it is forbidden, and found it difficult because a person may go out with a folded scarf on his shoulders, implying that even if he is not wrapped it is permitted, unlike a tallis, for a scarf is different since that is its way of wearing. According to me, Rabbeinu Yerucham’s words are settled as I wrote. It is also surprising that the Beit Yosef’s difficulty uses the wording ‘a person may go out with a folded scarf,’ for this language is not in the Gemara but only in the Tur. The principle that emerges in my humble opinion is that according to Rashi and the Tur, whenever it is a garment and one folds it and wraps himself in it, it is permitted. Therefore the Rashba permitted what people do, carrying a tallis under the cloak around his neck in the public domain, for they prohibited only where he placed it on his head and afterward lifted its edges, as Rashi explained, as the Beit Yosef wrote. Anything that is not a full garment, like that of the bath attendants, is forbidden even without folding unless afterward his head is covered, and with a sakhnis by tying, for thereby he makes it a garment, as the Rambam concluded, that it becomes like a belt. According to the Rambam, whenever it is folded it is forbidden to place it upon oneself even in a manner of wrapping. In practice, one should be stringent this way and not carry the tallis under the cloak unless he spreads it out and wraps himself in it; even if it does not reach all the way down but covers most of his body, that is sufficient. I have already mentioned that what the Beit Yosef and Shulchan Aruch permitted regarding our garments is very surprising. It is even more surprising, for when he gathers the garment he makes it like a gutter, and this was forbidden in the Gemara and poskim whenever not done for adornment. What does it help that we take the hands out of the garment? So it appears to me, although the Beit Yosef did not descend to all this; it is Torah, and we must learn.
