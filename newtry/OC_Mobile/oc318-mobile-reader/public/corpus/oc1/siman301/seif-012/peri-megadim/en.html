To hold. See Taz, who writes that the concern is degradation of Yom Tov. See siman 522, for on Yom Tov the prohibition of carrying does not apply in the same manner because of the principle that an act permitted for food preparation is also permitted for another Yom Tov need. Thus the concern is mainly weekday-like conduct, and it applies in a public domain or karmelit where many people are found. On Shabbos, however, there is also the issue of carrying outside an eruv. Levush gives the reason that perhaps he may come to carry the item 4 amot in a public domain, since he is capable of walking without it. A practical difference arises where he already went out into the public domain. Magen Avraham, seif katan 25, in the name of Eliyah Rabbah, writes that where the entire concern is lest he carry 4 amot, if he already went out he need not remove it there. Where the issue is carrying a burden, however, he must remove it. See Shabbos 66a, Rashi and Tosafos there. Regarding a cane with a nail at its end that certainly makes a hole as one walks, this is a psik reisha, though he has no benefit from it. Where he walks on unpaved ground, one may consider whether the hole is of no use to him and the prohibition is rabbinic. A psik reisha in a rabbinic prohibition from which he derives no benefit may perhaps be permitted. I discussed this at length in my novellae to Shabbos 66a. See Magen Avraham, siman 314, seif katan 5, who discusses psik reisha and shows that even in a rabbinic prohibition it is forbidden, as is evident in siman 337 regarding dragging a bench when this will certainly make a furrow. The case of a psik reisha from which he derives no benefit means that he truly has no benefit from it. Accordingly, I do not see a clear basis to permit a cane with a nail that certainly makes a hole. One might nevertheless argue that it is destructive in the public domain, is digging in an unusual manner, and involves other grounds for leniency. The matter requires study; see Magen Avraham, simanim 314 and 337. The Tur cites the ruling in Beitzah 25b that a person may be carried in a chair on Shabbos if the public needs him. The Mechaber omitted it here and recorded it in siman 522 regarding Yom Tov. Here, in a public domain, there can be a Torah prohibition, while a karmelit was prohibited lest one come to a public domain. Although a living person carries himself and the bed is secondary to him, and the Sages did not decree in the usual case, the Mechaber nevertheless omitted the leniency on Shabbos. It may be that Yom Tov is different because there is no Torah-level carrying concern in this context, only weekday-like conduct, and where the public needs the person there is no such concern, particularly for the honor of Torah. On Shabbos, however, the activity remains within the framework of melacha and was prohibited even where the immediate prohibition is rabbinic. Rama also omitted this leniency, which indicates that he agrees. It is possible that even through a non-Jew it is forbidden in a public domain, since the Sages attached this prohibition to a Scriptural verse, as Beit Yosef explains in siman 244. Yet according to those who permit a double rabbinic prohibition for a mitzvah, one may argue that where the public needs someone for Torah study, carrying him through a non-Jew is permitted, since a living person carries himself. Bach writes that it is permitted even on Shabbos because the prohibition is rabbinic. The practice in some places of seating the sandak together with the infant on a chair and having Jews carry them to the synagogue on Shabbos is certainly forbidden even in a karmelit. The infant is not secondary to the sandak, and in a public domain one would be liable for carrying him. See Magen Avraham, siman 308, seif katan 71; siman 331, seif katan 5; and Taz, siman 266, seif katan 4, regarding carrying through a non-Jew where our public domains are treated as a karmelit.
