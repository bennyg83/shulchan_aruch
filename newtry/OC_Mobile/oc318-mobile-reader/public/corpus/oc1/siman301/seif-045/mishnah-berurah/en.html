161) In water. Whether soaked by rain or because he fell into a river and his garments became wet.
162) He may walk in them, etc. The garments a person wears were not forbidden to him. Even if they became wet while off his body, he may put them on and walk in them. Presumably this applies only when he has no other clothes.
163) He may not spread them out. Magen Avraham inclines to permit spreading garments on which only a little water fell, since observers will not suspect laundering. Many later authorities make no distinction.
164) And even. Infant cloths may be spread inside the house, and even in the sun in a courtyard, because the child's excrement proves they were not laundered. This may be permitted even without excrement if not wholly soaked with urine, since everyone knows infants urinate in them. They may not be placed by an oven where the heat reaches yad soledes bo. Chayei Adam.
165) In the innermost rooms. The Sages made no distinction in their enactment. Tosafos and Rosh explain that when observers would suspect a Torah prohibition, such as laundering here, it is forbidden even privately. When they would suspect only a rabbinic prohibition, it is forbidden only publicly. Magen Avraham, Taz, and other later authorities cite this.
166) It is forbidden. A damp towel brought from the bathhouse on Friday should be spread to dry before Shabbos, not after sunset. Siman 342 implies that if needed for Shabbos the next day, it may be spread during twilight, provided it is not wrung out.
167) He need not remove them. Even if spread before the public. Suspicion arises only when one spreads them on Shabbos, giving reason to think he laundered them that day; had he laundered them yesterday, he would have spread them then. People will not assume they merely became wet, since not everyone knows that. Levush.
