(1) "A person," etc. In order to make the distinctions in this siman easier to understand, I will first give a brief introduction based on the Gemara and the halachic decisors. Two verses are written. One states, "so that your servant and your maidservant may rest like you." This refers to a slave who underwent brit milah and immersion for the sake of slavery and accepted all the mitzvot incumbent upon a slave, meaning all the mitzvot in which a woman is obligated. Shabbos is therefore included, and he may not perform melacha on Shabbos. This verse teaches that the obligation of the slave's rest also rests upon his master, who is commanded to prevent him from performing melacha even for the slave's own needs. Another verse states, "and the son of your maidservant and the stranger shall be refreshed." The Sages expounded this as referring to a slave bodily owned by a Jew who did not undergo brit milah and immersion but accepted the 7 Noahide mitzvot. His master must prevent him from performing melacha for the master on Shabbos, and likewise every Jew is forbidden by Torah law to instruct him to perform melacha for them on Shabbos. For his own needs, however, such a slave may perform melacha on Shabbos, because Shabbos is not among the 7 Noahide mitzvot. His law is like that of a ger toshav, who is not commanded concerning Shabbos, although a Jew is still forbidden by Torah law to instruct him to perform melacha for the Jew, as indicated by the verse. Regarding a slave bodily owned by a Jew who did not undergo brit milah or immersion and did not accept even the 7 Noahide mitzvot, the authorities disagree whether we are likewise commanded concerning his rest, as will be explained. (2) "Concerning the rest of his slave." The master is commanded to guard him and prevent him from performing melacha even for the slave's own needs, as stated above. It is permitted to lease such a slave to a non-Jew during the weekdays if he stipulates that the non-Jew not have him perform melacha on Shabbos. This differs from one's animal, concerning which siman 246, seif 3, states that one may not lease it to a non-Jew under such an arrangement because the non-Jew is not trusted to honor the condition. Here, the non-Jew cannot force the slave to work against his will, and the slave is trusted concerning this. [Pri Megadim] (3) "Those that apply to a slave." Meaning all mitzvot in which a woman is obligated. (4) "He is like a ger toshav." A ger toshav is one who accepted the 7 Noahide mitzvot and undertook not to serve idolatry, even in partnership. (5) "For himself." Meaning his own melacha, such as sewing his garments, repairing his shoes, and the like, or working for wages in order to have food beyond what his master supplies him. (6) "But not for his master." Even if the master did not explicitly instruct him to perform the melacha, it is forbidden, because ordinarily he acts with his master's interest in mind, and the Torah warned the master to prevent this, as stated above in seif katan 1. Even rabbinic melacha is forbidden for the master's benefit. Some are lenient regarding rabbinic melacha if the master did not explicitly instruct him. (7) "And it is forbidden to every Jew." Meaning by Torah law. Since he has already accepted the 7 Noahide mitzvot, everyone agrees that he has the status of a ger toshav, and the Torah forbids a Jew to have melacha performed through him. (8) "And if he did not accept." Meaning that even if he accepted no mitzvah at all, nevertheless, since he is a slave bodily owned by a Jew, this view holds that his law is the same as one who accepted the 7 Noahide mitzvot, and it is forbidden for him to perform melacha for any Jew. (9) "To tell a Jewish-owned slave." Only telling an ordinary non-Jew, which is a rabbinic shevut, was permitted in the cases mentioned there. A non-Jewish slave owned by a Jew, concerning whom the Torah commands rest, has no such permission except for pikuach nefesh. (10) "And some disagree and permit this." This refers to a slave who is a complete non-Jew and accepted no mitzvot. Although the first view equates him with one who accepted the 7 Noahide mitzvot and therefore forbids even another Jew to instruct him, the dissenting view holds that the Torah prohibited only melacha performed for his master, as indicated by "the son of your maidservant shall be refreshed." For another Jew he is no more stringent than an ordinary non-Jew, so the prohibition is only rabbinic and it is therefore permitted for the needs of an ill person even where there is no danger. (11) "If he was doing," etc. Although it was explained above that the master is commanded concerning the slave's rest, whether the slave accepted the 7 mitzvot or not, that applies specifically when the master is pleased with this melacha, for then we assume that the slave ordinarily acts with the master's interests in mind and the master must stop him. If it is evident that the slave is not acting for the master, it is treated like melacha for himself and the master need not prevent it. (12) "And for another Jew," etc. Specifically regarding his master, because he is his slave, we assume that whatever he does is for his master. Regarding another Jew, even if the slave acts knowing that the Jew will benefit, as long as the Jew did not instruct him and the slave or ger toshav began on his own, we assume that he principally acts for his own benefit in order to profit from the work. It is forbidden only when the Jew instructs him, as stated above in seif katan 7. (13) "It is permitted." See the end of siman 252, seif 2, where in some circumstances one must nevertheless protest on rabbinic grounds. (14) "Provided that he does not benefit." This is rabbinic, because the slave is no better than an ordinary non-Jew who performs melacha knowingly for a Jew, from whose melacha the Jew may not benefit on Shabbos, as stated at the beginning of siman 276. (15) "And some say that every," etc. They hold that the Torah warned concerning a Jewish-owned slave on Shabbos only where he has at least some mitzvot upon him, meaning the 7 Noahide mitzvot, as explained in Beit Yosef. See Eliyah Rabbah, who cites several authorities that in practice one should be stringent like the first view. It is forbidden to instruct an apostate Jew or Karaites to perform melacha for him on Shabbos or Yom Tov, because of lifnei iver. [Pri Megadim] (16) "And every slave," etc. Whether this also refers to a slave who did not undergo brit milah and immersion, see Tosefet Shabbos and Pri Megadim. (17) "It is forbidden to go out," etc. The ruling is first stated generally and then explained. If there are 2 grounds for leniency, namely the seal is made of clay and hangs from his neck, he may go out wearing it. There is no concern that he will remove it and carry it 4 amot in a public domain, because he fears his master will say that he removed it to appear free. There is likewise no concern that it will break by itself in the public domain and that he will carry the broken piece home, because a broken clay seal has no value. Holding it in his hand would not prove his servitude, because it signifies servitude only when it is hanging from his neck or garment. If there is only 1 ground for leniency, however, either because the seal is hanging from his garment or because the seal is metal and valuable, he may not go out with it into a public domain, as explained below. (18) "But not on his garment." Perhaps the seal will break by itself and he will fear his master will say that he removed it in order to appear free. He may then fold his garment so the place of the seal is not visible and carry the garment upon his shoulder, which makes the garment a burden. (19) "And with a metal one," etc. Since it is valuable and his master is particular that it not be lost, we are concerned that it may break and he will carry it 4 amot in a public domain. (20) "In every manner." Meaning even when it hangs from his neck. Since he made the seal for himself, he does not fear his master if he removes it, and we therefore fear that he will take it in his hand and carry it 4 amot in a public domain.
