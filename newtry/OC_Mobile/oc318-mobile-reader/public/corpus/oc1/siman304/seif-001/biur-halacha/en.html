"And likewise, if someone died on the first day of Yom Tov," etc., "it is forbidden to tell a Jewish-owned slave." See siman 526 in Beit Yosef. Ramban there inclines to permit use of a slave who did not undergo brit milah and immersion. The approach of the Shulchan Aruch here follows Baal Halachot Gedolot. Tosefet Shabbos sought to read this distinction into the concluding words, "and some disagree"; see there. "And some disagree and permit this." As explained in Mishnah Berurah, where the slave did not accept the 7 Noahide mitzvot, the Torah prohibition concerning his Shabbos rest applies according to this view only to his master and not to another Jew. This appears to apply specifically where the slave is not acting for his master's benefit. If the slave is doing the melacha to please or benefit the master, the master must stop him. Where it is clear that the act is genuinely for the slave himself, there is no obligation to prevent it. For another Jew, the law is more lenient. Even when the slave knows that another Jew will benefit, if that Jew did not command him and the slave initiated the work himself, it may be presumed that he acts for his own benefit, such as for payment or another advantage. The prohibition arises when the Jew instructs him, or where it is evident that he is acting specifically for the Jew. "Permitted." See the end of siman 252, seif 2, where in certain rabbinic cases one must nevertheless protest against a non-Jew's work. "Provided that he does not benefit." This restriction is rabbinic. The slave is not more lenient than another non-Jew who performs melacha for a Jew, from whose Shabbos melacha the Jew may not benefit, as stated at the beginning of siman 276. "Some say every slave," etc. This view holds that the Torah did not warn a Jew concerning the Shabbos rest of a gentile slave unless the slave accepted at least some mitzvot, namely the 7 Noahide mitzvot, as Beit Yosef explains. Avnei Nezer cites several authorities who act stringently in accordance with the first view. It is likewise forbidden to instruct an apostate Jew or a Karaite to perform melacha on Shabbos or Yom Tov because of lifnei iver. [Pri Megadim] "Every slave," etc. Regarding a slave who also did not undergo brit milah or immersion, see Tosafos and Pri Megadim. "It is forbidden for him to go out," etc. The remaining discussion concerns the slave's seal and the laws of carrying it on Shabbos. Where there are 2 grounds for leniency, such as a clay seal hanging from his neck, it may be permitted. A clay seal has little independent value, so if it breaks there is no substantial concern that he will carry it. Where there is only 1 ground for leniency, such as when the seal hangs from the garment, or when it is metal and therefore valuable, carrying in a public domain is forbidden under the distinctions explained below. "Not on his garment." If the seal breaks, the slave may fear that his master will say he removed it in order to appear free. He may therefore fold his garment to conceal the place of the seal and carry the garment on his shoulder as a burden. "And if it is metal," etc. Because it is valuable, the master is particular that it not be lost. There is therefore concern that if it breaks the slave will carry it 4 amot in a public domain. "In every manner." Even if it hangs from his neck, when the slave made the seal for himself he does not fear his master if he removes it. There is therefore concern that he may take it in his hand and carry it in the public domain. These distinctions show that the laws here involve 2 separate questions: the master's command concerning the slave's Shabbos rest, and the ordinary Shabbos prohibitions that apply to the slave's own carrying. Each is governed by its own rationale and set of distinctions.
