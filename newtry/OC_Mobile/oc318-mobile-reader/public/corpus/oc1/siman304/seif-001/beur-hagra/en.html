"A person." The general rule is that there are 3 categories of Canaanite slave. The first is a slave who underwent brit milah and accepted the mitzvot, like a full convert, except that a convert is obligated in all mitzvot incumbent upon a man, while a slave is obligated in the mitzvot incumbent upon a woman. The second is an eved toshav, analogous to a ger toshav. The third is an ordinary gentile slave, like any other gentile. The latter 2 are without brit milah. Their laws are explained below. "Regarding the rest," etc. Yevamos 48b derives from "so that your servant may rest" the case of a slave who underwent brit milah. "Who underwent brit milah and immersion." We rule like R' Yose there and like R' Yochanan that one is not a convert until both brit milah and immersion have been performed. The same applies to a slave, because their law in this respect is the same. See there concerning the case of Binyamin. "And accepted upon himself." This follows Rif, who requires formal acceptance, based on the statement in Yevamos 48b, "if he did not wish," etc. See Rosh at the end of siman 38, Rif there, and the end of chapter 19 of Shabbos. "The mitzvot that apply to a slave." See Chagigah, chapter 1, concerning all the mitzvot in which a slave is obligated. "But if," etc. Kerisus 9a, where R' Shimon distinguishes a ger toshav; the halacha there follows R' Shimon. "Rather, he accepted," etc. Avodah Zarah 64b, in accordance with the Sages. See Rashba, responsum 59, at length. "The same applies, and it is permitted." Kerisus there. "But not," etc. Kerisus there and Yevamos there, from the verse "and the son of your maidservant shall be refreshed," as explained below. "And it is forbidden to every Jew." This is inferred from the qualification that he may work "for himself." Moreover, the law is analogous to a ger toshav, who has no master. The exclusion therefore must mean that only work for himself is permitted, but a Jew may not command him to work. "To tell him." The prohibition here concerns specifically instructing him, and this is the distinction between another Jew and his master, as explained below. "And if he did not accept." Maggid Mishneh writes in the name of Rashba, based on the passage in Yevamos concerning "the son of your maidservant" and an without brit milah slave. Rashba explains the verse in this manner because the baraita does not use the expression eved toshav as it does in Kerisus, and because otherwise no separate verse would be necessary, since he would already be included in the law of a ger toshav. "According to this." Regarding the prohibition of melacha, there is no practical distinction from another non-Jew with respect to instructing him, because telling any non-Jew to perform melacha is forbidden, as stated in Shabbos 150a. Likewise, work for his master is forbidden, as explained below. The difference is that with an ordinary non-Jew the prohibition is rabbinic, whereas here it is Torah-level, as appears in Yevamos. The practical consequence is as stated immediately afterward. "For the needs," etc. See Shabbos 129a. "Likewise." Beitzah 6a; see also 22a. "And some disagree," etc. Meaning, they disagree regarding a full gentile slave whom the earlier view equates with an eved toshav, implying that even another Jew may not instruct him. The dissenting authorities permit another Jew to instruct him because the Torah warned only the master concerning the slave's rest, as stated in Yevamos. The passage in Kerisus that compares the slave to a ger toshav applies specifically to an eved toshav. Accordingly, another Jew who is not his master may instruct him to perform what is needed for an ill person. The phrase "and some disagree" therefore refers back to the statement concerning even an ordinary gentile slave. Eshel Avraham writes likewise, although Beit Yosef did not cite a dissenting opinion on this point. "Nevertheless," etc., "and another Jew," etc., "provided," etc. All these distinctions are explained in Shabbos 151a: if the slave acts on his own initiative, one need not stop him when the work is not being done for the master's benefit. This is no more stringent than the law of a minor, whose father is likewise commanded concerning his rest, yet when the minor acts independently for himself the father need not stop him in every case. For the master, however, anything the slave does is ordinarily presumed to be for the master's benefit. Regarding another Jew, even if the slave knows that the Jew will benefit, so long as the Jew did not instruct him and the slave began on his own initiative, the act is presumed to be for the slave's own benefit or profit, and the Jew need not stop him unless it is clear that he acts for the Jew. "Provided that he does not benefit." Rabbinically, the slave is no better than any other non-Jew who knowingly performs melacha for a Jew. The Jew may not benefit from that melacha on Shabbos, as stated at the beginning of siman 276. The 6 distinctions stated here are reflected in the Mishnah: a non-Jew who kindled a lamp for himself may be used; if he kindled it for a Jew it may not. One need not tell a non-Jew who comes to extinguish a fire not to do so when he acts on his own initiative. A slave acting for his master is different because his actions are presumed to be for the master's benefit. These principles explain the rulings here and the distinctions between work for himself, for his master, and for another Jew.
