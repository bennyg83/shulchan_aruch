Before beginning, he should wash his hands. This is only for cleanliness, and no blessing is recited. Maharshal, responsum 86.
Concerning the removal of chametz. Many explanations have been given for why some blessings were formulated with the word “concerning” and others with an infinitive beginning with lamed. Rosh and Ran discuss this at length in the first chapter of Pesachim, as do Tamim Deim, simanim 179 and 186; Raavan, siman 35; Kol Bo; Rokeach; Rambam, chapter 11 of Hilchos Berachos; and Levush. Levush explains that we recite “concerning the removal” rather than “to remove” because this mitzvah can be performed by another person, in accordance with Ran and Riva. Olas Shabbos writes that according to the Mechaber’s ruling below in siman 475 that one recites “concerning the eating of maror,” the Mechaber must principally accept the explanation of Rosh and Rambam. Levush copied Ran’s explanation without noticing that it does not fit the Mechaber’s ruling. His inference that the wording “concerning the eating of maror” accords with Rambam is incorrect. Raavad challenges Rambam on precisely this point, Kesef Mishneh leaves it unresolved, and Raavad indeed holds that the proper blessing is “to eat matzah and maror.” Rosh, according to Riva’s explanation, likewise holds this view. Levush himself noticed the main difficulty regarding the blessings on matzah and maror below in siman 275 and gave his explanation. In my opinion, the principal reason we say “concerning the eating of matzah and maror,” which also protects Rambam from Raavad’s challenge, is that Scripture itself says, “They shall eat it with matzos and bitter herbs.” We therefore do not alter the scriptural formulation. A similar principle appears in Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 77.
Concerning removal. The later authorities agree that if one recited “to remove,” he fulfilled the obligation. Rosh writes that all agree the infinitive formulation is valid. We nevertheless recite “concerning” to teach the novelty that such a blessing is permitted. Thus the Sages say that one can recognize a Torah scholar from his blessings. See Taz, siman 166, seif katan 45, who struggles with this. According to Responsa Maharam, siman 76, the difficulty may be resolved. See Responsa Rama, siman 35.
The removal of chametz. Tur writes in the name of Rosh that Shehecheyanu is not recited over bedikas chametz because the inspection is necessary for the festival, and we rely upon the Shehecheyanu recited on the festival. Maharshal asks that the prohibition of chametz begins before the festival, from the sixth hour onward, at the time of slaughtering the Pesach offering. See Taz’s answer. I heard a correct answer from my father-in-law, the Gaon Rabbi Wolf. Rosh writes in the first chapter of Pesachim that removing chametz from the house is not derived from the command to eliminate it, but is required so that one not violate the prohibitions of seeing and finding chametz. Below, siman 443, Maggid Mishneh is cited as holding that according to Torah law one does not violate those prohibitions until night. Rosh therefore correctly writes that the inspection serves the festival, because the prohibitions begin at night.
As long as he has not completed the inspection. This implies that after completing the inspection he may no longer recite the blessing. Kol Bo, page 46d, the source of this ruling, writes explicitly: “He may recite the blessing as long as he has not completed the inspection. Every mitzvah that extends over time is still considered before performance as long as he blesses before completing it. Thus, if one wrapped himself in a tallis or sat in a sukkah without reciting the blessing, he may bless while he remains wrapped or seated.” This clearly supports the conclusion that after completion he cannot bless. Bach and the later authorities explain Rama and Kol Bo this way. It follows Rambam, chapter 1 of Hilchos Berachos and the end of chapter 3 of Hilchos Ishus, that once the act is complete one may no longer recite the blessing. There is a difficulty from Rama’s ruling in siman 158, seif 11, regarding washing the hands. If one forgot to recite the blessing until after drying, he may still recite it. This follows Hagahos Maimoniyos against Rambam, whereas here Rama rules like Rambam. In my opinion Rama distinguishes as follows. Regarding washing, it is impossible to recite the blessing immediately before the act because the hands are unclean. The blessing may therefore be recited after washing and drying. A similar principle is explicit in Yoreh Deah, siman 28, regarding covering the blood. Alternatively, the mitzvah is not yet complete until after one eats the bread, because one may not interrupt between washing and HaMotzi. After eating the bread, however, one truly may no longer bless, as the later authorities write in the name of Maharshal and as Rama rules here. Shach, Yoreh Deah, siman 19, seif katan 3, also rules this way. A newly printed work called Orach Mishor challenges Shach and overturns the words of Kol Bo and Rama. He is mistaken. Kol Bo and Rama are correct, and Shach’s proof is clear and compelling. Pri Chadash also discusses this law at length.
He has not completed the inspection. There are different opinions regarding what constitutes completion. According to Taz and Magen Avraham, one may still bless during the fifth hour when burning the chametz, because according to Rosh that is the completion of the inspection. The concluding language of Rama and Kol Bo, however, implies that once the nighttime inspection is complete, he may no longer bless. Bach rules this way. Although Kol Bo himself follows Rambam that a blessing may be recited throughout a continuing mitzvah, the removal is completed only upon burning, and the inspection is intended for the removal, as Rosh writes. One must say that according to Kol Bo the principal blessing was instituted over the inspection. Although the wording is “concerning the removal of chametz,” the term removal includes the inspection, as Kol Bo writes there. Once the inspection is complete, the blessing is no longer before performance. There is some proof from Rosh, Tur, and Kol Bo, who cite an opinion that if one spoke during the inspection he must repeat the blessing because he interrupted during the mitzvah. If the mitzvah continued until the daytime removal, why would he not repeat the blessing during the day after such a long interruption? It must be that the inspection was completed at night. Rosh, however, follows his own view that speech is not an interruption. He may therefore hold that the inspection concludes only with the daytime removal. This protects Bach from Magen Avraham’s challenge. Bach’s view is primary, to avoid entering a doubt concerning a blessing. In such a case, one may recite the blessing without the Divine Name and kingship.
He should take care not to speak. If he spoke about matters unrelated to the inspection before beginning, he must repeat the blessing.
Until he completes the entire inspection. If he spoke after beginning the mitzvah, he need not repeat the blessing. Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah writes: “This is disputed. I would say that the halachah is that one must repeat the blessing, though this should not be publicly ruled, because the blessing has not been fulfilled until the entire inspection is completed.” He cites Bircas Avraham and Mordechai in the first chapter of Pesachim in the name of Geonic responsa. In truth, these Geonim are the opinion cited by Tur, as Kol Bo also records in their name. All the leading poskim disagree, as Rosh and Tur write. The reasoning that the blessing has not been fulfilled until the inspection is completed actually supports the opposite conclusion. Tosafos, Rosh, and Ran in the chapter Kisui HaDam; Rif and Rosh at the end of Rosh Hashanah; Rokeach, siman 367; and Rashba in a responsum cited by Beit Yosef, Orach Chaim, siman 692, all write that when one has recited a blessing over a mitzvah, interrupted with speech, and cannot avoid completing the mitzvah, no second blessing is required. He must complete it in any event. This differs from one who speaks between one act of shechitah and another, because there he could choose not to slaughter additional animals. Here he is obligated to inspect all his rooms and houses. Speech is therefore not an interruption, even between one house and another. Kol Bo writes: “The Geonim wrote that if one has several houses to inspect, he recites only one blessing, provided he does not interrupt.” This may refer only to the preferred initial practice. Alternatively, these are the same Geonim cited by Mordechai who require a repeated blessing after an interruption. According to the established rule that where one must complete the entire mitzvah speech is not an interruption, no distinction should be drawn. The same law is clear regarding Hallel, Megillah, and shofar, and the later authorities agree. In a doubt concerning blessings, we follow the lenient view.
