In Magen Avraham, seif katan 6, the text should read: "It is not comparable to what is written in siman 585," etc.; if another performed the brit milah, that other person recites the blessing, and see Tur, siman 641. The intent appears to me as follows. Certainly, where the person obligated in the mitzvah performs the mitzvah himself, he can fulfill the blessing through another person, as explained in siman 8, seif katan 8, in the name of Hagahot Ashiri. Thus regarding shofar, the blower may properly recite the blessing, because the person obligated to hear the shofar hears it and thereby performs the mitzvah himself, while the blower discharges him with the blessing. What Magen Avraham calls "the hearer" means the person obligated to hear the shofar sound, who hears it from this blower, hears the blessing, and fulfills his obligation through it. Here, however, the obligated person performs no act of mitzvah at all; another person performs the mitzvah. One might therefore say that the other person should not recite the blessing because the mitzvah is not incumbent upon him. This is unlike separating terumot and maaserot, where an appointed agent recites the blessing, as is implied regarding challah. There, the agent who separates is himself connected to the matter, because he too may not eat from the dough before separation. That consideration does not exist here. Nevertheless, it is possible that if the homeowner is present at the time of the blessing, Magen Avraham agrees that the agent may properly recite the blessing. Since the homeowner, who is obligated to perform the search, fulfills his obligation through this agent, the agent is like him and it is as though the homeowner searched himself. The homeowner therefore bears an obligation to recite a blessing over this search and fulfills it through the blessing recited by the searching agent. Magen Avraham, however, inferred from the wording "if the homeowner wishes," etc., that only in such a case do they rely upon the homeowner's intention; otherwise the searchers recite the blessing themselves. This is difficult. If the homeowner is present while the other person is about to search, it is obvious that it is preferable for the homeowner himself to recite the blessing and for the agent to search under the homeowner's blessing, since the homeowner is the obligated party. It must therefore mean that even if the homeowner does not wish to fulfill his blessing through the agent's blessing, the other person who searches nevertheless recites the blessing. Magen Avraham then asks why he should bless when the homeowner is not there to fulfill his obligation through this blessing and the agent himself has no obligation to search. He answers that this resembles brit milah, where the other person who performs the act recites the blessing, apparently even if the father is absent, because the mohel is nevertheless performing a mitzvah. Here too, the searcher performs a mitzvah even though the mitzvah is not personally incumbent upon him. This comparison is somewhat difficult, because brit milah is different. If the father does not perform the brit milah, beit din is obligated to do so. Thus the mitzvah is one in which the agent himself has a halachic connection. By contrast, if a homeowner refuses to search his own house, no obligation rests upon another person to search that homeowner's house. I found that Dagul Merevavah likewise leaves this requiring study. Magen Avraham then writes: see Tur, siman 641. There it is explained that one who performs a mitzvah for others also recites the blessing. Magen Avraham cites these words of Tur also in siman 366, seif katan 20, and there also cites Rambam that one who affixes a mezuzah for others recites the blessing. He distinguishes a case in which the one reciting the blessing performs an act. See Bach, siman 676, who writes in manuscript that where the mitzvah rests on the person's body, such as lighting Chanukah lights, one who lights for another does not recite the blessing, whereas where the object itself requires a mitzvah act, such as separating terumot and maaserot, the agent recites the blessing. This distinction can be understood according to Tosafos Rid in Kiddushin, who explains that agency does not work where one says to another, "Put on tefillin for me," or, "Sit in the sukkah for me," because the mitzvah rests upon the body of the person himself. Accordingly, even with Chanukah lights, when another person lights for him, this is not an ordinary law of agency. Rather, the mitzvah incumbent upon the person is that a Chanukah light be kindled in his house, and there is no distinction whether he himself lights it or another person lights it. The person lighting merely effects the state required for the obligated person, comparable to telling another person to place tefillin upon him. Since the lighter does not have the status of an agent in the usual sense, he does not recite a blessing because the mitzvah is not incumbent upon him. Where the mitzvah rests upon the object, however, such as separating terumot and maaserot, the appointed agent is legally like the homeowner and therefore properly recites the blessing. The essential distinction between an obligation resting on the person and one resting on the object is that an object-based obligation is not constant. If a person has no produce, or does not wish to eat it, there is no obligation upon him to separate terumah. Tefillin and Chanukah lights, however, are personal obligations that rest upon the individual. Regarding bedikat chametz, it requires study whether it resembles Chanukah lights, because the mitzvah rests upon the individual to search his domain for chametz, and ordinarily every person has a place in which he keeps chametz during the year and the Sages enacted that it be searched on the 14th; yet another person's search for him works just as another person's lighting works for Chanukah, perhaps not through the law of agency. Or perhaps, since the essence of the mitzvah is that his domain be searched of chametz, it is an obligation upon the object, like separating terumah, and agency is fully effective. In any event, when the homeowner is standing there, either way the searching agent may properly recite the blessing. This is also explained in siman 670 in Bach's words cited by Magen Avraham. Regarding brit milah, my grandfather, the Gaon and author of Tevuot Shor, siman 28, writes that we apply the rule that a person's agent is like himself. His words require some examination, but this is not the place. As for the words, "And the custom is to place chametz nuksheh," this means hardened chametz and refers to Rama's statement, "And the custom is to place," etc. It is also possible that this is a separate reference marker upon Rama's words.
