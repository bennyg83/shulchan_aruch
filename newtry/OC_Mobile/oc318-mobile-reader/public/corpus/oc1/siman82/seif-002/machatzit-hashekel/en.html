(Subsection 2) "And there are those who say, etc."—and the first reasoning holds, etc.; and they do not disagree regarding the law, and both hold that it must crumble by throwing and by rolling—only they disagree in reality, and the practical difference is for examination. For per the first reasoning, if we saw it crumble by rolling, we do not rely on it and it requires examination whether it will crumble by throwing; and if we saw it crumble by throwing, that suffices, for then we know it is obvious that it would crumble by rolling. And per the second reasoning it is the reverse: we need to examine specifically by rolling, and if it crumbles by rolling, we rely that it is obvious it crumbles by throwing as much—in Baer Heitev, see there.

In Taharot chapter 3—and this is what we hold: anything that disqualifies terumah, namely second-degree impurity—for although in non-sacred food second-degree does not make third-degree, in terumah second-degree makes third-degree (and it is called "disqualified," for what is called "impure" imparts impurity to another object touching it, which is not so with what is called "disqualified"—it is not impure but only itself, yet it does not impart impurity to another object touching it; and since third-degree does not make fourth-degree in terumah, therefore third-degree in terumah is called disqualified and not impure). And that thing that disqualifies terumah, namely second-degree—if it touched liquids, even non-sacred liquids, the liquids become first-degree; and this is one of eighteen things they decreed in tractate Shabbos, chapter 1. And we taught in the end of chapter 3 of Taharot: a soft mass that is a liquid touched second-degree and became first-degree—if afterward it hardened, the name of liquid was nullified from it and it is called food, and it picked up liquid impurity that they had been called first-degree; and now that it became food, it is as if food touched liquid and became second-degree. And although if one leaves it in the sun it will again become liquid, nevertheless so long as it has not dissolved and is hardened, it is called food. Therefore we follow the present state—see there in Mishnah 2 and Turei Zahav there; and see below, siman 198, Magen Avraham, subsection 7.

For it implies excrement like pottery even though it has no bad smell—for we say (folio 25): Rava said excrement like pottery is forbidden, and they conclude there that it requires examination that it should crumble apart, and then it is permitted; and when it crumbles apart we require examination that it should not have a bad smell, as stated in the Talmud. If so, dry excrement like pottery that does not crumble apart—even though it has no bad smell—it is forbidden; for otherwise what difference is there between crumbling and not crumbling? That one throws it and it crumbles apart, thus it should read.

And if so, here too—the meaning is: as with excrement like pottery—even though the smell has gone, nevertheless it remains in its prohibition since it already had a bad smell and excrement still remains on it (as stated afterward regarding an old privy) until it crumbles apart, for then the name of excrement is nullified from it and it is called dust, and new circumstances have come. Here too with excrement hardened by cold—even though it has no bad smell—it is forbidden, for the matter of the name of excrement was not nullified. And one could say: at least excrement hardened by cold should be permitted like dry excrement.

"And on this Magen Avraham wrote, since it can return, etc."—the meaning is: granted, when it crumbles apart due to dryness, it is reasonable that excrement no longer remains on it and it is called dust, for it will always remain so; not so when hardened by cold—even though it crumbles apart, nevertheless it is not logical that the name of excrement should leave it, for it can return to its former state.

"What is stated regarding an old privy, etc."—since it was, etc., and it is also possible to return to its former state if one again uses it and there is excrement in it.
