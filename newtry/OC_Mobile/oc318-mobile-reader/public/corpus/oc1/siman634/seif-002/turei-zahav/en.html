It must contain enough to square, etc. The Beis Yosef explained that this means that it must have a circumference of twenty-nine handbreadths and two fifths. His reason, it appears to me, is that we require the measure of seven by seven in a square, as I wrote. In every square there is a diagonal, meaning two parts more than the straight line; this must likewise be so in a circle, because in a circle there is no diagonal, only the simple line in the circle must be like the diagonal line in the square. And so we say explicitly in the Gemara on page 8 according to Rabbi Yochanan, who holds that the measure of a sukkah is four by four in a square, that in a circle we require the measure of the diagonal line of the square. So too according to us, where the measure of a sukkah is seven handbreadths by seven handbreadths, the line of the circle must be like the diagonal line of the square, which is nine handbreadths minus a fifth, for the diagonal exceeds the simple line by two fifths. Since the line of the circle is nine handbreadths minus a fifth, the circumference around the circle is automatically three times this measure, for anything that is one handbreadth wide has a circumference of three. Therefore the circumference here must be thirty handbreadths minus three fifths, and with this you can make within it a square of seven by seven. But if the line of the circle is not nine handbreadths minus a fifth, but less than this, even if it is possible to cut it into sections and there would be forty-nine sections there, each one a handbreadth by a handbreadth, namely with the line of the circle being eight handbreadths and one eighth of a handbreadth and the circumference being twenty-four handbreadths, for then there are likewise forty-nine sections, each one a handbreadth by a handbreadth, as my father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote about this, nevertheless there will not be in the middle of the circle a proper square of seven by seven. And we require specifically squareness by the essence of the law. Now let us copy the words of the Beis Yosef in Yoreh De'ah, siman 286: "The Rosh wrote in the laws of mezuzah: Although in all the books it is written only, a house that does not have four amos is exempt from mezuzah, the Rif's text is: that does not have four amos by four amos, for it is reasonable that it is not fit for dwelling in any other manner. This is unlike the Rambam, who wrote that if its length was greater than its width, since it has four amos by four amos, it is obligated in mezuzah; for four amos by four amos is what we require for all those cases enumerated in the Baraisa in the first chapter of Sukkah." These are his words. And this is the language of the Rambam: "If it has enough to square four by four equally, even though it is round or five-sided, and needless to say if its length was greater than its width, since it has enough to square four by four, it is obligated in mezuzah." Regarding practical halachah, we adopt the Rambam's view, for only the Rosh disagrees with him, and the language "four amos by four amos" from which the Rosh learned is not decisive, for anything that contains enough to square four by four is included in four by four, since anything that contains enough to square thus is fit for dwelling. Until here is the language of the Beis Yosef in Yoreh De'ah. It is implied from the words of the Beis Yosef that he explains that according to the Rambam there, one does not need four by four in an actual square for mezuzah; rather, since it contains so much that one could make from it a square with four by four, that is enough. So too it appears that our master the Tur understood there, for he wrote: "The Rambam wrote that if it contains enough to square four by four, even though it is round, etc.; and my father, my master the Rosh, of blessed memory, wrote that it is not obligated unless it is square four by four." These are his words. I was very astonished at the covering of the eyes that I saw in our rabbis regarding this: how did the Rosh, and after him the Tur and Beis Yosef, understand from the Rambam's words that it does not need to be four in width and four in length? If so, why should it differ from sukkah here, where we require a square, as I wrote from the words of the Beis Yosef himself here, who gave the measure as twenty-nine handbreadths, as I wrote? Furthermore, what is the meaning of "equally" that the Rambam used, which would be unnecessary according to their understanding? Furthermore, why did the Rambam write "and needless to say if its length was, etc."? What obviousness is there in this more than in the first cases? The matter is clear that the Rambam also holds that we specifically require an actual square of four by four regarding mezuzah. That is why he used the language "equally"; he was careful that they not understand from his words that we do not require an actual square, but only that the circle contain so much that one could join and make a square. Rather, we specifically require that within the circle there actually be a square of four by four, without combining. But lest you ask: if so, it is obvious that it is obligated in mezuzah; therefore he wrote "even though it has no corners," to exclude the view of Acherim in the Gemara in the first chapter of Sukkah, who hold that a round sukkah is invalid because it has no corners, even though it has a square of the required measure within it. He teaches us that we do not rule so; rather, since it has an actual square equally within it, we are not concerned about corners. These are also the Rambam's words here regarding sukkah, and this is his language: "A round sukkah: if its circumference has enough to square seven by seven, even though it has no corners, it is valid." Thus he mentioned the novelty only regarding its lack of corners, implying that in any event it has the measure of length and width in an actual square. For if not, he should have written a greater novelty, namely, even though its length or width is not seven. Rather, it is obvious that we require an actual seven by seven in a square, and the only novelty here is from the aspect of the corner, to exclude Acherim whom we mentioned. This is also his intent regarding mezuzah: certainly the essential square requires four by four, but the novelty is from the aspect of the corner. And this is what he wrote, "and needless to say if its length was greater, etc.": meaning that it has there a length of five amos and a width of four amos; automatically there is at least an actual square of four by four, only there is an additional part in the length beyond the square. Because of this it is included in "not square," for every square has equal length and width. Nevertheless, if you cut off the excess length, you will have a complete square of four by four; it does not harm you that there is more than the fitting square. It emerges that according to the Rambam the law is also as the Rosh holds, that we require an actual square of four by four. But, with forgiveness due to the honor of the lights of our eyes, the Rosh and those who followed him did not intend this in their understanding of the Rambam's words; rather, they understood from his words that an actual square is not needed at all, and even if the length or width is less than the measure, it is valid. This never entered his mind. So too appears to be the view of the Maggid Mishneh, who wrote in Hilchos Sukkah that the Rambam who validates a round sukkah derives it from Rabbi Yochanan regarding a sukkah made like a kiln, etc.; and in the Gemara there it is proven according to Rabbi Yochanan that we require an actual square of four by four. Automatically, according to us too this is so regarding a sukkah whose measure is seven by seven, and as the Beis Yosef wrote here, that it requires a circumference of twenty-nine handbreadths and two fifths, so that there is a complete square inside the circle of seven by seven. His words in Yoreh De'ah that we mentioned are imprecise. Both regarding sukkah and regarding mezuzah, we always require an actual square of the measure, and not by combining the sections to make up the measure, whether according to the Rambam or according to the Rosh. It is only that the Rosh did not descend to explain the Rambam's words this way. This appears clear to me, with the help of Heaven.
