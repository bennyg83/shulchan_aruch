(Small paragraph 1) Seven by seven, etc. These are Rashi's words on page 2b: even though it is very long, and if one were to cut it into strips one would find in it forty-nine strips, each one a handbreadth by a handbreadth, which is the measure of seven by seven, for it too contains forty-nine strips that are a handbreadth by a handbreadth. So it is also implied by se'if 2, where he wrote that it must contain enough to square, etc.; this implies that there must be a square of seven by seven within the circle. It does not help that it is possible to cut strips from the circle that total forty-nine, each one a handbreadth by a handbreadth.
The Bach pressed himself regarding the Tur, who also wrote this language with respect to a round sukkah: "and it has enough to square seven by seven." These are his words.
And so Tosafos wrote on page 3, etc. This is the proof of the Bach. It is stated at the beginning of Sukkah, according to several amoraim, that the dispute of Rabbi Yehudah and the Sages regarding a sukkah that is higher than twenty amos, where the Sages invalidate it and Rabbi Yehudah validates it, applies in every manner, whether it is very wide or of an exact minimal measure. But Rav Huna said in the name of Rav that they disagree only about a sukkah that is four amos by four amos; if it is larger than this, the Sages agree that it is valid. And Rav Chanan bar Rabbah said in the name of Rav that they disagree only when it holds only his head, most of him, and his table; but if it is larger than this, the Sages agree that it is valid. The Talmud challenges from a Baraisa: Rabbi Yehudah said to the Sages, there was an incident with Queen Helene, who was sitting in a sukkah higher than twenty, and her seven sons sat with her, and the Sages said nothing to her. Although a woman is exempt, nevertheless the Sages should have stopped her because of her seven sons. Granted, according to the amoraim who say that they disagree in every manner, both in a large sukkah and in a small one, this is fine. But according to the one who says that they disagree specifically in a small sukkah, is it the way of a queen to sit in a small sukkah? And if it was a large one, the Sages agree that it is valid, so what proof did Rabbi Yehudah bring for his words? The Talmud answers that she had a large sukkah for the members of her household, but the large sukkah opened to a small chamber, meaning small sukkos, and they were higher than twenty; Helene, because of modesty, sat in the small chamber. This is the point of their dispute: the Sages held that her sons sat in the large one, and she sat in the small chamber, which was higher than twenty. What of it? A woman is exempt from sukkah. Rabbi Yehudah held that her sons sat with her in the small chamber, and the sons were obligated in sukkah, yet they said nothing to her; therefore one must say that higher than twenty is valid. Rashi asked that it still remains difficult for Rabbi Chanan bar Rabbah, who holds that they disagree only when it holds only his head, most of him, and his table. If so, how can one say that her sons sat with her? Is it possible for eight people to sit in a sukkah that holds only the head, most of him, and table of one person? About this Tosafos answered that it was very long and was not seven handbreadths wide, and her sons sat along that length. These are the contents of their words. Thus, even if it is not seven wide, nevertheless since it was very long it is valid, because it is possible to make from it forty-nine strips, each one a handbreadth. This is what he wrote.
For it is obvious that it is a scribal error. As the Turei Zahav wrote: one way or the other, if Tosafos meant that it was very long, meaning more than seven handbreadths, until, if one cut it into strips, it would contain forty-nine strips, each one a handbreadth by a handbreadth, it would still be difficult: how could eight people sit in such a sukkah, since in any event when joined together it holds only the head, most of him, and table of one person? And if they meant that it was extremely long, so that if it were cut it would yield more than forty-nine strips of a handbreadth by a handbreadth, and therefore her sons could sit with her, nevertheless what forced them to say that it was not seven handbreadths wide? Even if it was seven handbreadths wide, it could still have been very long, since in any case one must say that it held more than seven by seven. Rather, perforce the correct text must be: "and it was only seven handbreadths wide." If so, one can well say that it was very long, until it held much more than the measure of seven by seven handbreadths.
For it is referring to the words of Rashi and the Sages and Rabbi Yehudah; this is the correct reading. That is, they are discussing one that is seven handbreadths wide, and Tosafos did not change this and write that it was not seven wide, since in any case one must say that it held more than the measure of seven by seven, as above.
And on the contrary, it is proven from there, etc. For they wrote that it was only seven wide. This was unnecessary, for to answer their difficulty it would have sufficed had they answered that it was very long. Rather, they wrote this to exclude the reasoning of the Bach. Twenty-eight handbreadths and two fifths: since the Bach holds that we require a square of seven by seven, the diagonal, which is two fifths greater according to the rule that every amah in a square has an amah and two fifths in its diagonal, therefore the diagonal totals nine handbreadths and four fifths. If one makes such a thing in a circle, the circle must contain the entire diagonal of the square, for in a circle the entire matter is the diagonal, meaning nine handbreadths and four fifths. According to the rule that anything with a width of one handbreadth has a circumference of three handbreadths, the cord surrounding the circle is three times nine handbreadths and four fifths. This equals twenty-nine handbreadths and two fifths.
And what the Bach wrote, "why is it different from mezuzah"; this is the correct reading. This refers to the Rambam [and there is a scribal error in the Bach, where Rambam was exchanged with Rosh]. That is, if we say that here we specifically require a square of seven by seven, and it does not help if it is not seven wide but is very long, behold, with respect to mezuzah:
The Rosh disagrees with the Rambam. The Rosh holds that what we rule, that a house which does not have four amos by four amos is exempt from mezuzah, means that if it is longer than four amos but is not four amos wide, it is exempt from mezuzah, even though if one were to cut it into strips one would find the measure of four amos by four amos. The Rambam holds that in such a case it is obligated in mezuzah. If so, according to the Rambam the question is difficult: why, regarding sukkah, do we require the square measure? It should have sufficed with six by six.
It is not exact; rather, he means less than seven by seven.
And so it is implied in the Gemara, page 3, regarding the small chamber. This is the Gemara mentioned above. According to the way the Magen Avraham explained the words of Tosafos mentioned above, that they specifically copied that it was only seven handbreadths wide, for if it was narrower than seven handbreadths it would be invalid even though it was very long, in order to exclude the view of the Bach as above, it is difficult: granted, the Bach's words are not correct, nevertheless the small chambers did not need to be seven wide, and even so they should be valid by reason of "the s'chach that extends from the sukkah," for the small chambers opened to the large sukkah, as implied by the language of the Talmud, "it was necessary only for the small chamber in it." Rather, perforce one must say like the words of the Magen Avraham, that in such a case it is not validated because of "the s'chach that extends from the sukkah," since the place is narrow for sitting. One can also say that the proof of the Magen Avraham is from the fact that Rabbi Yehudah brought proof from Helene, for he held that her sons sat with her in a sukkah higher than twenty. What proof is there? Perhaps the small chambers were validated because of "the s'chach that extends from the sukkah," for an area higher than twenty is validated through "the s'chach that extends from the sukkah," as the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 633, small paragraph 5. Rather, one must say that this applies specifically according to us, where the reason for invalidating higher than twenty is because of a temporary dwelling. But according to Rabbi Chanan, that the Sages and Rabbi Yehudah disagree specifically about one that holds only his head, most of him, and his table, where the reason is, as Tosafos wrote, that it is like a chicken coop; see there. If so, above twenty is called invalid s'chach, and it is not permitted by means of "the s'chach that extends from the sukkah." [And so one must say according to the one who says that they disagree specifically about four amos by four amos, where the reason for invalidity is because there is no shade from the s'chach: above twenty is called invalid s'chach.] From this the Magen Avraham learned regarding our case, a corner that does not have seven by seven, that it is not validated through "the s'chach that extends from the sukkah." This proof is primary.
