But footwear made of reeds, etc. The Gemara relates that the Amoraim went out on Yom Kippur wearing the non-leather materials mentioned here. Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi wore cork sandals; Rav Yehudah wore footwear made of reeds; Abaye wore palm fibers; Rava wore grass; and Rabbah bar Rav Huna wrapped a cloth around his feet. Rami bar Chama challenged this from the law of an amputee’s wooden prosthetic shoe, which Rabbi Meir permits on Shabbos and Rabbi Yose forbids, while both agree that it is forbidden on Yom Kippur. Rava answers that the prosthetic is indeed considered a shoe, and their dispute concerns Shabbos. Rashi explains that it is therefore forbidden on Yom Kippur. This refers specifically to the amputee’s wooden shoe. The other materials remain permitted, for Rava himself wore grass footwear. All the authorities rule this way, except for Baal HaMaor cited by Ran. Semak cites a Tosefta prohibiting even cloth socks, but the authorities did not follow it because the Amoraim themselves wore such items initially. Nimukei Yosef likewise concludes that anything not called a shoe, such as cloth foot coverings, is permitted on Yom Kippur. One who is delicate or experiences discomfort may wear such cloth coverings, but leather footwear is forbidden even without a heel. His reference to discomfort does not mean illness, for an ill person may wear actual shoes. It merely describes the ordinary reason one would wear cloth coverings rather than walk barefoot. He later permits them without qualification, even in the public domain. Bach rules that one should be stringent and walk barefoot, relying on the phrase concerning discomfort, but this is not compelling. One should not depart from the accepted custom. Ran testifies that all Israel wore these materials. Whoever wishes to be stringent may do so privately, but should not teach that the public must be stringent. Terumas HaDeshen similarly warns against imposing a stringency in a get that would cast aspersions on earlier practice. Here too, since the Amoraim acted this way initially, claiming greater piety than they is astonishing. See siman 100.
