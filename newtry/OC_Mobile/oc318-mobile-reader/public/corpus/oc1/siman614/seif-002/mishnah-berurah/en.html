(3) Made of leather. This refers to both terms. A sandal is made of hard leather, while a shoe is made of soft leather.
(4) The amputee's prosthesis. A person whose leg was amputated makes a form resembling a foot with a small receptacle into which he inserts the end of his leg. This is called a prosthesis. The Mechaber is discussing one made of leather. The same law applies if it is made of wood and covered with leather, as explained below.
(5) But one made of reeds, etc. Anything not made of leather is not halachically called a shoe, but a garment. Some acharonim are stringent regarding a wooden shoe even when it is not covered with leather. Some are likewise stringent not to go out wearing felt footwear, called valenki, made in the form of our shoes. Since it protects the foot and the wearer does not feel barefoot at all, they maintain that it does not constitute affliction. According to this view, one should likewise be stringent regarding rubber galoshes. Although one should not protest against those who are lenient, since the Shulchan Aruch and most acharonim permit these items, one who is able should preferably be stringent and wear cloth socks, as is customary. If he must go outdoors, however, it is preferable to wear the felt footwear or galoshes rather than leather shoes. Leather shoes are forbidden by law, whereas these are avoided only as a stringency.
(6) Or made of cloth, etc. In all these cases the footwear must not be covered with leather, either above or below, as stated by the acharonim.
(7) It is permitted even to go out, etc. With footwear that is forbidden, one may not even walk around the house from bed to bed, as stated in the Gemara.
(8) Into the public domain. During the rest of the year it is forbidden to go into the public domain wearing this item because it is not the normal manner of wearing clothing. On Yom Kippur, however, since leather footwear is forbidden and he cannot wear an ordinary leather shoe, this is considered the normal manner of dress for that day.
(9) Made of leather. This is not benefit in the normal manner of wearing footwear. The prohibition applies only when he wears it on his feet. Nevertheless, one who is stringent will be blessed. Pri Chadash agrees with Radbaz, cited by Magen Avraham, that there is no concern at all. This applies when he is not reciting the Amidah. While reciting the Amidah, however, it is forbidden to stand upon pillows or cushions, even when they are not made of leather, because standing upon them then appears haughty. This applies even when they are less than three tefachim high. A person who is sensitive to the cold may stand on a small amount of grass to separate his feet from the ground.
