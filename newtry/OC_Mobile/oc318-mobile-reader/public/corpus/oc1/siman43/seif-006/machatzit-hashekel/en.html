Se'if katan 6: 'Without a pit, etc.' As stated in siman 83. This means when they are constructed as explained there: the opening is four cubits from the pit and the structure slopes, so that the excrement rolls and immediately falls far away, and the urine does likewise. This requires study, because Rashi comments upon the preceding Gemara. Berachos 23a states: 'Our Rabbis taught: One who enters a privy removes his tefillin four cubits away and enters. R' Acha bar Huna said in Rav Sheshet's name: They taught this only of a permanent privy; at a temporary privy he removes them and immediately relieves himself. They asked: May a person enter a permanent privy wearing tefillin to urinate? Ravina permitted it; Rav Ada bar Ahavah prohibited it. They asked Rava, and he answered: It is forbidden, for we are concerned that he may defecate while wearing them'; another version says, 'lest he pass gas while wearing them.' Rif, Rambam, and Rosh omit R' Acha bar Huna's statement in Rav Sheshet's name distinguishing a permanent from a temporary privy. This implies that they make no such distinction. Beit Yosef writes that their omission requires explanation; see there. We must say that this statement was absent from their text. The Rosh writes that Rav Hai Gaon was troubled: why ask whether one may enter a permanent privy to urinate? The baraita already states that even entering it without urinating is prohibited and the tefillin must be removed four cubits away. Rav Hai therefore reads the question as, 'May a person enter a discreet privy?' meaning one destined to become permanent but presently temporary. The Rosh writes that this emendation is unnecessary; the reading 'May he enter a permanent privy?' is correct and presents no difficulty. The baraita's requirement to remove the tefillin four cubits away is not caused by entering; it discusses one who intends to defecate. The Rosh concludes in Hilchot Tefillin: 'For this reason the baraita does not specify a permanent privy, because even if it is not permanent, once he enters to defecate he must remove them four cubits away.' This wording also implies that his text lacked R' Acha bar Huna's statement. According to the Rosh's reasoning, there is no basis to distinguish between a privy in an open field and one constructed over a pit. Since he intends to defecate and the prohibition is caused by defecation, what difference does the place make? As the Rosh himself writes, since defecation requires removal four cubits away everywhere, the same applies to urinating while wearing them, because he may defecate. This applies in a place normally used for defecation (a permanent privy) even if constructed over a pit. Rashi, who distinguishes a privy in the field from one over a pit, follows Rav Hai and holds that the prohibition is caused by entry. His comment does not address the question about one who intends to urinate, where the prohibition arises lest he defecate; that prohibition applies even to a privy over a pit. Rather, Rashi comments upon the baraita, 'One who enters a privy removes them,' which discusses entering without intending either to defecate or urinate. Even so, because it is a permanent privy he must remove them four cubits away. Here Rashi properly distinguishes: one may enter a privy constructed over a pit if he does not intend to defecate or urinate, because entering it involves no degradation, as no excrement or urine is present there. If he enters to urinate (the Talmud's question, whose conclusion prohibits it lest he defecate) Rashi concedes that there is no distinction. We rule like the Rosh, however, that the prohibition is not mere entry but applies when one wishes to defecate or urinate. There is therefore no distinction between a field privy and one over a pit. Magen Avraham's words are now understood. Chiddushei HaRashba likewise has our reading: 'R' Acha, etc.: They taught this only of a permanent privy; at a temporary privy he removes them and immediately relieves himself.' Rashba objects that although it is temporary, once he defecates he ought to move four cubits away under the rule, 'Your camp shall be holy'; all the more so when he intends to defecate. This last objection is Magen Avraham's, not Rashba's.
'According to what was written, etc.' When it is constructed over a pit, however, he removes them and immediately urinates; he need not move four cubits away. Although Magen Avraham first writes that even one who wishes to urinate in a permanent privy must move four cubits away, because we tell him that he may defecate while wearing them, Magen Avraham nevertheless writes that Beit Yosef does not hold this. There is no basis for criticizing Beit Yosef. He may hold that although actual defecation requires a distance of four cubits even over a pit, urination requires removing the tefillin only by decree lest one defecate; when the privy is over a pit they were not so stringent as to require four cubits. Beit Yosef has no proof for this in Rashi's words, but it can be maintained logically. In my humble opinion, however, I do not understand how to distinguish here between a field privy and one over a pit.
