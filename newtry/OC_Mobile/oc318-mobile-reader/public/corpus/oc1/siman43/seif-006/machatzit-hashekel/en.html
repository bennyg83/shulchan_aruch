(siman katan 6) "Without a pit" etc. — as stated in siman 83, meaning when they are made as explained there: its opening is four amot from the pit, and it is sloped so that excrement rolls and falls immediately far away, and likewise urine, etc. And it requires investigation: Rashi in the Gemara refers to the preceding matter in Berakhot 23a — "Our Rabbis taught: One who enters a bathroom removes his tefillin at a distance of four amot and enters. Rabbi Acha bar Huna said in the name of Rav Sheshet: They taught this only regarding a permanent bathroom; but in a temporary bathroom he removes them and relieves himself immediately." They asked: May a person enter a permanent bathroom wearing tefillin to urinate? Ravina permitted; Rav Elazar bar Avina forbade. They asked Rava, who said: It is forbidden — we are concerned lest he defecate in them. And another version: lest he pass wind in them. The Rif, Rambam, and Rosh omitted that which Rabbi Acha bar Huna said in the name of Rav Sheshet, "They taught this only regarding a permanent bathroom," etc. — implying they did not distinguish between permanent and temporary. And the Beit Yosef wrote that a reason is needed for their omission; see there. Therefore one must say it was not in their text. And the Rosh wrote that Rav Hai Gaon had a difficulty: what is being asked? If one may enter to urinate in a permanent bathroom — this is explained in the baraita, that even entering, even if he will not urinate, is forbidden in a permanent bathroom, and he must remove them at a distance of four amot. Therefore Rav Hai emended the question to: may one enter a modest bathroom — meaning one that will ultimately become permanent (but is now temporary). And the Rosh wrote that this is unnecessary, and the correct text in the question is: may one enter a permanent bathroom. And there is no question from the baraita above. For that which the baraita required removing at a distance of four amot is not because of the prohibition of entering, but deals with one who wishes to relieve himself (and the Rosh concluded in Hilchot Tefillin: "Therefore the baraita did not teach 'permanent bathroom,' for even if it is not permanent, once he enters to relieve himself he must remove them at a distance of four amot," etc.). From this language too it is implied that in his text there was not that which Rabbi Elazar said in the name of Rav Sheshet, "They taught this only regarding a permanent bathroom," etc. Per the Rosh's reason, there is no logic to distinguish between a bathroom on the surface of the field and one made in a pit. Since his intent is to defecate and the prohibition is because he defecates — what difference does it make in which place he defecates? And as the Rosh himself wrote in Hilchot Tefillin as above: since because of defecating one must remove them at a distance of four amot in every case, likewise it is forbidden to urinate in them for the same reason — lest he defecate — and this is in a place where one is accustomed to defecate, namely a permanent bathroom, even if it is in a pit. However, Rashi, who distinguishes between a bathroom on the field and one in a pit, holds like Rav Hai that the prohibition is because of entering, and does not refer to the question of one who wishes to urinate — which is forbidden from concern lest he defecate, and this is forbidden even in a bathroom made in a pit as above. Rather, he refers to the baraita that teaches "one who enters a bathroom removes them" — which deals with one who does not enter to defecate or urinate; nevertheless, since it is a permanent bathroom, he must remove them at a distance of four amot. With this Rashi's distinction is well explained: in a bathroom made in a pit one may enter if he does not intend to defecate or urinate, for there is no disgrace in entering a pit bathroom, as there is no excrement or urine there. But if he enters to urinate — as the Talmud asks and concludes it is forbidden lest he defecate — Rashi teaches there is no distinction. But the Rosh, who holds like us that the prohibition is not because of entering but only regarding one who wishes to defecate or urinate, should not distinguish between a bathroom on the field and one in a pit. From now the words of the Magen Avraham will be understood. And likewise in the Rashba's novellae, who reads like our text: Rabbi Elazar, etc., "They taught this only regarding a permanent bathroom; but in a temporary bathroom he removes them and defecates immediately" — and on this he had a difficulty: even though it is temporary, once he defecated he should require distancing four amot, for we require "and your camp shall be holy," all the more so when he wishes to defecate. This is from the words of the Magen Avraham and not from the words of the Rashba.

And according to what was stated etc.: but when it is in a pit he removes them and urinates immediately, and certainly not four amot — even though at first he wrote that even when one wishes to urinate in a permanent bathroom one must keep four amot away, since he said "lest he defecate in them." Nevertheless, although it seems correct to the Magen Avraham, nevertheless the Magen Avraham wrote that the Rav Beit Yosef does not hold so, and there is no place to seize upon the Beit Yosef — for one may say he holds that if he defecated he requires distancing four amot even when made in a pit. Nevertheless, if he urinates, which does not require removal except because of a decree lest he defecate — since it is in a pit they did not impose so stringently as to require distancing four amot, even though the Beit Yosef has no proof for this from Rashi's words; nevertheless one may say so by logic. But I do not know according to his view how to distinguish in this between on the surface of the field and a pit.
