And some say even, etc. Rosh's view appears to permit entering a privy wearing tefillin when he does not need to relieve himself. This is difficult: is it better than a bathhouse, where one removes them as in siman 45:2? Indeed, a privy is more severe, for Krias Shema is permitted in a new bathhouse but forbidden in a new privy. I understood this question from the gloss of the author of Shach, who noted, 'See below, siman 45:2.' It therefore appears that everyone agrees one may not enter a privy wearing tefillin when not urinating. The inquiry concerned only entry to urinate, since this is a vital human need; the conclusion forbids it because we fear he may defecate while wearing them. Tur and Shulchan Aruch in se'if 1 say, 'It is forbidden to enter, etc., to urinate' as the greater novelty: even urination is forbidden, certainly entry without need. Proof is that he must remove them four amos away, and according to Rashi this is because of entry, as in se'if 1. Entering a bathhouse wearing tefillin is forbidden because when bathing one removes his clothes regardless. Tur did not cite Rav Hai because the matter is obvious. This is also implied by se'ifim 7 and 8.
In his right hand. Not in his left, because he needs the left hand for wiping, as written in siman 3:10.
A strap. A strap also possesses sanctity, since the Divine Name is formed in it. Beis Yosef writes that concerning entry to a permanent privy, Rabbeinu did not say to give them to another person; apparently this is because it is a mitzvah to guard them oneself. But se'if 1 concerns urinating while standing at a permanent privy. He cannot bring them in his hand because he must wipe droplets, nor on his head because we fear he may defecate; he must therefore give them to another person. Here he relieves himself seated and may hold them in his hand rather than give them away, as explained above. At a temporary privy he may urinate while wearing them on his head. Responsa Rema MiPano 59 permits entering filthy alleyways wearing tefillin even when an openly filthy place is present; it is good to cover them with one's hat, and one may instead remove and later replace them. Berachos 24 supports this: Rabbi Abbahu walked while reciting Krias Shema and fell silent upon reaching filthy alleyways. Surely he did not recite Krias Shema without tefillin; had he removed them the Gemara should have said so. Evidently, one may enter such alleyways wearing them. Nevertheless, one might say that a person who knows he will pass through filthy alleyways should put them on at the synagogue, not at home. Shabbos 127 relates an even stronger case: Rabbi Yehoshua refused to enter a gentile's house wearing tefillin, saying that sacred words should not enter a place of impurity. Olas Tamid writes that he should put them on at home and cover them in filthy alleyways.
