Se'if katan 9: 'And some say even, etc.; this implies that according to the Rosh, etc.' The Shulchan Aruch's wording itself proves nothing. One can say that according to the 'some say,' even when he does not intend to relieve himself he must move four cubits away; according to the first view, which is the Rosh's, four cubits are required only when he relieves himself. Yet mere entry might be forbidden even according to the first view when he does not relieve himself. Rather, Magen Avraham means that this follows from the Rosh's own words. I cited them in se'if katan 10, but will repeat them for clarity: 'They asked: May a person enter a permanent privy and urinate? Are we concerned that he may defecate, etc.? Although it was taught above that one who enters a privy removes his tefillin four cubits away and enters, he need not remove them because of entry but because he intends to relieve himself. Rav Hai Gaon changed the reading to “May a person enter a discreet privy?” because of this difficulty, but it is no difficulty, as I explained.' These are the two views recorded here in the Shulchan Aruch. According to the Rosh, it apparently follows necessarily that one who does not intend to relieve himself may enter. If entry itself were forbidden and the only distinction concerned whether he must move four cubits away, the Rosh's answer would be insufficient. Granted, without intent to defecate he need not move four cubits away, but entry would remain forbidden. Why, then, would the Gemara ask whether one may enter a permanent privy to urinate? Even without intending to urinate, entry alone would be forbidden. We must therefore say that according to the Rosh, one who does not intend to relieve himself may enter.

'And a new privy, etc.' In siman 83, seif 2: 'Know, etc.' According to Rashi, the reason is entry, as stated in seif 1 and as Magen Avraham writes in se'if katan 6, based upon Rashi's distinction between a privy constructed over a pit and one that is not. Rashi would therefore face the aforementioned objection of the Rosh and Rav Hai: why does the Gemara ask whether one may enter a permanent privy to urinate? Entry itself should make it forbidden. Rashi apparently had our reading, 'May one enter a permanent privy to urinate?' rather than Rav Hai's reading, 'a discreet privy.' We must therefore say that Rashi holds urination is different because it is a human necessity. This proves Magen Avraham's reasoning. Rashi holds that although mere entry normally requires a distance of four cubits, urination, because it is a human necessity, would permit entry were it not for the decree lest one defecate. The Rosh disagrees. He holds that if entry itself required four cubits, even the human necessity of urination would not permit entry, since entry would be so severe as to require four cubits. He therefore had to explain that the four-cubit distance is not caused by entry but by the intent to defecate. Once entry itself does not require four cubits but is merely prohibited, logic permits distinguishing urination as a human necessity: entry would be permitted were it not for the decree lest he defecate. When the Rosh answers, 'He need not remove them because of entry, etc.,' he means the removal mentioned in the cited baraita (removal four cubits away) is not caused by entry but by his intent to defecate. At first glance, the main answer is still absent from the Rosh's words. Granted that entry does not require four cubits, entry itself remains forbidden. Why, then, does the Gemara ask whether one may enter a permanent privy to urinate? We must answer that urination is a human necessity, yet that essential answer is absent. One can explain the Rosh's intent differently. Since the baraita's four-cubit requirement is not caused by entry but by intent to defecate, the baraita does not establish whether one who has no intent to defecate must remove the tefillin; perhaps even entry is permitted. The Gemara's question about entering to urinate therefore poses no difficulty. We independently infer that entry is forbidden even without intent to defecate because it is no better than entering a bathhouse, which is forbidden even without intent to bathe. We consequently distinguish urination as a human necessity. The Rosh did not mention the bathhouse objection, so he had no need to resolve it; perhaps the distinction based upon human necessity was obvious to him. Had entry required a four-cubit distance, its severity would have prevented this reasoning from permitting entry even for a human necessity; the Rosh therefore needed his stated answer. The Rosh came only to resolve Rav Hai's objection from the baraita requiring four cubits, and his answer sufficiently resolves that objection.
'But in a bathhouse it is forbidden.' This means when he enters to bathe, for bathing is also included among human necessities: it is one of the five afflictions of Yom Kippur, and Nedarim 81a indicates that according to one opinion even laundering is called a human necessity; all the more so washing the body. See there. The Tur therefore did not cite Rav Hai's view, because it is obvious that a privy is no better than a bathhouse. Although Rav Hai appears to require four cubits even when the person does not intend to defecate, making the privy more stringent than a bathhouse, and the Tur should seemingly still have cited his view, one can answer as follows. Rav Hai does not explicitly say that one who has no intent to defecate must move four cubits away. He may also hold that the baraita requires four cubits because the person intends to defecate, but maintain that even without such intent, entry itself is at least forbidden although four cubits are unnecessary. He therefore needed to emend the text to 'a discreet privy.' Although it is very strained to interpret Rav Hai this way, the opposite is not explicit. The Rosh, by contrast, explicitly shows that one who has no intent to defecate need not move four cubits away and has the same rule as one entering a bathhouse. The Tur therefore omitted Rav Hai's view. See Magen Avraham, se'if katan 14, for another answer concerning the bathhouse.
