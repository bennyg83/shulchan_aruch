(siman katan 9) "And some say: even" etc. — it implies that per the Rosh, etc. And certainly from the language of the Shulchan Aruch there is no implication that one may say: in the view of "some say," even without relieving himself one must distance four amot. But per the first view, which is the Rosh's view, one need not distance four amot except when relieving himself; but entering — one may say that even per the first view it is forbidden even if he is not relieving himself. Rather, his intent is that from the Rosh's own words it implies so, and I already brought his words in siman katan 10. Nevertheless I will repeat to copy the Rosh's words to be lenient, and these are his words: "They asked: may a person enter a permanent bathroom and urinate — do we fear lest he defecate," etc. "And even though it was taught above: one who enters a bathroom removes his tefillin at a distance of four amot and enters — this is not because of entering that he must remove them, but because he wishes to relieve himself. And from this difficulty Rav Hai Gaon would read: may a person enter a modest bathroom," etc. "And it is no difficulty, as I explained," end quote. And these are the two views brought here in the Shulchan Aruch. If so, it appears that per the Rosh one must say that if he does not wish to defecate, entry is permitted. For if one thought that entry is forbidden and the distinction is not between one who wishes to defecate or not, but only whether he must distance four amot — then the Rosh's answer is insufficient, "not because of entering," etc. — namely that when he has no intent to defecate he need not distance four amot; nevertheless entry is forbidden. And what is being asked: if it is permitted to enter a permanent bathroom to urinate? One could answer: even if he does not wish to urinate, entry alone is also forbidden. Rather, one must say that per the Rosh, when he does not wish to relieve himself, entry is permitted.

A new bathhouse, as explained in siman 84.

And "a new bathroom" etc. — in siman 83 seif 2 you will know, etc. And according to Rashi the reason is because of entering, as stated in seif 1, and as the Magen Avraham stated in siman katan 6: from Rashi's distinguishing in a bathroom whether it is in a pit or not, Rashi's difficulty should arise from the Rosh's and Rav Hai's difficulty above: what is being asked — if it is permitted to enter a permanent bathroom to urinate, one could answer it is forbidden because of entering. And Rashi appears to have had before him the text as we have: may one enter a permanent bathroom to urinate — not a modest bathroom as Rav Hai emended. Therefore one must say Rashi holds that for urinating it is preferable since it is a person's life — thus the Magen Avraham's view is proven. Rather, Rashi holds that although because of entering one must distance four amot, nevertheless for urinating, since it is a person's life, were it not that we decreed lest he defecate, entry would be permitted; and in this the Rosh disagrees. He holds it is impossible that because of entering one must keep four amot even for urinating — although it is a person's life we would not permit entry, since entering is so severe that four amot are required. Therefore he had to say the reason for distancing four amot is not because of entering but because he wishes to relieve himself. And since because of entering one need not distance four amot, only entry into it is forbidden — one may well distinguish by logic that for urinating, since it is a person's life, entry would be permitted even into it, were it not that we decreed lest he defecate. And what the Rosh stated in his answer, "not because of entering one must remove them," means: the removal mentioned in the baraita he brought — at a distance of four amot — is not because of entering but because he wishes to defecate. And although at first glance the main leniency is missing from the Rosh's words — namely that because of entering one need not distance four amot, nevertheless entry is surely forbidden — if so, what is being asked: may one enter a permanent bathroom to urinate? One must say: since it is his life, etc., and this is the main leniency, and this is missing from the Rosh's words. However, one may say the Rosh's intent: since the reason for distancing four amot in the baraita is not because of entering but because he wishes to defecate, it is still not clear from the baraita whether, if he does not wish to defecate, he must remove them; and possibly entry is also permitted. If so, there is no longer a question on what is asked — whether entry to urinate is permitted — except that we compel that entry is forbidden even if he does not wish to defecate, for it is not preferable to a bathhouse (entry is forbidden even if he does not intend to bathe). Therefore we must distinguish that urinating is different, for it is a person's life; and this difficulty from the bathhouse the Rosh did not mention, therefore he had no need to resolve it. And possibly it was obvious to the Rosh this distinction regarding what is one's life (except that if one had to distance four amot because of entering, since entering is so severe, the logic that it is a person's life would not suffice for the Rosh to permit even entry; therefore his answer was needed as above). And the Rosh did not come to resolve anything but Rav Hai's difficulty from the baraita that teaches one must distance four amot; and on this the Rosh's answer suffices.

But in a bathhouse it is forbidden — meaning when he enters to bathe; for bathing too is included in a person's life, as it is among the five afflictions on Yom Kippur, as explained in Nedarim 81a — even laundering is called "his life" according to one opinion; all the more so bathing the body, on the surface. Therefore the Tur did not bring Rav Hai's view, for it is an obvious matter that it is not preferable to a bathhouse. And although according to Rav Hai it appears that even if he does not wish to defecate he must keep four amot and it is stricter than a bathhouse — nevertheless the Tur should have brought Rav Hai's view. One may say: since it is not stated explicitly in Rav Hai's words that even if he has no intent to defecate he must keep four amot, it is also possible he holds that which the baraita required distancing four amot is because he wishes to defecate; but he holds that according to this view, even if he does not wish to defecate, nevertheless entry into it is forbidden, even though he need not distance four amot. Therefore he had to emend "modest bathroom," even though it is very strained to interpret Rav Hai's words so; nevertheless, since the opposite is not stated explicitly, and per the Rosh it is explicit that one need not distance four amot when he has no intent to defecate, and his law is like a bathhouse — therefore the Tur omitted Rav Hai's view. And see what the Magen Avraham wrote in siman katan 14, another answer regarding the bathhouse.
