He should not begin any work. It is prohibited from half an hour before the time, lest he become absorbed. Magen Avraham.
And he should not eat. Tasting is permitted. Maharil and the later authorities agree. See Magen Avraham, siman 235, seif katan 4, and siman 271, seif katan 10.
Even if he has a fixed time for study. Maharil writes in the laws of Pesach, siman 192, that the same applies to Maariv. One who did not pray with the congregation may not perform work or study before praying, as stated in the first chapter of Berachos. Bach objects that Berachos states the opposite: A person returns from the field in the evening, enters the study hall, and if accustomed to read Scripture, reads; if accustomed to study Mishnah, studies; then recites Shema and prays. This shows that Torah study is permitted and was not considered a concern of negligence. Likewise, in the activities listed in the first chapter of Shabbos as prohibited before Minchah, Torah study is not included. Bach and Taz offer various distinctions. Other distinctions are also possible. The primary answer, however, appears simple. Work, eating, and similar activities are prohibited from half an hour before the inspection or prayer lest one continue too long. Torah study is different. Otherwise, a person would sit idle every day before prayer, although the Torah says, “You shall meditate upon it day and night.” Several tannaim and amoraim whose Torah was their occupation would even omit prayer entirely, until it became necessary to say that the time for Torah is distinct from the time for prayer. Before the time of prayer, therefore, one should not cease learning. On the contrary, the Sages said that one should rise to pray from a matter of halachah. The passage describing a person returning from the field also concerns the period before the time of prayer, as Talmidei Rabbeinu Yonah explain, cited by Bach and Magen Avraham here and in siman 252. The same applies to bedikas chametz. One should not stop Torah study before the time for the inspection begins, as Mahariv implies by equating the two. This is also explicit in the Gemara and Rashi at the beginning of Pesachim, and it is the view implied by the other poskim. Rambam, chapter 2 of Hilchos Chametz, and Semak may appear to prohibit even beforehand, but after examination they may also be interpreted in this manner. This appears the primary practical ruling, unlike the later authorities. See what I wrote in seif katan 1 regarding Beit Yosef’s view.
He should not study. The later authorities cite Responsa Rav Avraham ben Chaim: Those who deliver a halachic teaching in the synagogue after prayer may study, because they will certainly afterward go home. This may also answer Bach’s difficulty from the first chapter of Berachos. Magen Avraham writes that analytical study is forbidden even outside one’s home, because one may become absorbed.
Until he has inspected. Regarding one who has not yet prayed or recited Shema, see Bach’s discussion of the differing opinions. Magen Avraham concludes that because the time for inspection is before the stars emerge, one should inspect first and then pray, unless he regularly prays with a minyan in the synagogue, in which case he should pray first. He follows Bach’s view that the inspection begins before the stars emerge. According to what I wrote above, however, the primary time for inspection is after the stars emerge. One who regularly recites Shema and prays at the proper time should not change his practice, because when a frequent and an infrequent mitzvah coincide, the frequent mitzvah takes precedence. Although Rosh and Maharil imply somewhat that one should do nothing before the inspection, their statement is not literal regarding prayer. They were not discussing this question.
To study while it is still daytime. Meaning that he began permissibly, as Beit Yosef writes. This is unlike Magen Avraham, who explains that he began during the prohibited half hour before night. His words are rejected according to what I wrote above. The language implies that if he began during the prohibited period, all opinions require him to stop. It also appears that this dispute concerns Torah study specifically. Regarding work and other activities, all agree that he must stop. This requires study. In siman 232, regarding activities forbidden before Minchah, if he began even improperly he need not stop. Bedikas chametz is Rabbinic, and certainly where he began permissibly he does not interrupt even for the Torah obligation of Shema, as explained in siman 235. This is even more difficult according to the alternative opinion cited by Rama, which is Tur’s view. The later authorities labor to draw several strained distinctions. In my opinion there is no difficulty. Torah study is different because it has no fixed endpoint. One is meant to fulfill “You shall meditate upon it day and night.” There is therefore greater concern that the person will continue for a long time. Even if he began, Tur requires him to stop, because in any event he will eventually interrupt to avoid missing the time for prayer or inspection. It makes little difference whether he stops now or later. Work and similar activities have a natural endpoint. Even if they continue for some time, the Sages did not trouble him to stop midway merely out of concern that he might continue, because he may finish and still have time to pray, as explained in siman 232. This also answers Baal HaMaor’s challenge in the first chapter of Shabbos concerning the statement, “People like us interrupt Torah study for Shema and prayer.” He asks that even regarding ordinary work the rule is that once one began, one need not interrupt. Baal HaMaor answers that the case concerns insufficient time. According to my explanation, there is no difficulty. Torah study has no endpoint, and in any event one must eventually stop to avoid missing prayer. It makes no difference whether he stops now or later. For the same reason, no concluding blessing is recited after Torah study, as Beit Yosef writes in siman 46. There is some support for this distinction from Shabbos 11. The Gemara asks: If the first clause already taught that once they began they do not interrupt, why repeat it? It answers that the latter clause refers to Torah study, from which one does not interrupt. The difficulty remains that this is a progression from the lesser case to the greater. If one need not interrupt ordinary activities for prayer, all the more so should one not interrupt Torah study. Even according to Baal HaMaor, who interprets Torah study as not interrupted despite insufficient time to pray, the essential qualification is missing from the text. According to what I wrote, the passage is clear. Only regarding Torah study is there a dispute whether one interrupts. Regarding work and eating, once one began, one need not interrupt.
