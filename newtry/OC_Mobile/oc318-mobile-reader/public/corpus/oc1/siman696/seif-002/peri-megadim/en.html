And there are those who say. See Taz. At first glance, from the Tur, who brought the words of Maharam of Rothenburg, "but on Purim night, after he prays Maariv at home, he goes to the synagogue to hear the Megillah," one can say that specifically Maariv is prayed somewhat before nightfall, which is not so for Shacharis, when he goes to the synagogue to pray, for he holds that there is no mourning on Purim. See Eliyah Rabbah, s.k. 5. But the Rosh brought the language of Maharam, which the Beit Yosef brought: there is no mourning on Purim, and after he prayed Maariv at home he goes to the synagogue for the Megillah, for the service of the Megillah overrides it, all the more so D'Rabbanan mourning. This implies that the Megillah overrides it, but prayer, even Shacharis, is at home. It also implies that even if he has a minyan at home, public reading is preferable, like the Temple service where thirteen kohanim merited roles in the daily offering, yet public performance is preferable. See Magen Avraham, s.k. 6, and with Hashem's help it will be explained there. From here it follows that private matters are observed on Purim, even not leaving the doorway of his house at all for optional matters; and according to the Taz, even for prayer he does not go out, only public mourning such as removing shoes and the like is not observed on Purim. It is also difficult according to the opinion that a mourner may leave the doorway of his house for a mitzvah matter, to pray with ten or for Kiddush Levanah; for according to the Mechaber, seif 5, it implies that even if he has no minyan at home he does not go out. The Rav, who ruled that he does go out, did so because of publicizing the miracle, and because there is no mourning on Purim; but otherwise, no. See Yoreh Deah 393, Eliyah Rabbah 131, and siman 425 regarding Kiddush Levanah, and this requires study. One should not call the mourner to the Torah, for public mourning is not observed on Purim. See siman 548.
