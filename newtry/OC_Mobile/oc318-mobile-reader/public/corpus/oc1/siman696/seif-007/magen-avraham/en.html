A positive commandment of an individual. And some say that this is so even for a positive commandment of the public, as written in siman 548, seif 3.
And all the more so he is obligated. The words of the Rema require great analysis. Granted, regarding meat and wine, it is permitted because of the joy of Purim. But regarding Krias Shema and davening, the reason he is exempt is so that he will be free for the needs of the deceased. If so, what difference is there between Purim and weekdays? Although one could say that he holds that at night he is obligated in Krias Shema, for its law is like the second day of Yom Tov, as written in siman 548, seif 5, nevertheless it remains difficult: how can this be derived all the more so from meat and wine? What connection is there between them? This requires analysis.
That this is specifically at night. It requires analysis why he should be permitted meat and wine at night, since there is no obligation at all to eat at night, as written at the beginning of siman 695. If so, how did he write “a positive commandment of the public,” and so on? There is an additional difficulty, for in Yoreh De'ah, siman 341, he ruled that even on Shabbos, if one wishes not to eat meat, he has permission to do so, as Talmidei Rabbeinu Yonah wrote: he is not obligated to eat meat and wine, and so on; and what they forbade on a weekday is because if he is drawn after pleasures he will be distracted from burying the deceased. See siman 552, seif 10, that when he refrains because of mourning it is forbidden. If so, there is no positive commandment at all, even by day. Perhaps one can say that Purim is different, for joy is written regarding it, and there is no joy except with meat. Nevertheless, it is difficult, for regarding Yom Tov as well joy is written, yet there is no obligation to eat meat except when the Beis HaMikdash stands; see siman 529. Therefore it appears to me that at night he should at any rate not eat meat, since even otherwise there are those who refrain, as written in siman 695, seif 2 in the gloss. And that which he wrote, “and it appears to me, specifically at night,” refers to the Megillah.
The burial of his deceased comes first. See what I wrote in siman 687, that there are those who disagree, and the custom is to bury him after leaving the beis haknesses. It is difficult: how is he obligated in reading the Megillah, since he is exempt from all mitzvos? One can answer that the reason he is exempt is so that he should be occupied with the needs of the deceased, but here it is forbidden to bury him until he first reads. If so, he is obligated even in davening and Krias Shema, similar to Pesach, where he is obligated even though he is an onen, as the Rosh wrote in the name of the Maharam. The Mordechai wrote that they even suspend the Avodah in order to hear the Megillah reading with the community, all the more so mourning; if so, the same applies to an onen. Furthermore, there is no prohibition for an onen to go to the beis haknesses, as written in Yoreh De'ah, siman 341. Nevertheless, it appears to me that he is exempt from davening and Krias Shema. It also appears to me that even though he heard the Megillah with the community, he should read it a second time after the deceased is buried, for it is an explicit Talmudic teaching that an onen is exempt from all mitzvos. Although we say that Megillah reading precedes, that is with regard to someone else; but the onen is exempt until he buries him. However, according to what I wrote in siman 71, that if he wishes to be stringent he is permitted, he fulfills his obligation with what he heard in the community, for the essential law is that Megillah reading precedes the deceased, and if so he is obligated in Megillah reading. Nevertheless, it appears to me that he should not put on tefillin, for they are called glory; and see the beginning of siman 71.
For it is no better than Shabbos and Yom Tov. That is to say, on the second day of Yom Tov, when he buries him, and on Shabbos, when he waits near the boundary for the sake of burial, he is exempt from all of them; all the more so here. See what I wrote above.
