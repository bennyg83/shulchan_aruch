Seif 9. It is forbidden to say ‘Shema, Shema’ twice, and so forth. Note: In the Selichos for the second day, the text was instituted to read, ‘Nevertheless, we are to Him, and our eyes are to Him.’ In my humble opinion, this is incorrect. The Mishnah in Sukkah, chapter 5, states that they would say, ‘We are to Him, and our eyes are to Him.’ The Gemara there, on page 53, asks that one who says ‘Shema, Shema’ is silenced, and answers that they would say, ‘We are to Him, and our eyes hope toward Him.’ Rashi explains that because they mention Him twice regarding two different matters, rather than repeating the same matter twice, there is no concern. This implies that otherwise it would be forbidden to say the expression twice. One cannot say that the Mishnah places the two phrases together, ‘We are to Him, and to Him are our eyes,’ and that this alone is forbidden, whereas in our text the words ‘and our eyes’ interrupt between them and there is therefore no concern. This is untenable. First, there is no logic to such a distinction, for according to Rashi the matter depends on whether the two mentions refer to two different matters. What difference is there whether one says ‘to Him’ immediately afterward or at the end? Ultimately, the word ‘to Him’ is said once with reference to ‘we’ and once with reference to ‘our eyes.’ If this counts as two different matters, there is no concern even when the two expressions are adjacent; and if it does not count as two different matters, why should inserting the words ‘and our eyes’ beforehand help? Furthermore, if the interruption were sufficient, why would the Gemara add the longer phrase, ‘and our eyes hope toward Him’? It could have answered briefly that the order must be reversed: ‘We are to Him, and our eyes are to Him.’ It is therefore clear that even this does not help unless the language explicitly states that the eyes are hoping. Accordingly, our present version is comparable to saying ‘Shema, Shema,’ and is improper. It should therefore be corrected so that Hashem’s Name is said only once: ‘Nevertheless, we and our eyes are to Him.’ This appears correct to me. Examine the matter carefully. Seif 13: After the first verse one must say, ‘Blessed is the Name of His glorious kingdom forever and ever,’ and so forth. Note: The Tzelach, in the fourth chapter of Pesachim, sought to justify those who say this phrase aloud. He explained that when the Gemara says, ‘Should we say it? Moshe did not say it,’ its concern is only that the phrase would constitute an interruption between the sections. Since Moshe did not say it, we should not introduce such an interruption ourselves, and it must therefore be recited quietly. Outside the middle of a section, however, it may be said aloud. Although his words initially appear plausible, they are not correct in practice, because on Yom Kippur the accepted custom is to say it aloud in the middle of Krias Shema. If the Gemara’s concern relates to the phrase itself, namely, that it should not be said aloud because it is a lofty praise appropriate only for the ministering angels, the custom on Yom Kippur is understandable, since on Yom Kippur we are like the ministering angels and may therefore recite it aloud. But if the concern is only that it interrupts between the sections, what difference is there between the rest of the year and Yom Kippur? What connection does our resemblance to the ministering angels have to interrupting between the sections? Moreover, when the Gemara says, ‘Should we not say it? Yaakov said it,’ where is it demonstrated that Yaakov said it between the sections? The primary explanation is therefore certainly the straightforward one. Accordingly, since our custom is to say it aloud during Krias Shema on Yom Kippur, it is improper to say it aloud at any time during the rest of the year, even outside Krias Shema. Otherwise, one would be adopting two contradictory leniencies. This appears correct to me. Examine the matter carefully.
