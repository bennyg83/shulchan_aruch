One should teach them, etc. But the Bach wrote that no scholar or great person should abolish the custom in places where they practiced this way, for only when an individual says it before the congregation is it forbidden; but when the entire congregation says it to arouse intent, it is permitted. This is difficult, for the Talmud in Sukkah 45 challenges the Mishnah where they said, 'we are to Him and to Him are our eyes': is this so, but whoever says Shema, Shema, etc.? There they said it in public. The Levush raised this. One must say that there it is different, since they were doing it merely as song and praise and not to arouse intent. Later, Sefer Magen Avraham was printed, and he rejected the Bach entirely, writing that this Talmud had escaped the Bach, and it is not so. It also appears to my humble opinion that there is some proof for the Bach from Sukkah 45, which says that one says, 'to Him we give thanks and to You we praise.' At first glance this is difficult, for it is as though he said Shema, Shema; rather, it is different in public. Since Rashi there explains that 'modim' is not language of thanksgiving but of acknowledgment that we acknowledge Him and do not deny Him, perhaps it is like accepting the yoke of the kingdom of Heaven. It also appears to me to answer that since he interrupts with 'to You we praise,' it is not like saying Shema, Shema one immediately after the other. Similarly the Shelah and Nachalas Tzvi wrote that those who have the custom to say Shema when the shaliach tzibbur says Vaya'avor are not included in this concern, since their saying is not immediate and the confession always interrupts. I saw in Sefer Ta'avah La'einayim that he answers that Rashi corrected this by writing that 'modim' is not language of thanksgiving, etc.; meaning that 'modim' has two meanings, either thanksgiving or acknowledgement, and if so the first is one meaning and the second another, and since each is a different matter there is no concern in the repetition. This is astonishing, for perforce even the first is not language of thanksgiving, because then the Talmud's question would remain, that he would be associating the Name of Heaven with the altar. It appears to me that for this reason Rashi corrected it and explained that it is not language of thanksgiving. There is more to wonder about in this matter, but the intent of my book is practical halacha. Regarding saying Shema Yisrael when the shaliach tzibbur says Vaya'avor, the Shelah, page 213, concludes that it has a resolution and is a good custom in my eyes, because echad has the gematria of thirteen, and it is fitting to mention it with the Thirteen Attributes. But I saw in Lechem Chamudos, page 41: it appears to me that it is included in 'we silence him,' or at least it is improper and should be abolished. I already wrote that Nachalas Tzvi wrote like the Shelah, and it is astonishing that he did not mention the words of Lechem Chamudos. In any case it appears that one should not protest against those who do so. It also appears that the first time, certainly every person should practice saying it. Regarding those who say 'Baruch shem kevod malchuso le'olam va'ed' three times on Yom Kippur, the Taz wrote that since it refers to Shema Yisrael, which he said once, there is no room for error.
