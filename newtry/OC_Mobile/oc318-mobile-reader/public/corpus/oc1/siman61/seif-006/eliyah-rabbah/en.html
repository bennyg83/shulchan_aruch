One must, etc. The Levush writes: one must pause between every word. This is from the Tur and Perishah. It appears that this means that one should not say the word hurriedly, but slowly, as one counts coins. The requirement to leave space between adjoining words is a different matter: one should not say 'al levavecha' in one breath, but 'al' in one breath and 'levavecha' in its own breath; this is the space he leaves between breaths. But the other words may be said in one breath, provided he pauses between each word. See what I wrote above, siman 5.
In heaven and on earth, etc. The language of the Semak is that one should think that He is unique in the seven firmaments and on the one earth, making eight. Rabbi David Abudraham wrote that the three letters of echad include three worlds: one should intend with the alef to the Holy One, blessed is He, Who is first; with the ches to the eight spheres; and with the dalet to the four elements upon which this world is founded. Etz Chaim wrote: when saying 'Hashem Elokeinu Hashem echad,' one should have intent for the four letters of the Name of Havayah; when saying 'Baruch shem kevod malchuso,' one should have intent for the four letters of the Name Adnus; and when saying 'le'olam va'ed,' one should have intent for the unified Name.
Alludes to the hump, etc. Meaning that He lives in the height of the world; but one should not lengthen the ches excessively.
And he should lengthen the dalet, etc. The Bach explained that when he emphasizes the dalet, he should lengthen the breath that emerges from his mouth long enough to think, etc.; not like those who lengthen the dalet through the tzeirei vowel beneath the dalet and thereby interrupt the intent.
To tilt the head, etc. I found that the tilting should be east, north, west, south, but not east, west, south, north. The Levush writes that one should lengthen the alef a little and intend that He is one, but should not lengthen it so that it sounds like 'ei chad,' meaning, G-d forbid, 'not one.' It also appears to me that there is another reason: 'ei' can mean 'woe,' as in the verse 'woe to the one who falls.' Migdal Oz wrote: I received a tradition that the alef is almost snatched, with little contemplation upon it, and one lengthens the ches one third and the dalet two thirds.
