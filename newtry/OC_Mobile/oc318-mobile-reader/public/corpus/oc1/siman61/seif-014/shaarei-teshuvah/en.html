“In this.” See Baer Heitev and Sefer Shtei Yadot, which writes that in most communities the custom is to read Keriat Shema with the cantillation marks, but people do not pause at the end of a verse as one who reads from the Torah does. On the contrary, one must be even more careful to pause at the end of a verse than to observe the melody of the cantillation. One should therefore take care with the mark tircha and not read carelessly when reaching the phrase “Whoever approaches, approaches the Mishkan of Hashem; will he die?” lest the words be read as a blasphemous statement. At the end of the paragraph of tzitzit, in “I am Hashem your God; truth,” the word “I” bears a tircha. I will explain why. David said, “Please, Hashem, for I am Your servant,” etc. His saying “I am Your servant” does not exclude the possibility that another person is also His servant; it means only that I am Your servant, while others have likewise been found to be His servants. Similarly, “I am Your servant, the son of Your maidservant,” does not mean that Shlomo was not called Your servant. Thus, if one says “I am Hashem your God” without the tircha, it can sound like “I am Hashem your God,” while another could perhaps also be your god. When one reads “I” with the tircha, however, it means: “I, and no other,” etc. It does not seem far-fetched to me that the Holy One, blessed is He, originally concluded this paragraph in this manner in order to allude to His unity at the end of Keriat Shema, so that one concludes with the same theme with which one began.
