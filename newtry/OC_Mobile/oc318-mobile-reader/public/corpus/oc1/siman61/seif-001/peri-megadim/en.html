See Taz, and see siman 2, seif katan 2, and below, siman 91, seif 3, that even placing one's hand upon his head, where there is no issue of following their statutes, is nevertheless insufficient to permit uttering Hashem's name. From the Midrash one may say that there is no Torah prohibition, but the Sages prohibited it. Regarding the Mechaber's statement that one should read with intent, Levush explains that this means intent of the heart, understanding what he says, not intent to fulfill the mitzvah. These are two separate requirements. See siman 60, Magen Avraham, seif katan 4, and siman 63, at the end of the passage beginning "And therefore." They compared it to a royal proclamation because one must understand what is written in it, rather than merely read it. See Perishah. This is what Rav Amram added, as quoted by Tur: "and it should be new," with a dalet, not "and anew," with a reish. First, one must understand what is written in it. From then onward, he should not read hurriedly merely to discharge the obligation of recitation, but should regard it each day as new and understand what he says. See siman 62, Magen Avraham, seif katan 1. At the very least, one must understand the first verse even when reciting in the sacred language, and, with Hashem's help, this will be explained there. A baal keri, despite the attendant indignity, recites Krias Shema, as stated in siman 88. The law concerning an intoxicated person is explained in siman 99, seif 1, in the gloss of Peri Chadash.
