With intent. The Eliyah Rabbah brought in the name of the Kol Bo, and it is in the Yerushalmi at the end of the first chapter of Berachos, that the Ten Commandments are alluded to in Shema. In 'Hashem Elokeinu' is alluded 'I am Hashem your G-d.' In 'Hashem echad' is the command 'You shall have no other gods.' In the verse 've'ahavta' is alluded 'You shall not take the Name in vain,' for one who loves the King does not swear falsely in His Name. In 'u'chesavtam' is alluded 'You shall not covet,' for it says 'your house,' and not your fellow's house. In 've'asafta deganecha' is alluded 'You shall not steal,' meaning your grain, and not that you gather your fellow's grain. In 'va'avadtem meheirah' is alluded 'You shall not murder,' for one who kills is killed. In 'lema'an yirbu yemeichem' is alluded honoring your father and mother. In 'velo sasuru, etc., and after your eyes' is alluded 'You shall not commit adultery.' In 'lema'an tizkeru, etc.' is alluded 'You shall remember Shabbos,' which is equal to all the mitzvos. In 'va'asisem es kol mitzvosai' is alluded all the other mitzvos.
With awe and fear. It appears that this awe and fear is in the following manner: when he reads Shema, he should intend to accept upon himself the yoke of the kingdom of Heaven, to be killed for the sanctification of the unique Name. This is the meaning of 'with all your soul,' even if He takes your soul. Regarding this the verse says, 'for Your sake we are killed all the day.' With this intent he will read it with awe, fear, trembling, and shuddering.
With trembling and shuddering. The Tur wrote in the name of Rav Amram that a person should make Shema every time he reads it like a new royal decree, that is, a written command of the king to the people of his country. He should think in his heart: if a flesh-and-blood king sent a new royal decree, certainly all the people of the country would read it with awe and fear, trembling and shuddering; all the more so Shema, which is the decree of the King of kings, the Holy One, blessed is He, that each person must read it with awe and fear, trembling and shuddering. The Perishah wrote that it is compared to a royal decree, a written command of the king, to teach that one should not read Shema snatched, hurriedly, and with the words jumbled together, but slowly, word by word, and with pause between one matter and another, like a person reading the king's command, who reads each command slowly by itself in order to understand it.
