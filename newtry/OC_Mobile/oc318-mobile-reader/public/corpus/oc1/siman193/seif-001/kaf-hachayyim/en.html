1) [Seif 1] Two who ate together, although one can discharge the other with the blessing of HaMotzi, etc. The distinction between the initial and concluding blessings is that at the beginning of the meal, when they recline and establish themselves together, they intend to join. Therefore, each fulfills his obligation through the other's blessing. At the end of the meal, however, the established framework has ended and they separate from one another. Therefore, each recites the blessing for himself. R' Yosef explains in the name of Rashbam that the reason is that Birkas HaMazon is of Torah origin, whereas the initial blessing is rabbinic, and the Sages were not as stringent about it, so one may fulfill his obligation through another's blessing. Rosh, third chapter of Berachos. Tosafos, Mordechai, Rabbeinu Yonah, and Beis Yosef write the same. Levush writes that we do not follow the latter reason, because according to it, one person could discharge another with every blessing over fruit, both initial and concluding, since they are all rabbinic. Rather, the first reason is primary: specifically bread has an established framework, so if they established themselves together one may discharge another, but this does not apply to other types, neither for the initial nor concluding blessing; see there. Perishah, paragraph 1, cites this. Taz, paragraph 1, however, writes that even according to the latter reason distinguishing Torah and rabbinic law, fruit must be treated separately. Although its blessing is rabbinic, each person must recite it for himself, as stated in the gloss to siman 213; see there. All this follows Rema, of blessed memory, in the gloss to siman 213, seif 1, who writes that with fruit, even for the initial blessing, each recites for himself, because sitting together is ineffective for one to discharge another. According to Maran, of blessed memory, there, however, sitting together is effective for one person to discharge another with the initial blessing even over fruit; see there. See above, siman 167, seif 11, and our words there, paragraphs 79 and 80, and understand this.
1) There: The second does not know how, etc. If he does not know how to recite the blessing in Lashon HaKodesh but knows how to recite it in another language, while he understands Lashon HaKodesh, they may not divide. Rather, the one who knows recites it in Lashon HaKodesh and the one who does not know fulfills his obligation by listening. Bach; Eshel Avraham, paragraph 2.
2) There: If he understands Lashon HaKodesh, etc. The Tur writes that women therefore do not fulfill their obligation by listening if they do not understand. Rashi, of blessed memory, however, writes that one fulfills his obligation even if he does not understand the language, and brings proof from the Mishnah in Megillah that one who speaks a foreign language and hears the Megillah in Ashuris fulfills his obligation. Rosh, of blessed memory, in the third chapter of Berachos rejects this proof because publicizing the miracle is different, as the Gemara states there; see there. Beis Yosef cites this and writes that Tosafos, Rabbeinu Yonah, and Mordechai rule likewise. Rema, of blessed memory, writes in Darkei Moshe, paragraph 1, that women should therefore preferably recite the blessing themselves. Nevertheless, the present custom follows Rashi, and he never saw anyone protest; see there. Bach likewise writes that the custom follows Rashi: regarding every obligation incumbent upon women, whether prayer, Megillah, or Birkas HaMazon, they rely upon listening to others who intend to discharge the women as well, although the women understand nothing. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, paragraph 2; Olas Tamid, paragraph 2; Magen Avraham, paragraph 2; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2. Magen Avraham writes there that it is nevertheless proper for them to repeat every word with the person reciting Kiddush or the blessing, because it is impossible to concentrate and listen properly, as stated in siman 183, seif 7; see there. It appears that this applies specifically when they can repeat after the person reciting the blessing and he waits for them to say it with him. If this is impossible, one may rely upon Rashi even initially, because this is preferable to their not reciting a blessing at all, as Taz, paragraph 2, writes; see there. If they know how to recite the blessing or prayer, it is obvious that if they do not understand they should recite it themselves and not rely upon others, thereby satisfying all opinions. Even if they understand Lashon HaKodesh, it is preferable that they recite the blessing themselves, as stated above in siman 183, seif 7, and in our words there, paragraph 37; see there.
3) There: He must direct his intent, etc. The Shulchan Aruch below, at the end of siman 213, rules likewise; see there.
4) There: If he does not understand, etc. This applies specifically to listening: one who does not understand does not fulfill his obligation. A person who himself recites the blessing, however, fulfills his obligation even if he does not understand what he articulates. Levush; Olas Tamid there; Shulchan Gavoah, paragraph 1; Shaarei Efraim, siman 13. We wrote the same above in siman 124, paragraph 4; see there. Although Rosh in the third chapter of Berachos and Rabbeinu Yonah there imply that even one who articulates the words must understand them, one may say that this applies initially, but when no alternative is possible he fulfills his obligation; see there. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 2, however, distinguishes as follows: when reciting in Lashon HaKodesh, one fulfills his obligation even without understanding the language; when listening, which requires intent, one must specifically understand Lashon HaKodesh; and in any other language, even the person reciting must understand it. See there. Tosafos in Sotah 32a appear to agree; see there. We wrote the same above in siman 62, paragraph 3, and siman 101, paragraph 16; see there.
5) There: If he does not understand, etc. This implies that in every case, if he does not understand, he does not fulfill his obligation by listening, even with three, where one discharges two, or with ten. This is also implied by Beis Yosef, siman 124; see there. Birchas Avraham, however, writes that this applies specifically when one individual discharges another individual. When one individual discharges a group, such as a prayer leader discharging a congregation or the leader of a Zimun discharging a group of three or ten, the individual discharges the group even if they do not understand; see there. Knesses HaGedolah, siman 62, Hagahos HaTur, Olas Tamid in this siman, paragraph 2, and Shulchan Gavoah, paragraph 1, cite this. One should rely upon this only to prevent them from entirely missing the blessing, as stated above in paragraph 2; see there.
6) There: For they all became obligated in Zimun. This means that although if one leaves, three will remain and make a Zimun, the one who leaves will not fulfill his obligation, because he too became obligated in Zimun. Magen Avraham, paragraph 3.
6) There: Six may divide, etc. This applies if none of them recited the blessing. If one already recited it for himself, they may not divide. Although siman 194, seif 1, states that two may make a Zimun with a third who already recited the blessing for himself, that applies after the fact, but initially they may not do so. This is certainly true if one of them already joined two others in Zimun. Likewise, the rule that twenty may divide applies only if none of them recited the blessing for himself or joined others in Zimun. Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 4.
