(6) At a time when the shepherds eat: that is to say, if it is the way of this householder to feed from it also his shepherds who are Jews, if so, when he kneaded and baked, he intends that it also be for human eating.
(7) One fulfills with it: for since his shepherds have a share in it, it stands with the presumption of being guarded for the sake of matzah; and even though presumably much mursan and waste is mixed into it, as is the way of dog-dough, even so one fulfills with it.
(8) And if not: that is to say, that the shepherds do not eat, and he kneads and bakes only for the need of the dogs; and in this case, even if it were clean bread, one does not fulfill with it, as he concludes with the reason.
(9) That this is not guarded for the sake of matzah: the wording is not precise, for in truth, since he had no thought at all of human eating, the name "bread" does not apply to it at all, and even regarding the blessing of Hamotzi; but because he is dealing with the matter of Pesach, he added a further reason, that for this as well one does not fulfill with it.
(10) Such are the words of the Rambam: the Rav abbreviated here, and the substance of his intent is that according to the Rambam it deals both with fine bread and with bread that has much mursan mixed into it, and the main distinction is whether he had in mind only for dogs or also for human eating. And the Rav comes to say that there is another rationale here that does not depend at all on his thought: that where much mursan is mixed into it and it is not fit for shepherds to eat from this, the name "bread" does not apply to it, and even if he explicitly had in mind also for the shepherds, his intent is nullified, for it is not the way of people to eat such bread. And conversely, if the bread is fine, even if he made them for dogs, the law of bread applies to it for all matters, and only regarding matzah one does not fulfill with it, because it is not guarded for the sake of matzah.
