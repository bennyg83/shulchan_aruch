To raise the tips, etc. The Shelah explains that one should bend the hand so that the fingertips are higher than the palm, rather than raising the entire hand upward. He further records a kabbalistic practice that after raising the hands and before reciting the blessing one should say the verse, 'Raise your hands in holiness and bless Hashem.' The Shulchan Aruch of Rabbi Yitzchak Luria writes that one should raise the hands before drying them, opposite the face. One should also intend to bring the joints of the arms inward toward the body, in accordance with the mystical teaching of 'all their backs toward the house,' referring to what are called elbows in the vernacular. He then recites the blessing.
And some say that if, etc. The Rama states this without qualification. The Levush properly presents it as 'some say,' because the later view that if one washed as far as the arm there is no need to raise the hands follows the reasoning that the purpose of raising is to prevent water beyond the joint from becoming tamei. According to that reason, lowering the hands does not help, since water may run from the palm and become tamei. In practical law, it appears to me that one must specifically raise the hands. Although I will explain that there are dissenting opinions, the principal reason is that most poskim hold that water beyond the joint does not itself become tamei. Rather, the concern is that water that became tamei within the required area may flow outward and then return, as the Levush states in his first explanation. Even according to this reasoning, however, one may say that although water outside the joint is not initially tamei, if it subsequently returns within the joint it can render the hand tamei and invalidate water that remained there. The second waters cannot purify such returned water, because it came from a place where washing had not been performed. Maharshal writes this in his commentary to Semag, positive commandment 27, and in siman 36 he rules that raising specifically is required. The Bach goes even further and writes that if one lowered his fingers he did not fulfill the obligation and would be eating without netilat yadayim, because the water would not reach the tips of his fingers. According to this, it appears to me that even if one pours a full reviit at once he must raise the hands.
[Levush] They attached it as a Scriptural support, etc. Maharshal writes that this is surprising: why should one be required to raise the hands merely because of an asmachta? See the preceding seif katan, where the reason for raising is a matter of law.
And some disagree, etc. So appears to me to be the principal view, as will be explained.
Likewise one who immerses, etc. Lechem Chamudot, folio 226, expresses surprise at the Rama and his disciple Rabbi Mordechai Yaffe. Since they write that some disagree, referring to the Aruch's view in the Beit Yosef that water flowing outward can become tamei on the arm, they should also write that some require raising the hands even after immersion. Malbushei Yom Tov likewise leaves this as a difficulty. In the sharpness of his analysis, however, he was not precise. According to most poskim, water does not become tamei on the arm, and the reason for raising is that the first water that became tamei within the required area flows outward and returns. According to that reasoning one would obviously require raising even if the washing reached the arm. The Rashba rules that raising is unnecessary because he does not accept that concern; he worries only that water will become tamei in the palm and return to the fingers. This is clear from the Beit Yosef. Thus when the Rama and Levush write that some disagree, it is because most poskim agree with the Aruch on this result, not because they rely only on the Aruch's specific reasoning. Lechem Chamudot apparently follows the Beit Yosef's statement that according to the Aruch even one who immerses must raise his hands. I do not agree. If so, the Aruch would contradict the Tosefta. Rather, even according to the Aruch no raising is required after immersion. The rule that water flowing outward becomes tamei on the arm applies when one pours less than a reviit. If one pours a reviit, and certainly when one immerses, no tumah applies to that water at all. Just as it does not become tamei from the hand, it does not become tamei from the arm. This is obvious to me. It also seems that the Beit Yosef retracted this view in the Shulchan Aruch. Otherwise his rulings would contradict one another. He rules that when one washes up to the arm no raising is required, which shows that he accepts the view associated with the Aruch and the concern is only that water in the palm return to the fingers. Why then does he rule that one who immerses need not raise his hands, since water could flow beyond the fingers and return to render them tamei? If the case is that he immerses up to the arm, then even ordinary washing up to the arm would require no raising according to him. Clearly he retracted his earlier statement. I found support for this in Magen Avraham. I also found that Yam Shel Shlomo, end of siman 36, writes that according to all opinions and explanations, where one pours a full reviit at once or immerses the hands there is no need to raise them. Perhaps he intended the reasoning I have given.
[Levush] And perhaps the reason is, etc. The Beit Yosef writes regarding the Or Zarua that he sees no reason for this ruling, and the Levush therefore labored to supply one. Olat Tamid explains that since the third water comes to purify the water on the hands, we assume that just as the second water might have flowed beyond the joint, the third water too flows beyond the joint and purifies the second water. This is surprising, because water does not purify water that has gone beyond the joint, as explained in seif 1. Damesek Eliezer, folio 336, also labors to provide a reason, but his explanation is surprising; see there. In my humble opinion the Or Zarua follows Rashi, as is his usual method. Rashi holds that the second water can purify even what is beyond the required measure, but the concern is that perhaps the second water did not actually reach beyond that measure, as explained by the Beit Yosef. The Or Zarua therefore holds that if one pours a third time, we assume that between the second and third pourings the water certainly reached beyond the required measure. I found the same in the Derishah. According to us, however, who rule that water does not purify beyond the joint, raising is still required.
