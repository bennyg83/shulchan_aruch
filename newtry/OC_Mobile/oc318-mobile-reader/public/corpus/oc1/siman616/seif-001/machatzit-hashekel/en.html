(Se'if katan 1) To tell, etc. That he derives benefit, etc. Beis Yosef. See what the Tur wrote in the name of Rabbeinu Yehudah, that it is permitted to tell a non-Jew to wash an infant even with hot water heated that day, because an ordinary infant is considered sick regarding hot water, and for something involving no danger one may tell a non-Jew and he does it. End quote. About this the Beis Yosef wrote that it implies that a Jew himself may not wash them. Although feeding him is permitted for a Jew himself, washing is different, for if a Jew washes him, the adult who washes him also derives benefit. If so, the same applies to anointing: it is forbidden for a Jew to do it with his hands. But they are initially permitted, etc. It is stated in Yoma 78b: The Rabbis taught, children are permitted in all of them except wearing shoes. It asks: what is different about wearing shoes? People will say, adults did it for him, meaning adults put them on him, and adults are warned regarding minors not to do for them a forbidden act. But these other matters too, why are they permitted? People will say adults did it for them. It answers: regarding those, people will say they did it for them yesterday, meaning they washed or anointed them yesterday and they are still clean. But regarding shoes, one cannot say that they put them on yesterday and they are still on the child's feet, for necessarily he removed his shoes at night, since one should not sleep with shoes on his feet, for he tastes the taste of death.
And it asks: But it teaches that they are initially permitted. Rashi explained: if one comes to ask, we tell him, 'feed him and wash him,' and then it is well known that he did it today. End quote. It concludes: rather, those things that are not the child's growth, the Rabbis decreed regarding them; those that are the child's growth, the Rabbis did not decree. End quote of the Gemara. Necessarily the Talmud is not speaking through a non-Jew. First, the phrase 'initially permitted' does not imply that. Second, it is impossible that it speaks through a non-Jew, for what would the question have thought, that for this reason wearing shoes is forbidden because it was necessarily done for him today and people will assume adults did it for him? It is possible to attribute it to being done by a non-Jew. Rather, necessarily the Talmud is not speaking of a non-Jew. The initial thought was that it was permitted only when the child does it himself, and adults do not stop him; only the Sages were concerned that people would err and think adults did it for them, since the child himself has no understanding to do it alone. To this it responds that they are initially permitted, which implies even an adult may do it.
And the Bach wrote that it is permitted, etc. Meaning, Rabbeinu Yehudah did not intend to forbid washing by a Jew; rather, he mentioned a non-Jew in order to permit heating hot water today through a non-Jew.
And so it appears in Mordechai, chapter 2 of Beitzah, siman 680, and this is its language: A person may not heat hot water on Yom Tov, etc. Rabbeinu Yehudah wrote that it is permitted to wash a small infant, as stated in chapter 2 of Yoma, that children are permitted in washing and anointing on Yom Kippur, for anything that is the child's growth the Rabbis did not decree. One must say that they did not decree regarding a child to wash, but it is forbidden for an adult to perform kindling for the purpose of any washing.
For it is not equal for every soul, etc. End quote. It implies that Rabbeinu Yehudah permitted a Jew to heat hot water on Yom Tov for the need of an infant, and the same applies that on Yom Kippur it would at least be permitted to heat through a non-Jew. In this the Mordechai disagrees. But regarding the washing itself, which is permitted through a Jew, they do not disagree, and according to everyone it is permitted. Only this did the Bach write: if the non-Jew heated water today, then the washing should specifically be through the non-Jew; see there.
For it is a complete Mishnah in Shabbos chapter 19.
But even without milah it is permitted; meaning, according to the Bach, and likewise according to the Beis Yosef, who holds that the washing permitted for a child on Yom Kippur means with hot water. If so, why in Shabbos chapter 19 do we say specifically before the milah it is permitted? In every child it should be permitted.
The text should read: because it is because of affliction, no.
Therefore it is permitted for an adult to wash them, according to the simple meaning of the Talmud's wording, 'but they are initially permitted.' There is no concern that the adult derives benefit, since he does not intend for pleasure, as washing off mud and excrement was permitted at the beginning of siman 613.
But washing with hot water, etc. Even for an infant it is forbidden, etc. The Eliyah Rabbah asked: how can one say that the Gemara, which speaks through a Jew, is speaking of washing with cold water? It concludes there that wherever something is the child's growth, we do not decree, and washing is also the child's growth; it brings a proof from Abaye, who said, 'The growth of an infant is hot water for washing and oil for anointing.' Thus even hot water is permitted. In my humble opinion this is not difficult at all. First, in any case it brings a proper proof regarding anointing, that it is the child's growth. Also regarding washing, although hot water is more fully the child's growth, nevertheless the primary growth is the washing itself; therefore when it is with cold water, there is some growth, and the proof is brought properly also for cold washing.
As written in siman 328, se'if 17, that an ordinary infant is like a sick person who is not in danger. Therefore rabbinic prohibitions involving telling a non-Jew were permitted for him. This was the Tur's intent in what he wrote in the name of Rabbeinu Yehudah, as above: for this reason he mentioned a non-Jew, because he speaks of hot water, as he concluded, and this is forbidden through a Jew even on Shabbos, but telling a non-Jew to heat it is not forbidden. What the Tur wrote in the name of Rabbeinu Yehudah, 'even with hot water heated today,' means that it was heated for the need of the non-Jew.
In Eruvin.
Page 68, the incident of an infant whose hot water, which had been heated before Shabbos, spilled. It was permitted to heat through a non-Jew only to heat for the mother after childbirth, and the remainder would be taken for the infant. See there in Tosafos, and Tosafos in Gittin 8b.
And the words of Rabbi Yehudah, etc. These are the words of the Mordechai that I brought above, permitting heating on Yom Tov for the need of an infant, even by a Jew. For it is proven in Eruvin that on Shabbos it is forbidden through a non-Jew just as it is forbidden for an adult. The same applies on Yom Tov: since heating is forbidden through a Jew for the need of an adult, it is likewise forbidden for the need of a minor. It is also difficult regarding the Mordechai, who permits at least washing a child: since it is forbidden for an adult, why is it permitted for a child?
It is proven that this is the view of the Rema. For he brought there the words of the Mordechai mentioned above, that it is permitted to wash an infant with hot water, although on Shabbos it is forbidden except for the need of milah.
And nowadays, etc. For even during the week, sometimes infants are not washed for several days. If so, for us washing is not considered the infant's growth. So wrote the Magen Avraham above, siman 511, se'if katan 5. See what the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 331, se'if katan 9.
