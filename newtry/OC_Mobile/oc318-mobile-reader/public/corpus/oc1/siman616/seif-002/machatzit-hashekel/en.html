Seif katan 2, "They train," etc. See Tosafot. This appears in Tosafot Yeshanim and likewise in Terumat HaDeshen, siman 94. They ask about the Mishnah in Yoma 82, which states that minors are trained to fast for hours. Yet when a minor eats prohibited foods, beit din is not commanded to separate him. If they are not commanded even to separate him, how can there be a command to train him? Rabbeinu Yitzchak answers that the mitzvah of chinuch rests specifically upon the father, not upon another person. Therefore others are not commanded to separate the child. Tosafot then ask from the beginning of Sukkah, where Queen Helena sat in a sukkah with her 7 sons. This indicates that chinuch applies even where the father is absent. They answer that perhaps the children had a father who trained them in this mitzvah; and even if they did not, their mother trained them merely as a voluntary mitzvah practice. The same appears in Terumat HaDeshen. Tosafot write similarly in Nazir 28b, while on 29a the Gemara expressly concludes according to Reish Lakish that a mother is not obligated to train her son. Although R' Yochanan disputes Reish Lakish there and does not require that conclusion for his own explanation, it is preferable to minimize the dispute and say that R' Yochanan agrees on this point. See Magen Avraham, siman 343. "Nevertheless, it appears to me that she should not give," etc. Meaning the mother, although she is not obligated in chinuch. "And it is not comparable to sukkah, where she is permitted," etc. Otherwise, what did Tosafot ask from Queen Helena sitting in a sukkah with her 7 sons? It would have been forbidden for her to give them food outside the sukkah. "And she is not obligated to bring him in," etc., because the mother is not obligated in chinuch. "Furthermore, there it is a positive commandment." Meaning that eating in a sukkah is a positive commandment. A negative prohibition is more severe than a positive commandment, as stated in the first chapter of Yevamos in the discussion of "aleha" and as also appears in the second chapter of Yevamos. This is the conclusion of Terumat HaDeshen, siman 94. Therefore, since eating in a sukkah involves only a positive commandment, even giving the child food is more lenient. Magen Avraham at the beginning of siman 269, however, rejects this reasoning. We rule that one may not directly feed a minor even a rabbinically prohibited substance, all the more so one may not actively cause him to fail to fulfill a Torah positive commandment. We must therefore say that the principal answer remains the first one: sukkah is different because the act of eating itself is not prohibited. In truth, directly feeding a minor outside the sukkah is forbidden even for a woman, as Magen Avraham writes explicitly below in siman 640. What he now adds is another distinction. Even if Yom Kippur were exactly analogous to sukkah regarding direct feeding, there is still more room for leniency in sukkah because it concerns a positive commandment, whereas eating on Yom Kippur is a negative prohibition. Terumat HaDeshen, however, concludes by challenging the cited Tosafot and proving from Eruvin that a mother too is obligated in her son's chinuch, and he therefore concludes that one should not be lenient. "That which does not," etc., "in his 12th year." Meaning when he is 11 complete years old, as Rama writes that leniency should be used only for a weak youth. "Because everyone studies," etc. We rely upon Rashi's view in Kesubos 50a according to Bach's interpretation of Rashi. When the child is 12 complete years old and enters his 13th year, however, Rashi and Bach agree that he must complete the fast rabbinically, except where he is weak, in which case we rely upon the opinion brought by Rama. Eliyah Rabbah cites Bach regarding Rama's statement that one may rely on the lenient view for a weak youth, and writes that our custom is to treat all children this way because they all study, and nowadays they are generally considered weak. In truth, Bach was lenient only when the child was 11, as stated above. It is possible that Eliyah Rabbah wrote the broader point from his own reasoning based upon Bach. His further statement, that nowadays children are generally considered ill, means that weakness has increased in later generations. Bach concludes that since we are not expert in determining when a situation becomes a danger to life, a doubtful danger is treated leniently.
