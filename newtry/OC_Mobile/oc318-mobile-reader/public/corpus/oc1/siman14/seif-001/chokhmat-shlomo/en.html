Seif 1. A woman is qualified to make them, and some are stringent, etc. Were I not hesitant, it would appear that these opinions do not disagree. Certainly, when acting on her own, a woman is disqualified from making tzitzis with which a man will fulfill his obligation. If, however, a man appoints her as his agent to make tzitzis for him, a non-Jew is invalid because non-Jews are not capable of agency, whereas a woman is valid. Although we rule that one who is not personally subject to a matter cannot appoint an agent for it, here a woman may voluntarily perform a time-bound positive commandment, and according to those who permit her to recite a blessing, all the more so. She is therefore considered personally connected to the mitzvah, comparable to the discussion concerning a slave in Kiddushin 23b. Since she may perform the mitzvah for herself and may make tzitzis for herself, she may also serve as a man's agent. Thus, the Mechaber's ruling that a woman may make them refers to a case of agency, while Rama's statement that some are stringent refers to a case without agency, in which her act does not enable a man to fulfill his obligation. This also resolves Magen Avraham's question from the verse's wording, “the children of Israel,” and not the daughters of Israel. That exclusion applies when she acts independently, but not when she acts as the man's agent. Much more could be said, but time does not permit. In my responsum to the community of Czernowitz I wrote that it appears permitted. A proof may be brought from Chullin, chapter 5, where Rabbi Afotriki asks from the verse, “It shall remain seven days,” and the Gemara distinguishes between nighttime for consecration and nighttime for offering. This proves that although nighttime is unfit for offering, one may nevertheless consecrate the animal at night. All the more so may tzitzis be made at night, even though nighttime is not the time of their mitzvah. Rosh brings proof from the validity of sukkos made by those included in the mnemonic Ganbach, even though they are not obligated in the mitzvah. In my opinion, that passage proves the opposite. The Gemara says that one baraisa prefers the Ganbach cases because their sukkos are permanent, and includes the Rakbash cases through the word “all,” even though their sukkos are not permanent. The other baraisa prefers Rakbash because they are obligated in mitzvos, and includes Ganbach through “all,” even though they are not obligated. Why is the additional word needed to include Ganbach? A kal vachomer should suffice: if Rakbash, whose sukkos are not permanent, are valid, then Ganbach, whose sukkos are permanent, should certainly be valid. One cannot answer that Ganbach are not obligated, for there should be another kal vachomer from tzitzis and similar mitzvos that require manufacture for the sake of the mitzvah, yet are valid when made by a woman. A sukkah, which does not require being made for the sake of the festival, should certainly be valid when made by one who is not obligated. Rather, this indicates that a woman is invalid for tzitzis because tzitzis must be made for the sake of the mitzvah. This resembles tefillin, where one who is not subject to binding is not subject to writing. Since the act must be performed for the sake of the mitzvah, and even one who is obligated produces invalid tzitzis if he acts without that intention, the person performing the act must himself be obligated in that mitzvah. One who is not obligated lacks sufficiently forceful intention for an act that requires intention for the sake of the mitzvah. A sukkah is different. It does not require manufacture for the sake of the festival, and therefore the builder need not be obligated. I am therefore surprised by Rosh's proof, for a sukkah is not comparable to tzitzis, and the passage appears to prove the reverse. Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 3 that according to the stringent opinion a woman is also disqualified from making a sukkah or lulav. His intent appears to concern initial practice, for the Gemara explicitly validates the Ganbach and Rakbash sukkos after the fact. Peri Megadim likewise understands him this way, and Beit Yosef expressly writes that initially a woman should not make a sukkah. In my humble opinion, however, there is proof that she may even place the sechach initially. The two baraisos otherwise adopt opposite priorities: one prefers permanence over obligation, while the other prefers obligation over permanence. Tosafos often questions how two opinions can adopt directly opposing lines of reasoning. It therefore appears that the distinction is between initial practice and after the fact. Although the sukkos in question were already made, the issue may still arise initially or after the fact. If one already ate there on the first night, must he eat again in another sukkah? That is the after-the-fact case. The initial case is whether one may enter and eat there in the first place. At first glance, the preference for a permanent Ganbach sukkah is difficult, because the essential requirement is a temporary dwelling, not a permanent dwelling. It is strained to say the baraisa follows Rabbi Yehudah, who requires a permanent dwelling. If that were the answer, the two baraisos would not contain opposite reasoning: the Ganbach baraisa would follow Rabbi Yehudah, for whom permanence is superior, and the Rakbash baraisa would follow the Sages, for whom a temporary dwelling is required. Since the Gemara does not explain them this way, both appear to follow all opinions. Why, then, is permanence an advantage? The answer is that even the Sages require a temporary physical structure only when it is made for the mitzvah. Although the building itself is temporary, the intention to make it as a sukkah gives it a form of permanence, because the mitzvah establishes it. A Ganbach or Rakbash sukkah, however, was not made for the mitzvah. Since the mitzvah did not establish it, its actual permanence becomes an advantage. This explains why one baraisa prefers Ganbach. Some authorities rule that mitzvos do not require intention, and even those who require intention sometimes agree that where a physical act is involved, no additional intention is necessary. Thus, if one ate in a sukkah on the first night without specific intention for the mitzvah, he may nevertheless fulfill his obligation. We can therefore distinguish between initial practice and after the fact. When one initially wishes to eat in a Ganbach or Rakbash sukkah for the mitzvah, the fact that it was made by those obligated in mitzvos is a greater advantage than its physical permanence. Once he intends to use it for the mitzvah, his present intention gives it the mitzvah's permanence, and physical permanence is no longer an advantage, since a sukkah should be a temporary dwelling. The fact that it was made by those obligated is then more significant. Since tzitzis may not initially be made by one who is not obligated, the baraisa teaches that a sukkah is valid initially even when made by such persons, because a sukkah need not be made for the sake of the mitzvah. The other baraisa concerns an after-the-fact case, where he already sat there without intention for the mitzvah. There is no novelty in validating a sukkah made by those obligated, because even tzitzis, which require intention, are valid after the fact according to some authorities when made without it, and a sukkah is certainly valid. The novelty is physical permanence. Since he did not intend it for the mitzvah, the mitzvah did not establish it, and one might have thought that a nonpermanent structure is not regarded as a sukkah at all. The baraisa teaches that he nevertheless fulfills his obligation. Thus, the baraisa that lists Ganbach first concerns the after-the-fact case and gives precedence to permanence; the baraisa that lists Rakbash first concerns initial use and gives precedence to those obligated in mitzvos. The conclusion is that the Gemara indicates that tzitzis made by a woman are invalid initially but valid after the fact, while a sukkah made by a woman is valid even initially. Nevertheless, I am not worthy to rule against the great authorities. Therefore, where it can be arranged easily, one should try not to have a Ganbach-type person make the sukkah. It is permitted to take another person's tallis and recite a blessing over it, etc. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 8, who concludes that although the borrower is exempt, he may recite a blessing just as women recite blessings. I saw that Siddur Derech HaChayim, by the Gaon of Lissa, disagrees. He writes that Tosafos and Rosh above discuss one who borrowed a tallis and attached his own tzitzis. If he borrowed both the tallis and its tzitzis, it resembles borrowing strings, and he certainly cannot recite a blessing, just as one cannot recite a blessing over a borrowed esrog. In my humble opinion, Magen Avraham is correct, and the comparison is not valid. One who borrows an esrog cannot recite the blessing because the Torah obligates him to take an esrog that belongs to him, not a borrowed one. Since he remains obligated to obtain his own esrog, and would have to take it later and recite the blessing, a blessing over the borrowed esrog would be in vain. Perhaps if he knows that he will have no esrog of his own that day, he may indeed recite a blessing over the borrowed one. The first day would then be no worse than the second day. Chacham Tzvi writes similarly regarding other objects that are invalid on the first day but valid on the second: if no other object is available, one may recite the blessing over it even on the first day. The same may apply to a borrowed esrog. If, however, one has neither tallis nor tzitzis, he is not personally obligated in tzitzis at all. He may therefore recite a blessing over a borrowed tallis, just as women do, and just as one who borrows a tallis and attaches his own tzitzis may recite a blessing. What difference is there whether the garment or the strings are borrowed? In either case, the law does not require him to do anything further. Although he is exempt from making the mitzvah object in the most ideal manner, he may recite a blessing over the voluntary mitzvah. Magen Avraham's ruling is therefore correct, and Derech HaChayim's comparison is not compelling.
