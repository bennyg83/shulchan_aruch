[1] That he made, etc. Meaning, he attached the strings, or made the first winding and knots. Levush. It appears to me that if a Jew made the first winding, meaning one wrap and one knot, and a non-Jew completed the rest, it is valid. All the more so is it valid if a Jew completed the remaining windings and knots without explicit intention. This is implied by Beit Yosef in siman 11 and here. Bach and all the later authorities write that if a non-Jew made them, they are invalid even if a Jew stands over him. Olas Tamid remains uncertain where a Jew assists him. In my humble opinion it is clearly invalid, for Shabbos 93a rules that assistance has no halachic substance. Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah, siman 271, mentions assistance regarding a Sefer Torah only because it helps establish that the work was performed for the sake of the mitzvah. Since a Jew assists, we say that the non-Jew acts according to the Jew's intention, as is implied by Beit Yosef and Bach there. Here, however, the invalidity is not because of lack of intention, but because of a Scriptural exclusion. See above, siman 11, seif katan 4.
[2] Some are stringent, etc. One reason is that the verse says “the children of Israel,” and not the daughters of Israel. Another is the comparison to tefillin: whoever is subject to binding is subject to making. The permitting authorities maintain that where the Torah expressly revealed an exclusion, it applies only there. Levush. It appears that the practical difference between the two reasons is that according to the second reason, even a minor who has reached the age of education may not make tzitzis, just as below in siman 39 regarding tefillin. Likewise, a minor could not make a sukkah or lulav because he is not obligated. According to the first reason, however, a sukkah and lulav are valid when made by one who is otherwise excluded, and a minor may likewise make tzitzis. Regarding Levush's words that where the Torah revealed an exclusion it applies only there, I cited in Eliyah Zuta the objection of Malbushei Yom Tov: this is not the correct explanation. Rather, since the Torah does not write the act of wearing within the commandment of tzitzis itself, we cannot expound that one who is not subject to wearing may not make them. So write Tosafos on Menachos 43a. I now believe that Levush intended the same. Since wearing is not written explicitly there, we do not derive the law of tzitzis from tefillin by saying that just as women are exempt from tefillin, they are exempt from making tzitzis. We say instead that where the Torah revealed the exclusion it applies only there. Had wearing been written explicitly in the passage of tzitzis, the same exclusion would have applied there. This is explicit in Beit Yosef in the name of Mordechai. Magen Avraham questions this from Kiddushin 36a and Menachos 61a, where both non-Jews and women are excluded from “the children of Israel.” It appears to me that Menachos 42a records Rav Ashi's opposite derivation, validating non-Jews to make tzitzis from the wording, “Speak to the children of Israel, and they shall make for themselves tzitzis,” meaning that they may have others make it for them. This derives from the apparently superfluous word “for themselves.” According to the authority who invalidates non-Jews, why is that word needed? It must include women, so that “the children of Israel” excludes only non-Jews, not women. According to the authority who validates non-Jews, why does the verse need both “the children of Israel” and “for themselves”? He maintains that ordinarily one would exclude non-Jews because anyone not subject to wearing is not subject to making. The verse therefore had to validate them. The authority who invalidates non-Jews does not accept that principle regarding tzitzis, as explained above. Without a verse, non-Jews would therefore be valid. He derives their invalidity from “the children of Israel” and women's validity from “for themselves.” This is the dispute. With this explanation I clarified for the yeshivah the words of Tosafos on Menachos 42a, s.v. “From where,” which says, “It should not have said, etc., regarding Israel.” I explain this in my novellae.
[3] And it is preferable, etc. The custom is to permit women initially to repair the tallis and to spin and twist the strings. Malbushei Yom Tov and Lechem Chamudos ask why this needed to be stated.
