It is permitted to take a friend's tallit and recite the blessing over it. {Rama: On this: because it is pleasing to a person, etc.} And it seems to me that specifically to lay it aside every day is forbidden, but incidentally it is permitted to lay aside a friend's tallit; and the same applies to tefillin, only that he may not remove them from their place at all—not from his house and not from the synagogue—rather he should don them and lay them aside there. (Tomer Devorah and Yam Shel Shlomo.) And if he finds them folded he must fold them, since the owner folded them—this reveals his intent that he objects to lending them.

However, etc. {Rama: On this: the general rule of the matter is that without consent it is forbidden for anyone from the outset;} and when one deposits a book with a Torah scholar, even if the scholar erred in it there is permission. And the same applies to a pledge deposited with a Torah scholar: even if he erred in it after the loan, as long as he does not learn from his books they pay no attention, since at the time of the loan he said nothing to him—the Torah scholar acquires it as a borrower; only whatever he damaged he must pay him, as stated above. And not as the Sema argues in Choshen Mishpat siman 72, whether if he stipulated from the outset at the time of the loan that he would learn from his books, then the condition is effective even without damage; but if he did not stipulate this explicitly from the outset it is forbidden to learn from them. And it is simple that if the books were lost at that time when he is learning from them, he is liable for the loss.
