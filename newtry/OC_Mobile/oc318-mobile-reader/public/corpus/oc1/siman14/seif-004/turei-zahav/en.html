It is permitted to take, etc. Meaning, without the owner's knowledge. For the same reason Rama rules below in siman 649 that one may take another person's lulav without permission after the first day. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 100, compares that case to this one, because no damage is expected and a person is pleased for a mitzvah to be performed with his property. It is difficult why Terumas HaDeshen and Rama permit it only after the first day, but not on the first day. Tzitzis is also D'Oraisa, yet we rely upon the owner's presumed consent and recite a blessing without his knowledge. One may answer that the Torah does not prohibit wearing a garment without tzitzis. It merely obligates a person to attach tzitzis to his own garment, as explained above in siman 13 in the name of Mordechai and Ri. Here that obligation does not apply because the garment is not his. Yet siman 8, seif 9, is more stringent regarding the blessing over tzitzis than regarding wearing, requiring inspection specifically because of the blessing. If the presumption that a person is pleased for a mitzvah to be performed with his property is strong enough for the blessing over tzitzis, it should all the more suffice for the first-day lulav obligation. We should presume that the owner certainly gives it as a gift on condition that it be returned. One may distinguish that on the first day, when the obligation is D'Oraisa, the owner is more particular that the esrog not be damaged. Even a slight deficiency or puncture can invalidate it, so presumed consent is ineffective. Still, why is an explicitly borrowed lulav invalid on the first day? Since the lender knows that the borrower can fulfill the obligation only if it is given as a gift on condition that it be returned, we should presume that was his intent. A gift on condition that it be returned is effective for an esrog, as Kiddushin 6b teaches. One must apparently say that since he used the language of lending, we do not reinterpret it as a gift. But why is this different from Rosh's ruling above that one who borrows a fringed tallis must recite a blessing because we presume the lender intended a gift on condition that it be returned? The same difficulty arises from Rosh in the first chapter of Kiddushin concerning a borrowed ring. If one lends a ring to his friend knowing that he wishes to betroth a woman with it, the betrothal is valid. We testify that he intended to transfer it with whatever legally effective language was necessary, at least as a gift on condition that it be returned, since ordinary people do not know the technical law. This is a full betrothal, as ruled in Even HaEzer 28. All the more so should the same reasoning validate the first-day lulav obligation. It appears that kiddushin differs from lulav. A borrowed or stolen lulav is invalid because of the Scriptural decree, “for yourselves,” meaning your own. The Torah therefore invalidated every case expressed as a loan, and we may not reinterpret the language as a gift. In kiddushin, by contrast, borrowed or stolen property is invalid by logic, because the man must give the woman money that belongs to him. Since no verse excludes the case, we may assess the lender's intent and say that he meant to use any legally effective formulation. See what I wrote in siman 368 concerning Rosh's words. This, however, renews the difficulty regarding tzitzis, where the verse “your garment” excludes a borrowed garment. Sukkah 27b likewise teaches that without the verse “every citizen,” a borrowed sukkah would have been invalid like a stolen one because the Torah says, “You shall make for yourself.” Thus, tzitzis appears precisely comparable to a borrowed lulav. This requires extensive study. Although I am unworthy even to join Maharshal in disputing Rosh and those who follow him, this difficulty compels us to be concerned for a doubtful blessing and not recite a blessing over a borrowed fringed tallis, in accordance with Maharshal. This is all the more true when one takes it without the owner's knowledge. Rosh writes that he observed people during Selichos not reciting a blessing over borrowed talleisim and called them mistaken. According to what I have written, they acted properly. Their established practice therefore cannot serve as proof for the opposite ruling.
