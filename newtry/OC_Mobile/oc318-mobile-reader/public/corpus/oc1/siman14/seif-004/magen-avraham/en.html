Another person's tallis. Bach writes that this is permitted only occasionally, but regular use is forbidden. Yam Shel Shlomo writes that it is forbidden to take it from the owner's home to the synagogue, or the reverse.
And recite a blessing. This requires study. Taking the tallis may be permitted because a person is pleased for a mitzvah to be performed with his property, but why should a blessing be recited? When one borrows it with the owner's knowledge, it is as though the owner gave it as a gift on condition that it be returned, as Rosh writes. The same applies to a lulav: if it was lent for the purpose of fulfilling the mitzvah, the borrower fulfills his obligation, as stated in Even HaEzer 28:19 and Choshen Mishpat 195:4. Taking it without permission, however, is forbidden, as stated there and below in siman 649, seif 5, in the gloss. Why, then, is it permitted here? The answer must be as explained above: although the borrower is exempt from tzitzis, he may nevertheless recite a blessing, just as women do.
That he should fold it. He may fold it even differently from its original folds. On Shabbos it is permitted even if he does not fold it at all. This appears correct. See Knesses HaGedolah, siman 302.
Because we are concerned. We are concerned that he may read them extensively until they tear from excessive use. Nimukei Yosef. This implies that it is forbidden to learn from them even occasionally. Although siman 437, seif 3, teaches that a person is pleased for a mitzvah to be performed with his property even where there is some financial loss, that case involves only a minor loss. See Choshen Mishpat 292:20 in the gloss and Sema there, who writes that even if sefarim were deposited with a Torah scholar, he may not learn from them if it is known that the owner objects. Shach disagrees and permits it. His interpretation of Mordechai appears correct, but the authorities cited here by Beit Yosef imply that one who uses them is a borrower without permission.
