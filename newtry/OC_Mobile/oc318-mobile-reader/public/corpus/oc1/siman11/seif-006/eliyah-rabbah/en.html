Some say that even after the fact they are invalid, because this is a change that can return to its original state (Levush). Taz agrees with this latter view, but not for Levush's reason. Rather, he prohibits it because it is a mitzvah performed through a transgression. Levush's reason is difficult, for in Choshen Mishpat there, seif 2, it is ruled that a change of name effects acquisition even if the object can return to its original state, and here too there is a change of name. This also resolves the difficulty raised by Olat Tamid, quoting Shiyurei Knesset HaGedolah, against Rama's ruling that if he spun the stolen wool, etc., it is valid: they object that a reversible change does not effect acquisition. There is no difficulty, because here there is also a change of name. Nevertheless, as a matter of law it is all forbidden, as I wrote in the name of Taz. Yet Taz himself is difficult, for below, siman 649, seif 2, it is explained that where there is despair together with a change, the item is not disqualified as a mitzvah performed through a transgression. One can answer that since the change occurs through making the wool into threads, and we rule that spinning must be performed for the sake of the mitzvah, the change itself is accomplished with the assistance of the mitzvah. It is therefore comparable to one who consecrates a stolen animal, as Tosafos write in Bava Kamma 67a. Yet this too is difficult, because Taz challenges Beit Yosef, who interpreted Rambam here as referring to a change that can return to its original state. Taz writes: 'I am astonished that this holy mouth overlooked the Gemara in the first chapter of HaGozel, where it is explained that whitening wool is a change that cannot return to its original state. One who stole wool and made threads from it must already have whitened it, for without prior whitening it cannot be made into threads.' He therefore interprets Rambam on the basis of a mitzvah performed through a transgression. Rashi indeed explains there that he first whitens it and afterward spins it. If so, however, the change took place before the spinning. Moreover, why did Taz object to Beit Yosef? Rambam may be speaking of one who stole white wool and then spun it into threads, just as the Gemara establishes the Mishnah concerning one who stole wool and made a garment from it as referring to stolen wool that had already been spun. In truth, one may ask why the Gemara did not answer that it refers to white wool that he then spun and made into a garment, but this can be resolved. Rambam's words can certainly be explained this way. Taz further challenges Beit Yosef's interpretation that Rambam speaks before the owner's despair. If so, why is a Scriptural verse needed to disqualify it? In Bava Kamma the Gemara asks that before despair no verse is necessary. My father and teacher answered that a verse is required here because there is a physical change. This is somewhat difficult because the Gemara is discussing an offering, where there is also a change of name, and nevertheless asks why a verse is needed. In my humble opinion there was never a difficulty, for the verse can be needed for a case where one stole threads, which remain invalid even after despair. This must indeed be how Nimukei Yosef, who holds that one who stole wool and made threads from it has valid tzitzit, establishes the verse. Another answer follows Tosafos, Sukkah 30a, who write that only regarding consecrated property does the Gemara ask why a verse is needed, since otherwise the item is ordinary property.
