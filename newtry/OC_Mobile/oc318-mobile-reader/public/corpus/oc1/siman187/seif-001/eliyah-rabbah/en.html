[1] [Levush:] Clear and clean, etc. Since I have seen many variations in the prayer texts, I undertook to examine the early authorities in order to establish the proper text of Bircas HaMazon. The following conclusions emerged. One should say, “and in His mercy He gives bread,” as in Rabbi David Abudraham, not “He is the One Who gives.” In the phrase “we have lacked nothing, and may we never lack food,” some pronounce the first word with a kamatz under the chet and a patach under the samech, while others pronounce it with a chirik under the chet and a patach under the samech. Those who follow the second pronunciation must likewise vocalize the corresponding future form so that both words derive from the same root, as Mateh Moshe writes. Shelah states that he chose the first pronunciation. One should say, “for He sustains and supports all,” as written by Mordechai, Rabbi David Abudraham, and Mateh Moshe, unlike Orchos Chaim, which has, “for He is God Who sustains.” Mordechai explains that “sustains” refers to food and “supports” to other needs. In the phrases “all” and “benefits all,” the kaf is vocalized with a cholam because the words stand independently, whereas in “all His creatures,” which is construct, it is vocalized differently. The shin of “for You sustain” is vocalized with a broad kamatz so that the alef is clearly heard. The same applies in the Amidah to “we thank You, for You are,” as Radach and Mateh Moshe write. One should say, “at every time and at every hour,” because an eis is six hours and four periods change during each day: morning to midday, midday to night, and so forth. One should say, “we thank You,” using anachnu, not anu, because anu can denote affliction or a curse. In the prayer Nishmas, the word “and we” is written with a yud rather than a peh, for the word pi is a separate word. Radach and Mateh Moshe write likewise. The word “tend us” should be vocalized as a request that He tend us, not as a noun meaning “our Shepherd.” Likewise, “sustain us,” “support us,” and “maintain us” are requests that He do these things for us. The later authorities conclude that both on Shabbos and on weekdays one should say, “Who builds Jerusalem in His mercy.” In the first occurrence of Yerushalayim the first syllable is vocalized with a patach, and in the second with a kamatz, because it is at the end of the verse. One should pause briefly and then answer amen quietly. Beit Yosef writes in the name of Rabbi David Abudraham that one should not say “the God,” because one has already said “our God.” Rav Amram similarly writes regarding the blessing after the Megillah. Rambam's text, however, reads, “the God, our Father, our King,” and so read the Tur, Mordechai at the end of Berachos, Responsa Maharshal, siman 64, and Mateh Moshe. One should therefore not alter the established custom, but should say, “the God, our Father,” although the rationale requires explanation. Maharshal and Mateh Moshe cite Maharil as omitting “our Creator” and saying instead, “our Mighty One, our Redeemer, our Maker,” with lengthy explanations. I am astonished, for in the texts of Rambam and Abudraham I found “our Creator” and not “our Maker.” It therefore appears that one should not depart from our custom of saying both “our Creator” and “our Maker.” In the word hatov, the hei has a patach. One should not say, “the God Who every day,” since that could imply, Heaven forbid, two authorities, as though we bless only the deity who benefits us each day. Rather, say, “God, every day,” without the word “Who.” Bach writes this in siman 189 in the name of Kol Bo, and Shelah agrees. Taz there rules that one should omit “the God” entirely. In my humble opinion, one should not alter the custom, since Kol Bo and other poskim record it. The Tur writes that the Rosh would say: “He has benefited us, He benefits us, He will benefit us; He has dealt kindly with us, He deals kindly with us, He will deal kindly with us.” Shulchan Aruch and Bach in siman 189 rule accordingly and state that this is the custom, and this is how I have practiced. This excludes those who say merely, “He benefited, benefits, and will benefit.” I now found in Kol Bo the order: “He benefits; He has benefited us; He will benefit us; He deals kindly with us; He has dealt kindly with us; He will deal kindly with us,” beginning with the present and then the past and future, after the pattern, “Hashem is King, Hashem was King, Hashem will reign.” Mordechai writes similarly in the name of Pardes and Rabbeinu Yonah, and Abudraham cites it in the name of the Rosh. Perhaps there is a scribal error in the Tur and the order should likewise be arranged this way. These authorities also imply that one should not say, “He will reward us on our behalf,” but, “He will deal kindly with us forever,” as Mateh Moshe writes. Or Chadash writes that both “all good” and “from all good” should be vocalized with a shuruk, but in Tashbetz, siman 317, I found “all good” with a cholam. Regarding the phrase, “upon this table,” even if boards are arranged from one table to another, as at large banquets, this is not called two tables unless there are actually two tables and a separate host sits at each. When all join in one zimun, one says, “upon these tables upon which we ate.” This is cited in the name of Beis Meir. The opening and conclusion appear contradictory, but perhaps it describes the usual case. The same law applies where there are actually two tables at one large meal, or one host eats with his household at separate tables: one says, “upon these tables upon which we ate.” One should say, “May the Merciful One bless my honored father,” not “my father” in a childish form. One blesses the host only when eating at his table and from his bread, even if one lives in his house. The Yerushalmi derives from “and you shall bless” that one should include the host. In the phrase, “as our forefathers Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob were blessed, in everything, from everything, with everything,” the relevant consonants receive a dagesh because in Bircas HaMazon they are not adjacent to a Divine name. When non-Jews or apostates are present, one should say, “So may He bless us, all of us together, children of the covenant.” Magen Avraham cites Taz in siman 189 as requiring the order, “us, children of the covenant, all of us together.” There is no real objection if the words are all said within the brief interval of speech, and indeed, according to his wording it resembles repetition. Kol Bo discusses this. Or Chadash, page 36b, implies that there is no difference between one woman and another, contrary to those who at a wedding say, “the groom and the bride, our brethren and teachers, and all who sit and recline here.” I found in Kol Bo, page 24, that they say: “May the Merciful One support, sustain, maintain, and exalt the groom and the pleasant bride, and all my teachers who recline here, as they were blessed,” and so forth. Mateh Moshe writes that one says “and say amen,” though this phrase is not said at the end of Bircas HaMazon. Magen Avraham in siman 189 writes that one cannot command the listeners to answer amen, unlike “He Who makes peace,” which is a prayer of the Holy One, blessed is He; he does not mention Mateh Moshe. Some say “from the nation of Hashem” rather than “from Hashem,” but it is correct not to alter the language of the verse and to say “from Hashem,” taking care not to swallow the alef. Likewise, one should articulate clearly, “and righteousness from the God of our salvation,” and so forth. Mateh Moshe and Orchos Chaim have, “May the Merciful One grant us the days of Mashiach and life in the World to Come.” Abudraham has, “May the Merciful One grant us, sustain us, and bring us near to the days of Mashiach and to life,” and so forth. I do not know why they altered Rabbi David Abudraham's text, especially since he brings a verse for each phrase. Rabbi David Abudraham further writes that on Shabbos, which represents the great King in contrast to the weekday, one says migdol with a vav and cholam, alluding to a great king, whereas on a weekday one says magdil without a vav and with a chirik, alluding to a lesser king. Moreover, magdil appears in Tehillim before David was king, while migdol appears in the Prophets after he had become king. The later authorities write that Yom Tov and Rosh Chodesh have the same law as Shabbos; the mnemonic is, “The new moon and Shabbos call to Scripture.” Kol Bo writes that one answers amen after each Harachaman, and likewise after every supplication and request, even when it contains neither a Divine name nor a blessing. Shelah agrees. Mateh Moshe writes that one should say, “He will make,” and not, “and He will make.” The later authorities write that after “for His kindness is eternal,” one should recite the verses, “I was young and have also grown old, yet I have not seen a righteous person forsaken, nor his descendants begging bread”; “Blessed is the man who trusts in Hashem, and Hashem shall be his trust”; “Hashem will give strength to His people”; and “Hashem will bless His people with peace.” When reciting Bircas HaMazon over a cup, before the blessing one should say, “I will raise the cup of salvations and call upon the name of Hashem.” The numerical value of “I will raise” alludes to “I will be what I will be.”
