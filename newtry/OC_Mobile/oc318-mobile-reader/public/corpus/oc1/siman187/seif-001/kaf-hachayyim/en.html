(1) [Se'if 1] Some say, 'Blessed is He Who satisfies,' etc. Berachos 48b teaches that Birkas HaMazon consists in order of HaZan, Birkas HaAretz, Boneh Yerushalayim, and HaTov VeHaMeitiv. Moshe instituted HaZan when the manna descended; Yehoshua instituted Birkas HaAretz upon entry into the land; David and Shlomo instituted Boneh Yerushalayim; David for Israel and Jerusalem, Shlomo for the great and holy Temple. HaTov VeHaMeitiv was instituted at Yavneh for the slain of Beitar: 'HaTov,' that they did not decay, and 'VeHaMeitiv,' that they were given burial. Rashba asks how these figures could institute blessings that the Gemara soon calls Torah law. He answers that they fixed the blessing texts. Torah law allowed any wording; Moshe, Yehoshua, and David established a form appropriate to each era. Before conquest and construction they necessarily spoke differently than afterward, just as we now pray to restore the kingdom and rebuild the Temple, whereas they prayed to preserve them and continue the land's tranquility. Rosh and Tur, siman 188, agree. Ramban writes that Torah law gives no fixed Birkas HaMazon text but commands each person to bless after eating according to his ability, like Binyamin the shepherd's 'Blessed is the Merciful One, Master of this bread.' The prophets instituted polished language, changing with the eras; Shlomo and his court instituted the subject, while each era uses fitting wording. Beis Yosef and Prishah cite him. Rashba, Rosh, Tur, Prishah, Bach, Tosafos, Levush, and Magen Avraham understand that the number of three blessings is Torah law, supported by the verses in the Gemara. Maran in Beis Yosef, siman 191, however, says Torah law does not fix their number, as Rambam in Sefer HaMitzvos lists only the command to bless after eating; the Gemara's verses are supports. Kesef Mishneh understands Rambam and Ramban likewise, and Midrash Rabbah, Mas'ei, implies the same. The practical difference: if three blessings are Torah-required, one who said Al HaMichyah instead of Birkas HaMazon, or omitted one blessing, did not fulfill the obligation; the two-blessing allowance for laborers in siman 191 is exceptional. Eliyah Rabbah 268:18 and Chayei Adam likewise rule that Al HaMichyah does not suffice. If their number is rabbinic, omission or Al HaMichyah does suffice. Taz, siman 168:6, appears to say so; Meshivas Nefesh 4 says three texts are rabbinic and Torah law is fulfilled with Al HaMichyah, making doubt lenient; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, siman 191, agrees. Because of the dispute, one who mistakenly said Al HaMichyah after eating to satiation should, if possible, eat more bread, bless Hamotzi, and recite Birkas HaMazon intending also for the first eating; alternatively, hear Birkas HaMazon from another and intend to fulfill his obligation. If impossible, he should contemplate the blessing in his heart, as above in siman 184, paragraph 22. If he had not eaten to satiation and the obligation was rabbinic, one may be lenient after the fact and consider him to have fulfilled it. See below, paragraph 18.
