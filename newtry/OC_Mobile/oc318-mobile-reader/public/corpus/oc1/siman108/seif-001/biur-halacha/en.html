If he erred or was prevented, etc. An error means that he thought he had prayed and later remembered that he had not prayed, or that he forgot to pray, as stated below in seif 8. The Peri Megadim writes: I am uncertain about one who is unsure whether he prayed Shacharis, and the time for Shacharis then passed, whether he should make it up at Minchah. Perhaps the enactment to compensate after its time passed was made only where he certainly did not pray, but not where there is doubt.
If he reversed the order. See the Mishnah Berurah, where we ruled in accordance with the Mechaber. The Gra likewise cites the source for this ruling without noting the Magen Avraham's objection, which seemingly indicates that he does not accept the Magen Avraham's view. Know that this applies only where he explicitly intended the first prayer as compensation. If he prayed both without any specified intent, all agree that he fulfilled his obligation. I am uncertain where he began the prayer as compensation, remembered in the middle, and then completed it properly with intent for the current obligation. Perhaps, since all the blessings of the Amidah must be recited in order, as stated below in siman 119, the entire prayer is considered one unit, and changing his intent in the middle is ineffective. Although for several laws only the first three blessings are treated as one unit, the law of changing intent may be different. There is apparently proof from siman 107: if one began praying under the mistaken belief that he was obligated, he must stop even in the middle of a blessing. The Rishonim explain that since he began as an obligatory prayer, he can no longer complete it as a voluntary prayer. The same may apply here, so that changing his intent is ineffective and he must stop in the middle. Alternatively, the Sages may have been lenient concerning compensatory prayer. This requires study.
