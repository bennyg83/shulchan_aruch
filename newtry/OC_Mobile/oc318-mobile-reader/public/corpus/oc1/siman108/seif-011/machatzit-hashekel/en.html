(Seif katan 11) For he thought that forgetting is called unavoidable. This means that we do not say that he should have considered beforehand that perhaps he would forget later, and therefore he should have prayed earlier, so that it would not be called unavoidable. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 422. That means that the law here derives from there, and these are the words of the Nimukei Yosef in Bava Kamma, chapter Keitzad HaRegel, on that which is stated: if a stone was lying in his lap, and he forgot it and it fell and caused damage, although he must pay for the damage, because it is derived from the verse wound for wound, to include an inadvertent act like an intentional act with regard to payment for damage, nevertheless he is exempt from the four other payments, namely healing, unemployment, embarrassment, and depreciation, for regarding them there is no inclusion; therefore, one who acted unintentionally is exempt. Although one could say that he should have thought beforehand that perhaps he would forget and it would fall and cause damage, nevertheless it is called unintentional. From this the Nimukei Yosef learned that the same applies in such a case regarding prayer: forgetting is called unavoidable, as with the four payments, and we do not say that he should have been concerned that perhaps he would forget.
See above, siman 527, regarding eruv tavshilin, that if one unintentionally failed to place an eruv tavshilin, he may rely on the leading scholar of the city who makes an eruv for the whole city, but if he did so intentionally, he may not. He wrote there as well that forgetting is called unintentional.
And siman 538. Regarding a matter of loss on Chol HaMoed. See Yoreh Deah, siman 232, where he wrote as follows: one who swore or vowed to do something within a year or on a certain day, etc., and if he did not do it immediately because he said, I still have time, and afterward he forgot or was prevented and did not do it, there is an opinion that this is called unavoidable, and there is an opinion that it is not called unavoidable. The Magen Avraham asks: the Nimukei Yosef in chapter Keitzad HaRegel learned two laws from the law of the four payments, the law of prayer explained here in Shulchan Aruch and the law of an oath mentioned in Yoreh Deah, siman 232, that in both cases forgetting is called unavoidable. Thus the Nimukei Yosef equated the two laws. If so, why did the Mechaber rule here without qualification and not bring the dissenting view that it is not called unavoidable, as he wrote in Yoreh Deah, siman 232?
And so it also appears in Choshen Mishpat, siman 21, from the language of the Ramban and the Beit Yosef, who wrote as follows: one from whom they made a formal acquisition that if he does not come on a certain day and swear, his fellow will prevail in his claims and collect everything he claimed without an oath, etc., and the day passed and he did not come, the conditions are fulfilled and he lost his right. But if he brings proof that he was prevented on that day, he is exempt from this acquisition. Thus he wrote that he brought proof that he was prevented on that day, which implies that on the day before that day he was not prevented, and even so it is called unavoidable; we do not say that he should have considered that perhaps an unavoidable circumstance would occur on that day.
The proof of the Agudah, which is the opinion cited in Yoreh Deah, siman 232, that it is not called unavoidable, was brought by the Beit Yosef in Choshen Mishpat, siman 207. It is from what we say in Gittin 75: according to the law, one who sells a house among the walled cities has time to redeem it for one year from its sale; if the year passes and he did not redeem it, it remains permanently with the buyer and does not go out at Yovel. The Mishnah teaches that at first, on the last day of the year, the buyer would hide so that the seller could not return his money to him and redeem it, in order that it remain in his possession forever. Hillel the Elder enacted that the seller may deposit his money in the chamber and enter his house. Now, if such a case is called unavoidable, why was Hillel's enactment needed? Let the law itself be so, since on the last day he was prevented because the buyer hid himself; and since he came on the last day to beis din and wished to redeem it, and beis din recognized his unavoidable circumstance, even if the last day passed, beis din should be able to compel the buyer to accept his money and return his house. Rather, it must be that such a case is not called unavoidable, for he should have been concerned that perhaps an unavoidable circumstance would occur on the last day, and he should have redeemed it earlier. He stipulated to redeem it within three years, in a manner that is not an asmachta, such as by saying that if he does not redeem it within three years, it shall be acquired to him from now, or similar terms that remove asmachta, as explained there in Choshen Mishpat. If an unavoidable circumstance occurred, it is absolutely his; this is because through an unavoidable circumstance he is exempt, for the Merciful One exempts one who is prevented by circumstances beyond his control. This is like that case in siman 21 in Choshen Mishpat, where one obligates himself that if he does not swear within a certain time, his fellow will be believed without an oath, and he will have to pay what he would not otherwise have been obligated to pay. Since an unavoidable circumstance occurred, the Merciful One exempts him, and he should not be obligated. But to obligate his fellow in something that his fellow was not obligated to do, to obligate him now because of this unavoidable circumstance, we have not heard. If so, in this case where he lent him money on his field and stipulated that if he does not redeem it within three years it will belong to the lender from now, then when the time arrived and he did not redeem it, it belongs to the lender retroactively. Because an unavoidable circumstance occurred, you wish to obligate the lender to return the field that he acquired by law; this we do not say. The same applies in such a case with a house among walled cities: were it not for Hillel's enactment, according to the law the buyer would not need to return it. Even though the seller was prevented, one cannot obligate the buyer, who acquired the house according to the law, because of the seller's unavoidable circumstance. The Shach wrote similarly in Choshen Mishpat, siman 21; see there, for he further distinguished several legal distinctions. See Yoreh Deah, siman 232, where the Taz also rejected the proof of the Agudah.
