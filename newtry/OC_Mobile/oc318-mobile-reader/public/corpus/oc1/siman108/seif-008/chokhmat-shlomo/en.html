Seif 8. One who did not pray, etc. Note: This ruling demonstrates that forgetfulness is considered unavoidable. See the Magen Avraham's discussion. I find proof as follows. Bava Metzia 51b says that Shmuel can agree even with Rabbi Meir, because Rabbi Meir invalidates a condition against Torah law only where the condition certainly uproots the law, whereas in the case of onaah it is uncertain whether the law will be uprooted. This is difficult in light of the explicit Mishnah at the end of Peah, chapter 6: one who says, 'I am harvesting on condition that whatever I forget I may take,' is nevertheless subject to the law of shichechah. Rambam, Rash, and Bartenura explain that the condition is void because it contradicts Torah law. Yet it is uncertain whether he will forget anything at all, just as it is uncertain whether onaah will occur. One might answer that monetary law is lighter than prohibition. Rabbi Yehudah holds that a condition concerning money is valid. Rabbi Meir may agree that an uncertain uprooting is valid in monetary matters, while in nonmonetary law even uncertain uprooting invalidates the condition. But Gittin 84b discusses a condition that a woman eat meat and likewise concludes that a condition against Torah law is invalid only where it certainly uproots the law, such as food, clothing, and conjugal rights, whereas if she might not eat and therefore might not be divorced, the uprooting is uncertain. Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer, simanim 38 and 144, rules accordingly. Thus even in prohibitory law an uncertain uprooting does not invalidate. Why, then, is the condition in Peah invalid? We must distinguish that in the case of divorce it is within her power not to eat and thereby not be divorced, whereas forgetting is not within a person's control. If forgetfulness is truly unavoidable, the condition in Peah contradicts Torah law because he cannot ensure that he will not forget. But if forgetting is negligence, it must be within his ability to guard against it, for otherwise it could not be called negligence. It would then resemble the divorce condition and should be valid because the uprooting is uncertain. The Mishnah therefore proves that forgetting is not within his control and is considered a genuine unavoidable circumstance. This is, in my view, a clear proof.
