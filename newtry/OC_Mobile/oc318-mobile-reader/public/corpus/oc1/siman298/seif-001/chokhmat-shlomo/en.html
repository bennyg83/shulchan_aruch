Seif 1: One recites the blessing over the flame, “Who creates the lights of fire,” etc. Note: See my work Sefer HaChayim for the difficulty I raised concerning this matter, and see also what I wrote in my responsum among my novellae to Orach Chayim, siman 649, which is still in manuscript.

There, seif 2: It is preferable to recite the blessing over a torch, etc. The Gemara in Berachos 53 teaches: If one was walking outside a city and saw a child holding a torch, he must investigate the child’s identity; see there through the end of the discussion. The Rif, Rambam, and Rosh did not cite this ruling at all, and the reason is unclear. I have not seen anyone note this omission, and it requires explanation.

At first I thought as follows. Why does the Gemara say that a child may merely have happened to pick up the torch? According to the view that beit din must separate a minor who eats forbidden food, the difficulty is obvious, for even if he had merely picked it up, others would protest. Even according to the view that beit din is not required to separate him, his father is certainly required to do so. His father’s failure to protest would therefore indicate that the child is a non-Jew. One cannot answer that the case concerns an orphan, for that is uncommon. Since half the children would be non-Jews and almost all Jewish children have fathers, there would be no reason to consider the unlikely possibility of a Jewish orphan.

We must therefore say that the case is during twilight, when the prohibition is only rabbinic. Since the halachah is that beit din need not separate a minor who commits a prohibition, one may even directly give him something prohibited only by rabbinic law. If so, his father likewise need not separate him, and it remains possible that he is Jewish.

According to this, however, the same case could arise with an adult. We rule that rabbinic restrictions were not imposed during twilight, and carrying a lamp is only a rabbinic restriction. It would follow that the baraita reflects the opinion that rabbinic restrictions do apply during twilight, which is why it speaks specifically of a child. According to our ruling that they do not apply during twilight, the case could equally involve an adult, and this may explain why the early authorities omitted the baraita.

On reconsideration, this is not sufficient. Carrying a lamp is only rabbinically prohibited even on Shabbos itself. If the point is that adults are not required to separate the child and may even give him a rabbinically prohibited object directly, then the same could occur during the day. The Gemara places the case near nightfall only because the blessing over fire is relevant then. Nevertheless, if rabbinic restrictions were not imposed during twilight, the case could still involve an adult, since the Gemara must be speaking after nightfall for the blessing to be recited, although it is still close enough to nightfall to fall within twilight.

The stronger explanation is this. The Gemara says that the child may merely have happened to pick up the torch. Yet since it was close to nightfall, the torch must have been lit before Shabbos ended, and thus the flame did not rest from prohibited labor. Although the child himself is not liable, the flame should be no better than one lit by a non-Jew, over which no blessing is recited. We must say that we assume it may have been lit permissibly for a woman in childbirth or for an ill person.

But this itself involves a double minority: perhaps the child is a non-Jew; and even if he is Jewish, perhaps the flame was not lit for a woman in childbirth or an ill person and therefore did not rest from prohibited labor. The baraita may therefore follow Rabbi Meir, who considers minority possibilities. The Gemara in Kiddushin and Niddah, regarding a child found beside dough, indicates that Rabbi Meir considers a minority even to reach a lenient conclusion. According to him, it is meaningful to investigate whether the child is Jewish, because the flame may have been lit permissibly for a woman in childbirth or an ill person. We, however, rule like the Sages. Therefore even in the case of a child we do not investigate, just as we do not in the case of an adult. Although the child may merely have picked up the torch, the flame still may not have rested from prohibited labor. There is consequently no practical distinction between a minor and an adult, and the omission of this baraita is understandable. Study this carefully. See also siman 100.
