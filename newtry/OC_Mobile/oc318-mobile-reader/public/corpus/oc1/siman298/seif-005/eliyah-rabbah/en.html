[9] And one does not bless, etc. It appears that even after the fact he has not fulfilled the obligation, as in siman 297:2; Magen Avraham agrees. This includes a gentile who lit it, adjusted it [Mahari], or (as Bartenura says in chapter Elu Devarim) performed work by its light.
[10] For a woman after childbirth or an ill person, etc. Magen Avraham says that where there is no danger it must have been lit by a gentile, while where danger exists it may even have been lit by a Jew. Responsa Menahem Azariah 18 implies likewise. In my humble opinion this is incorrect: without danger it is truly fire that did not rest from prohibited labor. Although a Jew may instruct a gentile to light it, the Jew himself may not do so, like a lamp a gentile lit for himself on Shabbos, which a Jew may not use although the gentile may. Levush's phrase 'even a Jew could have done it' likewise implies that without danger, when a Jew could not do it, the fire is disqualified. Talmidei Rabbeinu Yonah, chapter Elu Devarim, also implies this. Responsa Menahem Azariah says that if a gentile lights from his own bonfire for a woman after childbirth or an ill person, one may not bless, since even if he lights it on Motzaei Shabbos the first gentile and first flame are subject to the decree. In my humble opinion one may bless, for the decree applies only when the fire is in a gentile's hand on Motzaei Shabbos, as implied by Tosafos, Berachos 53a, and by Talmidei Rabbeinu Yonah's phrase that it had been burning since before Shabbos.
[11] Or a Jew from a gentile, etc. Taz extensively questions Maggid Mishneh: if a gathering is forbidden because of idolatry, why not bless over the permitted addition supplied by the Jew? This is all the more difficult according to Beis Yosef, who supposedly forbids even outside a gathering. After other questions, Taz permits outside a gathering but is stringent within one because Maggid Mishneh said so. It is astonishing that so great an author made this fundamental error: Maggid Mishneh and Beis Yosef forbade only a gentile who lit from a Jew at a gathering, for it has now become a gentile gathering, as Levush writes below. A Jew who lights even from an actual idolatrous flame is permitted. This is clear.
[12] But a gentile, etc. It appears that after the fact he has fulfilled the obligation, since this is only a rabbinic decree [Magen Avraham].
[13] Levush: It appears to me that when he returned, etc. In my humble opinion it is a lamp in a gentile's hand lit from a flame that did not rest, which entails only one decree. Talmidei Rabbeinu Yonah writes concerning the ruling that a gentile who lit from a Jew is permitted: it was unnecessary to say this, since the first permitted flame belonged to a Jew and no work was done on Shabbos, but it teaches us, etc. This implies that a first forbidden flame remains forbidden. Otherwise, perhaps that itself is what the ruling teaches.
[14] Levush: It is also possible, etc. This is already explained in Beis Yosef, and it is more reasonable than the preceding case because the flame is in a Jew's hand.
[15] Levush: But if he sits at a gathering, etc. This requires examination. Levush evidently ruled so because he understood Beis Yosef to say that everyone agrees, but Maggid Mishneh actually understands Rif to permit even at a gathering, forbidding only where it is known to have been lit for idolatry. Bach and Taz explain this, as does Nachalas Tzvi. Bach nevertheless rules stringently like Rambam, while Taz and Nachalas Tzvi dispute Maggid Mishneh and claim Rif also rules like Rambam. Their words do not appear correct; Rif supports Maggid Mishneh's reading. Moreover, Rambam's own Commentary to the Mishnah, chapter Elu Devarim, clearly shows that only spices are forbidden at a gathering because its entire purpose is idolatry, but not a lamp. Bartenura says this explicitly, and Rosh, Tur, and the other poskim imply likewise.
