(Seif katan 13) If the majority, etc. This requires study, for let us say that anything fixed, etc. Even where the majority are non-Jews, one should have permitted it, for we should say that anything fixed, etc., and be lenient. But the Gemara does not imply this. Meaning, afterward another baraisa is brought there, and it teaches: if he saw a child walking, etc., he must ask, etc., as the Magen Avraham wrote afterward. Since the baraisa states this without qualification, it implies that it speaks even where the majority are Jews, and nevertheless one must ask and we do not rely on the majority. The reason is as stated in Yoreh Deah, siman 1, that where it is possible to clarify, we do not rely on the majority. If so, it is impossible that the baraisa here speaks where he saw a man walking, etc. Granted, one could say that it speaks where it is not immediately near nightfall, so even an adult might be a Jew. Nevertheless, he should have asked and checked whether he is a Jew and not relied on the majority, and all the more so not permitted in a half and half case. Rather, it must be that it speaks where the lamp is not in a person's hand, but he sees from afar a lamp burning inside a house or upper chamber, where it is impossible to determine whether it is a non-Jew's house or a Jew's because of the distance of the place. Thus the difficulty returns: let us say that anything fixed, etc.
See siman 217, seif 7, where he wrote: if one was walking outside a town and smelled a good smell, if the majority are non-Jews he does not bless, for it is certainly of idolatry; and if the majority are Jews, he blesses. The law of half and half was not mentioned. However, the law of a lamp and the law of a smell are mentioned there in two baraisos, and in each one only two cases are taught: if the majority are non-Jews it is forbidden, and if the majority are Jews it is permitted. The Talmud asks regarding the baraisa of the lamp that its first clause is difficult against its last clause: the first clause teaches that if the majority are non-Jews one does not bless, implying that in a half and half case one does bless; and the last clause teaches that if the majority are Jews one blesses, implying that in a half and half case one does not bless. It answers that the inference from the first clause is precise: specifically when the majority are non-Jews one does not bless, but in a half and half case one does bless. And that which the last clause teaches, if the majority are Jews one blesses, and did not teach even in a half and half case, is because since the first clause taught majority non-Jews, the last clause taught majority Jews. These are the words of the Gemara. If so, this same inference can be made in the baraisa of smell, and there too one must answer as the Talmud answered, that the same applies in a half and half case, that it is permitted. If so, the Beis Yosef should also have written above in siman 217 that a half and half case is also permitted. Perhaps because regarding smell it is not explicitly mentioned in the Gemara that a half and half case is permitted, although the law is true that a half and half case is also permitted by smell, nevertheless he did not copy it in Shulchan Aruch. But there too, regarding smell, it is difficult, for even where the majority are non-Jews he should have permitted because anything fixed, etc.
And perhaps this is not called fixed, for the fire, meaning, etc. In Sefer Tosefes Shabbos he asked: do we not rule in Yoreh Deah, siman 110, that when it separated before us, we do not say that whatever separated separated from the majority? See there what he wrote. It appears to me that certainly we rule so. There is an opinion that this is D'Rabbanan, a decree lest he take from the fixed place, and according to some poskim such a case is fixed according to the Torah. The Peri Chadash wrote there, seif katan 19, that this is primary. Nevertheless, there is no difficulty. Certainly, according to what the Magen Avraham wrote at first, where he speaks of one who saw a person walking, etc., there is no difficulty, for perhaps he did not see him separate from his fixed place, but saw him only when he was already walking on the road. Sefer Tosefes Shabbos raised the difficulty only according to the conclusion of the Magen Avraham, that the fire separated, etc. In my humble opinion, the Magen Avraham does not mean that we say regarding the fire, whatever separated, etc. Rather, his intent is that the fire and the lamp or wood are two separate matters. Granted, the lamp and wood are fixed, but the fire over which he blesses is not fixed. Similarly, the Mordechai wrote in the first chapter of Chullin that for this reason we follow the majority of opinions in a Sanhedrin and it is not called fixed, because the speech that emerges from their mouths is considered separated. The Peri Chadash brought this there. Perforce, there too the intent is not that we say regarding speech that whatever separated separated from the majority, for the speech separated before us. Rather, the Mordechai's intent is also that the speech which separates from the speaking person is a separate matter and is not considered one entity with the person who spoke it. Therefore, although the Sanhedrin themselves are fixed, nevertheless their speech is not called fixed. The same applies here with fire and smell. The language of the Magen Avraham also indicates this, for he wrote: and perhaps this is not called fixed, etc. And it is well.
We say in the Gemara, etc. And this requires study, etc. And perhaps he holds that even the adults, etc. Meaning, that which by a child one must inquire after him did not trouble the Magen Avraham that he should have copied it, for one can say that he considered it obvious that wherever it is possible to clarify, one must clarify, for we rule that even on the basis of a majority and a presumption we do not rely where it is possible to clarify. What the baraisa needed to teach was only the law of an adult, where we say that he is certainly a non-Jew. Since for us even adults are not careful, a minor and an adult are equal, and one must ask. Therefore the Shulchan Aruch did not need to copy the law because it is so obvious.
