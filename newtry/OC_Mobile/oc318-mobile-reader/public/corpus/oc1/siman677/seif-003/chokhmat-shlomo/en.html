In my humble opinion, it now seems that there is a clear proof for this according to those who hold that it is forbidden to be stringent, from what we read in the Talmud in Berachos 33: this itself is difficult, etc., until: rather, say as follows, if he recited Havdalah in this and in that, blessings should rest on his head. What is the question? Let us say that certainly, to recite Havdalah over the cup and not recite Havdalah in prayer at all, prayer is certainly preferable to the cup. But if he recites Havdalah in prayer and does not intend to fulfill his obligation with that, but only with Havdalah over the cup, then although rabbinic commandments do not require intent, nevertheless if he intends not to fulfill, he certainly has not fulfilled. So it truly is regarding counting in siman 489, where one counts with the congregation without a blessing and intends not to fulfill, and blesses afterward at night. It is proven there in Magen Avraham that even if mitzvos do not require intent, if he intended not to fulfill, he did not fulfill, even though he counted then. If so, here it is proper to say that blessings should rest on his head, since in truth he recited Havdalah in prayer and over the cup, and it is not a blessing in vain, since he intended not to fulfill with the Havdalah in the prayer. Nevertheless he did not alter the text of the prayer, since he mentioned it in the prayer as well, and he is among those who beautify the mitzvah. But if he did not recite Havdalah in prayer at all, one who recites Havdalah over the cup is worse than one who recites Havdalah in prayer and not over the cup. What then is the question? Perforce, this proves like the view of Terumas HaDeshen: since in any event, if he wanted, he could have fulfilled his obligation with the Havdalah in prayer, once he recited Havdalah it is considered a blessing in vain. This is unlike counting, where he cannot fulfill at that time because it is daytime. Examine this, for in my humble opinion this is a clear proof. Now while there, I found this very difficult: what is the question? Perhaps the meaning is that one who recited Havdalah in this and in that means where he first recited Havdalah over the cup and afterward in prayer; in that case it is not a blessing in vain. What then is the question? Perforce, the intent must be that if so, it is an interruption in the prayer. This creates difficulty for the view of Rabbeinu Yonah, brought by Beis Yosef in Orach Chayim, siman 108, that mentioning the event of other days in prayer is not an interruption. He learned this from what we say, that if he recited Havdalah in both, he fulfilled. But here it is compelled that Havdalah is an interruption. Now, if not for the words of Rabbeinu Yonah, it would seem that there is no proof at all from Havdalah. Certainly, where he mentions something from whose very content it is recognizable that it does not belong to the day, it is an interruption, since from within it itself it is recognizable that it is an interruption. But regarding Havdalah on Motzaei Shabbos, if he had not prayed at all, it would be relevant to recite Havdalah in this prayer. If so, from the body of the prayer it is not recognizable that it is an interruption, for it is not known whether he prayed or not. Therefore, whenever it is not recognizable from the thing itself, it is not an interruption. This follows the approach of what Turei Zahav challenged there, though I explained the reason more fully. Yet even according to this, our question remains difficult, for even if he first recited Havdalah over the cup, it is also not recognizable whether he already recited Havdalah over the cup or not. Therefore it seems that the main distinction is that there, prayer draws prayer after it. If it were not the time for prayer, there would be no place to pray as compensation; only because it is a time for prayer does he also pray for compensation. If so, the obligatory prayer of the current time causes the second prayer, and therefore its law is like it, and they are considered like one prayer. There is support for this from what we say elsewhere: once permission has been granted, it has been granted. Therefore it is not an interruption. But if we say that after reciting Havdalah over the cup one need not recite Havdalah in prayer, then he has no Havdalah in prayer at all, and if he recited Havdalah it is indeed an interruption, and the question is proper. But if we say like the view of Rabbeinu Yonah, who holds that anywhere one mentions something related to the event it is not an interruption, even where this reason does not apply, the question is indeed difficult: let it say that the meaning is where he first recited Havdalah over the cup. This requires very great examination, and it proves like Turei Zahav and not like Rabbeinu Yonah; examine it.
