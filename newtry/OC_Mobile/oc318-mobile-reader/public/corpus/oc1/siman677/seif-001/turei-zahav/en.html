Must give a perutah to the homeowner, etc. In Terumas HaDeshen, siman 101, he wrote regarding a married lodger who wants to kindle with a blessing away from his home, that one great authority wrote that he may do so. Although we say in the Gemara, once I married, I said, now I certainly do not need, that means only that he is not obligated; but if he wants, he may kindle, and it is not a blessing in vain, for he is included among the mehadrin. Another great authority wrote that since he does not need it, he is exempt, and if he does it he is called a commoner, and all the more so it is a blessing in vain, since we did not find this kind of mehadrin in the Talmud. Terumas HaDeshen concludes that he is properly called included among the mehadrin, for just as there is beautification in a lamp for each person in one house, so too there is beautification in a lamp for the husband and a lamp for his wife in two places. The Tanna did not definitively teach this kind of mehadrin because it is not so common. Since we find in the Talmud mehadrin and mehadrin min hamehadrin, this is not like adding to the Talmud in a way that detracts. Until here. Beis Yosef wrote on this: it does not seem to me that one may rely on this to recite an unnecessary blessing. Until here. Maharshal wrote in his abbreviated rulings as follows: a man and his wife certainly are sufficiently covered by one lamp. A married lodger, if he does not know that his wife is kindling for him, may kindle and bless. But if he knows clearly that she is kindling for him, he should not kindle, unless he is in a separate house because of one who sees; then he should kindle but not bless. Where he did not know that she kindled for him and came home that night, he must go back and kindle and bless, because the obligation of the Sages has taken effect upon him. But where he does not want to fulfill through his wife's lighting, which she kindles for him at home, it appears that this is not in his power, and he cannot bless. Until here. Now we have not yet merited to see the longer words of Maharshal, to know the source of his words. But when we look into it ourselves, the words of Terumas HaDeshen appear correct. What the one great authority wrote, that we did not find this beautification in the Talmud, does not seem correct to me at all, for when the Gemara said mehadrin, it meant that presumably each person would do so, and it is pleasant for every person to be included among mehadrin. This is not so in this matter, which is similar to other blessings: here the choice is given to a person to do as he wishes. If he wants, he fulfills with the blessing that his wife recites when she kindles, and if he does not want, he blesses for himself. It seems appropriate to compare this to what is written at the beginning of the third chapter of Berachos: Abaye said, two who ate have a mitzvah to divide, and Rashi explained this both regarding the blessing of zimun and the blessing of Hamotzi. Rosh wrote that the world did not practice this way, and also in Rashi we did not hear that they practiced this way regarding the blessing of Hamotzi, and so too in the incident of Bar Kappara, etc. Thus, in any event there is no prohibition if each one blesses Hamotzi; it is only that Rosh wrote that they did not practice this way. Heaven forbid that Rosh should say it is a blessing in vain, for Rashi even included this matter under the category of mitzvah. Furthermore, we find regarding the blessing of Yotzer Or in the commentary of Nimmukei Yosef, Responsa Rosh: I say it quietly with the prayer leader, although if I heard it from the prayer leader I would fulfill. The reason is lest I not concentrate properly on the words of the one blessing. Likewise regarding Birkat Hamazon, Beis Yosef in siman 183 wrote in the name of Orchot Chayim in the name of Rabbeinu Peretz that each person should recite Birkat Hamazon even when they recite zimun, because he may not be able to concentrate, etc.; but Maharam would not bless quietly, etc. Until here. We see that in a matter where one can fulfill through his friend's blessing but does not want to, he has no prohibition in his hand, since his intent is for the sake of Heaven. And the one who does not want to bless for himself, as Maharam did, also has intent for the sake of Heaven, not to multiply blessings when it is possible to fulfill through his friend's blessing. The same applies in this matter, and all the more so, since he is not with his wife and does not hear the blessing, he can say: I do not fulfill with my wife's blessing when I am not present, for the intent of the hearer and the one who recites is required. Even if he is in his home and does not want to fulfill with his wife's blessing, I do not know what prohibition there is if he blesses for himself. How is this different from a minor who reached the age of education, who kindles for himself with a blessing, while it is obvious that he fulfills through his father's blessing? We have already seen the practice that anyone who goes away from his home during Chanukah takes Chanukah lamps with him to kindle in the place where the spirit leads him to go, even though the members of his household who are with his wife kindle in his home. Although there is no proof from this, since the household members may not intend to discharge the homeowner's obligation but only their own, nevertheless that itself is difficult: why must he take the lamps with him? Let the household members with his wife intend and kindle for him as well. Rather, it is more pleasant to recite the blessing himself, as it seems to me in this matter. Rema also ruled this way nearby.
