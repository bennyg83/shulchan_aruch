There, seif 4. "What remains on the eighth day from the oil needed for the measure of lighting, etc." NB: Regarding what everyone asked, that the words of the Shulchan Aruch contradict one another, see my derashah for Chanukah of the year 5601, where I resolved this by distinguishing between an object of issur and an object of mitzvah; see there, with God's help. Regarding what everyone asked, that the words of the Shulchan Aruch here contradict what he wrote above in siman 672, the Taz brought in the name of Mahari to answer that here it speaks where he placed it without specification, while above it speaks where he stipulated only for the measure of lighting. The Taz challenged this from the Gemara in chapter Nigmar HaDin, that what is not fit for him is not seized; just as this applies according to Abaye regarding designation, so too according to Rava regarding an act. We already wrote above about this. Now it appears as follows, based on what we proved long ago in our novellae to Hilchot Sukkah, still in manuscript, against the Hagahot Ashri brought by the Magen Avraham, who holds that the sukkah does not become forbidden until one sits in it because designation is not significant. I proved from the teaching that one who set aside seven etrogim for seven days may fulfill the mitzvah with each one and eat it the next day. If designation were not significant here, then at bein hashmashot it would not be muktzeh, and there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbat; why should it not be permitted immediately? Perforce, here designation is significant. The reason appears to be that since a sukkah is forbidden in benefit because of "Chag HaSukkot," just as a chag-offering is for God, etc., and designation for intrinsic sanctity is significant, as the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 42, so too with a sukkah. The same applies to lulav, since we derive lulav from sukkah; see there. If so, the same applies to a Chanukah lamp, since it is similar to the menorah, as Rashi wrote s.v. hadlakah oseh mitzvah, similar to the menorah; here too designation is significant like intrinsic sanctity. Therefore, according to Abaye, with designation alone, since it is only speech and not an act, we say that what is not fit for him is not seized. So too according to Rava, with an act, in those cases where designation is not significant, since there is only an act, we say that for what is fit for him it is considered an act, but for what is not fit for him the act is not considered an act; the excess is as though it is not there, and no act was done at all with the excess. The act done to the fit portion does not forbid the portion not fit for him. But here with oil, since designation alone also forbids it, at the moment of the act designation takes effect on all of it, for we do not know whether he will need it or not, nor which portion will be extra. Thus the designation takes effect on all of it. If afterward he also performs an act, since there is both an act and designation here, all of it becomes forbidden. In this case the act done with part helps for the whole, because he designated the whole for this and it became like one body. The act upon part of it helps for all of it, since designation makes it one matter and not separate, and once he performed an act on part that was fit for him, this strong act helps for the whole and all is forbidden. In this all agree, and the words of the Mechaber are well resolved. According to this, one need not say that there it speaks where he stipulated; even without a stipulation, there it speaks where he did not designate it beforehand and there is only an act, so the Taz's reasoning applies, that what is not fit for him is not seized. But here it speaks where he first designated all of it and then lit it; with designation together with an act, all of it becomes forbidden. This is why the She'iltot, the source of this law, and all the poskim were precise and concluded, "for it was set aside for its mitzvah." Why was this necessary? It is known that this is why it was forbidden initially. Rather, it teaches that he set it aside from the beginning, so there was also designation; therefore it concluded that it had been set aside for its mitzvah from the beginning, meaning designation together with the act. This resolves it. Examine carefully. This is my present inclination. There is support for this distinction from what we hold in Choshen Mishpat siman 275 seif 7 and in the Sema there, that if one took possession of the property of a convert who died without heirs, he acquired only the amount where the yoke can go and return; but if the property is called by the convert's name, he acquires all of it. Thus, if it is not called by the convert's name, the act of possession helps only for itself and not for the other fields; but if it is called by the convert's name, the act of possession helps for everything called by his name. So too here, if designation is significant and all of it is called the Chanukah lamp, the later act upon part of the oil that burned helps for all of it, as though an act had been done with all the oil, and there is a strong act here. But if he did not designate it, the act on the oil does not help for the remainder. Examine carefully. Also, what the Magen Avraham and Pri Chadash wrote, that it is forbidden to keep it for the next year because of concern for mishap, is difficult. What proof is there from the other cases? There the issur is Torah law, so they decreed because of concern for mishap; but Chanukah itself is rabbinic, so why should we be concerned for mishap? It would be a decree upon a decree. However, see the Chanukah derashah I cited above, where I proved also from this that they treated it like Torah law because of the beracha; see there and examine carefully. Regarding leftover Chanukah oil, whether one may light it in the synagogue to pray by it, it appears obvious that it is permitted. Although it is forbidden in benefit, we hold that mitzvot were not given for benefit. Not only according to those who hold that even rabbinic mitzvot were not given for benefit, but even according to those who hold that rabbinic mitzvot were given for benefit, that is only where the prohibition of benefit is Torah law, for then we follow Torah law, under which he is not obligated in this mitzvah and thus benefits as one who receives reward as one not commanded. But with a rabbinic prohibition of benefit, certainly one may fulfill with it a mitzvah of their words either way: if we follow Torah law it is entirely permitted, and if we follow rabbinic law, then rabbinically he is commanded in it, and something in which one is commanded is upon him like a yoke and was not given for benefit. If so, Chanukah oil, which is rabbinic, may be used for a mitzvah purpose. Moreover, even with the Chanukah lamp itself there are those who permit use for a sacred purpose. Even those who forbid there do so only while it is burning for the mitzvah of Chanukah; then one may say that if he uses it for a sacred purpose it is not recognizable that it was lit for the mitzvah. Or one may say that it is degrading the mitzvah, since degradation applies even from one mitzvah to another. But once it remains from the Chanukah lamp and one burns it separately, recognizable display is certainly not required, and degradation of the mitzvah does not apply; if he merely burns it because it is forbidden in benefit, that is a greater degradation than lighting it in the synagogue. See also what the Taz wrote similarly above in siman 154, that even with an item forbidden to lower from its sanctity, that is only if it is still fit for its mitzvah; but if it is not fit for its mitzvah, it is better to use it for a lighter mitzvah than to bury it as is. If so, all the more here, it is better to use it for sanctity than to bury it as is. This is simple in my opinion, and I did not need to write it, except that I saw that the Peri Megadim in Mishbetzot wrote the opposite and permitted only by adding more oil and because of a double doubt, so I wrote to remove that view from the heart. With respect for his Torah honor, he erred greatly here and did not consider all that I have written. See there and examine carefully.
