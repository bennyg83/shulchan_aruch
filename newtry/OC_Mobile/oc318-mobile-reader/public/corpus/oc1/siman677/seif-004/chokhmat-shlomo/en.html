The oil remaining on the eighth day from the oil needed for the measure of lighting, etc. Note: regarding what everyone asked as a contradiction in the words of Shulchan Aruch, see the Chanukah discourse of the year 5601, where I wrote to resolve this by distinguishing between a matter of prohibition and a matter of mitzvah; see there, with Hashem's help. Regarding what everyone asked as a contradiction between the words of Shulchan Aruch here and above, siman 672, Turei Zahav brought in the name of Mahari to answer that here it is speaking where he placed the oil without specification, and above it is speaking where he stipulated that it was only for the measure of lighting. Turei Zahav wondered from the Talmud in chapter Nigmar Hadin, that what is not fit for him is not seized by it; and just as this is so for Abaye regarding designation, the same applies for Rava regarding an act. We already wrote above about this. Now it seems as follows, based on what we already proved in our novellae to the laws of Sukkah, which are still in manuscript, to prove against Hagahos Ashri brought by Magen Avraham, who holds that the sukkah is not forbidden until one sits in it, because designation is not significant. I proved from what is said: if one set aside seven esrogim for seven days, he fulfills with each one and eats it the next day. If we say that designation is not significant in this case, then at twilight it was not muktzeh, and if so there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos; why should it not be permitted immediately? Perforce, in this case designation is significant. The reason appears to be that since the sukkah is forbidden for benefit by force of the verse, the festival of Sukkos, just as the festival offering is to Hashem, etc., and regarding designation and inherent sanctity, designation is significant, as Magen Avraham wrote in siman 42, so too with the sukkah. The same applies to lulav, since we derive lulav from sukkah; see there. If so, according to this, the same applies to the Chanukah lamp, since it is like the Menorah, as Rashi wrote, beginning with lighting performs the mitzvah: it is like the Menorah. Therefore here too designation is significant, as with inherent sanctity. Therefore it is well understood. Certainly according to Abaye, regarding designation alone, since it is only speech and not an act, we say that what is not fit for him is not seized by it. The same applies to an act according to Rava in cases where designation is not significant: since there is only an act here, we say that for what is fit for him it is considered an act, but for what is not fit for him the act is not considered an act. What is extra is as though it is not there, and in the whole of it no act was done in the extra portion at all. The act on the part that was fit does not work to forbid the part that was not fit. But here with oil, since designation alone also forbids it, then at the time of the act the designation takes effect on all of it, for we did not know whether he would need it or not, and on which part the name leftover would fall. If so, designation takes effect on all of it. Then, if afterward he also performs an act, since here there is both an act and designation, all of it is forbidden. In this case, the act on part of it is effective for all of it, because in any event he designated all of it for this, and it becomes like one body; the act on part of it is effective for all of it because it became one through designation. Designation is effective at least to make it one matter and not a separate matter. Then, since he performed an act with part of it that was fit for him, and that was a strong act, this is effective for all of it and all of it becomes forbidden. In this everyone agrees, and the words of the Mechaber are well understood. According to this, there is no need to say that there it is speaking where he stipulated. Rather, even without stipulation, there it is speaking where he did not designate it first, and it has only an act; therefore it follows the reasoning of Turei Zahav, that what is not fit for him is not seized by it. But here it is speaking where he first designated all of it and afterward kindled it. With designation together with the act, all of it is forbidden. Therefore the She'iltos, which is the source of this law, and all the authorities, were precise and concluded, because it was set aside for its mitzvah. Why do I need all this? This is already known, that for this reason it was forbidden initially. Rather, the intent is to teach that he set it aside initially for this, so it was also designation. Therefore it concluded, because he set it aside for its mitzvah from the beginning, and this is designation together with the act; and it is well understood. Examine this, for so my view now inclines. There is support for the above distinction from what we hold in Choshen Mishpat, siman 275, section 7, and in Sema there, that when one takes possession of the property of a convert who died without heirs, he acquires only as far as a team of oxen goes and returns. But if it is called by the name of the convert, he acquires all of it; see there. Thus, if it is not called by the name of the convert, the act of taking possession is effective only for itself and not for the other fields; but if it is called by the name of the convert, the act of taking possession is effective for all of it, for everything called by his name; see there. If so, here too, if designation is significant and all of it is called the Chanukah lamp, the subsequent act with part of the oil that burned is effective, and it is as if an act was done with all the oil, so there is a strong act here. But if he did not designate it, the act done with the oil is not effective for what remains; examine this. Also what Magen Avraham and Pri Chadash wrote, that it is forbidden to keep them for the next year because of concern for a mishap, is difficult: what proof is there from those other cases? There, the prohibition is Torah law, and therefore they decreed because of concern for a mishap. But Chanukah itself is rabbinic prohibition; why should we be concerned for a mishap? It would be a decree upon a decree. But see the Chanukah discourse that I cited above; there I also proved from this that they treated it exactly like Torah law because of the blessing; see there and examine. Now regarding leftover oil in the Chanukah lamp, whether one may light it in the synagogue in order to pray by it: it seems obvious that it is permitted. Although it is forbidden for benefit, nevertheless we hold that mitzvos were not given for benefit. Not only according to those who hold that even rabbinic mitzvos were not given for benefit, but even according to those who hold that rabbinic mitzvos were given for benefit, that is only regarding whether a Torah-level prohibition of benefit is involved; in that case we follow the Torah level, for he is not obligated by the Torah in this mitzvah, and if so he benefits by Torah law, like one who receives reward as one who is not commanded yet performs. But regarding a rabbinic prohibition of benefit, certainly it is permitted to fulfill with it one of their mitzvos from either side: if we follow Torah law, it is entirely permitted; and if we follow rabbinic law, then rabbinically he is commanded in this, and something in which he is commanded is upon him like a yoke and was not given for benefit. If so, the Chanukah lamp, which is rabbinic, may be used for a mitzvah use. Moreover, even regarding the Chanukah lamp itself, some permit a sacred use. Even those who forbid it there do so only while it is burning for the mitzvah of the Chanukah lamp, for then one may say that if he uses it for holiness, it is not recognizable that it was kindled for the mitzvah. Or one may say that it is a disgrace to the mitzvah, for moving from mitzvah to mitzvah can also involve disgrace. But if something already remains from the Chanukah lamp, which they burn by itself, a recognizable sign is certainly not needed in this. Nor does disgrace of the mitzvah apply, for if he burns it just so, that is a greater disgrace, since he burns it only because it is forbidden for benefit. That is a greater disgrace to the mitzvah than lighting it in the synagogue. See also what Turei Zahav wrote similarly above in siman 154, that even for an item that is forbidden to lower from its sanctity, this is only if it is still fit for its mitzvah; but if it is not fit for its mitzvah, it is better to use it for a lesser mitzvah than to bury it as it is. If so, all the more so here, it is better to use it for the sake of holiness than to bury it as it is. This is obvious in my view, and I would not have needed to put it in writing. But since I saw that Pri Megadim in Mishbetzos wrote the opposite, and permitted only by adding to it and because of a double doubt, I wrote this to remove that thought from the heart of that sefer.
