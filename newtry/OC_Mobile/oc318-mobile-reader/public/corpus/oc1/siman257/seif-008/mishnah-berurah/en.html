A pot on top of a stove. This means that he places it over the opening at the top of the stove, and the bottom of the pot does not touch the coals below. The same applies if it stands on an iron stand inside our stoves. According to the Mechaber there, the food must also be fully cooked.
Because of the fire beneath them. Even if he scatters ashes over the coals, it is nevertheless considered something that adds heat.
Any amount, etc. This means in the manner the Mechaber describes immediately afterward.
Therefore, where, etc. He now briefly explains what he wrote at the beginning of the seif, that although it is permitted to leave the pot there, and what he wrote at the end, that it is permitted when the coverings do not touch the pot.
And the bottom of the pot does not, etc. The Mechaber follows his own view at the end of siman 253, seif 1. According to the Rama there in his gloss, it is permitted even if the bottom of the pot touches the coals, since the pot is uncovered above and is not insulated in garments.
Wide. The same applies if he places a wide board over the mouth of the pot so that the garments covering it from above do not touch the sides of the pot.
This is not insulation. All this applies when there is a fire in the stove. If there is no fire in the stove at all, some say it is not forbidden to place a pot on it and cover it with garments. Although the heat of the stove beneath it is substantial and adds heat, it is permitted, because the Sages forbade placing a pot on olive dregs and similar materials only because they are suitable for insulating within them, and they decreed lest one insulate in glowing ashes. Here, however, the earth of the stove is not suitable for insulation, so there is no concern. Another reason is that olive dregs generate heat by themselves, whereas the stove is hot only because of the fire and continually cools. Some authorities are stringent unless one places a vessel or wide board over the pot and then covers it with garments, as stated in the Shulchan Aruch. Nevertheless, one should not protest against those who are lenient. Based on this, one can understand the practice of placing coffee or a cooked dish on top of an oven before Shabbos and covering it with garments. Although according to the stringent authorities this is improper because the oven's heat adds heat, unless one places a wide board over the pot, one should not protest, because they have authorities on whom to rely. If, however, one places sand on top of the oven, even if the pot is not entirely insulated within it, but only its bottom stands in the sand and it is covered above with garments, this is certainly forbidden. Sand is a material that adds heat, and we decree lest one insulate the entire pot within it. If he turns a wide vessel over the pot or places a wide board over it and then covers it with garments, it is permitted even if the pot stands halfway in the sand, as stated by Chayei Adam, klal 20.
It is likewise permitted, etc. Until now the discussion concerned a stove. The Mechaber now explains that one may also place a pot in an oven before Shabbos. Even if the oven is not swept out or covered with ash, there is no concern that he will stoke the coals when he places a raw piece of meat in the pot. The same applies if the food is fully cooked. Otherwise, it is forbidden because of shehiyah, as stated above in siman 253, seif 1. Although the entire pot is inside the oven and the mouth of the oven is also covered with garments, and the oven's heat certainly adds heat, this is not considered insulation and is permitted because the garments do not touch the pot.
Provided that it does not, etc. If the bottom of the pot touched the coals, the Mechaber holds that this contact itself is considered insulation in something that adds heat and is forbidden. The Rama there in his gloss holds that this is not considered insulation; see there.
And they seal the mouth of the oven with clay. Regarding insulation, sealing with clay is not required at all, even according to the Rama. Rather, the ruling means that when the oven is sealed, it is permitted in every case, even when no raw piece of meat is placed in the pot and the food is still at the beginning of cooking. Without sealing, it would be forbidden to leave the pot in an oven that is neither swept out nor covered with ash, because of concern that one will stoke the coals. When it is sealed, it is permitted. Know that, as explained above, only raw meat does not create a concern of stoking, because stoking will not cook it sufficiently for the night meal. A pie, legumes, or dough products cook quickly, and even when placed in the oven raw close to nightfall, there is concern that after nightfall he will stoke the coals to hasten their cooking for the night meal. The oven must therefore be sealed with clay. In places where they do not customarily seal the oven with clay, it is forbidden to place such foods in the oven close to nightfall unless a raw piece of meat is placed among them, unless there is enough time for them to reach at least ma'achal ben Derusai before nightfall, or the coals are covered with ashes, as stated in siman 253.
It is permitted according to all opinions. Nevertheless, some say one must be careful not to insulate the pot in the oven with coals surrounding it on every side, because this is considered insulation in something that adds heat by means of the coals themselves. Others are lenient even in this case, because the oven is sealed with clay and he will not come to stoke the coals. This is also implied by the Or Zarua. It is proper to be stringent l'chatchila, also because the coals may remain glowing until the next day and he will be unable to remove the pot, as stated above in siman 253, seif 1, in the Rama's gloss. In any event, where the custom is not to seal the closure with clay, it is certainly forbidden to insulate the pot among the coals, even if its contents are fully cooked.
It is a mitzvah to insulate hot food for Shabbos. One for whom hot food is harmful may eat cold food.
And anyone who, etc. In our times, due to our many sins, a serious failing is common among those who contemptuously separate themselves from the mitzvah of eating hot food on Shabbos. Ultimately, this leads them to prohibition, because they instruct a non-Jew to heat hot water for them on Shabbos. Many times this also leads to Torah prohibitions involving three primary melachos: cooking, kindling, and extinguishing. They place the vessel containing tea leaves on the hot-water urn, called in our language a samovar, while the coals are burning, which constitutes cooking. Each time they remove the vessel and then return it, they transgress kindling and extinguishing, because while the vessel rests on the urn the fire is partially diminished, and when it is removed the coals flare up. One who does this intentionally is disqualified by Torah law from giving testimony and taking an oath, and cuts off his soul from the land of life, for it is a transgression punishable by kares and stoning. Every conscientious person should therefore not depart from the Jewish custom of insulating hot food for Shabbos and fulfilling the mitzvah of oneg Shabbos, as our ancestors always practiced. One who guards a mitzvah, etc. The Baal HaMaor writes in the chapter Kirah: 'Anyone who does not eat hot food, etc., must prepare, cook, insulate, delight in Shabbos, and grow stout; he is a believer and merits the end of days.'
