(Seif 3) If it became known to him on Shabbos, etc., that human dignity is great—see: the source of this law is from the Gemara chapter 3 of Berachos. I find it difficult on the Gemara: why did it not challenge from what is said in the chapter Kol Kitvei on 21a, that R. Yosi said, "I never violated the words of my colleagues—if they told me to go up to the platform I would go up," and behold there is truly a positive-commandment prohibition as explained in chapter 2 of Kesubos; why was he permitted to violate a positive commandment because of his colleague's dignity? Perforce human dignity is great and pushes aside a Torah prohibition. Tosafos there in Kol Kitvei wondered the opposite—what prohibition is there in this ascent? See Magen Avraham [siman 128] what he wrote on this; but if there is a prohibition their wonder is resolved, and behold on the contrary if there is a prohibition how was he permitted to act because of human dignity? There it is a "stand and act," and one must say that for a positive commandment it works well that human dignity pushes it aside, but for a negative commandment it does not push aside. From this there is proof for the view of those who hold a negative commandment is stricter than a positive one, and that which a positive commandment pushes aside a negative one is a scriptural decree; but for those who hold a positive commandment is stricter in the sugya of "they feed him the lighter of the light"—see there—the later authorities brought their dispute; it is very difficult from here, for if a positive commandment pushes aside human dignity, all the more so a negative commandment. It is also difficult: behold Tosafos wrote there that the prohibition is that it is a blessing in vain, and behold this is in the negative commandment of "you shall not take [the Name in vain]," and how was it permitted because of human dignity? For those who hold every manner of blessing is rabbinic it works well; but for those who hold even a manner of blessing is Torah law it is difficult why it did not challenge from here—and this requires analysis for now, investigate and understand. I was in doubt regarding the law that human dignity is great and pushes aside a Torah prohibition: if concern for disgrace continues for him through his negligence, does it push aside a Torah prohibition or not? At first glance it would seem to learn a fortiori from what we hold that a positive commandment pushes aside a negative one, yet nevertheless for a positive commandment here through negligence it does not push aside, as Tosafos wrote—and behold there is an a fortiori: a positive commandment pushes aside a negative one even a Torah negative commandment, yet through negligence it does not push aside; all the more so for human dignity, which does not push aside a Torah negative commandment but only a rabbinic one—all the more so through negligence it does not push aside. Yet if so it is difficult: what does the Gemara challenge in Berachos (20a) in what Rav said—one who finds shaatnez in his garment removes it even in the marketplace—and it challenges from what the master said that human dignity is great and pushes aside a negative commandment; what is the challenge? Say that there it deals with a case not through negligence, therefore it pushes aside; not so for one who finds shaatnez in his garment, which is through negligence, for he could have checked first whether it contains shaatnez—for regarding touching consecrated items we apply light to severe, and likewise in "their valuations became mixed" Tosafos considered it a negligent positive commandment, and all the more so here which is a negligent positive commandment—therefore it does not push aside; what is the challenge? It is even more difficult from what is said in Eruvin [41b] where it says there: R. Nachman said—if one became insane he has only four cubits—and it challenges: what is he teaching us? It is obvious: now one whom gentiles took out has only four cubits; one who became insane from distress—what is the challenge? Behold afterward we say there that he asked Rav Huna: if he needed to relieve himself, what? He told them: human dignity is great and pushes aside a Torah negative commandment; if so, say Shmuel teaches us that one who became insane has only four cubits even if he needs to relieve himself—there is no need to say human dignity because it is through negligence of becoming insane; rather it is certainly proven that even through negligence we say human dignity is great. In truth it is difficult: what is the reason and whence does the Gemara know to say so and to challenge—perhaps through negligence we do not say human dignity is great? This requires analysis. Behold in what Shulchan Aruch wrote: if it became known to him on Shabbos while in a karmelis that the tallis on him is invalid, etc.—behold such is the view of the Mordechai, who proved thus from the Gemara of Menachos that he establishes it in a rabbinic karmelis, etc. At first glance there is no reason for the matter, for although from the side of Shabbos it is only a rabbinic prohibition, nevertheless there is a Torah prohibition here because he is wearing a four-cornered garment without tzitzis; from this the Mordechai proved there is no prohibition to wear a garment without tzitzis, only where it is in his power to make tzitzis is he obligated to do so; but where it is impossible for him, it is permitted to go thus—and R. Eliezer of Metz disagrees. To reconcile according to his view the Mordechai's difficulty, it appears R. Eliezer of Metz will explain per the Rambam's view that a doubtful case is lenient from the Torah and strict rabbinically. Behold the view of some decisors that there is a distinction between a doubt of karet or not—for in a doubt of karet the Rambam concedes the doubt is strict from the Torah. Therefore it works well: behold in Berachos the Gemara challenges per the one who says human dignity does not push aside a Torah prohibition, from a baraisa of one who was going to slaughter his Paschal offering, etc., and answers that Scripture revealed "and for his sister," and challenges and we conclude from it, and answers "sit and do not act is different," and Rashi challenged there: behold for an unattended corpse it is "stand and act," and Rashi answered that there this prohibition is not stated regarding an unattended corpse—see there in Chiddushei Yeshanim and Penei Yehoshua what they wrote on the intent of Rashi's words. Behold in my work Sefer HaChaim [I wrote] that this will depend on the dispute whether impurity is set aside in the community or permitted—for per the one who says impurity was permitted in the community, one can say likewise for an unattended corpse; but if we say impurity is set aside in the community, behold it is also said "in its place at its time"—even in impurity there is no reason that an individual is set aside and the community is not set aside, yet nevertheless it is set aside; if so, likewise an unattended corpse is set aside, and if so one can again say we conclude from it that even for "stand and act" human dignity pushes aside a Torah prohibition. Yet in Sefer HaChaim we wrote: behold in the Gemara it asks there whether burial is because of disgrace or because of atonement—and see in Tosafos who explained that the question is whether it is because of disgrace alone or disgrace together with atonement—for disgrace certainly exists too; if so the Gemara again does not challenge and we do not conclude from it that one must say it is different there because human dignity together with atonement pushes aside the prohibition, not so alone—only that we wrote since this question remains in the Gemara whether because of disgrace or atonement, therefore the Gemara challenges and we conclude from it and establish that burial is because of atonement, and therefore it pushed aside and did not establish, only that one must say "sit and do not act is different" and a nazir with an unattended corpse. In truth one can say he holds like the one who says impurity was permitted in the community, and always one can explain because of atonement—and that is specifically there; but in Menachos with Mar brei d'Ravina—for Ravina holds in Yoma 6b explicitly that impurity is set aside in the community, and if so Mar brei presumably holds like his father as we say many times in the Gemara—since he holds it is set aside, the Gemara's difficulty works well and we can conclude from it. Yet one can reject: one can say there it is because of atonement together with disgrace, but human dignity alone does not push aside a Torah prohibition—yet behold this is an unresolved question and it is a doubt; therefore it works well that for Shabbos, which is court-imposed death, the doubt is strict from the Torah, and if so perhaps there with an unattended corpse it is because of atonement but human dignity does not push aside a Torah prohibition, and therefore he must answer with a rabbinic karmelis; but for the obligation of tzitzis behold it is only a positive commandment and the doubt is lenient from the Torah, and one can say burial is because of disgrace, and automatically it is proven that human dignity pushes aside a Torah prohibition even for "stand and act"—only rabbinically the doubt is strict, and again rabbinically human dignity is great; and the view of R. Eliezer of Metz who holds like the Rambam works well. But without human dignity one can certainly say there is a Torah prohibition to go without tzitzis, and not like the Mordechai—and the Mordechai can be said to hold like those who hold a Torah doubt is strict from the Torah, therefore he proves his law well as I explained here, investigate and understand; see what I wrote on this matter somewhat in my work on Yoreh Deah siman 372 regarding the law of priests' impurity in the aforementioned Rashi—see there and you will understand well, investigate and understand.
