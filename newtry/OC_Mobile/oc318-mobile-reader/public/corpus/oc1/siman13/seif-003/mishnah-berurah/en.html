(8) On Shabbos. If he knew before Shabbos that the garment lacked valid tzitzis but forgot to attach them, it appears that he is likewise not penalized. A proof may be brought from siman 355, seif 3, in Rama’s gloss concerning those who forgot to make an eruv. Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger.
(9) While in a karmelis. A karmelis is an area where carrying is prohibited only D’Rabbanan. He therefore need not remove the garment, because human dignity overrides a D’Rabbanan prohibition. Although tzitzis is a positive commandment according to the Torah, a Torah commandment is not ordinarily overridden because of human dignity. Here, however, the Torah did not prohibit wearing a garment without tzitzis; it commanded one to attach tzitzis. Since he cannot attach them on Shabbos, he does not violate the positive commandment, and only a D’Rabbanan prohibition remains. Human dignity therefore permits him to keep the tallis on. In a true public domain, where carrying is prohibited according to the Torah, he must remove it, whether it is a tallis gadol or a tallis katan, even if doing so leaves him exposed, because human dignity does not override such a Torah prohibition. The laws of Shabbos discuss whether a true public domain exists nowadays. If the wound section is intact and the invalidity affects only the loose ends, one may combine this with Ri’s view above, which validates it, and he need not remove it immediately, but must hurry home.
(10) Invalid. This includes either where a corner of the garment was severed, leaving only three corners, or where one entire tzitzis became severed. Even if it was already invalid when he left home, he is not penalized so long as he was unaware of the defect when he put it on, because human dignity is great. Even our tallis gadol, which is worn only for prayer and whose removal is not especially humiliating, need not be removed, because the Sages did not distinguish among garments. Rabbi Akiva Eiger questions this ruling.
(11) Beneath his garments. Although afterward it is not noticeable that he is walking without a tallis katan, the act of removing it publicly is especially humiliating.
(12) One of the tzitzis. This applies whether he arrived at the synagogue intending to put on his tallis and discovered before putting it on that the tzitzis were invalid, but would be embarrassed to sit publicly without a tallis, in which case he may wear it without a blessing, or whether he noticed while already wearing it that the tzitzis had become severed, in which case he likewise need not remove it. Birkei Yosef, Artzos HaChayim, and other later authorities. The later authorities agree that everything depends upon whether the wearer is personally embarrassed. If he is embarrassed, he may wear it without a blessing; if not, the leniency of human dignity does not apply.
(13) That he may wear it. This applies only if he learned of the invalidity that day. If he knew before Shabbos that the tzitzis were invalid, he may not wear it on Shabbos because he should have repaired it beforehand. If he forgot, one may be lenient. Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger.
(14) Because of human dignity. This applies only if he cannot obtain a borrowed tallis in the synagogue.
(15) Specifically on Shabbos. On Shabbos only a D’Rabbanan prohibition remains, as explained above. During the week, however, he continuously neglects a Torah obligation requiring him to attach tzitzis. Even passive neglect of a Torah commandment is not overridden because of human dignity unless the humiliation is great. Therefore, if one sees before putting on a tallis gadol that it lacks valid tzitzis, he may not wear it, because sitting without a tallis is only minor embarrassment according to Rama. Likewise, if he discovers while walking in the public domain during the week that one tzitzis of his tallis gadol became severed, he must remove it immediately, because nowadays removing a tallis gadol in the street involves only minor embarrassment. If he discovers the defect in the synagogue after already putting on the tallis, many later authorities are lenient and do not require immediate removal, because removing it publicly in the synagogue is highly embarrassing. He should hurry home, or into the anteroom of the synagogue, and remove it there. Chayei Adam is stringent, maintaining that removal of a tallis gadol is only minor embarrassment, since people ordinarily remove their talleisim publicly in the synagogue. Peri Megadim likewise adopts this stringent view in his introduction to the laws of tzitzis. The proper solution is therefore that of Artzos HaChayim: he should declare the garment ownerless, thereby exempting it from tzitzis, and reclaim it upon reaching home.
(16) It is forbidden. Magen Avraham concludes that Rama’s weekday stringency does not refer to the earlier case of a tallis katan. If one discovers that the tzitzis of his tallis katan are invalid, removing it from beneath his clothing would cause great humiliation, whether in the synagogue or in public, and he need not remove it. Human dignity overrides even passive violation of a Torah commandment where the humiliation is great. He must nevertheless hurry home, or into the anteroom of the synagogue, and remove it there, even if he is occupied with a mitzvah in the synagogue, because a Torah obligation is involved. On Shabbos he need not hurry to leave the synagogue while occupied with a mitzvah. One who notices that another person’s tzitzis are severed should not tell him until he reaches home, meaning that he should call him into the house and tell him there to remove it. Magen Avraham further writes that if no replacement tzitzis are available in the city, the weekday law is like the Shabbos law. The same applies to a tallis obligated only D’Rabbanan, such as a borrowed tallis after thirty days.
