For human dignity is great, etc. Human dignity overrides a D’Rabbanan prohibition. Carrying in a karmelis is likewise D’Rabbanan, and nowadays we have no true public domain, as stated in siman 303, seif 18.
And even a tallis, etc. Although it is not noticeable afterward if he walks without a tallis katan, removing it is humiliating. Beit Yosef writes that even our tallis gadol, which is not ordinarily worn as clothing and whose removal would not be humiliating, need not be removed, because the Sages did not distinguish among garments.
Human dignity. Although tzitzis is a positive commandment according to the Torah, and a Torah prohibition is not ordinarily overridden because of human dignity, it is nevertheless permitted here. The Torah did not say, “Do not wear a garment without tzitzis”; rather, it commanded one to attach tzitzis. Since on Shabbos he cannot attach them, he does not violate the positive commandment. There is nevertheless a D’Rabbanan prohibition, and it is permitted only because of human dignity, unlike Olas Tamid. During the week, however, whenever he wears the garment he violates the positive commandment to attach tzitzis. This is comparable to mezuzah and a parapet: one is not forbidden to enter a house lacking them, but remains obligated to fulfill the positive commandment. So writes Beit Yosef in the name of Mordechai. The Gemara on Shabbos 131a states, according to the view that tzitzis is an obligation upon the garment, that one who keeps a garment in his house without tzitzis violates a positive commandment even on Shabbos. This applies specifically according to Rabbi Eliezer, who maintains that tzitzis overrides Shabbos, so the mitzvah applies even then. According to our ruling that tzitzis does not override Shabbos, the positive commandment does not take effect on Shabbos in this manner. One might still ask why wearing the garment should be forbidden during the week, since Berachos 20a teaches that human dignity overrides a positive Torah commandment where the violation is passive. There it is evident that wearing a linen garment without tzitzis is considered passive. Tosafos in Yevamos 90b likewise proves that it is passive, for otherwise it would resemble kilayim, which one must remove even in the marketplace. It therefore appears that Mordechai permitted it even during the week, although he states that the wearer remains obligated to repair the garment. The responsum cited by Beit Yosef likewise concerns a weekday. The practical ruling follows Piskei Tosafos and Agur, who permit it only on Shabbos. The reason appears to be the distinction in Tosafos, Shevuos 30b: only great humiliation permits passive violation of a Torah prohibition, while minor embarrassment does not. Thus a Torah scholar must appear in beis din to testify concerning a prohibited matter even though doing so is beneath his dignity. Sitting without a tallis is only minor embarrassment. If, however, the tzitzis of a tallis katan become severed in a public place, he need not remove it even during the week, because exposing himself to remove it would be great humiliation. Shulchan Aruch mentions Shabbos to teach the greater novelty that even though a Shabbos prohibition is also involved, removal is unnecessary. On Shabbos he need not hurry to leave the synagogue. During the week he should hurry home and remove it. One who notices that another person’s tzitzis are severed should not tell him until he reaches home. The source implies that he should call him into the house and inform him there. If he knew before Shabbos that the garment was invalid, he may not put it on during Shabbos, since he should have repaired it beforehand. If no replacement tzitzis are available in the city, the weekday law is like the Shabbos law. My late brother, Rabbi Yehudah Segal of Krakow, asked why one may wear it on Shabbos because of human dignity. He could transfer ownership to another person as a gift and then borrow it back, making it exempt from tzitzis, as is done regarding immersion of utensils. The answer is that it would still require tzitzis D’Rabbanan because it appears to remain his, as with a borrowed tallis after thirty days, and all the more so here, where it truly belongs to him. Moreover, if this device were allowed on Shabbos, one could use it during the week as well and devise stratagems to exempt himself from tzitzis. When he simply remains in the garment without tzitzis, he knows that the leniency applies only on Shabbos because of human dignity. In addition, giving a gift on Shabbos is forbidden unless needed for Shabbos, as stated in siman 306. This is also implied by Shabbos 131a, which says that he could declare it ownerless, rather than saying that he could give it to a non-Jew or minor.
