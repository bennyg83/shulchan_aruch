Magen Avraham, paragraph beginning: We say in sukkah, etc. And Tosafot wrote that he holds that we say two walls according to their law. This means that the strip of four tefachim is placed in place of one wall, namely as one of the two walls that must be according to their law. Thus there is only one complete wall, unlike the Ran whom he cites afterwards.
And Maharsha wrote that this is according to Rabbi Yosei bar Yehudah, etc. He wanted to resolve the baraita that Tosafot cited there, which is taught in Sukkah 7: The wall of a sukkah is like the wall of Shabbos, provided that there is not three tefachim between one reed and the next.
Meaning that one may make all the partitions of the sukkah with reeds, provided that there is not three tefachim between one reed and the next. For a partition made of vertical elements alone is effective. On this Maharsha wrote: this is according to Rabbi Yosei bar Yehudah. For there in Eruvin 16b, Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Yosei bar Yehudah dispute regarding an individual or a caravan, meaning three people, who rested in an open valley and surrounded themselves with partitions in order to carry by means of them. They made the partitions with reeds, where there was not three tefachim between one and the next, which is vertical elements, or with ropes across the width, where there was not three tefachim between one rope and the next, which is horizontal elements. Rabbi Yehudah holds that for one person and for two people, carrying is permitted by means of these weak partitions only up to two se'ah, meaning that the enclosure may not contain more than a beit se'atayim. But if there are three people, who are then called a caravan, they are permitted to enclose by means of these weak partitions as much as they need.
And Rabbi Yosei bar Yehudah holds that even for a caravan they were permitted only two se'ah for each person, which together is six se'ah.
And Rav Nachman ruled there like Rabbi Yehudah. Tosafot holds that a sukkah has the law of a caravan: just as for a caravan, partitions are permitted according to their entire need, even more than two se'ah for each person, so too one should permit this for a sukkah because of the mitzvah of sukkah. Therefore, according to Rabbi Yehudah, whose view we accept, who permits weak partitions for a caravan, meaning either vertical elements alone or horizontal elements alone, according to their entire need, the same applies to sukkah even for an individual. According to him the baraita above on page 7, which permits a sukkah wall made reed by reed, fits well. As for why Rabbi Eliezer required a strip of four tefachim and did not suffice with reeds, as Tosafot asked, he wished to give a remedy even according to Rabbi Yosei bar Yehudah, although we do not rule like him. According to Rabbi Yosei bar Yehudah, since even for a caravan they were not permitted according to their entire need by means of weak partitions, the same applies that it was not permitted for sukkah.
But a partition of horizontal elements, etc. That is, he comes to resolve what Tosafot wrote there earlier, s.v. man de'amar, etc., concerning Rav Chisda's statement that a mat of four tefachim and a bit is permitted in a sukkah as a wall. How is it done? He hangs it in the middle, less than three tefachim below near the ground and less than three above near the s'chach. This is the law mentioned in se'if 9. Tosafot asked: why not suffice with two ropes, each a half-tefach and a bit wide, placing one near the s'chach within less than three tefachim, and the second within less than three tefachim of the first and also within less than three tefachim of the ground; it would be a partition ten tefachim high, as stated in Eruvin. Tosafot answered: there in Eruvin we say that they permitted this only for a caravan. These are Tosafot's words, referring to the Gemara in Eruvin 16 mentioned above. Maharsha found this difficult, for on the contrary, what is permitted for a caravan is also permitted for sukkah, as we say regarding reed by reed with less than three tefachim between them, as above. One cannot say that Tosafot is following Rabbi Yosei bar Yehudah, for Tosafot wrote that it was permitted only for a caravan, whereas according to Rabbi Yosei bar Yehudah even for a caravan it was not permitted in that way. Tosafot must therefore be following Rabbi Yehudah. On this Maharsha wrote that specifically partitions of vertical elements help for sukkah, meaning reed by reed with less than three tefachim between them. But horizontal elements, meaning by ropes, even according to Rabbi Yehudah, who permits such a case for a caravan, are nevertheless worse than vertical elements and were not permitted for sukkah. He brings some proof from Eruvin that vertical elements are preferable to horizontal elements. The same Tosafot, s.v. man de'amar, also mentioned that one might have thought to distinguish, with regard to two applications of lavud, between vertical elements and horizontal elements.
But the Ran wrote differently. He does not establish the statement of Rav Ami, cited above, as referring to a strip of four tefachim in the way Tosafot did, that this strip is one of the two walls according to their law, as above. Rather, it speaks where he already has two partitions, except that they are made like an alleyway, so that an additional strip of four tefachim is needed, as stated in se'if 3.
A proper measure. This means the measure of a significant place, which is four tefachim, and it also requires a tzurat hapesach. Regarding what we say on Sukkah 7, that in a sukkah made like a Greek gamma the third wall requires a generous tefach, as written in se'if 2, we say that it also requires a tzurat hapesach.
And it should read: a tzurat hapesach of three; and so it is emended in the Ran. For by means of lavud and a generous tefach there are four tefachim here, and for the width of the wall, which must be seven tefachim, only another three tefachim are needed.
And if so, we must say that what was taught, that a reed helps, etc. That is, since the Ran holds that even according to us, who rule like Rabbi Yehudah that a partition of vertical elements is permitted for a caravan, nevertheless it was not permitted for sukkah, we must say that sukkah partitions are stricter than a caravan. The baraita on page 7, which permitted reed by reed with less than three tefachim between them for a sukkah, is therefore difficult.
And if so, we do not need to force, etc. This means as was written above in the name of Maharsha, that Rabbi Eliezer, who requires a strip of four tefachim, follows Rabbi Yosei bar Yehudah, for according to him a partition of vertical elements does not help for a caravan, while we rule like Rabbi Yehudah, as above.
Since it is not a partition except for a caravan, etc. That is, what Tosafot wrote in their answer: since we say, etc., that any partition that is not of vertical and horizontal elements, etc., it is a weak partition; therefore it was not permitted for sukkah where there are only three walls.
And what Tosafot wrote, that there is no distinction. This means that Tosafot initially wrote, within their discussion, on another question that they asked from that baraita of reed by reed with less than three tefachim between them: there is no basis at all to distinguish between a sukkah of three walls and a sukkah of four walls.
That was before we knew the reasoning. That is, before they considered distinguishing between a complete partition and one made by lavud. Therefore they initially thought that since it helps with four walls, it should also help with three walls. But in the end, when they brought the case from Eruvin that one may rely on vertical or horizontal elements alone only for a caravan, it follows that a partition made by lavud is weaker than a complete partition. It is therefore proper to distinguish between four walls, where even a weak partition made by lavud helps,
But it does not help where there are only three walls. From the plain understanding of the Magen Avraham's words, it sounds that he comes to explain that according to his interpretation we do not need the great forced explanation written above according to Maharsha, to distinguish between vertical and horizontal elements. Rather, there is only a distinction between making three walls and making four walls. However, this is not so, for the Magen Avraham too must accept that forced distinction in Tosafot, s.v. mahu deteima, etc. Tosafot there asked regarding Rav Chisda's statement that a mat of four tefachim, etc., why two ropes, each half a tefach and a bit wide, would not suffice, and they answered that in Eruvin we say they permitted it only for a caravan, as above. Maharsha's question then remains: the baraita permitted reed by reed for sukkah, and that too was permitted only for a caravan. We must therefore distinguish between vertical and horizontal elements, as above. In that passage one cannot distinguish and explain as the Magen Avraham did, between four walls and three walls. First, regarding Tosafot s.v. bepachot mishloshah, where they asked on Rabbi Eliezer's statement about a strip of four tefachim that two reeds should suffice, one can reasonably read their intent as the Magen Avraham did, since this distinction is mentioned in their words when they wrote that there is no basis to distinguish between a sukkah of three walls, etc.; only at first, before they knew the reasoning, the distinction did not appear valid to them, as the Magen Avraham wrote. But in Tosafot s.v. mahu deteima, where there is no mention at all of a distinction between three or four walls, how could Tosafot leave their words unexplained? Rather, one must say as Maharsha did, to distinguish between vertical and horizontal elements. This distinction is mentioned in Tosafot s.v. man de'amar concerning another matter; see there. Moreover, even if this forced reading could be tolerated, how could Tosafot in s.v. man de'amar take it as obvious to distinguish between three and four walls, and then afterwards in s.v. bepachot mishloshah initially write that there is no distinction between three and four walls until they bring the principle that any partition that is not of vertical and horizontal elements, etc.? Therefore it appears to me that the Magen Avraham did not intend, in his explanation, to resolve matters so that we would not need to distinguish between vertical and horizontal elements according to Tosafot. We certainly must say this for Tosafot s.v. man de'amar, as above.
There is some proof for this from the wording of the Magen Avraham, who concluded: if so, we do not need to force Tosafot's words to be unlike the halacha; he did not mention that we need not say the great forced distinction with which he began. Rather, the Magen Avraham's intent when he first wrote that it is a great difficulty that horizontal elements do not help, etc., was primarily that such a legal ruling seemed forced to him. On this he brought the words of the Ran, who in fact does not distinguish between vertical and horizontal elements, but between three and four walls. If so, one can say that as a matter of law Tosafot too agree that there is no distinction between vertical and horizontal elements, and they also distinguish as the Ran wrote. This resolves matters, for we need not force ourselves to say, as Maharsha did, that Rabbi Eliezer's words are unlike the halacha, as the Magen Avraham wrote. Accordingly, according to the conclusion of Tosafot s.v. bepachot mishloshah, where they hold like the Ran in order not to have to say that Rabbi Eliezer is unlike the halacha, Tosafot also retracted what they wrote in s.v. mahu deteima, and they too hold that there is no distinction between vertical and horizontal elements. Their question in s.v. man de'amar is likewise answered by this distinction between three and four walls.
In the Shulchan Aruch, in the first gloss: and one need not stand it, etc. This excludes the view of the stringent authorities in the Tur, who require that they be stood in the direction of their growth. For we rule below in siman 651:2 that regarding a lulav, and likewise all mitzvah objects, one must take them in the direction of their growth, as it is written regarding the beams of the Mishkan: standing acacia wood, and from there they derive this for all mitzvot. If so, the stringent view holds that the same applies to walls. Granted, regarding s'chach the Bach wrote that although the s'chach lies horizontally, it is nevertheless called the direction of its growth, because on a tree too the branches extend and bend from the tree, shading the ground. The same would apply to walls as well: even according to the stringent view, one may make them this way, and it is not considered against the direction of their growth unless one reverses the top to the bottom.
Alternatively, s'chach is different, for all s'chach is placed not in the direction of its growth, and there is no other way to do it. The Tur wrote regarding their words that this is not necessary, since the walls are valid from any material, even from something that does not grow from the ground. With something that does not grow from the ground, the concept of direction of growth does not apply. Therefore, something that does grow from the ground is no worse, even if one does not stand it in the direction of its growth.
