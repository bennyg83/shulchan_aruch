(37) He placed sechach over an achsadra. An achsadra here has two opposite walls, part of it roofed, with sechach extending from beside that roof to the opposite edge, so that the sukkah has only two walls. The posts are two projections on the third side. Although an ordinary sukkah with two opposite walls requires a strip four tefachim plus a small amount, here the posts suffice because we say that the edge of the achsadra roof descends and closes the side, creating a third wall. Without posts we do not say that the roof edge descends and closes. These posts are effective even if they are visible only from outside the sukkah, and even though they were not originally made as sukkah walls. The Rama cites dissenting authorities who maintain that even posts do not help, even if each is one tefach wide, because the two walls are not joined in an L-shape, as stated at the end of siman 361. If, however, the walls form an L-shape and a one-tefach post projects on the third side adjacent to the achsadra roof, it is effective because the roof edge descends and closes. Rosh nevertheless implies that a tzuras hapesach is also required. Bach writes that this applies only where the posts are visible from outside but flush inside; if visible inside but flush outside, no tzuras hapesach is needed. Magen Avraham questions this and appears to maintain that according to Rosh a tzuras hapesach is always required. In his view, we do not say that the roof edge descends and closes here, just as in siman 361, because the achsadra roof was made for its own use and not for the sukkah.
(38) Whether they are visible from inside. Meaning, a person standing in the sukkah sees them projecting inward from the wall.
(39) Whether they are visible from outside, etc. Meaning, from outside they visibly project like a post, while inside they are flush with the wall.
(40) But others disagree, etc. Taz writes that when one makes a sukkah inside his house by opening the roof, as is customary, and uses the house walls as the sukkah walls, such as the east and south walls forming a corner, so that there are only two walls and a house beam lies above, all agree that the roof edge descends and closes. The beam was made for the house, and the sukkah is likewise inside the house, so it is as though there are three walls. Since this is not an actual wall but depends upon the descending roof edge, he must also make a tzuras hapesach by placing two poles beneath the beam, one beside the house wall and the other where the sukkah wall ends, namely where the sechach ends. Although ordinarily a tzuras hapesach alone does not suffice as the third wall, here it combines with the descending roof edge. Several later authorities cite this ruling. Peri Megadim and Bikkurei Yaakov, however, question it, because Magen Avraham concludes that the rule of a descending roof edge applies only where the roof is four tefachim wide, while an ordinary house beam is not that wide. Bikkurei Yaakov therefore concludes that it is preferable to place a strip a spacious tefach wide beneath the beam, within three tefachim of the house wall, and another pole where the sukkah’s sechach ends. This is valid without relying on the descending roof edge, like any other three-walled sukkah, and does not require the beam to be four tefachim wide, as explained in seif 2.
(41) One should not make a sukkah in this manner. Levush writes that this applies only initially; after the fact one may rely upon the first opinion.
