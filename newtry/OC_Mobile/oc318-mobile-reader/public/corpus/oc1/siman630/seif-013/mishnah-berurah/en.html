(58) One who supports his sukkah, etc. The statements that the bed’s legs are partitions and that the legs are walls show that this bed was surrounded by boards, one end fixed in the ground and the other extending ten tefachim above the bed. The bed itself consisted of boards fixed into these partitions, forming its floor. Because those boards were fixed into the partitions, the partitions are called the bed’s legs. The seif means as follows. One who supports the sechach upon the bed’s legs, namely upon the boards rising above the bed, so that those legs are the sukkah walls, has a valid sukkah if there are ten tefachim from the bed to the sechach. Otherwise it is invalid. Since the sechach rests on those boards, which are the bed’s legs, the bed itself is regarded as the sukkah floor, and ten tefachim are required from it to the sechach. If, however, he supported the sechach upon posts fixed in the ground, the sukkah is valid even if there are fewer than ten tefachim from the bed to the sechach. Although the bed’s legs serve as the walls, the sechach is not supported upon the bed or its legs, and the bed is therefore not regarded as the sukkah floor. The ground in which the posts supporting the sechach are fixed is the floor. Since there are ten tefachim from the ground to the sechach, the sukkah is valid, even though there are fewer than ten tefachim between the bed and the sechach. If the bed were removed, a ten-tefach airspace would remain and the sukkah would stand by means of the posts. The bed is an independent object, and bringing it into the sukkah does not invalidate the sukkah by reducing its airspace.
(59) If it has the required height, etc., it is valid. Although the sechach is supported upon a bed that contracts tumah, this does not matter, because susceptibility to tumah was stated regarding the sechach, not the walls. Nevertheless, initially it is proper to avoid this, because some authorities are stringent. See Magen Avraham in siman 629, seif katan 8.
