Se'if 2 in the gloss. And if the handbreadth and the wall reach the s'chach, one does not need a reed on top of them. Note: See Magen Avraham, small paragraph 2, regarding what he asked from the Yerushalmi, where it is explicit that it must be made for the sake of a form of a doorway; why, then, did they rule leniently even when it was not made for this? See Eliyah Rabbah, who disagrees with him. In my humble opinion, it appears that this law is proven in the Talmud, that we do not require intent for the sake of a form of a doorway in this case. This is from what is written in the Talmud there, page 7: Rabbi Asi found Rav Kahana making a broad handbreadth and also making a form of a doorway. He said to him: does the master not hold like this statement of Rava, "and it is also permitted by a form of a doorway"? He said to him: I hold like the other version of Rava, who said, "and it also requires a form of a doorway." Until here is the Talmud's language. This is difficult to me: from where do we know that these two versions disagree? Perhaps they do not disagree at all. What Rava said, "and it is also permitted by a form of a doorway," means where he makes it intentionally for the sake of a form of a doorway; in that case a form of a doorway alone is enough even without a broad handbreadth. And what he said, "and it also requires a form of a doorway," means where he does not intend for the sake of a form of a doorway, only it came about automatically, like the law of the Mordechai and the Rema where the handbreadth and the wall reach the s'chach; in that case both are needed, a broad handbreadth and a form of a doorway, and they do not disagree at all. If so, Rav Kahana, who intentionally made a form of a doorway, as implied by the Talmud's statement that he was also making a form of a doorway, could well be told according to the other view: why do you need another broad handbreadth? What did he answer, "I hold like the other version of Rava, that it also requires a form of a doorway"? Perhaps they do not disagree at all. From where do we know to make a dispute, especially within the words of Rava himself? Rather, it is impossible unless the Talmud held simply that there is no reason at all to distinguish between making it intentionally for the sake of a form of a doorway and not doing so, as the Eliyah Rabbah says, since making it for the sake of s'chach is like intent. Perforce they disagree. This is a clear and great proof from the Talmud against the Magen Avraham and the Yerushalmi. Examine this carefully. One can also say that regarding Shabbos we certainly do not require intent for the sake of a form of a doorway; only regarding sukkah, since they were more stringent with it, as Tosafos wrote similarly, that therefore we require a whole handbreadth, likewise the Yerushalmi holds that we do not say: since it is a wall for Shabbos, it is a wall for sukkah. But we rule that we do say this since, and since a form of a doorway helps regarding Shabbos, it helps for sukkah even on a weekday, as he holds regarding an alleyway that has a side-post. Examine and understand. It further appears in my humble opinion to resolve the difficulty of the Yerushalmi that the Magen Avraham brought, according to what is written below in siman 635, that one should not make the s'chach and only afterward the walls, and if one did so it is valid, while the Turei Zahav invalidates it. His main proof is from what is written in the Talmud, that one must shake it; see there. According to this, according to the one who says that one need not shake it, it is permitted to make the s'chach first and afterward the walls. Therefore it works well: the Yerushalmi holds like Rav, and automatically it is permitted to make the s'chach first and afterward the walls. Therefore it speaks where he made the s'chach first, and if so at that time it was not recognizable whether he would need a form of a doorway or would make four or three complete walls. Therefore, even though afterward he made two walls and a third of a handbreadth, proving that a form of a doorway is needed, granted that it is not invalid because of "You shall make" and not from what was already made according to Rav, nevertheless it does not have the legal status of a form of a doorway, since he did not make it for that purpose. But according to us, who rule that one must shake it, if so it is impossible to place s'chach unless the walls were first present. If so, perforce the handbreadth and the wall were first, for otherwise there are no walls. Since he placed s'chach over them, this proves that he intended this to be a form of a doorway, since a form of a doorway is required in this case. The unspecified act is as though explicit, that he intended this. Furthermore, if not so, the s'chach would again be invalid, since there are not yet three walls according to their law, and even if he afterward makes a form of a doorway it would be from what was already made. Perforce, from the fact that he placed the s'chach before making a form of a doorway, his intent was that this itself should be a form of a doorway, and they therefore ruled well that it is valid in such a case. It will be proven that they hold like the Turei Zahav, that otherwise it is invalid. This is fine; examine carefully. Now I saw the Eliyah Rabbah, who answers the Magen Avraham's question that this is different, since it was made to permit the s'chach and therefore also helps for a form of a doorway; see there. According to his words, and according to the words of the Turei Zahav, who holds that if one made the s'chach and afterward the walls, it is invalid because of "You shall make" and not from what was already made, a new question occurred to me about what the Talmud asks on page 11 against Rav, who holds that one need not shake it, from the Mishnah, "if one trained over it, etc." What are the circumstances? If it was when he had not cut them, why specifically because of "You shall make" and not from what was already made? Let it derive that they are attached. Rather, it is when he cut them, etc. What is the question? Let us say that it is speaking where there were only two walls, and the third wall's handbreadth and form of a doorway were the gourd and the vine. If so, at first it did not have the legal status of a form of a doorway, since it was attached from before. Therefore, although he cut them, and their cutting is their making, nevertheless he still has no form of a doorway, since it was not originally made for this. What will you say: since it was made to permit the s'chach, it also helps for a form of a doorway? If so, it is impossible for the name wall to take effect upon it unless the name s'chach first takes effect upon it. Therefore the name s'chach must first take effect, and afterward the form of a doorway; if so the s'chach was made before the wall, and it is "You shall make" and not from what was already made, according to the Turei Zahav. Granted, when it is not attached, one can say as I wrote: since it is valid in this way, we are witnesses that it is as though he intended this from the beginning. But with something attached, one way or the other: before cutting it does not help even if he intended it for the sake of a form of a doorway, since it is attached and he did not plant it originally for this; then, after he cut it, it does not help for a form of a doorway, since it was not made originally for this. And to say that since it was made to permit the s'chach it also helps for a form of a doorway, if so the name s'chach must first take effect, and only afterward it becomes a form of a doorway and a wall, and it is invalid because of "You shall make" and not from what was already made, according to the Turei Zahav. Although according to the one who says one need not shake it, the Turei Zahav agrees that it is valid when the s'chach is first, that is because the later making of the walls is its making. But in this case no wall was made; and one can say that their cutting helps for one thing but not for two things, to validate it both as s'chach and as a wall. Their cutting does not help for both. What, then, is the Talmud's question? This requires analysis; examine carefully.
