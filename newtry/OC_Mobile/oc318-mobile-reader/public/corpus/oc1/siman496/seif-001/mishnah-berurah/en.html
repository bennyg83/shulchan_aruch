(1) And they would excommunicate, etc. Although we now know the calendar calculations that determine the new month, the Sages were concerned that, because of the many troubles and displacements of exile, the calculation might eventually be forgotten. People might then treat a deficient month as full or a full month as deficient and come to eat chametz on Pesach. They therefore left the observance of the second day of Yom Tov in the Diaspora as it had been in earlier times.
(2) One who treats it lightly. This means one who does so intentionally. Even if he treated lightly a matter prohibited only as a shevus or violated a rabbinic techum, he is excommunicated. The acharonim write that even if the desecration was performed through a non-Jew, he is subject to excommunication, because everything forbidden on the first day is forbidden on the second. The poskim explain that, according to one view, the second day of Yom Tov is more severe in this respect than the first. On the first day, one is excommunicated only for violating a Torah prohibition. For violating a rabbinic shevus, he is not excommunicated, but receives lashes for rebellious conduct. On the second day, however, since its entire observance is rabbinic, violating any of its laws is considered as though one were uprooting the whole institution, and the Sages therefore treated it more stringently. This is unlike the first day, whose Torah status is not uprooted merely because one violated a rabbinic shevus. Other poskim disagree and hold that there is no distinction between the first and second days. According to them, the matter depends upon the judges' assessment of the circumstances, whether to treat the offender leniently with lashes or stringently with excommunication, although they have authority even to excommunicate him. The acharonim write that one who acted according to the ruling of a sage who erred should not be excommunicated, since he acted unintentionally.
(3) Rather, they would administer lashes. The poskim disagree concerning the prescribed number of lashes for rebellious conduct. See Magen Avraham and Eliyah Rabbah.
