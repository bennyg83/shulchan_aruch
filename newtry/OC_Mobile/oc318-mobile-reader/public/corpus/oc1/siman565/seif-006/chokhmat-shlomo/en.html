(There, seif 6.) One who fasts and publicizes himself to others to boast that he fasts is punished for this. We will see what Taz and Magen Avraham expanded regarding if they ask him whether he is fasting whether he should tell the truth or lie. In my humble opinion it appears from the Gemara and Rashi that even when they ask him he may lie. This is from what we learn in Sukkah 49b, “Walk modestly with your the Omnipresent” — this is taking out the dead and bringing in the bride to the chuppah; behold, these matters are a fortiori: if regarding matters that are customarily done publicly the Torah said “walk modestly,” matters customarily done in private all the more so. See Rashi there, s.v. taking out the dead, etc., and see Tosafot there s.v. matters customarily done in private, such as charity given to a poor person in secret, unlike bringing in a bride where others are involved and know from whose money it is — and R' Elazar said that everything requires modesty, end of his words. Now according to Rashi it is not clear at first glance the reason for the a fortiori: if these are customarily done publicly, what difference if their custom is public or private — in the end whoever knows knows and whoever does not know does not know. Rather the intent is thus: regarding those customarily done publicly, where certainly many will ask who is doing this and who volunteered, nevertheless the Torah said that even if they ask he should lie before them that he is not doing it and do it in private; all the more so regarding matters customarily done in private where many will not ask him — plainly he should do it privately and not begin on his own to publicize that he does so. With this the a fortiori fits well: if for lying the Torah permitted even to those beginning with him who ask him, all the more when there is no question he should not boast of it. If so it appears he may lie for the sake of Heaven and it is a sin for its sake, and better than revealing — so it appears correct in the explanation of this sugya, examine well. Behold Taz and Magen Avraham both proved that ordinarily when asked one should answer truth and not lie, from what he first answered in the fast “I sit” — see there. I saw in a newly printed book Yaalet Chen on the Torah in parashat Beshalach that he wrote to reject this proof because he asked why it concludes “and R' Yehudah, even on Shabbat,” and also why they first asked “Does a person loan his fast and repay?” — perhaps he accepted that day as a vow, etc., and his words are puzzling, for Magen Avraham and Taz were precise in their wording to prove from what he later said “a Torah scholar sits” that at first they did not know he was a Torah scholar — the law is with them; otherwise how could he later say “a Torah scholar sits” — they would have known from the start he was a Torah scholar, it would only be that he said, etc., “fine, a Torah scholar,” etc. — rather from what he later said “a Torah scholar sits” it implies that until then they did not know he was a Torah scholar, and therefore it was not Shabbat. What he challenged — how could they ask “Does a person loan his fast and repay?” — perhaps he vowed that day — one may answer that according to his view the fast he loaned was because of his vow; he could have asked about his vow and the opening would be that with the intent of a great man he did not vow, as written in Nedarim, and he could have asked him to inquire about his vow, but since we hold one does not ask about a mitzvah except under duress, he could not ask him thus about a vow of mitzvah; therefore he told him that in truth he could ask about it, and if it were a vow of mitzvah he could fast another day, and why fast specifically today — that is not in the category of vow of mitzvah, and he could have annulment only that perhaps there is nullification of mitzvah — tomorrow he may die and not fulfill his vow, a vow of mitzvah without annulment as we fear in chapter 1 of Nedarim lest he die — therefore he brought proof from what we say “a person loans his fast and repays,” proving we do not fear lest he die; if so he can again ask about his vow for what he vowed today, and this is not nullification of mitzvah since he can fulfill and fast later, and for today he is not commanded — therefore he had to answer them later that he is a Torah scholar. However regarding that fasting is good for a dream — one may say two intents: either the fast is healing for the dream or the opposite that eating causes strengthening of the dream's decree, but when one does not eat the dream is nullified; there is a difference if he eats a seudat mitzvah — if we say fasting is good for a dream, even eating seudat mitzvah is not considered like fasting and we require fasting specifically; if we say eating voluntarily harms the dream, if he eats seudat mitzvah he is not harmed and without this one could say the benefit of fasting is only that eating will not harm him — then in seudat mitzvah there would be no concern of harm, and since if he hosts guests it is seudat mitzvah as in Orach Chayim siman 533 — though Magen Avraham implies only for the host it is mitzvah and not guests, from Taz there it does not imply so; also siman 568 in Magen Avraham implies a meal where a Torah scholar dwells is seudat mitzvah, and in Laws of Chanukah it is explained that if one says songs and praises it is seudat mitzvah — they could make from this seudat mitzvah and need not fast, therefore he brought proof from R' Yosef: even on Shabbat, and there is no greater seudat mitzvah than that, nevertheless fasting is better in him, all the more other seudat mitzvah — only fasting specifically is required, therefore one may not loan his fast and repay — and Taz and Magen Avraham fit well, and the question of Yaalet Chen is resolved, examine well.
