Seif katan 8, "it is permitted," etc. The concern is squeezing. Although Beitzah 18a states that according to all opinions a person may immerse on Shabbos, and for this reason even according to our ruling that immersion at its proper time is not itself a mitzvah, immersion connected with procreation or the mitzvah of marital relations is permitted and these remote concerns were not taken into account, nevertheless where she could have immersed earlier the custom adopted a stringency and took these concerns into account. Terumat HaDeshen likewise rules stringently, although for another reason. "Although even Mordechai," etc. We do not rule like Mordechai, for in cold water bathing is permitted, as stated in seif 1. Magen Avraham therefore writes "even according to Mordechai." His restriction to a vessel is because an observer may think one is bathing in hot water, whereas in a river everyone sees that the water is cold. Nevertheless, once the custom is to prohibit bathing, whatever its precise basis, immersion too can appear like repairing a status. The Gemara says that although a utensil that became impure may not be immersed on Shabbos or Yom Tov because immersion resembles repairing it, a person may immerse because it appears that he is merely cooling himself. This remains true even in the rainy season and even in foul water, because a person sometimes enters such water to remove mud or filth. Nowadays, however, when people refrain by custom from bathing even in cold water, one who immerses is plainly recognized as doing so for ritual immersion, so the act appears like repairing. Beit Yosef cites Kol Bo in Yoreh De'ah, siman 199, as prohibiting because of squeezing the hair. Although Beit Yosef rejects that concern on the ground that there is no Torah-level squeezing of hair, his intent is only that this consideration should not override immersion connected with a mitzvah. Rambam indeed rules that squeezing hair is not liable by Torah law, but a rabbinic prohibition remains, as explained in siman 330. Thus bathing itself can properly be prohibited for this reason. Once bathing is prohibited, ritual immersion can again become recognizable as an act of repair. The statement that the immersion requirement here is rabbinic is not precise. Regarding a penitent apostate, the immersion mentioned at the end of Yoreh De'ah, siman 268, is only a customary stringency modeled after a convert. By strict law he remains a full Jew, albeit one who had sinned, as Nimukei Yosef writes in Yevamos. We do not find in the Talmud a formal requirement that one who returns in repentance must immerse. Accordingly, such an immersion is no more an act of repair than the immersion of a convert according to R' Yehudah, who holds that once 1 stage of conversion has been completed the person already has Jewish status for practical purposes. Although we rule like R' Yose that both brit milah and immersion are required for conversion, R' Yehudah's rule demonstrates that an immersion which merely adds a further stringency does not necessarily constitute repairing. Sefer Tosefet Shabbos questioned this in light of Rambam's explanation that a convert may not immerse on Shabbos because conversion requires a beit din of 3, derived from the term mishpat, and judgment may not be conducted on Shabbos. That reason, however, does not apply to a returning apostate, for mishpat is not written regarding him. The requirement of 3 for such an immersion is only by analogy to conversion. Thus there is no judicial prohibition. The remaining question is only whether the act resembles repairing, and Magen Avraham's point is that in this case it does not.
