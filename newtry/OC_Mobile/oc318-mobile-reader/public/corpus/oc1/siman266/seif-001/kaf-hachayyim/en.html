1) [Seif 1] And he has his donkey with him. If the donkey belongs to a non-Jew, however, he may place the wallet upon it, because he is not commanded to ensure that it rests. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1. Although Magen Avraham writes in paragraph 5 in the name of Ran that one is liable for driving even an animal that is not his, Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1, writes that all the halachic authorities appear to dispute Ran and maintain that one is liable for driving only his own animal; see there. Erech HaShulchan, paragraph 1, however, writes that since a non-Jew accompanies him, the Jewish person is certainly driving his animal, which recognizes his voice, and Eliyah Rabbah's question is therefore not difficult; see there. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 2, writes the same. The later authorities also maintain that one is liable for driving another person's animal, as stated below in paragraph 13; see there.
2) Nevertheless, if he rented the donkey from a non-Jew, some say he is commanded to ensure that it rests, as stated in siman 246, seif 3. See our words there in paragraph 24. This applies specifically when he rented the donkey. If he hired the non-Jew to transport him to a particular place, however, he did not rent the donkey, since the non-Jew remains responsible for feeding the donkey and liable for it, and he did not specifically rent this particular donkey from the non-Jew. It is therefore obvious that he is not commanded to ensure that it rests. Magen Avraham there; Tosefes Shabbos there.
3) There: He gives his wallet to a non-Jew, etc. This is because a person cannot restrain himself when his money is at stake. If this were not permitted, he might carry it four amos in the public domain. This applies specifically to his own money, because he cares for property he acquired through effort. Taz, paragraph 1. This excludes a found object that has not yet come into his possession. If it came into his possession while it was still daytime, however, it is like his own money. All money ordinarily comes through effort, and even though he did not labor for this particular money, the leniency applies. Levush Serad.
4) There: He gives his wallet to a non-Jew, etc. If it is a heavy load, he should give the donkey to the non-Jew or declare it ownerless, as stated in siman 246, seif 3, provided he does not drive it. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2. This is because one is liable for driving even another person's animal, as Magen Avraham writes in paragraph 5. Levush Serad. See below, paragraph 13. It appears that in this case he need not follow the procedure in seif 2 of removing the load from the donkey and replacing it, since it is a heavy load and doing so entails great exertion. He may rely upon declaring the animal ownerless and upon the non-Jew driving it. He should distance himself from the donkey so that he does not drive it. You can know this is so, because otherwise there would be no reason to declare it ownerless, since seif 2 does not require doing so. It necessarily follows that here he need not remove and replace the load. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 2. Regarding the law of declaring one's animal ownerless or giving it to a non-Jew as a gift, see siman 246, seif 3, and our words there. The same applies here.
5) There: He gives his wallet to a non-Jew, etc. Some forbid sewing gold coins into one's belt and carrying them on Shabbos because one is afraid to entrust them to a non-Jew while traveling. I found, however, that Rabbi Yehudah of Troyes permitted it, since the coins are covered on every side and the person does not appear to carry a muktzeh article. Bach. See below, siman 301, seif 33.
6) There: Even if he did not pay him, etc. Rokeach, siman 136, writes that if night has not yet fallen, he pays him. This is also implied by Rosh. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3. Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 1, writes the same. He must not, however, explicitly mention that the non-Jew should carry it on Shabbos. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 3.
7) There: Although he gave it to him after nightfall, etc. Although money is muktzeh, the Sages did not prohibit moving muktzeh where a financial loss is involved. For this reason they permitted moving the wallet in increments of less than four amos, as stated below in seif 7. Beis Yosef. Nevertheless, ab initio he should give it to the non-Jew while it is still daytime and should not move muktzeh on Shabbos. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1.
8) There: Although he gave it to him after nightfall, etc. This is because a person cannot restrain himself when his money is at stake. If this were not permitted, he might carry it four amos in the public domain. See what is written in siman 334, seif 2, that some say this is forbidden nowadays because there is no public domain. Magen Avraham, paragraph 3. This means that moving muktzeh on Shabbos was permitted because of the concern that a person cannot restrain himself when his money is at stake and might carry it four amos in the public domain. Nowadays, according to the opinion that there is no public domain, moving muktzeh would be forbidden, because even if he fails to restrain himself and carries four amos, he commits no Torah prohibition, since the area is not a complete public domain according to Torah law. The dispute whether there is a public domain nowadays is recorded in siman 345, seif 7, and according to the Shulchan Aruch's unattributed opinion, there is one even nowadays; see there. Regarding Magen Avraham's principal ruling, however, Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 4, writes that it is unlike siman 334, seif 2. There, moving the item is rabbinically forbidden and carrying through our contemporary public domain is also rabbinically forbidden. Here, by contrast, there is greater reason to permit telling a non-Jew to carry, which is a rabbinic prohibition compounded with another rabbinic prohibition. This is why the ruling here permits it without qualification. These are his words.
9) There: But if he found a lost object, etc. The reason is that he is not as agitated about it, since he did not labor to acquire it. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 3.
10) There: He may not give it to a non-Jew, etc. Nevertheless, it appears that he may say to the non-Jew, 'My wallet fell and I do not know what to do.' Tiferes Yisrael; Pischei Olam, paragraph 6. It appears that he may likewise say, 'Would it please you to bring it to my home?' since he does not use the language of a command, but presents it only as a possibility. See below, paragraphs 37 and 65.
