[8] And some permit, etc. This is difficult, because bread of a private householder is prohibited. If this refers to baker's bread, then it was also made for Jews. One must therefore say that most of it was made for non-Jews, as stated by the Taz. See Yoreh Deah siman 113.
[9] [Levush] Since he baked it, etc. If a Jewish servant baked it, even for himself, it is prohibited the first time, as stated by the Beit Yosef. See siman 517, seif katan 5.
[10] [Levush] And although a non-Jew, etc. This is the question raised by the Beit Yosef. The Magen Avraham comments on it and refers to siman 517, where he writes in the name of the Rosh that it is prohibited as a decree lest one climb and detach produce. He means that the prohibition is not because of muktzeh. In my humble opinion, the Beit Yosef was correct, for at the beginning of Perek Ein Tzadin the Rosh writes that the reason regarding most such cases is muktzeh. One should not ask that the Rosh contradicts himself, for Tosafot in Beitzah 23 and responsum 122 of the Ramban already resolve that both reasons are correct. Regarding turnips and radishes, which require digging with a hoe, there is no concern that one will personally undertake such exertion, so the reason must be muktzeh. Regarding fruit that one expected would fall and is therefore not muktzeh, the reason is the concern that one may climb and detach it. See siman 322, seif 3.
[11] And in pressing circumstances, etc. This applies specifically to an individual living in a village who cannot borrow bread from another Jew. On Yom Tov, however, one may be lenient, as stated by the Bach.
[12] [Levush] Such as drawing water, etc. It is clear that people also regard drawing water as a serious activity. This is explicit in Sefer HaTerumot, siman 247. The Taz overlooked all of this.
[13] [Levush] And some permit, etc. This requires study, for why does the Shulchan Aruch rule without qualification in seif 14 that one is stringent in cases of doubt? Perhaps a corpse is treated more stringently, as stated by the Magen Avraham. According to this, however, the Gemara in Shabbos 151 is difficult: what proof does it bring from the Mishnah concerning a bathhouse to the law of a corpse? See Tosafot there and examine carefully. One must say that the Gemara itself makes no distinction, and only the Beit Yosef independently ruled stringently regarding a corpse. This requires study. The principal question, however, appears to me to be no question at all, for one may say that seif 14 concerns a case where it is known that the non-Jew did it for a Jew, even though it was done privately. It appears from the later authorities that one may rely on the permitting opinions. See siman 515, seif katan 9. The Bach writes that even when a non-Jew brings a gift to a Jew, there remains a doubt whether he gathered it for himself and only afterward brought it to the Jew, unless it is known that he gathered it for the Jew. The Magen Avraham, here and in siman 515, extensively questions him. In my humble opinion, there is no difficulty, for the Bach distinguishes that specifically on Shabbos we are lenient in a case of doubt, whereas on Yom Tov we are stringent lest people come to treat it lightly. Nevertheless, Sefer HaTerumot, siman 248, implies somewhat that it is considered certain even on Shabbos.
