Magen Avraham, seif katan 4. The text should read: “For if so, he should not have brought proof from the Yerushalmi,” etc. Meaning, why did Beit Yosef need to bring proof from the Yerushalmi, which discusses the interval between a blessing and eating? He could have stated the stronger point that even if one had already begun, he must still recite another blessing because he has truly changed his mind. Rabbeinu Gershom would likewise not have disputed this. Rabbeinu Gershom’s primary reason was that Raavad ruled that we do not follow the Yerushalmi’s case of the lupine. Had the other authorities explained that the reason was an actual change of mind, Rabbeinu Gershom might not have disputed them. It is also possible that Magen Avraham refers to Beit Yosef’s statement, “The poskim ruled in the case of the lupine,” etc., which implies that on this basis the Shulchan Aruch ruled here that a new blessing is required even after he began reading. If so, however, the reason after he began would be that he changed his mind, and Beit Yosef should not have derived it from the Yerushalmi’s unresolved case of the lupine, which concerns the interval between blessing and eating. He should instead have said directly that if he began reading, this constitutes a change of mind and requires a new blessing. Furthermore, how do we know that Rabbeinu Gershom disagrees in that case? Perhaps he agrees that once he began, it is a change of mind and one must recite another blessing, and said only that before he began, his intent encompassed all the passages. This raised the further question: what basis did Beit Yosef have to say that they dispute a case where he already began? Perhaps all agree there that no new blessing is required. Concerning Magen Avraham’s statement on the words of Mabit, “Beit Yosef’s view also appears to be so,” the intention seems to be that Mabit distinguishes the case of a lupine, where the object over which the blessing was recited is no longer present, from this case, which resembles a basket of apples. Magen Avraham writes that Beit Yosef also holds this way, since all portions of Torah are equally good. That is, at the time of the blessing his intent encompassed both the passage shown to him and other passages, and therefore the blessing takes effect, just as with a basket of apples, where no additional blessing is required because his intent encompassed all the apples even though he recited the blessing while holding one. By contrast, in the fruit case where the original fruit is no longer present, the blessing has no object upon which to take effect. According to this, if he was shown a passage unrelated to the day’s obligation, it is comparable to the fruit falling from his hand, and a new blessing is required. Magen Avraham’s wording, however, that “we presume his intent,” unlike with fruit, suggests that he understands Mabit to hold that the lupine case concerns one who intended to eat only that particular item. Once it fell and he took another, he had changed his mind. Ordinarily, however, we presume that his intent extended to everything before him. Magen Avraham in siman 206, seif katan 8, explicitly explains Mabit this way. Magen Avraham therefore writes that Beit Yosef too distinguishes between the lupine case and this one, because here one’s intent presumably encompasses the entire Torah. It is more likely, however, that Magen Avraham understands Beit Yosef to hold that with fruit we do not ordinarily presume that one intended everything before him, but with Torah we do presume so because all its passages are equally good. Perhaps he understood Mabit the same way. When Mabit compares the case to a basket of apples, he means a basket where one intended to eat additional apples. So too, regarding Torah, such intent is presumed because all its passages are equally good. In siman 206, Magen Avraham later retracted this and explained Mabit to mean that even with fruit we ordinarily presume intent toward everything before him. Nevertheless, Magen Avraham’s discussion requires study, because Mabit explicitly gives the distinction stated above, namely that in the lupine case the item fell and is no longer present, unlike here. See Eliyah Rabbah, who cites his words. Perhaps Magen Avraham did not have Mabit itself before him, but only Knesses HaGedolah or Olas Tamid citing it. At present, I do not have Mabit available.
