The one called to the Torah. Beit Yosef cites Abudraham as follows: Rabbeinu Gershom wrote regarding an incident on Rosh Chodesh Teves, when two Torah scrolls are taken out. The chazzan mistakenly opened to the Chanukah reading and recited the blessing. The congregation informed him of the error, and he rolled the Torah scroll until he reached the Rosh Chodesh reading. Some say that he must recite the blessing again, as stated in the Yerushalmi that even an interruption of silence requires a new blessing. Here too, he interrupted in silence while rolling the Torah scroll. The Yerushalmi also states that if one took a lupine in hand, it fell, and he took another, he must recite a second blessing because he had not intended the second one. Here too, since he did not intend his blessing for this passage, he must recite the blessing again. Abudraham himself writes that they erred, because we do not follow the Yerushalmi’s view that silence constitutes an interruption, whether the silence is lengthy or brief. Nor do we follow the ruling concerning the lupine. Rather, whatever is placed before a person is covered by his blessing. Here too, the Torah scroll was before him and his intent encompassed all its passages. Even if he did not roll the same Torah scroll upon which he had recited the blessing, but instead opened the other scroll, there is reason to permit it, since both scrolls were brought out together to read the two passages relevant to the day. They may therefore be considered as both lying before him, with his intent directed to both. This was the ruling issued by the scholars in our city. The poskim, however, rule in siman 206 in accordance with the case of the lupine, and therefore here too he must recite another blessing. Beit Yosef. This implies that if he already began reading, he need not recite another blessing, because an interruption does not invalidate the blessing in the middle, as stated in seif 2. Nor need we be concerned that his original intent did not encompass this passage. This is comparable to one who had a basket full of fruit before him and intended to eat one fruit, but took another fruit in hand, recited the blessing, and ate it. If he then realizes that this was not the fruit he intended and takes the first one, he need not recite another blessing. This is certainly no worse than a case where other fruit of the same species was brought before him, in which case he need not recite another blessing, as stated in siman 206, seif 5. One cannot say that here he truly changed his mind, for if so, Beit Yosef should not have cited the Yerushalmi, which addresses the interval between the blessing and eating, and Rabbeinu Gershom would not have disputed it. Moreover, since Abudraham discusses only a case where he had not yet begun reading, what basis did Beit Yosef have to create a dispute even where he had begun? This requires study. Mabit, part 3, siman 294, writes that if the passage initially shown to him was also part of the day’s obligatory reading, such as when he was shown an earlier section, he need not recite another blessing. Even initially, one may read a section already read, as stated in siman 137, seif 6, and therefore his intent encompassed all of it. Likewise, if he was shown a later passage, it is comparable to a basket full of apples where one recited a blessing over one apple and later noticed a better one and took it, in which case no new blessing is required; see siman 206. If, however, he was shown a passage unrelated to the day’s obligation, he must recite the blessing again. Tikkun Yissachar, folio 56, rules likewise. Beit Yosef also appears to hold this way, for here he records two opinions, whereas in siman 206, seif 6, concerning fruit, he rules that a new blessing is required. This implies that with Torah we presume that one’s intent encompasses the entire Torah, because all its passages are equally good, unlike fruit. It appears to me that in the incident cited above, the congregation and chazzan rolled to the Rosh Chodesh passage of their own accord. Strictly speaking, they should have read the Chanukah passage first, so as not to cause the original blessing to have been recited in vain, especially since one opinion in the Gemara holds that even initially the Chanukah reading takes precedence.
