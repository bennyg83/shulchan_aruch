[2] And he read part, etc. This implies that if he interrupted before reading anything at all, he must return and recite the blessing. This is likewise implied by Bedek HaBayis, which compares this case to the blessing over a meal; see there. This is difficult, however, because above in siman 47, seif 9, regarding Bircas HaTorah, we rule that even if one interrupted before learning, he need not return and recite the blessing. It is unlike the blessing over bread or fruit because one is constantly obligated to learn Torah. Perhaps one may distinguish between the morning Bircas HaTorah, which applies to the continual obligation of Torah study, and the blessing of one called to the Torah, whose primary intent is directed to this particular reading. Since he did not begin, he must return and recite the blessing. Furthermore, I wrote there that Bach concludes that he must return and recite it; see there.
[3] And he need not, etc. This applies even if he already read three verses, as appears from Beit Yosef. Olas Tamid writes that Rabbeinu Ephraim implies that even if he diverted his attention from reading further and later resumed reading, he need not recite another blessing; as a practical matter, this requires study. In truth, according to Bedek HaBayis, which I cited as comparing this to the law of a meal blessing, the same law should apply after a diversion of attention, as explained below in siman 178. However, since Taz gives the reason that one presumably intended in his first blessing that even if he later diverted his attention he would return and read, this implies that even when he explicitly diverts his attention from further reading, he need not recite another blessing. One may therefore be lenient in a case of doubt concerning blessings. Maharil’s Derashos records an incident in which the reader mistakenly began with the blessing “Who has given us the Torah of truth,” and they rebuked him. He then began “Who has chosen us,” and Maharil rebuked him a second time and required him to return and begin from “Blessed are You,” the beginning of the blessing. Be’er Sheva, folio 102, writes that no author among the later authorities cited Maharil’s words, which is itself noteworthy, and that after great effort he found no basis for Maharil’s ruling. He argues at length and concludes that the order of blessings is not indispensable, just as we have learned regarding the blessings of Krias Shema. Therefore, if the reader did not remember until after he completed the concluding formula of the blessing, he should remain silent and, after the Torah reading, recite “Who has chosen us” as the final blessing, since the order of the blessings is not indispensable. Magen Avraham cites this as practical halachah in siman 139. I have taken it upon myself to defend the ruling of the great Maharil, who acted this way in practice, especially since I found that Mateh Moshe cites Maharil’s words as practical halachah. It appears to me that this is not comparable to the blessings of Krias Shema, for we rule that those blessings are independent blessings and were not instituted over Krias Shema itself, as Beit Yosef writes in simanim 46 and 60. In a related matter, I challenged my brother-in-law, Rabbi Yaakov, concerning what he wrote in a responsum questioning Taz in Yoreh Deah, siman 279, who ruled that one may not recite a blessing over that which will be read by heart. He objected that Krias Shema is recited by heart and we recite blessings over it. I answered that the blessings were not instituted over Krias Shema. He replied that Levush writes in siman 235 that they are not blessings over Krias Shema, for if they were, we should say “Who commanded us to read Shema,” just as we say “to engage in words of Torah.” This would seemingly imply that Bircas HaTorah, where we say only “Who gives the Torah,” was likewise not instituted over the Torah reading. I challenged this in turn: if so, one should be able to recite Bircas HaTorah even after reading without a blessing, just as one may recite the blessings of Krias Shema without reciting Krias Shema. Furthermore, the source of the law concerning the blessings of Krias Shema is cited by Beit Yosef in the name of a responsum of Rashba, and I found in responsum 319 that Rashba writes: “For he does not recite ‘Who commanded us to read Shema’ or ‘Who gives the Torah,’ in the manner instituted for the public Torah reading.” This explicitly shows that the blessing over the Torah reading was indeed instituted for the reading itself, unlike the blessings of Krias Shema. I also found in Abudraham, folio 31, that the reason no mitzvah blessing is recited over Krias Shema, as is done for other mitzvos, is that the Shema passage contains mention of Hashem’s name and also expresses His kingship, and is therefore itself regarded as a blessing. This rationale does not apply to Bircas HaTorah. Even according to Rabbeinu Yonah, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 59, who writes that one should not answer amen between the blessings of Krias Shema and Krias Shema itself, it appears to me that he too holds that these are blessings of praise that were merely placed adjacent to Krias Shema. Examine this carefully. The conclusion is that no proof may be drawn from the blessings of Krias Shema. Moreover, consider the matter itself. If one initially recited Borei Nefashos over beer, would he fulfill his obligation? Bircas HaTorah is also comparable to a blessing over benefit. I further found in Rabbi David Abudraham, folio 49, that the phrase “Who implanted eternal life within us” was not instituted in the opening blessing because Torah is not a tree of life until one takes hold of it. Accordingly, that wording is entirely inappropriate for the first blessing. I therefore say that one may rely on Maharil’s ruling. However, if he remembers before completing “Blessed are You, Hashem, Who gives the Torah,” it appears that he should begin from “Who has chosen us,” not from “Blessed are You.” Be’er Sheva likewise concludes there: If one mistakenly began “Who has given us,” and immediately realized the error within the time required for speech, he may not begin again from “Blessed are You, Hashem.” At first glance this requires study. What does he mean by remembering immediately within the time required for speech, since he has not yet completed the blessing? It is difficult to distinguish within the blessing itself between within and after that interval. Nor can it mean that he already completed the blessing, because Be’er Sheva writes that in such a case he should remain silent entirely, as cited above. It is also difficult to say that if he completed it and remembered within that interval he begins from “Who has chosen us,” whereas after that interval he remains silent. However, there is proof from siman 206, where one recited Borei Peri HaAdamah over beer and immediately realized the error and said Shehakol Nihyeh Bidvaro, and fulfilled his obligation. One may distinguish that there the blessing has no concluding formula, whereas here, once he concludes the blessing, an immediate correction may constitute an interruption. This is also implied in siman 59. The practical conclusion is that immediately after he completes the mistaken blessing he must return and begin from “Blessed are You, Hashem,” and this is the case addressed by Maharil. If he has not yet completed it, however, Maharil would appear to agree that he begins from “Who has chosen us,” not from “Blessed are You, Hashem,” as Be’er Sheva proves from the blessing of Yotzer Or in siman 59 and the blessing over beer below in siman 209, where one does not return to the beginning of the blessing. Even these proofs may be rejected. Yotzer Or is different, as Berachos 12b explains, because its wording was structured to mention the attribute of night during the day. The blessing over beer may also be distinguished, since that matter is an unresolved question and the poskim rule leniently that one need not return to the beginning of the blessing, because in a Rabbinic doubt we rule leniently, as Beit Yosef writes in siman 209. Bircas HaTorah, however, Be’er Sheva himself writes on folio 58, is D’Oraisa. Nevertheless, as a practical ruling, since he has not yet completed the blessing, he should not begin again from “Blessed are You,” for below in siman 144 I challenge Be’er Sheva’s position and conclude that Bircas HaTorah is D’Rabbanan.
