Anyone who is invalid, etc. See Beis Yosef. Taz says that because making tefillin includes forming the shin, an invalid person is disqualified from every stage. Pri Megadim explains that forming the shin is Torah law and the other stages rabbinic. Magen Avraham disagrees and treats covering and sewing like forming the shin, not merely as a decree. On Pri Megadim's reading of Taz, a minor old enough for education should seemingly qualify for the rabbinic stages, yet 'anyone who is invalid' appears to refer to all those listed above. Maharam Lublin, responsum 68, explains correctly: all the repairs and the shin are collectively called making tefillin and constitute one act, so its components cannot be divided. Nevertheless, if a thirteen-year-old whose signs are unknown covered or sewed them after an adult formed the shin, one need not invalidate them after the fact, since Noda BiYehudah is lenient even regarding writing.
In every stage of making them. This includes covering and sewing the batim, as the Mishnah Berurah states, and plainly also wrapping hair around the passages, which is indispensable even after the fact [Rambam, Hilchos Tefillin 3:1]. It certainly includes squaring the batim and titora and similar work. Maharam Lublin, responsum 68, likewise includes all work other than preparing the hide under covering and sewing. Magen Avraham's citation that scraping glue between letters or making another correction remains valid after the fact concerns writing where, for example, a gap made one word appear as two and one extended the preceding letter so it reads as one word. This is legally permitted even where kesidran is required, since it is not new writing, and invalid persons may do it just as they may erase. Correcting a currently invalid letter is different. Adding the crowns on sha'atnez getz does not invalidate even without them, so one need not be stringent after the fact. The same applies to tracing a still-recognizable letter merely to preserve it. There is room to question letters such as the yuds of alef, shin, and ayin that do not touch but are recognized by an ordinary child, or a yud lacking its left thorn: since their essential form exists, perhaps correction by an invalid person is no more new writing than correction out of order; or perhaps, since the letter is presently invalid and becomes valid through that person's act, it is invalid even after the fact. This differs from erasure, which does nothing to the letter's form. Maharam's discussion of a woman separating joined parts does not resolve this, since he was answering specifically about erasure. Pri Megadim writes that the dalet knot and the yud knot of arm-tefillin should be made lishmah and not by a woman or minor, treating them like writing and like the shin of head-tefillin. One need not be stringent when a thirteen-year-old whose signs are unknown makes them under an adult's instruction to act lishmah, since many are otherwise lenient and Tosafos, Shabbos 62a, also provides grounds. Certainly such a minor may make his own knot, for if he lacks signs his tefillin obligation is itself only rabbinic. A knot he made for himself before thirteen should be untied and remade after thirteen, since many poskim presume Torah adulthood at that age; if later examination finds two hairs, they are attributed back to the proper time. Many authorities question a woman's sewing of a Torah scroll, and scribes must be strongly warned against the custom of letting women sew at completion. After the fact, if her stitches can easily be identified and reopened, they should be repaired; otherwise Maharam Lublin is lenient, though Magen HaElef requires reopening even after the fact. Where the custom cannot be changed, the scribe must at least make the first, last, and an intermediate stitch before giving it to the women [Pischei Teshuvah, Yoreh De'ah 281].
