Or a minor. If a deaf-mute or mentally incompetent person wrote them, Pri Megadim leaves unresolved whether they may be preferable to a minor and whether it helps for others to stand over them and signal that they write lishmah. The signs of mental incompetence are explained in Yoreh De'ah 1:5. Regarding when a minor is deemed an adult if we do not know that he produced two hairs, the Mishnah Berurah states that a full beard suffices, following Choshen Mishpat 35. We omitted Magen Avraham's requirement of age eighteen, since all the later authorities challenge it; nevertheless, Chayei Adam and Shulchan Shlomo permit it even initially, so such writing cannot simply be invalidated. Pri Megadim attempts to explain Magen Avraham from siman 53:8, gloss, where after eighteen even a little beard is called a full beard. That case is different: the person is already known to have two hairs and the beard is required only for the congregation's dignity; indeed, at age twenty even no beard suffices. Here the beard itself must establish Torah adulthood, so an actually full beard may be required. Scattered beard hairs certainly are not a full beard, as follows from Tosafos, Yevamos 80b. It remains uncertain whether the 'little beard' described in siman 53 (large hairs on both sides of the face and the chin) counts as a full beard even for Torah law. The Gra explains that Tur relies on the presumption that the lower sign usually appears before the beard; this may mean even scattered beard hairs establish that two hairs probably appeared, except where it was known that they had not. In any event, before he has any beard he should certainly not write tefillin unless we know that he produced two hairs, even if he is eighteen or older. After the fact, if a bar-mitzvah boy wrote them and cannot now be examined, practical law is uncertain: Noda BiYehudah and Pri Megadim validate them, while Rabbi Akiva Eiger and Yeshuos Yaakov are stringent. If an eighteen-year-old wrote them, one should not be stringent after the fact, since some permit this even initially.
Or an apostate to idolatry. This concerns one not devoted to it; otherwise he is a heretic and the writing requires burning [Olas Tamid]. An apostate who sins to provoke has the status of a gentile. 'To provoke' means choosing prohibition when both permitted and prohibited food are present; one who indiscriminately takes whatever comes to hand is not yet in that category. For slaughter alone, Rema, Yoreh De'ah 2:5, treats indifference as provocation because he is no longer one subject to valid slaughter. Magen Avraham applies this to tefillin: one indifferent to the mitzvah is no longer subject to binding. Ran would apparently be lenient. The Gra implies that Rashi disqualifies even one not acting provocatively, and even for other sins. Shach rules that apostasy to provoke, idolatry, or public Shabbos desecration disqualifies after a single act until repentance; possibly the same applies immediately to indifference toward tefillin. The stringent view concerning an apostate for appetite is Tosafos, who treats writing sacred texts more severely than other matters; Pri Megadim also remains doubtful. At minimum, if he is an apostate concerning tefillin for appetite, it must be established that he knows the laws of sacred writing, including chok tochos, just as an apostate slaughterer must be known to know the laws. One who rejects either arm- or head-tefillin is no longer subject to binding, since Scripture joins both to 'you shall write them.' A scribe who habitually wears head-tefillin on his forehead despite warning may perhaps still write sacred texts, since the tefillin may have rested correctly for one moment. The Mishnah Berurah's disqualification of an informer after one act follows Pri Megadim; Pischei Teshuvah cites Chomos Yerushalayim otherwise. Many later authorities disqualify even an informer for appetite, though Pri Megadim questions this.
One who is not subject to binding, etc. Even if the Kutim were true converts and commanded in binding tefillin, they cast off the yoke and do not believe in the mitzvos; they are therefore called 'not subject to binding.' The same rationale applies to the other persons listed.
