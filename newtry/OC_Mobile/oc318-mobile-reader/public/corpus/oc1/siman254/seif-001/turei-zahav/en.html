But kid meat and poultry, etc., are permitted, etc. This means that they are permitted even before reaching the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai, since we are not concerned that he may stoke the coals. The prohibition in se'if 2 before the food reaches the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai, which appears to apply even to kid meat, concerns meat placed directly upon the fire. Since we see that he is not concerned that the meat may scorch, because he placed it upon the coals, we are concerned there that he may stoke them to hasten the roasting. After it reaches the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai, however, even beef is permitted, for since it is fit to eat, why would he stoke and thereby ruin it? Near the fire, by contrast, there is a distinction between kid meat and other meat. Kid meat is permitted even if the oven is not sealed with clay, meaning when it is cut into limbs. If it is whole, however, kid meat has the same law as beef and specifically requires sealing with clay. Sealing with clay is effective because exposure to air is harmful even to beef once the oven cools. This is the Rambam's view according to the Maggid Mishneh's explanation. The Tur, however, does not distinguish between meat placed directly upon the fire and meat placed near it, but only between cooking and roasting. He maintains that roasting, whether near the fire or upon it, has both a stringency and a leniency compared with cooking. The stringency is that before reaching the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai it is forbidden according to all opinions, lest he stoke the coals. This applies even if the meat is completely raw, although all agree above that raw food may be left in an oven, because here, with roasted food, we fear that he will stoke since this hastens roasting near the fire. The leniency is that according to the Rif and Rambam, in siman 253, cooked food may not be left even when continued reduction improves it, whereas here it is permitted once it has merely reached the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai. Since it is fit to eat, a small amount of heat suffices and he will not need to stoke. This is compelled by the Tur's wording, for he writes without qualification, 'One may not roast meat, etc., unless there is enough time for it to roast to the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai.' This implies even ordinary roasting, which is near the fire. Otherwise, he should have written here 'upon the coals,' as he wrote afterward concerning a cake and as the Rambam wrote, 'One may not place meat upon the fire, etc.' It is therefore clear that the Tur holds that even near the fire it is permitted after reaching the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai. According to this, it requires study why the Rama wrote in the name of 'there are those who are stringent,' meaning the Tur's view that everything placed upon the fire is forbidden, since that view also contains a leniency after the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai. The Rama's subsequent statement, 'Then one must distinguish between kid meat, etc., in the manner explained,' does not refer to everything stated above. According to the first view, the permissibility of kid meat rests upon the likelihood that it will scorch, and this applies near the fire or in an oven even when it is open. According to the Tur, however, the permissibility of kid meat is because exposure to air is harmful to it, which applies only in a closed oven that one will not open for this purpose. The Tur does not accept the reasoning based upon scorching and spoilage, and therefore even kid meat near the fire is forbidden before reaching the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai. Rather, the Rama's statement refers only to the rule that kid meat is permitted only when cut into pieces, for only then is exposure to air harmful to it; when whole, its law is like that of beef.
