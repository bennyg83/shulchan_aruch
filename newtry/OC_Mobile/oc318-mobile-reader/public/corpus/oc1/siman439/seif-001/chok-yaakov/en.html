Nine piles. This is Rambam’s wording in chapter 2 of Hilchos Chametz concerning these laws. Maharil, in the name of Maharshal, writes that Rambam’s approach here is very stringent and raises many difficulties. That criticism is based upon a corrupt text found in some editions of Rambam. Maggid Mishneh and Kesef Mishneh already established the correct text, and no difficulty remains.
If the loaf is small. Just as where it fell from the pile, even a large loaf may be attributed leniently because in a Rabbinic doubt we rule leniently and say that the mouse took matzah. This is Tur’s view. Rambam is stringent. For the reasoning behind Rambam’s view, see Beit Yosef in the name of Maggid Mishneh, chapter 2 of Hilchos Chametz; Lechem Mishneh there; Rosh Yosef on this siman; Responsa Maharit, part 1, siman 2; and Tamim Deim, siman 241, in Raavad’s strictures against Baal HaMaor in Pesachim. Bach rules that Tur’s view is primary.
We attribute it leniently and say that he ate it. This is because there is a double doubt: perhaps it was matzah, and even if it was chametz, perhaps the mouse ate it. Although the two doubts did not arise simultaneously, but one after the other, and although in a fixed mixture we ordinarily do not rule leniently through a double doubt, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 110, we are lenient here. I explained the reason in Minchas Yaakov, klal 43, seifim 27 and 42, and in Kuntres HaSefeikos, seif katan 45. See Magen Avraham’s discussion. The Mechaber quotes Rambam without qualification and requires inspection. This implies that he rules stringently in every case and does not permit these doubts. See Bach. Perhaps he also takes account of Baal HaMaor’s ruling like Rabbi Zeira, that if the mouse leaves something over, this is not considered a doubt at all. Olas Shabbos writes that the Mechaber also rules leniently here, and that Rama appears to agree because he did not formulate his gloss as a disagreement. This does not appear primary. The Mechaber rules stringently, but because he stated the matter without elaboration, Rama did not phrase his gloss as an explicit disagreement. For practical halachah, one should not depart from Rama’s ruling to be lenient, particularly where the chametz was nullified. See above, seif katan 2. Olas Shabbos further writes that the same law applies where there are nine piles of chametz and one of matzah. In Minchas Yaakov, klal 43, seif katan 42, I wrote that this requires consideration. See there for the reason.
Every fixed mixture is treated as half and half. If the chametz became mixed and no longer stands separately, it is no longer considered fixed. Magen Avraham. See Minchas Yaakov, klal 43, for the laws of fixed mixtures and what is considered fixed by Torah law or Rabbinically.
And if one item separated. Tosafos, Pesachim 9b, under the heading “This is like nine shops,” explains that the same applies if the mouse took the item outside our presence. It is then considered separated, and we follow the majority.
He need not inspect again. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak’s explanation in Tosafos there, the entire discussion of nine piles concerns whether one may eat the piece taken by the mouse. Accordingly, when one piece separated from the fixed mixture, it may be eaten during Pesach, just as where nine shops sell kosher meat and one sells neveilah, a piece that separates from the fixed mixture is permitted. Minchas Kohen, page 104b, likewise writes that it may be eaten during Pesach and is not prohibited even Rabbinically. Nevertheless, it appears to me that one should not rely upon this leniency in practice regarding the severe prohibition of chametz, especially since chametz is treated more stringently regarding nullification than other prohibitions. Moreover, Rabbeinu Yitzchak’s explanation is not accepted by all poskim. Rambam, Tur, and the other poskim record this law only concerning inspection, not eating, following Rashi’s explanation.
