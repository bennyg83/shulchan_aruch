1) [Se'if 1] And a mouse came and took, etc. The reason is that anything fixed in its place is regarded as equally divided, as stated afterward. This applies specifically when the mouse took the item in our presence from its fixed place. If it took it outside our presence, it is regarded as though the item separated outside our presence and was then taken, in which case we say that anything that separates is presumed to have separated from the majority. Tosafos, Pesachim 9b; Magen Avraham, subsection 1; Peri Chadash; Chok Yaakov, subsection 5; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1; Chok Yosef, subsection 4; Mekor Chaim, subsection 1; Chemed Moshe, subsection 1.
2) Similarly, if the piles became mixed and the mouse then took from them, we say that anything that separates is presumed to have separated from the majority. Magen Avraham, subsection 4; Chok Yaakov, subsection 4; Eliyah Rabbah, there; Chok Yosef, there; Mekor Chaim, subsection 2; Chemed Moshe, there; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 3.
3) There, gloss. Nevertheless, if the loaf is small, etc., we presume leniently, etc. The Tur explains that this is because there is a compound doubt: perhaps it was matzah rather than chametz, and even if it was chametz, perhaps the mouse ate it. The Tur further writes that if the loaf is too large for the mouse to consume entirely and one did not nullify it, one must search; but if one nullified it, no search is required under any circumstances. Beis Yosef explains the Tur's reasoning: once one has nullified it, the matter involves a doubt concerning a rabbinic law, which is resolved leniently. Rambam, chapter 2, however, writes without qualification that one must search. Maggid Mishneh writes there that this is also the opinion of Rashi and the Geonim. In the name of Ra'ah, he explains that although this involves a doubt concerning a rabbinic law, the original obligation to search for chametz was itself instituted because of a doubt. The Sages therefore treated a doubt concerning this obligation more stringently than other doubts involving their enactments, and this is the primary ruling. Beis Yosef cites his words. Rambam's unqualified language thus implies that one must search in every case, whether the loaf is small or whether one nullified it. This also appears to be the view of the Shulchan Aruch, which states Rambam's ruling without qualification and does not cite the Tur. Rema should therefore have presented his ruling as the opinion of some authorities. However, it is Rema's practice in many places to state a ruling without attribution when the Shulchan Aruch did not explicitly state the contrary. Ma'amar Mordechai, subsection 2, writes likewise. Erech HaShulchan, subsection 2, likewise writes that Maran's opinion is to prohibit even when there is a compound doubt. Maharikash, however, writes that if one nullified it, no search is required because of the compound doubt. Chok Yaakov, subsection 3, writes that one may rely on Rema where one nullified it. See there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1, likewise writes that one may rely on this where one nullified it or can still nullify it. See there. With a large loaf, however, one may not be lenient on account of nullification. Levush rules likewise, and Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2, agrees.
4) There. Anything fixed in its place, etc. The same applies to nine piles of chametz and one pile of matzah: we say that anything fixed in its place is regarded as equally divided. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1; Chok Yosef, subsection 3; Erech HaShulchan, there. Chok Yaakov, subsection 3, however, writes that the matter is uncertain. Chukas HaPesach, at the end of subsection 2, likewise writes that where there are nine piles of chametz and one of matzah, the item is prohibited despite a compound doubt. See there. It is possible that when the loaf is small and one nullified it, one may be lenient and need not search again.
