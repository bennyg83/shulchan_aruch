(א) With a sum. But from the language of Tur and Shulchan Aruch it is implied that it is forbidden to mention the name of money at all, even without a sum and calculation. Magen Avraham:

(ב) His purchase is known — that it has no fixed amount and lacks definite pricing — then it is forbidden to say give me in one coin or in two even if he takes what he gives him, for then one cannot say his intention is only to say what he needs since it is not known what he will give him. And Darkei Moshe wrote and it appears to me that similarly regarding a measure — they only forbade mentioning the name of measure except in a thing whose measure is not known; but in a thing whose measure is known, only informing the shopkeeper how much he needs — permitted, end of his words — meaning specifically when he says give me a measure of nuts or another thing they are not accustomed to measure, for then he does not know how much the measure holds and does not know if he will need more or not, and if so his intention is not to say how much he needs; but in wine and the like his intention is to inform how much he needs and it is permitted, and this is the permission for the buyer and the permission for the seller, see seif 1. And it requires study why they do not practice thus now. And Shevut Yaakov wrote that it is permitted to say fill this vessel for me up to the grooves on Motzei Shabbos, end of his words — see Magen Avraham. And see Taz who elaborated and was stringent in these matters on Motzei Shabbos. What people practice — that they write so-and-so left such and such money and so-and-so such and such, and tomorrow on Shabbat when giving wine the buyer takes a pin and makes holes corresponding to the amount of wine he buys — it is forbidden to look at that writing in which the sum of money was written, as Magen Avraham wrote siman 307 seif 12 Beit Yosef in the name of Rosh, and it is implied that it is also forbidden to write sums of money on notes; and when the buyer of wine places the note where the man's name is written — forbidden to read those notes, for they are ordinary documents; therefore they practiced to take a grain as a sign or another thing. And charity collectors perhaps are permitted with notes; nevertheless it is good to be stringent where possible with a grain, and so the early ones practiced. Magen Avraham and see there:
