1) [Seif 1] One may say to another person, 'Fill this utensil for me,' etc. One may specifically say, 'Fill this utensil,' but all opinions forbid mentioning a unit of measure. The author writes the same shortly below in seif 4. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 1. One may, however, say, 'Fill this utensil for me with a particular beverage and I will pay you after Shabbos.' Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1. See above, siman 307, seif 11.
2) There: Even if it is designated for measuring. This means that it is ordinarily used to measure merchandise for sale. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 1. A government standard measure or the like, however, which is not ordinarily used to measure merchandise for sale except when a barrel of liquor or similar item is purchased, or when someone specifically requests that it be used because it is an accurate measure, may not be requested when other measuring utensils are available. Saying specifically, 'Fill this utensil,' then effectively mentions a fixed quantity and appears to be a stratagem. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1.
3) The synagogue treasurers have a blank ledger in which the names of individual congregants are written, each on a separate page. When a person purchases the honor of reciting Kaddish or something similar on Shabbos, the treasurer ordinarily places on that person's page pieces of paper marked with large dots. He counts out dots equal to the price of the Kaddish and accumulates the pieces there. The next day he removes and counts them, thereby learning the amount to record. He does the same with the names of assistants so that he will not forget and cause the synagogue a loss. See Pischei HaDevir, paragraph 1, who discusses this at length and writes that one should not protest, because they have grounds upon which to rely since these are calculations for a mitzvah. When there are not many purchases and it is possible to pay attention and remember who pledged and how much, however, it is preferable to refrain; see there. See below, paragraph 8.
4) Sellers of pickled vegetables called turshi in Arabic customarily refuse to give any to a person who comes to take turshi on Shabbos unless he leaves collateral, such as a cord or scarf. The seller ties upon it one knot for each portion taken, not two knots upon one another, but each knot separately in its own place, so that the next day he can collect for as many portions as there are knots in the collateral. It appears that this involves no prohibition. There is no prohibition in taking collateral, as Rema writes above in siman 307, seif 11. The knots are nonpermanent and not craftsman's knots, so they may initially be tied, as stated above in siman 317, seif 1. Pischei HaDevir, paragraph 2; see there.
5) There: And he carries it to his home. This differs from weekday conduct and creates no concern of commerce. Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 2.
6) There: He says to him, 'Fill this utensil for me.' The seller fills it from another vessel that is not itself a measure of this quantity. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1.
7) There: But to measure with a utensil designated for measuring, etc. This clearly demonstrates an intent to measure and constitutes weekday conduct. Shulchan Aruch HaRav there.
8) There, gloss: Some are lenient and permit saying, etc. Rosh writes in Responsa, klal 22: Regarding the practice in your place of measuring wine on Shabbos, before Shabbos they write that one person deposited a certain amount of money and another person a different amount. The next day, when the wine is measured, the wine seller takes a needle and makes a number of holes corresponding to the measures he gives. Know that they commit two wrongs. First, they measure, whereas the Mishnah teaches that one may say to another, 'Fill this utensil for me,' but not with a measure. The Gemara explains that this means not with a utensil designated for measuring. Second, it is forbidden to look at the writing, for the Mishnah teaches that one may count his guests and portions orally but not from writing. The Gemara asks why, and Rav Bibi answers that it is a decree lest he erase, while Abaye says lest he read ordinary documents. These are his words. Beis Yosef, Olas Shabbos, paragraph 2, and Magen Avraham, paragraph 5, cite him. Olas Shabbos writes there that according to this, written tokens called zeichen in German, which record how much beverage one must give, are likewise forbidden. Lead tokens are also forbidden, because the author writes above in siman 307, seif 12, that even engraved writing on a tablet or ledger may not be read. These are his words. Magen Avraham there writes that the responsum of Rosh implies that one may likewise not write sums of money upon slips and, when a person takes wine, place the slip where that person's name is written. Reading such slips is forbidden as reading ordinary documents. The custom is therefore to use a seed as a marker or some other object. Charity collectors may possibly use slips, as stated in siman 307, but it is nevertheless preferable, whenever possible, to use a seed, as the early authorities practiced. These are his words. This means placing one seed for every measure where the person's name is written. Machatzis HaShekel.
9) There, gloss: He gives slightly less or more, etc. This involves no theft, because the owner knows that everyone customarily does so on Yom Tov. The custom is likewise to place a ring inside the measuring vessel. Mordechai; Yam Shel Shlomo; Darkei Moshe. This permission applies to the seller. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 3; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1. The statement that this permission applies to the seller means that when he does not measure precisely, he does not transgress by measuring on Shabbos or Yom Tov. The buyer's permission is explained in seif 4 in the gloss; see there. See the following paragraph.
10) There, gloss: This is the widespread custom, etc. This refers to Ashkenazim. Sephardim, who accepted the rulings of the Shulchan Aruch, must be stringent not to measure with a utensil designated for measuring and pour it into the buyer's vessels, even when giving slightly less or more. Where the lenient custom following Rema is already established, however, one should not protest. It is clear that those who follow the lenient view stated by Rema may say, 'Fill it for me another time,' meaning as on the first occasion when he gave slightly less or more. He may repeat this formulation as many times as necessary. Birkei Yosef, Shiyurei Berachah, paragraph 1.
