"The newborn child," etc. Magen Avraham writes that all the measures stated here involve pain to the child, and because none of them is a melacha the Sages did not decree against them. This follows Tosafos at the end of Perek Mefanin. In She'iltot of Rav Achai, Parashat Shemot, it appears that failure to cut the umbilical cord can endanger the child. The She'iltot asks whether one may cut the cord on Shabbos and whether tying alone is sufficient to avoid danger, or whether at times one may fail to notice the danger. It concludes from Rav Nachman's ruling like R' Yose that the cord may be cut where necessary because of possible danger. Chiddushei Ran and Meiri likewise write that failure to cut it can be dangerous. Rambam, chapter 2 of Hilchot Shabbos, includes care of a newborn within the general laws of danger. The authorities discuss a child whose viability is uncertain, such as one who may be a 7-month or 8-month child and whose hair and nails are not fully developed. Magen Avraham distinguishes between a case where the months are known to have been completed and a case of genuine doubt. The Gemara in Erchin concerning a woman who died while sitting on the birth-stool, where a knife is brought even through a public domain to attempt to save the child, shows that Shabbos is desecrated in a case of possible life. Magen Avraham answers that there the months of pregnancy were known to have been completed. He further connects this issue with the 2 explanations cited by Ramban in Torat HaAdam concerning a fetus whose mother smelled food on Yom Kippur. According to the view of Halachot Gedolot, even a fetus before 40 days, when it is regarded as mere fluid, warrants desecration of the prohibition in order to preserve the life that may develop. If so, all the more so should one desecrate Shabbos for a child already born whose viability is doubtful. According to Ramban's other explanation, however, a woman on the birth-stool is treated differently because the child is already considered as standing at the doorway of birth, which leaves room to distinguish a doubtful 8-month newborn. In my view the issues are not necessarily dependent upon one another. Ramban's statement that one does not desecrate for an ordinary fetus is because the Torah does not yet treat the fetus as an independent life for this purpose, while a child at the stage of actual birth is different. A newborn, by contrast, is already a separate person. Although in some contexts doubt concerning viability prevents liability for killing him because of the rule that the congregation must judge and save, pikuach nefesh is treated leniently in a case of doubt. One can nevertheless argue in the opposite direction. When the mother is fed on Yom Kippur, there is a strong presumption that she will ultimately give birth to a viable child who will observe many Shabbosos, as Ran writes. Here, however, the child has already been born and we see that his hair and nails are not completed, which creates a stronger indication that he may be an 8-month nonviable child. This is therefore not a simple comparison. According to Magen Avraham, who makes the matter depend upon the 2 explanations, Ritva in his novellae to Niddah appears to follow the approach of Halachot Gedolot that one desecrates even for a fetus. On that view, one would likewise desecrate Shabbos for a newborn whose status as a 7-month or 8-month child is doubtful.
