Seif katan 15: See there in Torat HaAdam and in the Ran in Yoma. According to the first opinion there, even in doubt whether its months were completed one desecrates Shabbat, like the case in seif 5, and here since, etc.; so it must read. What he wrote at the beginning of this seif katan, that Olat Shabbat objected, etc., that above speaks where its months were completed, etc., at first glance requires study. There is still a doubt that perhaps the fetus died first; nevertheless we desecrate Shabbat because perhaps it will be found alive, as explained there. According to what the Magen Avraham wrote in seif katan 14, that we always tear open even if it was not uprooted, although this is a minority case, what is the difference between this doubt and the doubt of a stillborn? It appears to me that regarding the doubt that perhaps it will not be found alive we are not concerned, as with every doubt of life where we desecrate Shabbat, because in any event it is already included in the category of living. What the Gemara says in Eruvin, "you might have thought this applies only where there is a presumption of life," etc., teaches that this too is called a presumption of life. The only doubt is perhaps it died first, and it is like any other person for whom we desecrate Shabbat on doubt. By contrast, with a doubtful stillborn, if it is a stillborn it is not included in the category of a living person at all; since the doubt is whether it ever entered the category of a living being, in such a case we do not desecrate Shabbat. Therefore with a doubtful seven-month or eight-month child we do not desecrate Shabbat. But with a woman sitting on the birthing stool whose months were completed, we properly desecrate, for it is already in the category of living, only there is doubt perhaps it died first, and in such a case we do desecrate.
What he wrote, "and one may say it depends on a dispute," etc., requires study for one who examines Ramban's words. Ramban holds that a fetus does not have the law of saving a life, even if its months were completed, because a fetus is part of its mother. So too is implied from his proof from Niddah, that only one who kills a day-old child is liable, but not one who kills a fetus, and even a day-old child only when its months were completed, as explained there. The exception is where the woman sitting on the birthing stool died; then we say it is no longer part of its mother and is like any other person. The Talmudic statement that a doubtful seven-month or eight-month child is not desecrated refers to circumcision, as written in Olat Shabbat. If so, the Magen Avraham's implication that according to Ramban one must at least know its months were completed even where the woman died requires study: from where does he know this? His proof that it is permitted to kill the fetus, meaning the case of a woman in difficult labor before the head emerged, is irrelevant, for the mother is then alive and it depends on her birth; it is part of her, and even if its months were completed it may be killed to save her because a fetus does not have independent life-saving status. But where the woman died, it is no longer part of her and is as though born, and perhaps even if we do not know its months were completed we desecrate. I found that Dagul Merevavah briefly raised this difficulty against the Magen Avraham. What the Magen Avraham wrote, "and here since there is a defect," is also astonishing and contradicts what he first wrote, that even without a defect one must know specifically that its months were completed. Perhaps there is a scribal error and it should read: "all the more so here, since there is a defect." More likely something is missing and it should read: "and according to the first opinion there, even in doubt whether its months were completed, we desecrate, like that case in seif 5; and here since there is," etc. Still, this does not resolve what he wrote, for the proof from permission to kill the fetus is no proof that one must know its months were completed when the woman died, as I wrote. Perhaps here too something is missing, and it should read that according to Ramban even where she died, if she did not die then even if its months were known to be completed we do not desecrate, for he brings proof from the permission to kill it. His intent would be that from Ramban's language, that the only concern is lack of a presumption of life, it implies there is no initial doubt of stillborn, only the doubt perhaps it died first. But lest one ask, if so why does Ramban distinguish whether she died or not, since where she did not die and the months were completed we should also desecrate, the Magen Avraham writes that if she did not die, even if its months were completed we do not desecrate, since it is part of its mother. If we want to uphold the Magen Avraham's words as printed, we must say that he holds the matter itself needs a reason: why do we not desecrate for a fetus, since all doubts of life override Shabbat? The reason "part of its mother" alone is insufficient, for in any event it will ultimately keep mitzvot, as Ramban wrote in the view of Behag. One must say that this reason, "better to desecrate one Shabbat," applies only once it has certainly entered the category of living. Anything still in the category of doubtful stillborn has not entered that category to allow desecration on the possibility that it will become living. Even if its months are known to be completed, as long as it still needs its mother to open the womb and emerge alive, it may be that at birth no soul will remain and it will be like a stillborn that never saw the light of the world. Therefore it has not yet entered the category of living. This fits Ramban's language that we do not desecrate for stillborns: even where the months are known, the reason is still that we do not desecrate for a doubtful stillborn. Its present life is only as part of its mother. But once she died, it no longer depends on her, is another body, and is alive, only the door is locked before it. According to this, even when she died one must know that its months were completed; otherwise the doubt whether it was a stillborn from the outset remains. Thus the Magen Avraham's proof from permission to kill it can be understood: where the mother lives, even if its months were completed, it may be killed to save her, showing that before emerging alive into the world it is not yet in the full category of life. But this is still not fully clear, and the more correct reading is as I wrote, that the later phrase refers to the first opinion. I saw in the gloss at the end of Mekor Chaim that he took the words plainly and explained that according to Ramban one must know, because before birth there is no majority rule that women do not miscarry, whereas here after birth one needs a defect showing the months were not completed. This is not clear, for in life-threatening matters we do not follow majority; and in any event here, where the doubt is seven-month or eight-month, the ordinary majority that women give birth at nine months no longer applies, as is clear in Yevamot.
