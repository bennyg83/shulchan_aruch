Seif katan 10, "one who is sitting on the birth-stool," etc. The fetus ordinarily dies first. Tosafos in Niddah 44a explain the statement that a 1-day-old infant inherits from his mother and transmits that inheritance to his paternal brother. For example, Yaakov married Rachel and Leah; Rachel bore Reuven and Leah bore Shimon. Leah died while Shimon was still alive, even if he survived her only briefly. Shimon inherits his mother, and after Shimon dies his paternal brother Reuven inherits from him. A fetus, however, does not inherit in order to transmit the inheritance to paternal brothers, because when the mother dies during pregnancy the fetus is presumed to have died first. Tosafos therefore ask from Shmuel's ruling in Erchin 7a that when a woman dies while sitting on the birth-stool on Shabbos, a knife is brought and her abdomen is opened in case the child is found alive. If the fetus always dies before the mother, what possibility is there? They answer that once she has sat on the birth-stool and the child has already become detached from its prior position, it has independent vitality, and the mother can die while the child remains alive. "And although it convulses," etc. Niddah, Bava Basra, and Erchin discuss an incident in which a fetus moved after the mother's death. Mar bar Rav Ashi explains that such movement is like the tail of a lizard after it has been severed and is not proof of life. Some responsa nevertheless rule that one opens the mother even when the fetus had not yet become detached, because although ordinarily the fetus dies first, sometimes the mother dies first and the fetus remains alive. This is inferred from Rashi's wording that "at times it happens that she dies first." To reconcile the Gemara according to this view one must say that the rule that the fetus dies first expresses the ordinary case, not an absolute rule. Rosh, by contrast, limits the permission to a case where the fetus has already become detached. Rama in responsa writes that this distinction is also found in Erchin concerning a pregnant woman taken out for execution. If she has not sat on the birth-stool, the fetus is part of her body; if she has sat on the birth-stool, they wait until she gives birth because the fetus is already regarded as a separate body. Rama understands that once it has become a separate body it may also possess independent life even if the mother dies. Magen Avraham notes that Rambam and the Mechaber state the case simply as "one sitting on the birth-stool." Although this ordinarily means that the fetus has become detached, he understands their lack of an explicit limitation to suggest that the permission is broader, and that they may follow Rashi: even without clear evidence of detachment, there are occasional cases in which the mother dies first. Rama's proof from Rashi rests on the word "sometimes." Rashi explains Shmuel's ruling by saying that sometimes it happens that the mother dies first. This implies that even before detachment the possibility cannot be excluded entirely. Tosafos themselves ask from other passages. If a fetus always dies before the mother, how can one slaughter an animal and find a live 9-month fetus inside? How can the Torah speak of an orphaned sacrificial animal whose mother died before it was born? They answer that when the mother was killed rather than dying naturally, the mother can die first, because the Gemara in Erchin explains that the rule that the fetus dies first applies to ordinary death through the Angel of Death. In execution or other violent death, the mother may die first. They also offer the case where the fetus has already become detached. Accordingly, even in monetary inheritance law the Mishnah's rule that a fetus does not inherit is a rule of certainty in the ordinary case. Where the mother was killed or where the fetus had already become detached, the fetus can survive her and inherit. Sema in Choshen Mishpat, siman 276, cites this distinction. Yet the codifiers there state the rule without qualification, which may indicate that they follow the broader understanding that, for inheritance, a fetus is not removed from the existing chazakah of the mother's heirs on the basis of a very remote possibility. Thus for monetary law, because the possibility that the mother died first is only an extremely small minority, the estate remains in the possession of the mother's established heirs. For pikuach nefesh, however, even a remote possibility of life can justify desecrating Shabbos. This explains how the rulings can coexist. The practical difference also explains the case of convulsing after the mother's death. Such movement does not establish life for inheritance, because it may be mere postmortem movement like a lizard's tail. Yet where there remains a genuine possibility that the child is alive, the laws of pikuach nefesh require attempting rescue.
