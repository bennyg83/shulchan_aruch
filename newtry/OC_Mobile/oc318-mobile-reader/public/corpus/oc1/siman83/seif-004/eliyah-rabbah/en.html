[4] But not inside it, etc. So wrote Beit Yosef because Rambam and Rosh ruled thus, and Rabbeinu Yeruham wrote in the name of Rosh. What Beit Yosef wondered about Rabbeinu Yeruham — that he later wrote the Rosh ruled leniently — I examined Rabbeinu Yeruham's words and there is no difficulty: his intent is that Rosh explained Rif's words, who wrote it is an unresolved matter in general and Rosh explained it leniently; but Rosh himself holds it is clear to be stringent — see there, for I abbreviated. And likewise Baal Halakhot Gedolot is stringent, and even after the fact if one already read or prayed he has not fulfilled [his obligation] — that is, it is rabbinic; and this has bearing for a case of doubt. See responsum of R' Yom Tov Tzahalon siman 72. And it appears that just as they forbade inside it, so too it is forbidden within his four amot — for Rav Chisda said: a person stands in the privy and prays, and Rashi explained there: distant four amot. Although one could say he explained thus for the questioner, from the plain implication that is the law for the conclusion — for it could enter your mind the gemara [means] even afterward one must distance as far as his eyes can see; if so, for the conclusion it would suffice for us to permit as far as his eyes can see, but within four amot is forbidden — and Derishah wrote explicitly in the name of Mordechai. Some slight support for this: it uses the language "opposite" and does not say "standing at his side and praying" as appears on daf 25a — "if it were ten high, one sits at his side and reads" — rather, perforce one must say specifically distant four amot is permitted. Inquiry is needed: the apparent implication of Tur and Shulchan Aruch and Levush is that no distancing is required at all; see above siman 81; and for practical law one may be lenient after the fact.
