And further - See Taz. I did not understand this, because the Mechaber's expression 'opposite it' indicates in front of it but outside four amos. This is also implied by Mordechai, who writes that he permitted them a perforated bench, based on the case of Rav Pappa standing opposite a privy at a distance of four amos. Perishah likewise writes that according to the Gemara's question it must have been at a distance of four amos. The ordinary meaning of 'opposite it' is four amos away, for there is a distinction between being at its side and being opposite it. Consequently, even according to the conclusion concerning a new privy that was designated, 'opposite it' means outside four amos. This is also the Mechaber's view in seif 2: it is permitted opposite it, but not adjacent to it as in seif 1. His statement, 'but not inside it,' means that within four amos is considered inside it. A perforated bench has the law of a new privy, because it was designated for this purpose; excrement was never on the bench itself, only in the vessel beneath it. Thus he properly said that one distances four amos even in front of it, and that suffices. Otherwise, what did Mordechai mean when he wrote, 'I permitted it for them'? On the contrary, he would have forbidden it like actual excrement. Rather, he permitted them to stand in front of it at a distance of four amos, like the case of Rav Pappa. It is not comparable to the first seif, because a bench is not considered partitions, as Taz writes in paragraph 1. See Magen Avraham, paragraph 5; with Hashem's help, this will be explained further there. See Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 7.
Whether - Regarding whether it is permitted to think words of Torah in a new privy, Baer Heitev in siman 85 writes that it is permitted, while Kesef Mishneh, chapter 3 of Hilchos Krias Shema, law 4, implies that it is forbidden to think words of Torah in a new privy. See there. It is true that the cases are not entirely equivalent, because opposite a new privy is permitted; one might therefore say that thought is likewise permitted, as stated. Nevertheless, thought is permitted outside a privy and bathhouse, whereas inside a new bathhouse it is not. From Rashi in Berachos 26a, s.v. bathhouse, who writes that thought is forbidden, it appears that the same applies to a privy containing no excrement: thought is forbidden, comparable to a bathhouse.
To say that the reference to one who prays in Rosh Chodesh concerns prayer through thought is untenable for several reasons, and there is no need to elaborate.
