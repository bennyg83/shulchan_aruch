(1) It is forbidden to recite — meaning before it as far as the eye can see, and behind it four amot; and if there is a bad smell there, one needs four amot from where the smell ends.

(2) They removed it from there — and its prohibition is from the Torah, since this place has already been accustomed to contain feces, it is not a holy encampment; therefore if one is uncertain whether it is an old or new bathroom, we follow the stringent view, as with any doubt regarding feces, above in siman 76, seif 7.

(3) When it has no partitions — such as behind a fence or behind houses. And it is obvious that even if they did not initially designate that place as a bathroom, since it is customary to relieve oneself there it is called an old bathroom. Proof from above, siman 42, where the poskim wrote that if one's custom is always to wrap tefillin in a cloth, it is forbidden to use it for another purpose, as if he had designated it — see there.

(4) Partitions — it appears this deals with partitions that reach the ground, or at least within less than three handbreadths of the ground, for we say lavud; otherwise it is forbidden according to Rav Hai Gaon, cited in Beit Yosef, siman 87 — see there.

(5) It does not reach him — and if it does reach him, it is forbidden until he distances four amot from where the smell ends; and with this it suffices even before it, per the Mechaber. And know that the Mechaber's view is to permit even with partitions not ten handbreadths high, as stated in Beit Yosef; and all the later authorities disagree with him on this. Regarding partitions ten handbreadths high, some later authorities agree with him, but many later authorities disagree even in this, for they hold that since the partitions are designated to receive feces, we judge them like feces and like a cesspool receptacle, below in siman 87, which is forbidden even without a bad smell. And similarly from the Gra's words in his Biur it also appears he is stringent here; and so in Peri Megadim and Derech HaChayyim and many other later authorities of our time — all are stringent, and hold one must distance four amot from the partitions and before them as far as the eye can see, even if the feces was removed and there is no bad smell. Therefore one must be careful not to recite, pray, or speak words of Torah in a courtyard opposite a bathroom as far as the eye can see, even if it is closed and the feces inside cannot be seen. Therefore it is proper to be careful when building a sukkah in a courtyard to arrange matters so bathroom partitions are not aligned opposite the sukkah entrance when it is open, for then one must be careful when speaking words of Torah and other holy matters. And similarly if opposite his house window is a bathroom and the window is open and he stands in his house facing the bathroom, he should be careful not to speak words of Torah then. Nevertheless, in a time of need one may rely to be lenient if no bad smell reaches him, for there is a doubt [namely Beit Yosef and Bach in this siman permit with partitions ten handbreadths high, and the Rosh in siman 79, seif 2 permits even with feces when it is in another domain even though one sees it], and squinting helps for all in such a case. The later authorities further wrote that what we are stringent about regarding bathroom partitions — to judge them from outside like feces — applies only to partitions made solely for a bathroom, such as if one made a special room in the courtyard for this; but if one built a bathroom in a house partition adjoining that partition, even though that partition is literally a bathroom partition, that partition does not have the law of a bathroom for this matter, and it is permitted to recite even adjacent, since the partition is not designated solely for the bathroom.
