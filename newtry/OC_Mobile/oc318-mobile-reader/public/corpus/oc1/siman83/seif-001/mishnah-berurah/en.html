(1) It is forbidden to recite. That is, in front of it as far as one can see and behind it for four amos. If a foul odor is present, one must distance himself four amos from the place where the odor ends.
(2) It was emptied. Its prohibition is from the Torah. Since this place has long been used for excrement, “your camp shall be holy” does not apply to it. Therefore, if one is uncertain whether it is an old or new privy, we rule stringently, like every case of doubtful excrement discussed above in siman 76, seif 7.
(3) When it has no partitions. For example, behind a fence or behind houses. It is clear that even if the place was not originally designated as a privy, since people ordinarily relieve themselves there it is called an old privy. This is proven from siman 42 above, where the poskim write that if one regularly wraps tefillin in a particular cloth, it is forbidden to use that cloth for another purpose, as though he had expressly designated it. See there.
(4) Partitions. It appears that this refers to partitions that reach the ground, or at least come within less than three tefachim of the ground, so that we apply the principle of lavud. Otherwise it is forbidden according to Rav Hai Gaon, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 87; see there.
(5) Does not reach him. If a foul odor reaches him, it is forbidden until he distances himself four amos from the place where the odor ends. According to the Mechaber, that distance suffices even when the privy is in front of him. Know that the Mechaber permits this even when the partitions are not ten tefachim high, as stated in Beit Yosef, but all the later authorities disagree with him in that case. When the partitions are ten tefachim high, some later authorities agree with him, while many others disagree even then. Their reason is that since the partitions are designated to contain excrement, they are judged like excrement itself, like a chamber pot below in siman 87, which is forbidden even when it has no foul odor. Gra's commentary likewise implies that he rules stringently. Peri Megadim, Derech HaChaim, and many other recent authorities are all stringent as well. They hold that one must distance himself four amos from the partitions, and when they are in front of him, as far as he can see, even if the excrement has been removed and there is no foul odor. One must therefore take care not to recite Keriat Shema, pray, or speak words of Torah in a courtyard while facing a privy within his line of sight, even if it is closed and the excrement inside cannot be seen. Therefore, when building a sukkah in a courtyard, it is proper to ensure that the privy's walls are not directly opposite the open entrance of the sukkah, for he would then have to observe this restriction while speaking words of Torah or other sacred words. Likewise, if a privy is opposite a window of one's house, the window is open, and he stands inside facing the privy, it is proper to refrain from speaking words of Torah then. Nevertheless, in pressing circumstances one may rely on the lenient view when no foul odor reaches him, because there is a double doubt: Beit Yosef and Bach in this siman permit partitions ten tefachim high, and Rosh in siman 79, seif 2, permits even excrement when it is in a different domain although one sees it. Closing one's eyes is effective according to all opinions in such a case. The later authorities further write that our stringency of judging privy walls externally like excrement applies specifically to walls made solely for the privy, such as a room in a courtyard built especially for that purpose. If, however, one built a house wall adjacent to that wall, then even though the wall itself is actually a wall of the privy, it does not have the status of a privy wall for this law, because it is not designated solely for the privy. It is therefore permitted to recite even adjacent to it.
