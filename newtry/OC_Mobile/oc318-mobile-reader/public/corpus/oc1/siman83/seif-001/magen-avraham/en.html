He has partitions. Even if they are not ten handbreadths high, so long as the ground of the bathroom is not visible — since it is as if one placed a vessel over the feces [Beit Yosef]. And the Bach ruled that we specifically require partitions ten handbreadths high; therefore one should be stringent in his words, for it appears to me that the plain meaning of the Talmud is that bathroom partitions do not help, for the partitions themselves are like a cesspool receptacle: just as a new bathroom without feces is forbidden, so too the partitions of an old bathroom. And this is clear from the wording of Rashi and the Me'or, who wrote "there are partitions between him and the bathroom" and "he extended his hands inside the partition," and did not write "the bathroom has partitions" — implying that the bathroom's partitions themselves do not help [see above, siman 76, seif 4]. And similarly in the explanation of the Rashba: there must be something intervening between him and the bathroom, for otherwise the prohibition would follow from the bathroom itself, end quote. And similarly Tosafos in Sukkah 36a in several proofs that wherever a bathroom is mentioned, it means within partitions; if so, we learn that even if it has partitions it is forbidden to recite opposite it. They say on 23a: one who enters a bathroom removes tefillin at a distance of four amot — implying even if it has partitions, from "one who enters." However, this may be rejected as the Rabbenu Yosef wrote in siman 43. And it is difficult: if the case is without partitions, where it is not called a bathroom but only the seat area — then in a new one it would be permitted to recite within the partitions opposite the seat area; if so, what does Ravina ask in Nedarim 7a, "this house for a bathroom, and this one too — what is its law?" Behold, the house is not forbidden at all, only the seat area, and ordinary use does not apply here; and he answered that if it has partitions it is forbidden to recite inside even if new. If so, what is the Gemara's question — perhaps R' Chanina deals when it has no partitions and Ravina necessarily deals when it has partitions, and then it is forbidden to recite opposite the seat area within the partitions, whereas outside the partitions even in an old one it is permitted. Furthermore, if partitions help to forbid reciting within them even in a new one, whence do we lighten for an old one to recite opposite them? Therefore it appears to me to forbid even partitions ten handbreadths high. And the proof the Rabbenu Yosef brings from Rava's question — that one must distance as far as the eye can see — on what R' Perem said, "a person stands opposite a bathroom and prays," and what is the question, behold partitions interrupt — necessarily deals when it has no partitions, end quote — is not a proof. For you could challenge: perhaps even though it has no partitions, nevertheless the case is when there is no feces; but rather the questioner holds that the law of a bathroom and feces are equivalent, as he challenges afterward from Rav Yosef. If so, even if it has partitions he holds they have the law of feces; therefore it appears to me there is no basis to be lenient, and one who is lenient should at least see that they are ten handbreadths high as above. And if a bad smell reaches him, it appears to me it is forbidden for all — siman 89, seif 2. And similarly the HaLachot Chadashot, that bathroom partitions do not help for smell.
