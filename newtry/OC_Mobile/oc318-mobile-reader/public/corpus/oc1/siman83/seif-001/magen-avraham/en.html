It has partitions. Even if they are not ten handbreadths high, so long as the ground of the privy is not visible, just as when one covers excrement with a vessel. Beit Yosef. Bach rules that the partitions must specifically be ten handbreadths high. One should be stringent in accordance with his view, for the plain meaning of the Gemara appears to me to be that the partitions of a privy do not help. The partitions themselves are like a chamber pot, for just as a new privy containing no excrement is forbidden, so too the partitions of an old privy are forbidden. This is also implied by Rashi and Baal HaMaor, who write that there are partitions between him and the privy and that he extended his hands inside the partition. They do not write that the privy itself has partitions, which implies that the privy's own partitions do not help. See what I wrote in siman 76, seif 4. Rashba likewise writes that there must be something separating him from the privy, for otherwise it would be forbidden because of the privy itself. Tosafos in Sukkah 36 also proves in several ways that wherever a privy is mentioned, it had partitions. It therefore follows that even when it has partitions one may not recite opposite it. The Gemara on 23 states that one entering a privy removes his tefillin at a distance of four amos, which implies that this applies even where it has partitions, since it says 'one entering.' This proof can, however, be rejected as Beit Yosef writes in siman 43. There is a further difficulty: if the case concerns a place without partitions, so that only the seat itself is called the privy, then in a new privy it should be permitted to recite within the partitions opposite the seat. What, then, was Ravina's question in Nedarim 7a when one said, 'This house shall be a privy, and this one as well'? The house itself would not become forbidden at all, only the seat, and ordinary use does not apply to that. One cannot answer that if it has partitions it is forbidden to recite inside them even when new, for then why did the Gemara object? Perhaps Rabbi Chiya discussed a place without partitions, whereas Ravina necessarily discussed one with partitions, in which case it would be forbidden to recite opposite the seat while inside the partitions, though outside the partitions it would be permitted even for an old privy. Furthermore, if the partitions cause it to be forbidden to recite inside them even when new, from where do we derive that one may be lenient with an old privy and recite opposite them? It therefore appears to me that one should prohibit even where the partitions are ten handbreadths high. Beit Yosef brings a proof from the Gemara's challenge to Rava, who said that one must distance himself as far as he can see, from Rafram's statement that one may stand opposite a privy and pray. He argues that if the privy had partitions, there would be no difficulty, because the partitions intervene, and therefore the case must concern one without partitions. This is no proof, for according to that reasoning one could likewise say that even without partitions the privy contained no excrement. Rather, the questioner evidently held that the law of a privy and that of excrement are the same, as the Gemara subsequently challenges from Rav Yosef. Accordingly, even when it has partitions, he held that it has the status of excrement. It therefore appears to me that one should not be lenient. One who is lenient should at least ensure that the partitions are ten handbreadths high, as stated above. If a foul odor reaches him, it appears to me that it is forbidden according to all opinions; see siman 79, seif 2. Lechem Chamudos likewise writes that the partitions of a privy do not help with respect to odor.
