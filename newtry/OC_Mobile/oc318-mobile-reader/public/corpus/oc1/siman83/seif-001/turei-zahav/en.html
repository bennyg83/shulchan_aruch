And it seems to me, etc. — Beit Yosef likewise on what we say in the Gemara in the chapter "One whose dead relative lies before him": Rava said Rav Huna said this was stated only that one distances four amot from feces behind it, but before it one distances as far as the eye can see — I do not accept this; and R' Chanina: a person stands opposite a bathroom at a distance of four amot and prays. The Gemara asks: regarding a bathroom that has no feces? And they conclude it is forbidden even if there is no feces, and R' Chanina deals with a new bathroom. Beit Yosef wrote: it appears to me that forbidding Kerias Shema opposite a bathroom applies specifically to a bathroom without partitions, like the bathrooms in the time of the Talmud sages; but if it has partitions like our bathrooms, even though there is feces in it one may recite opposite it adjacent and need not be concerned, since the partition interrupts and it is a separate domain. Proof: Rava challenges from R' Chanina — and if a bathroom with partitions, what is the question? Behold, there it is a bathroom with partitions; rather certainly their ordinary bathroom was without partitions. If so, when R' Perem said a person stands opposite a bathroom, there must have been feces before him and he says one recites, etc. And it is possible that even with partitions not ten high, so long as the bathroom ground is not visible, it is permitted to recite opposite it, similar to placing a vessel over feces. And although one could say a bathroom is stricter — even without feces it is forbidden to recite opposite it — nevertheless, since partitions prevent seeing the bathroom ground, the inclination is to permit reciting opposite it, end quote. And from this his words here in the Shulchan Aruch are understood. And the Magen Avraham wrote on the leniency in Beit Yosef regarding partitions less than ten high that it is not clear, for necessarily even in the time of the Talmud sages there were partitions, since we teach about them in many places: "I entered after him into a bathroom" — and "I entered" applies only where there are partitions, but they were less than ten handbreadths high, therefore it has the law of feces, end quote. And I say those rabbis overlooked this: Tosafos in the chapter "The stolen lulav" (Sukkah 36) on what was taught in Shabbos: one may bring into a bathroom three stones of korzela — Rashi explained: a bathroom in a field, not surrounded by partitions, and here there is only rabbinic carrying, and because of human dignity they did not decree; and Tosafos wrote on this: from "to bring in" it implies there are partitions, and the term "bathroom" implies a house. And in chapter 23 it says women may spin thread in a bathroom because of seclusion — implying it was a modest place surrounded by partitions and those standing outside could not see; nevertheless they permitted bringing in, even though one brings from a karmelis to a private domain, etc. — behold before you that their bathrooms were surrounded by partitions, for Tosafos call them a private domain; and see in siman 3. If so, Beit Yosef's view permitting even below ten is hidden, and also Magen Avraham who permitted at least with a partition ten high — whereas here it seems even ten high and more. I wonder at Beit Yosef's words permitting whenever feces is not visible because partitions cover it — did we not say "a bathroom that they designated" even though there is no feces? And is it better that there is feces not visible because of partitions than no feces at all, which needs no covering? And it is difficult to me that afterward the Gemara says Rava: these Persian bathrooms are permitted, etc., as the Tur wrote here — necessarily Persian bathrooms had partitions, for the Tur brings this from our bathroom; and nevertheless the only leniency is because they are in a pit — implying but if not in a pit partitions do not help. Therefore it appears clear to me that a bathroom has actual feces law for this matter, since its partitions are designated to receive feces — it is like feces even in a partition ten high; and similar to a perforated bench below and a cesspool receptacle Beit Yosef wrote in siman 87 in the name of the Rosh: since it is designated for this, it is like a bathroom even if there is no bad smell there. And it appears the stringency of a bathroom here applies only to partitions made solely for a bathroom, such as making a special room in one's courtyard; but if one built a bathroom in a house partition adjoining that partition, even though that partition is literally a bathroom partition, that partition does not have bathroom law for this matter, and it is permitted to recite even adjacent, since the partition is not designated solely for the bathroom. All this appears clear; one may be lenient on this matter that he wrote here regarding partitions that is not precise; therefore wherever one has a special bathroom room in his courtyard, one may not recite Kerias Shema or pray in the courtyard whenever the bathroom is before him as far as the eye can see, like the law of feces — namely a bathroom without a pit but feces placed on the ground like those in the days of the Talmud sages in the field, as above — clear.
