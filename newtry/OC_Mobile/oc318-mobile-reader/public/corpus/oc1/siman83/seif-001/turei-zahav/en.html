It appears to me, etc. Beit Yosef writes this regarding the Gemara at the end of the chapter Mi Shemeiso: Rava said in the name of Rav Huna that the requirement to distance oneself four amos from excrement applies only when it is behind him, but in front of him he must distance himself as far as he can see. The Gemara challenges: Did Rabbi Chiya not say that a person may stand opposite a privy at a distance of four amos and pray? It answers that the case concerns a privy containing no excrement, and concludes that even when it contains no excrement it is forbidden; Rabbi Chiya referred to a new privy. Beit Yosef writes that it appears to him that the prohibition against reciting Krias Shema opposite a privy applies specifically to a privy without partitions, like the privies in the era of the Sages of the Gemara. If, however, it has partitions, like our privies, then even though it contains excrement one may recite adjacent to it without concern, because the partition intervenes and makes it a separate domain. His proof is that the Gemara challenges Rava from Rabbi Chiya. If Rabbi Chiya referred to a privy with partitions, what would be the difficulty? Rather, their ordinary privies must have lacked partitions. Thus, when Rabbi Chiya said that one may stand opposite a privy, the excrement was before him, and yet he said one may recite. It is possible that even partitions lower than ten handbreadths suffice, so long as the ground of the privy cannot be seen, because this resembles covering excrement with a vessel. Although one could argue that a privy is more stringent, since one may not recite opposite it even when it contains no excrement, nevertheless, because partitions prevent one from seeing the ground of the privy, reason inclines toward permitting recitation opposite it. These are Beit Yosef's words, and they explain his ruling here in the Shulchan Aruch. My father-in-law, Bach, writes concerning Beit Yosef's leniency regarding partitions lower than ten handbreadths that it is not correct, for the privies in the era of the Sages of the Gemara necessarily had partitions, as many passages speak of entering them, and entering applies only where there are partitions. Their partitions were merely lower than ten handbreadths, and therefore the privy had the status of excrement. These are his words. I say that both of these authorities overlooked Tosafos in the chapter Lulav HaGazul, Sukkah 36, concerning the teaching that on Shabbos one may bring three rounded stones into a privy. Rashi explains that the privy was in a field and had no partitions, so the only issue was Rabbinic carrying, which the Sages waived for human dignity. Tosafos write that the expression 'bring into' implies that there were partitions, and the term 'privy house' itself implies a house. In the chapter Kol Kisvei it is stated that women should converse in the privy because of the prohibition of seclusion, which implies that it was a private place enclosed by partitions, so that those standing outside could not see inside. Even so, the Sages permitted carrying the stones inside, although one was carrying from a karmelis into a private domain. Thus, the privies of their time were enclosed by partitions, and Tosafos explicitly call them private domains. See what I wrote in siman 3. Beit Yosef's view, permitting even partitions lower than ten handbreadths, is therefore refuted, as is Bach's view that at least partitions ten handbreadths high permit it, for here it appears that even partitions ten handbreadths high do not help. I am even more astonished by Beit Yosef's statement that whenever the excrement is not visible because the partitions cover it, it is permitted. The Gemara says that a privy is forbidden even when it contains no excrement. Is a privy containing excrement that is merely hidden by partitions better than one containing no excrement at all, which requires no covering? There is a further difficulty from the Gemara's subsequent statement that Persian privies are permitted, as Tur cites here. Persian privies necessarily had partitions, since Tur compares our privies to them, and yet their permissibility depended specifically on the excrement falling into a pit. This implies that where there is no pit, partitions do not help. It therefore appears clear to me that a privy has exactly the status of excrement for this purpose. Since its partitions are designated to receive excrement, they themselves are regarded as excrement, even when ten handbreadths high. This resembles the perforated seat discussed below and the chamber pot concerning which Beit Yosef cites Rosh in siman 87: because it is designated for this purpose, it has the status of a privy even when it contains no foul odor. It appears that this stringency regarding the partitions of a privy applies specifically when they were made solely for the privy, such as when one constructs a special room for this purpose in his courtyard. If, however, one builds a privy against an existing wall of a house, then although that wall actually serves as a wall of the privy, it does not have the status of a privy wall for this purpose, because it was not designated exclusively for the privy. One may therefore recite even adjacent to it. All this appears clear to me. One should not rely on the leniency stated here based on the partitions, for it is imprecise. Therefore, whenever there is a privy in one's courtyard in a room designated for it, one may not recite Krias Shema or pray in the courtyard while the privy is before him within his line of sight, as is the rule for excrement. This refers to a privy without a pit, where the excrement rests on the ground, like those in the era of the Sages of the Gemara in the fields. This appears clear.
