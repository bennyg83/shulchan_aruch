(18) An unnecessary blessing. For example, one recites a blessing during a meal over foods already covered by HaMotzi. That blessing is entirely unnecessary. The poskim further write that even when the blessing would not presently be in vain, it may still be included in the category of an unnecessary blessing. For example, if the table is set before him and he intends to wash his hands and eat, but before washing he takes foods that he plans to eat during the meal and recites blessings over them, this too is prohibited because he causes unnecessary blessings. All the more so, one who interrupts between a blessing and the act, thereby causing the first blessing to become entirely ineffective, certainly acts improperly. See above, siman 46, Mishnah Berurah seif katan 14, and Magen Avraham here regarding completing one hundred blessings on Shabbos.
(19) In vain. Meaning, even though he recites the standard formula of a blessing as praise and thanksgiving, since the blessing is unnecessary it is prohibited. All the more so, Heaven forbid, if he mentions Hashem's name for no purpose. This applies not only to the four-letter Name but also to the other Divine names. Likewise, if he utters a Divine name in another language for no purpose, meaning not as praise or thanksgiving, there is also a prohibition. Acharonim. Eliyah Rabbah writes in the name of Sefer Chassidim: If you hear another person mentioning Hashem's name, do not interrupt him by saying, “Do such-and-such for me,” because he will stop to listen to you and you will have caused him to utter Hashem's name in vain. If, however, you hear him mention Hashem's name while cursing another person, interrupt him, for he will sin by completing the curse.
(20) Like one who swears falsely. This is Rambam's language, based on the Gemara's statement that whoever recites an unnecessary blessing violates “You shall not bear the Name,” etc. Nevertheless, several Rishonim hold that the principal prohibition is D'Rabbanan, because he mentions the Name in a blessing as praise and thanksgiving, and the verse is merely an allusion. But if one mentions Hashem's name for no purpose outside a blessing, all agree that he violates a Torah prohibition, namely the positive commandment, “You shall fear Hashem your God,” which warns against uttering Hashem's name in vain, as stated in Temurah 4a. Reverence requires that His great Name be mentioned only in obligatory praise and thanksgiving, not without purpose. In any event, both according to Rambam and according to the other poskim, if one is uncertain whether he recited a particular blessing, he repeats it when the obligation is D'Oraisa, but does not repeat it when the obligation is D'Rabbanan. Several Acharonim imply that repeating it in the latter case is itself prohibited. The Acharonim further write that even a double doubt requiring a blessing does not permit reciting it. For example, he is uncertain whether he ate a kezayis, and even if he did not, perhaps the halachah follows the view that one recites an after-blessing over a whole entity even when it is smaller than a kezayis. Nevertheless, the doubt is resolved leniently and he does not recite the blessing.
(21) And it is forbidden to answer after him. He is no better than a child who mentions Hashem's name while learning. Although the child commits no prohibition by pronouncing it, one still does not answer amen, because the words do not have the status of a blessing. All the more so here, where the person committed a prohibition by uttering Hashem's name in vain. Gra.
