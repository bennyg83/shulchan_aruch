If. See Taz. The general intent of his words is that in Megillah 19b it concludes that the mishnah's deaf person is not according to Rabbi Yose, and we hold like Rabbi Yehudah, that if he did not make it heard to his own ears he has fulfilled. Why, then, did it rule here that he has not fulfilled? Beis Yosef answered that megillah is for publicizing, etc. But in the Gemara it implies that Shema and megillah are equal. The Taz answered that certainly one can distinguish between one who did not make it heard to his own ears, where since it is fit for mixing, the absence of mixing does not prevent fulfillment at least after the fact, and a deaf person, who is not fit for hearing. But regarding the initial law, when it says that Rabbi Yehudah even initially says that it is acceptable without making it heard, it is certainly impossible to say that this is different because it is not fit for hearing; for if so, why initially is it permitted with one who hears but did not make it heard, because the absence of mixing does not prevent after the fact? Also, regarding what it says, perhaps Rabbi Yehudah forbids initially, and it did not say that a deaf person is invalid even after the fact because he is not fit for hearing: even so, since it did not know the verse of Shema according to Rabbi Yehudah and thought that Shema means in any language that you hear, and it was only on the basis of reasoning that it was stringent initially, if so a deaf person is valid after the fact. This is unlike the conclusion, that Rabbi Yehudah follows Rabbi Elazar ben Azaryah, who derives from Shema that initially one must make it heard to his own ears. Since the verse is written plainly, it implies even after the fact, and this refers to a deaf person. But one who hears is fit to make it heard, and with him it does not prevent fulfillment. The mishnah is Rabbi Yehudah, and a deaf person is invalid even after the fact, unlike Rashi's explanation that the mishnah is speaking initially, etc. In Divrei Chamudos there is an error in the word "we derive" in the conclusion; it should read that Rashi explained in the conclusion that it is initially, while the Rambam, of blessed memory, holds that a deaf person is invalid even after the fact because he is not fit for hearing.
According to this. A new law emerges for us regarding Grace after Meals and the like, where one person blesses and others hear and fulfill: a deaf person who speaks but does not hear cannot discharge others with his blessing. In the Gemara in Berachos it implies that other mitzvos are learned from Shema, and Rav Yosef alone holds that the disagreement is regarding Shema, but in other mitzvos he too fulfills. According to Rav Yosef, the mishnah of Megillah is either divided by cases, that a deaf person is invalid initially but not after the fact, while a minor is invalid even after the fact; or a deaf person is different because he is not fit for hearing, as the Taz wrote. See siman 53 and 95 regarding a prayer leader, and this requires analysis. See Rambam, chapter 4, halachah 23: everyone obligated in megillah is obligated in Chanukah. But there is a deaf person who does not hear, who is exempt in megillah and obligated in Chanukah. At first glance this is difficult. According to what the Taz wrote, that in the conclusion the mishnah follows Rabbi Yehudah, why did the Rambam rule that a minor does not discharge an adult, like the first Tanna of Rabbi Yehudah? In the conclusion it is entirely Rabbi Yehudah, and there are two types of minor: the first clause is where he has not reached the age of education, but where he has reached the age of education no one disagrees that he discharges an adult. Rather, perforce he rules like what the Gemara said at first, that the mishnah is Rabbi Yose, and Rabbi Yehudah disagrees regarding a minor. The reason he does not disagree regarding a deaf person can be that a deaf person is invalid initially. See Lechem Mishneh, chapter 1 of Megillah, and Chidushei HaRashba, Berachos 15; see there. If so, it is difficult at first glance. One can say that Shabbos 23a says that if a deaf person, imbecile, or minor lit, it does not help, while a woman certainly lights, meaning to discharge others from their obligation. Since it says simply that a minor does not, this implies even if he reached the age of education. Therefore the Rambam ruled in megillah too like the plain statement of the Talmud. The Mechaber in 675 appears from his words to rule like the Baal HaIttur, that one who reached the age of education discharges; see Magen Avraham, os 4. One can say that he follows his own view, as written in Beis Yosef here, where he distinguishes that a deaf person in megillah is because of publicizing the miracle, and the first clause of the mishnah is not like Rabbi Yehudah; and he rules like the first Tanna, as stated.
Afterward. I saw that the Pri Chadash wrote that in the baraisa the Sages disagree with Rabbi Yehudah, and he ruled like them, and in Arachin, page 31, "all" comes to include a minor; end quote. What he wrote about the baraisa is Megillah 20a: it is taught that Rabbi Yehudah says, the Sages said to him, etc. And from Arachin, see Tosafos under "he ruled." This is correct, and with Hashem's help it will be explained further in Magen Avraham.
Incidentally. By the way, I saw fit to mention what Eliyah Rabbah wrote in siman 62 in the name of Maharam Alashkar, whether one person can discharge his fellow in Shema, for speech is required. This is difficult from megillah according to Baal Halachos Gedolos, who holds that women do not discharge men because these are obligated in hearing and those in reading, and nevertheless all are fit to read the megillah and one discharges many. According to Avudraham there, it is difficult. But according to what he concludes there, that a prayer leader discharges with ten, it is settled. There it is said that answering Amen is like saying it, and I do not know what answering Amen has to do with Shema. It is forced to say that Amen on the blessings before and after it is like saying Shema. The same applies to megillah, where they bless before and after it. See Magen Avraham, os 10, regarding what the Mechaber wrote, that where there are not ten, each reads only for himself. The reason is that wherever there are not ten, one does not discharge his fellow, for reading is required. He rules like Baal Halachos Gedolos, that women do not discharge men, because women are specifically obligated in reading, and the proof is that in section 2 he did not write in the first opinion the opposite of what is in the second opinion, as written in Magen Avraham and in the rules of issur and heter in Pri Megadim to Yoreh Deah; this requires analysis. See Pri Chadash, who wrote: it has been clarified that if he read and did not make it heard to his own ears, he has fulfilled. I do not know what he teaches us. Afterward I saw that one might have thought as Beis Yosef wrote, to distinguish that megillah is for publicizing the miracle, while according to the Taz a deaf person is different. According to this, this law requires analysis. One can further say that if we say regarding megillah that hearing is primary, and it is not comparable to Shema, where "you shall speak of them" makes speech primary, and for this reason there one person does not discharge his fellow, then for this reason Rabbi Yehudah holds there that if he did not make it heard to his own ears he has fulfilled; but with megillah, hearing is primary. See what I wrote above in the name of Eliyah Rabbah, section 2, and this requires analysis. In the name of my father and teacher, of blessed memory, I found: a deaf woman does not discharge other women according to Baal Halachos Gedolos and those who follow him, for she is called one who is not obligated in the matter, since the essence is hearing, like a deaf person with shofar.
