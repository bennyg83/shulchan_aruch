In Magen Avraham, seif katan 9. “One might ask, does wine not provide nourishment?” Meaning, if so, why did the Rosh write that it comes to stimulate the appetite? Moreover, the reason that it provides nourishment should suffice, since by virtue of its connection to eating it is considered part of the meal, as written below in the name of the Rashba. Magen Avraham answers, “See below, siman 471.” Meaning, there it is explained that a large quantity stimulates the appetite, while a small quantity provides nourishment. The Rosh there discusses the first two cups before the meal, which are considered a large quantity, as Magen Avraham writes in siman 471. He therefore needed to explain that, since they stimulate the appetite, they are connected to the meal. With a small quantity, this reason is unnecessary because it provides nourishment. Magen Avraham then writes that wine drunk after the meal is also exempt from an after-blessing because it provides nourishment, and so forth. After the meal, even a large quantity cannot be described as stimulating the appetite. Since it provides nourishment, we say that by virtue of its connection to eating it is considered established with the meal. Some emend the text to read, “And furthermore, it provides nourishment.” This does not appear correct to me. It would be more plausible to emend it by deleting the words “one might ask, does it not,” as a scribal error, and to read simply, “A small quantity provides nourishment.” Such an error could readily arise from Ashkenazic handwriting, as is known. The meaning would then be as I explained: the Rosh gives the reason of stimulating the appetite for a large quantity, while for a small quantity that reason is unnecessary because it provides nourishment. Wine drunk after the meal is likewise exempt for this reason.
