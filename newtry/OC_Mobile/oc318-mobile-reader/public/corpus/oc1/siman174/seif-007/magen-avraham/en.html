Because it is not customary, etc. This implies that spirits, which are not ordinarily drunk constantly, are not considered to come because of the meal and require a blessing. Even one who regularly drinks them has an idiosyncratic practice that is disregarded. Mateh Moshe; Lechem Chamudos; Maharshal. Even if one drinks them before the meal to stimulate the appetite, they are no better than fruit, which requires a blessing. This is certainly so for those who drink them during the meal or afterward merely to warm the stomach and aid digestion, for this is not called coming because of the meal but is a separate matter. Levush, siman 177. The Tur at the end of siman 272, however, writes that something that stimulates the appetite is considered to come because of the meal, as explained above in seif 6. Tosafos in Pesachim 116a likewise write that appetizers are foods that stimulate eating, such as small fish, dates, and vegetables, which people would eat between courses. Bread therefore exempts them, since they come for the needs of the meal and cause one to eat more. Bach, siman 176, and Rashi to the Gemara write similarly. See siman 168, seif 8. Mordechai likewise writes that the Gemara’s suggestion that bread should exempt wine is because wine’s strength causes a person to desire food. The statement of Mordechai and Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 4, in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah, that foods that do not come because of the meal include fruits that come not to satiate but to digest the food, must therefore mean that there is a distinction. When something comes to stimulate the appetite, it is needed for the meal because it causes one to eat more, and it does not require a blessing. When it comes to aid digestion, however, it serves no need of the meal at that time and requires a blessing. Sefer HaChinuch of the Raah, however, states that if one eats a salted olive or the like during the meal to stimulate his appetite, he recites a blessing before it but not after it. According to the Raah, the same would apply to one who eats salted lemons during the meal. Nevertheless, it appears to me that one should follow Tosafos, as Mordechai writes in the chapter Naarah that the halachah always follows Tosafos. See also Maharam Padua, siman 25. In any event, it appears to me that one must recite a blessing over spirits. If one drinks them before eating, most people do not intend to stimulate their appetite, since they regularly drink them every morning even without a meal, and therefore a blessing is required. If one drinks them during the meal, his intent is to stimulate the appetite and no blessing is required. If one drinks them after the meal, his intent is to aid digestion and a blessing is required. On Shabbos, however, none of this is necessary. If he recited Kiddush over wine, the spirits are exempted by it, as explained in seif 2. If he recited Kiddush over beer, they are exempted by the blessing Shehakol, as explained in seif 4. See siman 177, seif 4; siman 206, seif 5; and siman 176.
Because of the bread. This applies specifically when one eats a fixed meal. If he ate only a small amount of bread, see siman 212.
Each time. It appears to me that this applies specifically to water, which a person drinks only when thirsty, and therefore each time is considered a new decision. Beer and mead, however, plainly are not considered a new decision. Beit Yosef’s citation from the Agur that even for other beverages one must recite a blessing each time refers to beverages like licorice drinks and similar drinks that are consumed only when thirsty.
Before washing. This is because he holds that after washing it would constitute an interruption. See what I wrote in seif 4, that he was not concerned for those who hold that drinking before washing does not exempt. Nevertheless, it requires study whether one who drank a reviis must recite a concluding blessing. Seemingly, according to the opinion that no blessing is required over beverages during the meal, a concluding blessing should be required before the meal, in accordance with the opinion regarding Havdalah wine explained in siman 299, seif 7. Only wine before the meal, which comes to stimulate the appetite, does not require a concluding blessing, as explained in seif 6, but other beverages do require one. So it appears from the Tur, siman 473. See what I wrote in siman 177, seif 3. According to Baal HaMaor there, no concluding blessing is required if one will also drink beverages during the meal, because Bircas HaMazon exempts everything. If he will not have beverages during the meal, he must recite a concluding blessing over what he drank before the meal. See what I wrote in siman 299, seif 8. If he has a cup of wine for Bircas HaMazon, he need not recite a concluding blessing over the beverages drunk before the meal, because he will recite a concluding blessing over the cup of Bircas HaMazon, and there is no intervening act that establishes a separate concluding blessing. So write Ran and Ramban in Milchamos. See simanim 178 and 474. In any event, one who fears Heaven should drink less than a reviis before the meal and thereby avoid the uncertainty. So is written in the intentions of the Arizal. The statement there that if he drinks a reviis his blessing is in vain appears to refer to a case where he recited a concluding blessing. If he drank a reviis, as is common at large meals where people drink before eating, he should follow the ruling cited above from Ran and Ramban. See siman 179, that one must be careful not to leave before the meal, for leaving constitutes an interruption.
And he should recite the blessing with intent to drink. In Birchos Maharam Mintz it is written that he should give the beverage to another person who is not eating, have that person recite the blessing, and intend to exempt him.
