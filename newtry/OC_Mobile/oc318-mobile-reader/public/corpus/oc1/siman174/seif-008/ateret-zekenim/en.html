He should say, “With your permission, my masters,” and should not say “birshus.” Some write that after he says “savri” over wine, he should also say “birshus.” With due respect, the author did not examine the matter carefully and overlooked what Beit Yosef wrote in siman 166 in the name of Shibolei HaLeket, citing Rav Hai Gaon. It is clear there that even during the meal one should not say “birshus,” and so forth. Likewise, what he wrote, that for Kiddush and Bircas HaMazon one need not say “savri” at all but only “birshus,” is explicitly contradicted there, where it states that one need not say “birshus” but only “savri.” This is the opposite of the ruling issued by that authority. As to his question, why should the one reciting the blessing presume to lead without permission, Rav Hai Gaon wrote there in a responsum that in his time those reclining at the meal would choose the most distinguished among them and authorize him to recite the blessing. See there. For this reason the present custom likewise developed that the one reciting Bircas HaMazon asks the participants, in the vernacular, “My masters, shall we proceed?” and then receives permission to recite Bircas HaMazon.
Likewise, wherever a blessing is recited over wine, one does not say “birshus.” Some say that “savri” is said only during a meal, but for Kiddush or Havdalah one should not say “savri,” only “birshus.” Or Zarua; Levush rules likewise.
