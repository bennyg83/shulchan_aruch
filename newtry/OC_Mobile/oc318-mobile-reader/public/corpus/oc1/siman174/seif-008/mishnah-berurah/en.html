(40) Even if they reclined together. Meaning, unlike the general rule in siman 167, seif 11, that when people recline together one may discharge them all, which is preferable because the King is glorified among a multitude. For a blessing recited during the meal, they should not do so.
(41) To answer amen. Although the established rule is that hearing is equivalent to answering, and answering amen is not indispensable for fulfilling the blessing, we are concerned that they may answer amen and place themselves in danger. Rosh and Tur.
(42) Because we are concerned, etc. Meaning, they are then occupied with eating and their throats are not clear. The poskim explain that according to this opinion, even if they stop eating and prepare to hear the blessing and answer amen, it still does not help. We remain concerned that they may fail to stop eating carefully and come to danger. Perishah.
(43) Some say, etc. They hold that one person cannot discharge another with the blessing over wine during the meal because the diners are occupied with eating and will not concentrate on hearing the blessing and fulfilling their obligation. Therefore, if the one reciting the blessing first says, “Savri, my masters,” they all stop eating and direct their attention to the blessing, and this is effective. They also hold that there is no danger, since they stopped eating.
(44) One blesses for all. They must all taste from the cup immediately and may not interrupt between the blessing and drinking. Magen Avraham.
(45) He should say, “Savri, my masters.” Meaning, when one person recites the blessing during the meal and discharges all the others, he should not say, “Birshus, my masters,” for that phrase merely requests permission to bless. Rather, he should say, “Savri, my masters,” meaning that they should stop eating and direct their attention to hear the blessing and fulfill their obligation, as explained above.
(46) Wherever, etc. Meaning, whenever one person discharges another, such as Kiddush, Havdalah, and similar cases. At a wedding or bris milah, where the custom is not to say Savri at all, the reason is that Savri is said in place of Birshus only where permission must be requested. At a wedding or bris milah, where no permission is requested, Savri should not be said.
(47) We do not say Birshus. Although it would seemingly be more appropriate to say Birshus, which requests the diners’ permission that the speaker discharge them with his blessing, because during the meal one must say Savri so that they stop eating, the custom developed to use this expression wherever one blesses in order to discharge others.
