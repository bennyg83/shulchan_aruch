Seif 4: Some say mitzvot do not require kavanah, and some say mitzvot require kavanah, etc.—NB: behold, even according to this view itself there is a dispute whether one who intends explicitly not to fulfill—whether in that case too he fulfills or not. And it now seems to me there is clear proof that if one need not have kavanah, even one who intends explicitly not to fulfill fulfills; and the practical difference is for a rabbinic mitzvah, for all agree one need not have kavanah—even one who intends explicitly not to fulfill fulfills. And the proof is from tractate Menachot [7b]: there Rabbi Yochanan said, etc., "sacred vessels sanctify without awareness," etc.—and the difficulty is: what of it that they sanctify without awareness? Here it is possible that he intends explicitly not to sanctify so that afterward he can gather; and perforce it is proven that if we do not require awareness, even if he stipulated explicitly it also does not help, and perforce it sanctifies; so too if mitzvot require kavanah, even if he intends or says explicitly not to fulfill, he fulfills—and this is clear proof in my humble opinion, with God's help, and examine well. Behold, what was difficult for the view of those who hold that if he intended to fulfill, all agree he does not fulfill—from tractate Arakhin and tractate Sukkah—see what I wrote to resolve this in my responsa Toldot Yaakov siman 112, Laws of Mezuzah, in responsa to the community of Vishnitz, end quote, and examine well. See in Magen Avraham s.k. 3 in the name of Radbaz that for a rabbinic mitzvah all agree one need not have kavanah—and I wondered at what Pri Megadim wrote: the Ran in Rosh Hashanah regarding one who hears from a casual actor does not fulfill—for even per the one who says one need not have kavanah, if he intended for a different mitzvah it is worse, for a species destroys it—see there. And one should examine in rabbinic mitzvot what the law is if in this case too, in rabbinic law, kavanah is required since he intended for a different mitzvah, or in rabbinic law even in such a case one need not have kavanah. And it now seems prima facie to bring proof for this from tractate Yoma [88a]: there Rav said, "the prayer of Ne'ilah exempts that of Ma'ariv," and he challenged from the beraita: on Motza'ei Yom Kippur one prays the order of seven [blessings], etc.—see there. And the difficulty is: why does he challenge—let us say Rav deals with when he comes to consult first, then we instruct him to intend in the prayer of Ne'ilah to exempt even Ma'ariv, and then he is truly exempt; but the beraita deals with when he did not intend in Ne'ilah to exempt Ma'ariv—in that case it is not worse than if he prayed Ma'ariv and did not intend to fulfill thereby, for per the one who holds it is an obligation he does not fulfill—all the more so in Ne'ilah he is not exempt if he did not intend for the sake of Ma'ariv. And one could say the challenge is according to his view that he holds in Chullin regarding a niddah who was compelled and immersed that she is permitted, and he holds that mitzvot do not require kavanah—what of it, for here since he also prayed Ne'ilah, certainly he requires kavanah, as the Ran wrote above, and perforce the reason is: since prayer is rabbinic, in rabbinic law even if it is for a different mitzvah one need not have kavanah, and examine well. And one should not challenge that to resolve "one who intends explicitly not to fulfill" according to this explanation it is not so—for behold the beraita teaches us the law lechat'chila one does not do, and it said: in the morning one prays seven, etc.; in Ne'ilah one prays seven and confesses; in Ma'ariv one prays the order of seven—if so, if we require specifically that he intend not to fulfill Ma'ariv, it should have mentioned this too: in Ne'ilah one prays seven and intends not to fulfill Ma'ariv—and perforce since it did not give him this advice, perforce it deals with when he did not intend explicitly not to fulfill; but if even stam he does not fulfill, it is a fine difficulty, for since it did not advise him to intend to fulfill Ma'ariv, perforce it deals with stam, therefore he did not fulfill Ma'ariv; and Rav deals with when he wishes to intend to exempt also Ma'ariv, then Ne'ilah exempts—and perforce for a rabbinic mitzvah, even if he intended for another purpose, one need not have kavanah; examine well. And see in my composition on Orach Chayim Mishnat Hasidim siman 8 in responsa to the community of Podehayev what he wrote on this law, end quote, and examine well. Behold the view of Rabbeinu Yona: even per the one who says mitzvot do not require kavanah, nevertheless speech requires kavanah certainly—and perforce speech is not like action. And in my humble opinion one can prove from Rashi in tractate Menachot [2a]: "but thought that is evident to all," etc., and he said: this thought regarding kodashim is verbal expression with the mouth, etc.—what did Rashi need with this, and it is not Rashi's way to be a posek but an explainer, and this matter is not relevant here specifically to sacrifices—he should have explained so. But it appears Rashi's intent is to resolve what was difficult for the rabbis: why do the rabbis disqualify—behold we hold that action removes from the domain of action and from the domain of thought; so too here, his actions prove about him, for he is for this minchah—why should it be disqualified because of his thought that he thinks the opposite; therefore Rashi explains that this thought is speech, and speech is literally like action, as we say "movement of the lips is action"; so this act of speech removes the act that proves the opposite. And one could say the reverse: the act that proves removes the act of speech—but in any case, since both are equal, what is the superiority of this over that—one could say the reverse too, that the act of speech removes immediately the act that proves; and since one can say thus and one can say thus, we go stringently again. And further: if they are equal, this cancels against that and thought in the heart remains and disqualifies as well; or even if stam remains it is invalid—for in general stam is valid in a sacrifice, and thought in the heart does not disqualify because there is at least an act of offering, and it is not an act that proves action; at least stam is for its sake and valid, and thought in the heart does not disqualify here. But here, since there is speech not for its sake, which is also like action, therefore the act cancels against the act and it is as if there was no act at all; therefore stam also disqualifies, and all the more thought in the heart disqualifies—such is Rashi's intent in my humble opinion. And from this it will be proven that Rashi holds speech is literally like action, not like Rabbeinu Yona; examine well. Behold it is explicit in the poskim that even for those who hold one need not have kavanah, nevertheless one who intends explicitly not to fulfill does not fulfill; and one should be in doubt whether it suffices in the heart—if in his heart he intended not to fulfill, does he also not fulfill, or not: only things of the heart are not things, and only if he says he intends not to fulfill does he not fulfill. And here is a simple resolution from what we learned in the Yerushalmi tractate Shabbat chapter Rabbi Eliezer on milah: "Israel circumcises the Cuthean and the Cuthean does not circumcise Israel, because he intends for Mount Gerizim"—the words of Rabbi Yudan; Rabbi Yosi said: and where do we find that milah requires kavanah—let him circumcise and go until his soul departs, end quote. And behold "let him circumcise and go until his soul departs" has no understanding, and Ketzot HaChoshen's explanation is very strained—what is this curse to me or another; but it appears the intent is: the view of Tosafot that a gentile stam acts, and what is stam is for its sake and valid for a gentile, and what is not stam not for its sake is invalid for a gentile. Accordingly this is Rabbi Yosi's intent: Rabbi Yudan holds that even stam is for its sake—we require kavanah for the sake of the mitzvah, and therefore he disqualifies a gentile lest they intend for Mount Gerizim; and even though it is only things of the heart, nevertheless it is not harmful but not effective, and it is like stam, and stam is also invalid. But Rabbi Yosi: what is stam for its sake is valid without kavanah; only when he intends not for its sake is it invalid—and behold here too things of the heart are not harmful, only if he says explicitly so; but in the heart, things of the heart are not things. And therefore Rabbi Yosi said: where do we find milah for its sake—for stam is for its sake, and this is that one should not hang this on his pleasure; is it that he will circumcise and go until the soul of the one circumcised departs—certainly he does not intend that his soul depart, and therefore he intends only to fix the person, and therefore it is stam for its sake, and therefore we do not require kavanah; again, even if he intends not for its sake, things of the heart are not things. And it is proven from this that where one need not have kavanah, even if he intends not for its sake it does not help to accept it—only if he says explicitly so, and not in the heart, as I wrote now; perhaps God will grant me in another time to explain more; examine very well.
