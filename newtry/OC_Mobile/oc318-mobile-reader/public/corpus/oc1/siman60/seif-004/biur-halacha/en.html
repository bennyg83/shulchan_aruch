Some say that mitzvot do not require intent. This applies whether he performs the act himself and does not intend to fulfill his obligation through it, or whether he hears from another, such as Megillah or shofar, and did not intend by hearing to fulfill the mitzvah, as in Beit Yosef simanim 213 and 219. The same applies to all mitzvot dependent on speech, such as Shema and Birkat Hamazon. Some say that in a matter that consists only of speech, everyone agrees that intent is required; see Beit Yosef siman 589. This is only where he intends the action he is doing but does not intend to fulfill the mitzvah through it, such as one who blows shofar as song or for practice, and similarly with other mitzvot. But if he is merely occupied with something else and the mitzvah happens to come to hand, such as one who blows into a shofar and a proper blast emerges, and similarly with other mitzvot, according to all opinions he has not fulfilled. This is in Ramban, Milchamot, Rosh Hashanah, and Shitah Mekubetzet, Berachot 13a; something similar is evident from Talmidei Rabbeinu Yonah regarding one who reads to proofread. Know further that this applies only if he knows that he is still obligated in the mitzvah he is performing. But if he thinks he is exempt from it, such as one who took a lulav on the first day of Sukkot while thinking it was the eve of Sukkot, or thought that this lulav was invalid, according to all opinions he has not fulfilled. A proof is from siman 475 seif 4, where if he thought it was a weekday or that this was not matzah, he has not fulfilled his obligation. In a matter of eating, lack of intent is treated as intent, as the Magen Avraham wrote there, and nevertheless he has not fulfilled. One cannot reject this by saying that there it is only according to our general ruling that mitzvot require intent, and only in matters of eating, because of the benefit he receives, we cannot nullify his act as though he had not acted, so it is treated as though he intended; therefore, if he did not know it was Pesach, we cannot say of him that it is as though he intended, whereas if mitzvot never require intent perhaps even in such a case he would fulfill. This is incorrect, for the Ran in Rosh Hashanah brings proof to this law: otherwise, why did Shmuel's father need to state the case of Persians forcing him? He should have taught a greater novelty, that even in one of these cases he fulfills. If so, the difficulty would still remain according to Rava there, who holds that mitzvot do not require intent. Rather, certainly even if mitzvot do not require intent, in such a case he has not fulfilled. This is a great difficulty on the author of Levush there, who concludes that the reason for this law is that mitzvot require intent. And if he expressly intends not to fulfill, according to all opinions he has not fulfilled; see Beit Yosef siman 589.
And some say that they require intent. Meaning, this is so by Torah law. Rashbam wrote this explicitly in Pesachim 114b, s.v. af al gav, and so the plain sense of the sugya in Rosh Hashanah 28b implies, from the Gemara's statement "but it is written, a remembrance of teruah," etc., indicating that their dispute is in a Torah matter. So wrote the Levush and other later authorities, unlike Peri Megadim, who is uncertain about this. Know that all this concerns other mitzvot. But a mitzvah dependent on eating, such as eating a kezayit of matzah on Pesach, and likewise eating a kezayit in the sukkah on the first night, the Shulchan Aruch's view below in siman 475 seif 4 is that one fulfills after the fact even without intent; the Bach is stringent there even in this, see there. See Bach and Peri Megadim in siman 8 and siman 625, from whose words it seems that intent in the mitzvot of tzitzit and sukkah is indispensable like other mitzvot. According to this, if one is called to the Torah and takes his tallit or the congregation's tallit to ascend the bimah, when he is rushed and presumably does not intend in wearing it to fulfill the positive mitzvah of tzitzit, he automatically transgresses the positive mitzvah unless he intends for the sake of the mitzvah; then he may also recite the blessing. People are not careful about this. Perhaps their reason is that since he does not wish to wear the tallit then and wears it only briefly for the honor of the Torah, this is not a wearing that obligates him in tzitzit, similar to one who wears a garment to show its size to a buyer, who is then exempt from tzitzit. The advised practice is to do as Shaarei Ephraim wrote, whose words I brought above in siman 14 seif 3 in Biur Halacha; see there.
To fulfill. See Mishna Berurah seif katan 9, where I wrote that one who blesses with children has not fulfilled his obligation. Nevertheless, it appears to me that if he did not eat to satiation, so that his obligation of Birkat Hamazon is rabbinic, perhaps after the fact one may rely on the Radbaz, with whom several later authorities agreed, that in a rabbinic mitzvah one does not require intent to fulfill and need not recite the blessing again. Although the Taz wrote that this is like intending not to fulfill and brought proof from the Rosh, this is surprising, for the Rosh and Rif follow their own view that mitzvot generally require intent, as the Rosh wrote in Rosh Hashanah. But according to one who holds that mitzvot do not require intent, perhaps here too he fulfilled, like one who blows shofar for practice. In any case his proof requires great study, as Yad Ephraim and Eliyah Rabbah challenged him; see there. Lechatchilah, one who is obligated in Birkat Hamazon and wants to bless with children to educate them should do one of two things: either intend to fulfill for himself as well, and thereby fulfill, as we wrote in Mishna Berurah in the name of Eliyah Rabbah, or expressly intend not to fulfill and afterward bless for himself.
And so is the halachah. See Mishna Berurah, where I wrote "and so from Biur HaGra," etc. In seif 4 of the Shulchan Aruch, in Biur HaGra, he strains to establish the Shulchan Aruch's ruling according to the opinion that mitzvot do not require intent. But in truth the Shulchan Aruch himself ruled in siman 60 that they do require intent; it would have been better to answer that counting the Omer is rabbinic, as Eliyah Rabbah answers. Rather, he evidently holds that one should not distinguish in this. As to whether one must repeat a blessing when the first performance, even of a Torah mitzvah, was without intent, we wrote in the name of the Magen Avraham that one should not bless again, and I found that Kenesset HaGedolah wrote so too in siman 489; see there. See Peri Megadim in the introduction, who is uncertain whether the Shulchan Aruch's practical ruling that mitzvot require intent is definite or because a Torah doubt is ruled stringently, with ramifications for rabbinic law. In his work on siman 589 and siman 489 he wrote that it also has ramifications for blessing, though this contradicts what he wrote in the introduction; see there. He was not the first in this, for Olat Shabbat also wrote that the Shulchan Aruch's reason is because of doubt, as cited in his name in Eliyah Rabbah siman 213, and therefore several places were difficult for him; see Eliyah Rabbah there. From Peri Megadim in siman 489 seif katan 8 it seems that he also wants to explain the Magen Avraham this way, that this is why the Magen Avraham holds one should not bless again, because of doubt. But when we examine the Magen Avraham carefully there is no proof of this; his intention is that regarding the blessing one must be concerned for those great authorities and not bless again. Eliyah Rabbah in siman 213 also rejected Olat Shabbat's words and holds that the Shulchan Aruch had no doubt at all. Biur HaGra in this siman, seif 4, s.v. ve-chen halachah, also explicitly proves that he holds like Eliyah Rabbah. Know that what we wrote in Mishna Berurah, where it is evident, etc., such as with Shema through the order of prayer, and with other mitzvot through the blessings or preparation for the mitzvah, is like what they said in Yerushalmi, chapter 10 halachah 3, regarding matzah: whether he intended or did not intend, since he reclined, there is a presumption that he intended. And in Yerushalmi Rosh Hashanah, regarding one who was passing behind a synagogue and heard the sound of shofar or the sound of Megillah, etc., it says this applies when he is passing, but if he stood, there is a presumption that he intended. Its meaning is that specifically if he stands behind the synagogue, in general he is not situated for the mitzvah; but if he stands inside the synagogue and hears the sound of shofar, presumably he stands with intent, as Yeshuot Yaakov wrote in this siman. Similar cases can be found in all mitzvot. Know further that even according to Chayei Adam, regarding Shema on Friday night according to our custom, where we recite it in the synagogue with its blessings before its time and rely on reading it again at its time, even if it happened that he read it in the synagogue at its proper time but did not intend to fulfill, it appears that by law he must read it again. Here it is not evident from his reading it in the order of prayer that he read it to fulfill, for he always reads it in the order of prayer even before its time, in order to juxtapose redemption to prayer.
