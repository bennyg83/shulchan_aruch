Seif 4. “Some say that mitzvos do not require intent, and some say that mitzvos require intent, etc.” Note: Even within the first opinion, there is a dispute whether one who expressly intends not to fulfill the obligation nevertheless fulfills it. It now appears to me that there is clear proof that, according to the view that intent is unnecessary, one fulfills the mitzvah even when expressly intending not to fulfill it. A practical difference arises regarding a D'Rabbanan mitzvah, for according to all opinions it does not require intent, and thus one would fulfill it even when intending not to do so. The proof is from Menachos 7b, where Rabbi Yochanan says: “This teaches that sacred vessels sanctify without the owner's awareness.” This is difficult. What follows merely from their sanctifying without awareness? Here one could expressly intend that the contents not become sanctified, so that he could later perform kemitzah. Necessarily, when awareness is not required, even an express stipulation is ineffective, and the vessel sanctifies the contents regardless. So too, where a mitzvah does not require intent, one fulfills it even if he expressly intends, or even states, that he does not wish to fulfill it. In my humble opinion, with Hashem's help, this is clear proof. Examine it carefully. Regarding the difficulty raised from Arachin and Sukkah against those who maintain that, if one intended to fulfill the mitzvah, he nevertheless did not fulfill it, see what I wrote to resolve this at length in a responsum in my work Tuv Taam VaDaas, Mahadura Kama, siman 112, Hilchos Mezuzah, in my responsum to the community of Vizhnitz. See there and examine it carefully. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 3, citing Radbaz that according to all opinions a D'Rabbanan mitzvah does not require intent. I was uncertain, however, in light of the Ran's statement in Rosh Hashanah concerning one who hears from a person who is merely practicing, that he does not fulfill the obligation. The Ran writes that even according to the view that intent is not required, intending the act for a different mitzvah is worse, because an act of the same category can negate it. See there. One must therefore determine the rule for a D'Rabbanan mitzvah: does even such a mitzvah require intent where he intended it for another mitzvah, or is intent unnecessary for a D'Rabbanan mitzvah even in that case? At first glance, proof may be brought from Yoma 88a. Rav states there that Ne'ilah exempts one from Ma'ariv, and the Gemara challenges him from a baraisa teaching that after Yom Kippur one recites an abridged form of the eighteen blessings. See there. The challenge is difficult. Why not say that Rav discusses one who asks beforehand, in which case we instruct him to intend during Ne'ilah to exempt Ma'ariv as well, and he is indeed exempt; whereas the baraisa discusses one who did not intend during Ne'ilah to exempt Ma'ariv? Such a case is no better than one who recited Ma'ariv without intending to fulfill its obligation, who would not fulfill it according to the view that Ma'ariv is obligatory. Certainly, then, Ne'ilah should not exempt him when he did not intend it as Ma'ariv. One cannot answer that the Gemara challenges Rav according to Rav's own view in Chullin, regarding a menstruant who was compelled to immerse, that she becomes permitted, because Rav maintains that mitzvos do not require intent. That does not resolve the matter, for here he certainly intended his prayer as Ne'ilah. According to the Ran cited above, intending the act for another mitzvah should be worse. Necessarily, the reason is that prayer is D'Rabbanan, and regarding a D'Rabbanan mitzvah intent is unnecessary even when one intended the act for another mitzvah. Examine this carefully. One should not object that the Gemara could still answer that the baraisa concerns one who expressly intended not to fulfill Ma'ariv through Ne'ilah. This cannot be correct, because the baraisa teaches the proper initial practice. It states that at Shacharis one recites seven blessings, at Ne'ilah one recites seven and confesses, and at Ma'ariv one recites an abridged eighteen. Had it been necessary specifically to intend during Ne'ilah not to fulfill Ma'ariv, the baraisa should have mentioned this as well, saying that at Ne'ilah one recites seven and intends not to fulfill Ma'ariv. Since it offers no such instruction, it necessarily concerns one who did not expressly intend not to fulfill Ma'ariv. If even ordinary lack of intent meant that he did not fulfill Ma'ariv, the Gemara's challenge would be proper, because the baraisa did not advise him to intend to fulfill Ma'ariv, while Rav could be discussing one who wishes to intend that Ne'ilah also exempt Ma'ariv. Necessarily, then, for a D'Rabbanan mitzvah intent is unnecessary even when the act was intended for another mitzvah. Examine this well. See also my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5599, Hilchos Tzitzis, siman 8, in a responsum to the community of Podhajce, where I discussed this rule. See there and examine it carefully. Rabbeinu Yonah maintains that even according to the view that mitzvos do not require intent, an obligation performed through speech certainly requires intent, because speech is not equivalent to an action. In my humble opinion, one may prove otherwise from Rashi's explanation in Menachos 2b. Rashi writes concerning “a thought evident to all” that the thought relating to offerings means verbal expression. Why did Rashi need to state this? Rashi's manner is to explain, not to issue halachic rulings, and this point is not uniquely relevant there. It would have been more appropriate to explain it in Zevachim. It appears that Rashi intended to resolve the following difficulty. Why do the Sages invalidate the offering? We rule that an action overrides both a prior action and a prior thought. Here, then, the person's acts demonstrate that he is offering this particular meal-offering. Why should it be invalidated by his contrary thought? Rashi therefore explains that this “thought” is verbal expression, and speech is itself considered an action, as stated elsewhere: movement of the lips constitutes an action. Thus, the action of speech overrides the physical action that demonstrates the opposite intent. One might reverse the argument and say that the demonstrative physical action should override the verbal action. Yet since the two are equal, what makes one stronger than the other? One can equally say that the verbal action overrides the demonstrative action. Since either possibility exists, we rule stringently. Furthermore, if they are equal, cancel one against the other. What remains is the thought in the heart, which also invalidates. Even if only an undifferentiated act remained, it would be invalid here. Ordinarily, an unspecified offering is valid, and a thought in the heart does not invalidate it, because at least an act of offering is present. Although it is not an act that proves a particular intent, it is nevertheless an act, and the offering is therefore presumed to be for its proper purpose, while an unspoken thought does not invalidate it. Here, however, there is verbal expression for an improper purpose, and that too is an action. When action is canceled against action, it is as though no action occurred at all. An unspecified designation therefore invalidates, and certainly a contrary thought in the heart invalidates. In my humble opinion, this is Rashi's intent. It follows that Rashi holds speech to be fully equivalent to action, unlike Rabbeinu Yonah. Examine this carefully. The poskim state explicitly that even according to those who hold that intent is unnecessary, one who intends not to fulfill the obligation does not fulfill it. One may ask whether intention in the heart suffices, so that inward intent not to fulfill also prevents fulfillment, or whether thoughts in the heart have no legal force, and only if he verbally states that he intends not to fulfill does he fail to fulfill. This may be resolved from the Yerushalmi, Shabbos, chapter Rabbi Eliezer DeMilah, halachah 2: “A Jew may circumcise a Samaritan, but a Samaritan may not circumcise a Jew, because he intends it for Mount Gerizim. These are the words of Rabbi Yudan. Rabbi Yose said: Where have we found that circumcision requires intent? Let him continue circumcising until the person's soul departs.” The final phrase, “until the person's soul departs,” is difficult to understand, and the Korban HaEdah's explanation is exceedingly strained, for what difference is there between this curse and another? It appears, however, that the meaning is as follows. Tosafos maintain that a non-Jew acts without specific intent. Therefore, an act whose unspecified performance is deemed for its proper purpose is valid when performed by a non-Jew, whereas an act whose unspecified performance is not deemed for its proper purpose is invalid when performed by a non-Jew. Rabbi Yose understood Rabbi Yudan to hold that even where an unspecified act is deemed for its proper purpose, express intent for the mitzvah is required. A Samaritan's circumcision is therefore invalid lest he intend it for Mount Gerizim. Although that intent consists only of thoughts in the heart, and thus cannot actively harm, it also cannot help. The act remains unspecified, and an unspecified act would be invalid on Rabbi Yudan's premise. Rabbi Yose, however, holds that where an unspecified act is presumed to be for its proper purpose, it is valid without affirmative intent, and only express intent for an improper purpose invalidates it. In that case, thoughts in the heart have no legal force and cannot harm; only a verbal declaration would invalidate. Therefore Rabbi Yose said: “Where have we found that circumcision requires intent?” An unspecified circumcision is presumed to be for its proper purpose. Nor can one say that the circumciser acts merely for personal benefit, for would he continue cutting until the circumcised person's soul departed? Certainly he does not intend to kill him. His only purpose is to correct the man's status, and the act is therefore presumed to be for its proper purpose. Since affirmative intent is unnecessary, even if he inwardly intends it for an improper purpose, such thoughts in the heart have no legal force. This proves that where intent is unnecessary, an inward intention not to perform the act for its proper purpose is ineffective; only an express verbal statement has force. This is how the matter appears to me at present. Perhaps Hashem will grant me the merit to explain it more fully at another time. Examine this carefully.
