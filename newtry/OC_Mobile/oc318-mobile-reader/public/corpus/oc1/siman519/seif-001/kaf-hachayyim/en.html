1) [Se'if 1] The shutters of shops may be removed, etc. This refers to shops that are not attached to the ground but resemble a wooden cabinet closed with a door. When the owner wishes to open it, he removes the door, places it before the shop, and arranges small merchants' wares upon it for sale. He may remove the door to take out spices and other items needed for Yom Tov. Although we rule that building and dismantling apply rabbinically even to utensils, the Sages permitted this for the joy of Yom Tov when needed for food preparation. He may also return the door to its place. Although replacing it is not needed for Yom Tov, the Sages permitted the concluding act because of the initial act. If they did not permit him to replace it, he would not open the shop and would refrain from contributing to the joy of Yom Tov. If he has a similar cabinet in his house, however, he may remove its door but may not replace it. In a house the concluding act was not permitted because of the initial act. Since the cabinet is in his own domain, he will not refrain from the joy of Yom Tov, because he is not afraid to leave it open in his house. Tur and Beis Yosef; Levush; Taz, subsection 1; Chemed Moshe, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 1; Mikraei Kodesh, siman 8, subsections 1 and 2; Mishnah Berurah, subsections 1 and 2.
2) Taz writes there that all this shows a distinction regarding replacing the door between shops and houses. According to Rashi, it is forbidden in houses whenever no public need is involved; consequently, when there is a public need, it is permitted even in houses. According to Rif, it is permitted even in houses when there is concern that the owner will not leave the cabinet open. Opening, however, is permitted in every case, even when no public need is involved. These are his words. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 3, however, writes that all the halachic authorities except Rashi give Rif's reason, unlike Taz, who rules according to both. These are his words. According to Eliyah Rabbah, even where a public need exists, the door may not be replaced in a house if there is no concern of theft should it remain open. Mishnah Berurah in Shaar HaTziyun, subsection 2, writes likewise. This also appears from the unqualified words of the later authorities: regarding houses, no distinction is made between a public and private need. Whenever there is no concern of theft if the cabinet remains open, its door may not be replaced.
3) There. The shutters of shops may be removed, etc. When does this apply? When there is no doorway. If there is a doorway, he should use the doorway. Yerushalmi. Rashba writes likewise in Avodas HaKodesh, gate 1, subsection 12. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1, cites him. Beis Yosef writes that since the halachic authorities did not make this distinction, they evidently hold that the halachah does not follow this Yerushalmi and removal is permitted in every case. Eliyah Rabbah responds that Beis Yosef did not see Avodas HaKodesh. Furthermore, the unqualified words of the halachic authorities are self-explanatory, for they give the reason that the owner might refrain from the joy of Yom Tov. If access is possible through a window or doorway, removal is therefore forbidden. These are his words. Bigdei Yesha writes that it is possible that even this is considered a concern that the owner will refrain from the joy of Yom Tov, since he sells to the public and repeatedly forcing himself to use the narrow access entails great exertion. These are his words. Even according to Bigdei Yesha, this applies specifically when he sells to the public and repeatedly taking out the goods entails exertion. Otherwise, it is forbidden to remove the shutters, and he should take the goods through the window or doorway if possible.
4) There. The shutters of shops may be removed, etc. This refers to shop doors belonging to a chest, box, or cabinet, meaning types of boxes, which are not attached to the ground. Regarding structures attached to the ground, building and dismantling apply, as written in siman 313, se'if 4, and siman 308, se'if 9. It is forbidden to remove or replace their doors. Magen Avraham, subsection 1; Chemed Moshe, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 4; Mikraei Kodesh, siman 8, subsection 3; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 1. This applies specifically when one completely removes the door from the shop. If he merely opens and closes it while it remains fixed in place, he may open and close it even when the shop is attached to the ground, as written below, subsection 9. See there.
5) There. When they have a hinge in the middle. This means that there is a hinge in the middle of the side wall, and the door has a corresponding projection inserted into the hinge. This does not appear so much like building. Tur; Levush; Taz, subsection 2; Chemed Moshe, subsection 1. Maamar Mordechai, subsection 1, writes that Rashi's explanation implies that the projection on the door that enters the hole in the side wall is itself called the hinge. See there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2, writes likewise.
