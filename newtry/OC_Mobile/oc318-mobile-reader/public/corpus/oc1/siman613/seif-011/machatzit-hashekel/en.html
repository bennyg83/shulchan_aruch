(Seif katan 11) She should not wipe, etc. This prohibition is not as severe as wringing. They therefore were concerned only for the severe prohibition, namely wringing. For this reason the Gemara was lenient on Tishah B’Av, when there is no prohibition of wringing, although there is a prohibition of washing, as Rama writes above in siman 554, seif 14.
And the Rif wrote, etc. I must first present the words of the Gemara and the Rif. Yoma 78a states: Zeira bar Chama said to Rabbi Yosef, son of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi: “Son of a lion, come and I will tell you a fine practice of your father. He had a cloth on the eve of Yom Kippur, soaked it in water, and made it like dried utensils; the next day he wiped his face, hands, and feet with it. On the eve of Tishah B’Av he soaked it in water,” without requiring that it be made like dried utensils, “and the next day passed it over his eyes,” etc. Rabbi Yaakov said to Rabbi Yirmiyah bar Tachlifa: “You told us the reverse,” meaning that regarding Tishah B’Av you said he made it like dried utensils, but regarding Yom Kippur you did not say so, “and we challenged you from wringing.” That is to say, on Yom Kippur, since wringing is forbidden, if the cloth was not made like dried utensils and still contained water, wiping his face, hands, and feet could lead to wringing. According to this version, if it was made like dried utensils, although it remained somewhat damp, there was no longer concern for wringing and it was permitted. Rashi records another version that he heard: On the eve of Yom Kippur they brought him a cloth and he wiped his hands with it, and the next day passed it over his eyes. According to this, wiping with a cloth soaked in water was not permitted at all, only with a cloth slightly soiled from wiping his hands. On the eve of Tishah B’Av, however, he soaked it and made it like dried utensils, and the next day, etc. Rabbi Yaakov then said that the reverse had been taught: on the eve of Yom Kippur he soaked a cloth and made it like dried utensils, etc., and we challenged from wringing. According to this version, even soaking it and making it like dried utensils is forbidden because of wringing. Wiping his hands on a dry cloth on the eve of Yom Kippur and passing it over his eyes on Yom Kippur, however, is permitted even according to this version. The Rif writes: “Zeira bar Chama said to Rabbi Yosef, son of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi, etc. On the eve of Tishah B’Av they brought him a cloth, soaked it in water, and he wiped his hands and feet with it; the next day he passed it over his eyes. On the eve of Yom Kippur he soaked it in water and made it like dried utensils; the next day he passed it over his eyes,” etc. “We concluded that on the eve of Tishah B’Av he soaks it in water and makes it like dried utensils. On the eve of Yom Kippur he wipes his face, hands, and feet with it, and the next day passes it over his eyes. He may not initially soak it in water, because of concern that he may wring it while passing it over his face. This is the halachah.” End quote. Magen Avraham copied the Rif’s conclusion. This wording of the Rif is difficult to reconcile. I found in Lechem Mishneh, chapter 3 of Hilchos Shevisas Asor, halachah 5: “Although the Venice edition of the Rif reads, ‘We concluded that on the eve of Tishah B’Av he soaks it in water,’ etc., this certainly is not the Rif’s text. It is either a scribal error or a gloss of a student, for those words accord with the version Rashi heard, whereas the Rif’s text follows Rashi’s first version, and the two versions differ greatly. The phrase ‘We concluded’ therefore contradicts the version previously recorded by the Rif and certainly is not his. It is absent from the Constantinople edition. The Rif’s view is surely that on the eve of Tishah B’Av he soaks it in water without making it like dried utensils, whereas on the eve of Yom Kippur he makes it like dried utensils.” End quote. Magen Avraham’s words are nevertheless correct according to the version Rashi heard. Still, none of this proves that one may be lenient against Hagahos Maimoniyos and Semak. Even if the strict law permits it, their words impose a stringency for us beyond the law of the Gemara, as appears from their language cited by Beis Yosef. Logically, however, one may say that although they were stringent when one soaks a cloth on the eve of Yom Kippur and makes it like dried utensils, if he merely wipes his hands with it on the eve of Yom Kippur, when there is not such a concern of wringing, they concede that it is permitted even for us. Perhaps Magen Avraham means that he too was troubled by the Rif’s phrase “We concluded,” since according to the Rif’s version even soaking the cloth on the eve of Yom Kippur and making it like dried utensils is permitted. He therefore explains that “We concluded” does not mean that this was the Gemara’s conclusion. According to the strict law of the Gemara, it is certainly permitted on the eve of Yom Kippur to soak it and make it like dried utensils. Rather, later Sages, after the completion of the Gemara, concluded that one should be stringent, in accordance with Hagahos Maimoniyos and Semak.
Nevertheless, they permitted him to wipe his hands with a cloth on the eve of Yom Kippur and to pass it over his eyes the following day. This resolves Magen Avraham’s words concerning Rama, whose ruling is copied from Hagahos Maimoniyos and Semak.
And the Rif wrote, etc. Rama, Hagahos Maimoniyos, and Semak likewise agree that one may wipe his hands with a cloth on the eve of Yom Kippur and pass it over his eyes on Yom Kippur. For even the Rif, who is more stringent than the Gemara in the manner described by Hagahos Maimoniyos and Semak, nevertheless permits this case.
