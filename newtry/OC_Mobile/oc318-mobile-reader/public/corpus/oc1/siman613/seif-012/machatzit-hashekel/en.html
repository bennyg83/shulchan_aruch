(Seif katan 12) A bride, etc. “This requires great study, for Rashi in Kesubos wrote,” etc. The Mishnah in Yoma teaches that Yom Kippur is forbidden, etc., and regarding washing, etc., a king and a bride may wash their faces. On 78b the Gemara asks: Why may a bride do so? So that she not become repulsive to her husband. Rav asked Rav Chiya: Until when is she considered a bride? He answered: As we learned, ornaments are not withheld from a bride for thirty days. Rashi explains: “Ornaments are not withheld, etc. If one had prepared the slaughtered animal, etc., and the groom’s father or the bride’s mother died, the deceased is placed in a room, the groom and bride are brought to the chuppah, he consummates the marriage and separates, and afterward the deceased is buried. Ornaments are not withheld from the bride because of the mourning for all thirty days. This proves that she requires adornment throughout the thirty days.” End quote. It is clear from this that the teaching “ornaments are not withheld” does not refer back to the preceding case, but is an independent statement, as Rosh explains in Kesubos 4a: “It is an independent statement. Its meaning is that if mourning occurred to a bride during the thirty days of her wedding celebration, ornaments are not withheld from her. She adorns herself as a bride even during the seven days of mourning, so that she not become repulsive to her husband. All thirty days of her wedding are a time of joy for groom and bride, and she must not make herself unattractive. Mourning does not apply to her regarding these matters at all.” According to this, Rav Chiya properly proved that she is called a bride for thirty days and may wash on Yom Kippur, just as during mourning, so that she not become repulsive. After thirty days she is like other women. If a different relative dies after thirty days from her wedding, she must observe mourning, and we are not concerned that she may make herself unattractive. Rashi in Kesubos 4a, however, writes: “Ornaments are not withheld, etc., throughout the thirty days, although these are days of mourning for others, and all the more so after thirty days.” See Rosh there. This implies that Rashi understands the statement to refer back to the case in which the slaughtered animal was prepared, etc.: even after burial, when mourning begins, ornaments are not withheld from the bride for thirty days, so that she not become repulsive; all the more so after thirty days, when the mourning is lighter. According to this, the phrase “ornaments are not withheld for thirty days” means thirty days from the burial. Magen Avraham therefore asks: What proof can be brought from there that she is called a bride only until thirty days? On the contrary, there ornaments are permitted even after thirty days. I saw in a Shulchan Aruch with Baer Heitev that it writes regarding Magen Avraham: See Mordechai in Moed Katan, who raises this question according to Rashi and answers it; see there. Mordechai, Moed Katan, siman 524, writes: “We learned in the first chapter of Kesubos: If one had prepared the slaughtered animal, etc. Rashi explains: all the more so after thirty days. ‘Ornaments’ implies even removal of hair, as taught in the first chapter of Moed Katan: A woman may perform her adornments on Chol HaMoed, etc., braid her hair, apply cosmetics, remove her hair and nails. One may ask from Yoma, etc., where it explains: Until when is she a bride? As we learned, ornaments are not withheld, etc. This implies that until thirty days she is permitted, but afterward she is no longer called a bride and is like all other women. One may answer that this applies regarding Yom Kippur. Regarding mourning, however, since she is permitted during the first thirty days, which are the principal days of mourning, all the more so is she permitted after thirty days. This is how Rashi may be explained.” End quote. Further clarification is still needed. Although Mordechai resolves the contradiction from Kesubos, whence did Rav Chiya know that specifically until thirty days she is called a bride and no longer? Perhaps Rav Chiya regarded it as obvious that after thirty days she is like all other women and no leniency was granted to her on Yom Kippur. He needed proof only that at least until thirty days she is called a bride and there is concern that she not become unattractive. This likewise appears from Rashi in Yoma, who concludes: “This proves that she requires adornment throughout the thirty days.” His proof concerns permission throughout the first thirty days. He therefore properly cites evidence from mourning. Although mourning is lighter, as she is permitted even after thirty days, this is because mourning itself is then lighter. During the first thirty days, which are the principal period of mourning, they nevertheless were lenient for her, proving that she requires greater adornment. Thus, although mourning is D’Rabbanan, it can teach the law of Yom Kippur, because whatever the Sages instituted, they instituted in the pattern of Torah law.
