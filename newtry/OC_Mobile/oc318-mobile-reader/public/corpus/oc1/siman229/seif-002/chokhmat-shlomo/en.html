Seif 2. One who sees the sun at its turning point, which occurs once every twenty-eight years. Note: I saw that the later authorities cite Responsa Panim Meiros as ruling that one recites the blessing even on a cloudy day when he cannot see the sun. I am utterly astonished by this, for the Gemara explicitly states, “One who sees the sun,” which plainly requires actual sight. Otherwise, why did it use the phrase “one who sees”? It should have said that on the morning of the tekufah one recites the blessing. Moreover, why should this differ from the moon? We also know that the moon renews itself every month, yet one does not recite the blessing without seeing it. Furthermore, the Baraisa lists the two together: “One who sees the sun at its turning point and the moon in its renewal,” etc. Just as the blessing over the moon requires sight, so too the blessing over the sun requires sight. The proof offered in Yad Ephraim from a responsum that one recites the blessing over a king who is aboard a ship is no comparison. Blessings over the luminaries are comparable to blessings over benefit, and one must actually derive benefit from their light. Moreover, even apart from that, the cases are entirely dissimilar. Although one need not see the king’s body, he must at least see the royal honor; otherwise he does not recite the blessing, since the blessing is not over the king’s body but over the honor. He therefore sees the honor in any event. The proof is obvious: could one imagine that a person sitting in his house, merely knowing that the king is passing somewhere, should recite the blessing? Certainly not. On the contrary, that case proves the opposite. Just as there one recites the blessing only when seeing the royal honor, and otherwise does not, so too here, since the blessing is over the sun, one does not recite it unless he sees it. This is also proved by the Gemara in Berachos, where a heretic asked Rav Sheshes, “The pitchers go to the river; where do the broken jars go?” What was the question? Rav Sheshes was going in order to recite the blessing. Necessarily, actual sight is required. Rav Sheshes merely answered that because he sensed the matter, it was considered like sight. Heaven forbid that one should recite the blessing on a cloudy day without seeing the sun. It also appears to me as a matter of practical halachah that the primary ruling follows Levush and Magen Avraham, that one should not recite the blessing after three hours of the day, contrary to those who permit it until midday. Even assuming that the matter is doubtful, all blessings are Rabbinic, and in a Rabbinic doubt we are lenient. It would therefore be a doubtful prohibition to utter Hashem’s Name in vain. Furthermore, since the Aruch explains the phrase “the sun at its turning point” differently, after three hours there is a double doubt against reciting the blessing. I also saw that Eliyah Rabbah cites the Aruch as holding that one does not recite this blessing at all every twenty-eight years because of uncertainty regarding blessings, and writes that this was the practice. Although we do not follow that practice, after three hours, when there is a double doubt, one should not recite it. One who recites it with Hashem’s Name and kingship recites a blessing in vain. This is how I ruled in practice in the year 5601, when the time for the blessing fell on the second day of Pesach and the sun did not appear until one hour before midday. I recited the blessing without Hashem’s Name and kingship. In my opinion, this is the correct ruling, with Hashem’s help. Examine this carefully.
