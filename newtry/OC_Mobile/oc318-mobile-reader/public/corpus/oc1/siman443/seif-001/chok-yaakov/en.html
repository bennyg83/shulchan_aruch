From the beginning of the hour. Therefore one should hurry to eat in the fourth hour, etc. Maharil writes: if someone died on erev Pesach, they take the deceased out to the cemetery, return home and eat, and afterward bury him, end quote. The Magen Avraham wonders about Maharil: how can they interrupt for eating? The Ashkenazic custom was hidden from him, for this is what they customarily do even on other days: they take the deceased out to the cemetery and return home until the diggers have dug and the other needs of the deceased are prepared; afterward they return and call people for the burial of the deceased. If so, there is no interruption here at all. Furthermore, if they eat before taking the deceased out, the eating and the destruction of chametz will naturally continue until its time, and afterward, because of taking the deceased outside the city and burying him, they will be prevented from attending to the needs of Yom Tov and kneading the matzos. Therefore they first took him out and then interrupted for eating; otherwise they would be completely prevented from attending to the burial of the deceased. This is clear to me.
Even a large amount together. Even kutach or liquor, although its owner's name is associated with it, may be sold before Pesach, at a permitted time, to a non-Jew, even if he knows that the non-Jew will not finish it before Pesach. This is the consensus of the later authorities, unlike the Bach, who is stringent in this, and that is an excessive stringency.
And from the beginning of the sixth hour and onward they forbade it also in benefit. The Tur concludes: and if he betrothed a woman with it, she is not betrothed. The Beis Yosef asks from the Tur's words here against his words in Even HaEzer, and the Bach and the Taz present the Beis Yosef as having made a complete error. Also in Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer siman 28, the later authorities are very astonished at the Beis Yosef in Shulchan Aruch. In my humble opinion, however, the words of the Beis Yosef here and there in Even HaEzer are correct without any doubt at all. I will therefore first set out the source of the law. We learn in Pesachim folio 7 regarding what Rabbi Yehudah said, that one who checks must nullify, etc. The Shas asks: let him nullify it in the sixth hour. The Shas answers: since a rabbinic prohibition has taken effect on it, it is like a Torah prohibition; it is not in his possession and he cannot nullify it, for Rav Giddel said in the name of Rav: one who betroths a woman from six hours and onward, even with wheat of Kordanya, we are not concerned for the kiddushin. Rashi explains: from six hours and onward means from the beginning of the sixth hour, for it is a rabbinic prohibition, end quote. Tosafos wrote: this is not clear to Rabbeinu Tam, for 'from six hours and onward' implies from the end of six hours; nevertheless the proof is from wheat of Kordanya, which is hard and is chametz nukshah, which is also forbidden only rabbinically. The later authorities understood here and in Even HaEzer that even according to Rashi, wheat of Kordanya is only rabbinic chametz, so that there are also rabbinic hours, namely the beginning of the sixth hour; nevertheless there is no kiddushin even with two rabbinic factors. In truth, in my humble opinion, this is not at all compelled in Rashi's view. It is possible that according to Rashi too, with two rabbinic factors there is no kiddushin, but he holds that wheat of Kordanya is not chametz nukshah, or he holds like the opinion that chametz nukshah is a Torah prohibition. So one must say, and there is a clear and necessary proof from Rashi's view in the chapter Kol Sha'ah, Pesachim folio 28, where he wrote on Rav Giddel's statement: 'from six hours' must mean from the beginning of six, for if it were after six, it is forbidden according to the Torah; what does he come to teach us? It is obvious that one cannot betroth with a benefit-prohibition. It is explicit in Rashi's view that if wheat of Kordanya became chametz, it is a Torah prohibition; otherwise what did Rashi prove by saying that if after six it is forbidden according to the Torah, what does he come to teach us? He could still be teaching us about rabbinic chametz nukshah. Rather, it is certainly Torah-level chametz. This is also implied by Tosafos at the beginning of Pesachim, who originated this law that it is rabbinic chametz on their own, not that this is Rashi's view. Indeed, from Tosafos at the end of HaIsh Mekadesh, Kiddushin folio 58, on the sugya of non-sacred animals slaughtered in the Temple courtyard, where Rabbi Shimon says she is betrothed, the Shas asks that apparently according to Rabbi Shimon non-sacred animals slaughtered in the Temple courtyard are not prohibited according to the Torah, and challenges from another source, etc. It answers that here we are dealing with one who slaughtered and it was found to be a tereifah, which is slaughter that is not fit, and even if slaughtered in the Temple courtyard they are permitted in benefit. Tosafos wrote there: it implies that non-sacred animals slaughtered in the Temple courtyard are not a Torah prohibition, only a rabbinic prohibition of benefit, and it is difficult: how can he betroth with them? Rav Giddel said in the first chapter of Pesachim that one who betroths from six hours and onward, even with wheat of Kordanya, which is forbidden only rabbinically, we are not concerned for the kiddushin. One can answer that there, at least the hours are Torah-level according to the way Rabbeinu Tam explained there, but here there is only a rabbinic prohibition. According to Rashi's explanation too, there is no difficulty according to the conclusion, for even according to Rabbi Shimon she is not betrothed, end quote of Tosafos. From Tosafos writing that according to Rashi there is no difficulty according to the conclusion, it implies that before the conclusion the matter did not fit Rashi's explanation, because even with two rabbinic factors she is not betrothed. The Maharsha explains Tosafos there this way, and this is also the view of the Rav of Bartenura at the beginning of Kol Sha'ah. According to this, one must force Rashi's language in Kol Sha'ah that I cited and explain it as follows: since he says 'even with wheat of Kordanya,' it implies that the law itself refers to rabbinic hours, for if it could be speaking of Torah-law hours, why say 'even'? Even with wheat of Kordanya, which is rabbinic, the whole law would have been stated only for that, because where the chametz is Torah-level and the benefit-prohibition is Torah-level, what would he be teaching us by saying she is not betrothed? Rather, he certainly speaks of rabbinic hours, and therefore he says that even with two rabbinic factors she is also not betrothed. After these words of Tosafos the later authorities may have followed. But in my opinion and the opinion of the Beis Yosef, this explanation is forced and very distant from Rashi's meaning. The intent of Tosafos at the beginning of HaIsh Mekadesh can be explained that they spoke only by way of broad analysis: according to their own approach, that wheat of Kordanya is only rabbinic, even if Rav Giddel's words are explained as Rashi explains, namely with rabbinic hours, nevertheless according to the conclusion there is no difficulty. But in truth, Rashi's own view is that he is speaking of Torah-level chametz, and one must say so, because otherwise the plain meaning of the Kiddushin sugya is difficult for Rashi. There the Gemara says, 'apparently he holds that non-sacred animals slaughtered in the Temple courtyard are not forbidden according to the Torah,' which implies that with a rabbinic prohibition of benefit she is betrothed. Although according to the conclusion this does not stand, that is because Rabbi Shimon holds that non-sacred animals in the Temple courtyard are a Torah prohibition and the case is where one slaughtered and it was found to be a tereifah; but with this point the plain implication of the Shas is that with rabbinic benefit-prohibitions she is betrothed. Thus the Beis Yosef's words are correct: the Tur's ruling here follows Rashi and the Rambam regarding Torah-level chametz and rabbinic hours, while in Even HaEzer he discussed Rabbeinu Tam's approach and the Rosh's view. This resolves the contradiction, and the challenges of the later authorities are not decisive.
And some say until two hours before midday. The consensus of the later authorities is like the first view, until a third of the day, for we calculate the day as twelve proportional hours, whether the day is short or long. So too the Taz concluded that this is the main law. In the work Minchas Kohen he concludes that for this matter, which is rabbinic, we calculate the day from alos hashachar until tzeis hakochavim.
