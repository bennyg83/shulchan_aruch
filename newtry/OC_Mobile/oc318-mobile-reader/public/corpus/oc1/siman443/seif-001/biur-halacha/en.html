Even a non-Jew's chametz is forbidden to benefit from. The later authorities wrote in siman 448 that according to this it is forbidden even to smell hot bread of a non-Jew. In the work Chemed Moshe he questions the basic law of smelling chametz bread, even of a Jew, according to what we rule in Yoreh De'ah siman 108, that something not intended for scent may be smelled; and likewise regarding bread, see his words. In truth, however, the main point is not clear, whether bread is not considered intended for scent. See above, siman 214, in the gloss, where there are two views about this, and see there in Biur HaGra. Also in Yoreh De'ah siman 108 itself it is stated according to one opinion that with chametz on Pesach, whose prohibition is even in the smallest amount, we say that scent is significant; see there. Apart from the law itself, there is reason to forbid it for another reason, lest one come to eat it. See Minchas Yaakov, klal 36 s.k. 38, who discusses these details at length and brings there in the name of the Rokeach something like this. Above, in siman 216 se'if 2, in Biur Halacha s.v. 'hamosak,' we wrote in a similar case to forbid, especially regarding chametz, from which one is not separated the whole year.
They forbade it also in benefit. It is obvious that at that time it is forbidden to sell it to a non-Jew, for the sale itself is considered benefit. However, after the fact, if he sold it, the Peri Megadim in siman 448, Mishbetzos Zahav s.k. 6, inclines that it helps at least so that after Pesach it will not be called chametz that Pesach passed over; so too ruled the work Binyan Olam and Responsa Sho'el U'Meishiv, part 2, siman 40. Some later authorities incline that even after six hours, after the fact if he sold it, it is not afterward called chametz that Pesach passed over, since according to several poskim he does not transgress bal yera'eh until night. See Noda BiYehuda Tinyana, siman 63, Sha'agas Aryeh siman 79, Sha'arei Teshuvah siman 448 s.k. 16, and Nishmas Adam in the laws of selling chametz, question 1. Where one threw his chametz into the public domain after midday, whether it is permitted after Pesach to take possession of it again, see below in siman 448 in Sha'arei Teshuvah, and in the Nishmas Adam cited above, and in Sdei Chemed.
And some say until two hours before midday. See Mishnah Berurah. The reason is written in Terumas HaDeshen, that we rely on the first answer of the Gemara, that a person does not err by more than two hours, and these hours presumably are ordinary equal hours. At first glance, according to this view there could sometimes also be a stringency, for example in countries where the days before Pesach are shorter than ours: it would not be permitted to eat two hours before midday, whereas according to the first view, where we calculate proportional hours, it would certainly be permitted even within two hours, so long as it is still within a third of the day. So too it is implied in Nahar Shalom and Mor U'Ketziah. In my humble opinion, one can perhaps say that in this case Terumas HaDeshen also agrees to be lenient and not to be more stringent than the first view. For although he relies on the first answer, that a person does not err, etc., there is still another explanation, namely that the fourth hour is mealtime for everyone and a person does not err in it; these explanations do not dispute one another, as Terumas HaDeshen wrote. If so, one should not forbid eating within two hours out of concern that through this he may err and eat after midday, for until a third of the day it is mealtime for everyone. Presumably even in countries where the day is short, mealtime is until a third of the day, even if it extends into the two hours before midday, and therefore a person does not err in it. In any case, as a matter of law there is no practical difference, for in such a case we can rely on the first view to be lenient, especially since it is the main view for many poskim.
