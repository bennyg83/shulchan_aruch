Two hours earlier. The reason is because of a cloudy day, for a person is liable to err by two hours since the sun is not shining.
They forbade it also in benefit. In the Tur it is written: if he betrothed a woman with it, she is not betrothed. It seems to me that this is because he is speaking of actual chametz, whether according to Rashi and the Rambam or according to Rabbeinu Tam as brought by the Beis Yosef. There is no dispute between Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam except where there are two rabbinic factors, namely before the end of the sixth hour, when that time is rabbinic, and chametz nukshah, which is rabbinic. Even there, according to Rashi and the Rambam we are not concerned for kiddushin; according to Rabbeinu Tam, only where there is one rabbinic factor, either the time or the chametz, and one Torah-law factor, are we not concerned for kiddushin, but where there are two rabbinic factors we are concerned for kiddushin. If so, the Tur's words here are according to all opinions, unlike the Beis Yosef, who wrote that here he wrote according to Rashi and the Rambam. However, the Tur in Even HaEzer at the end of siman 28 wrote that according to the Rosh, following the approach of Rabbeinu Tam, the time is the main factor and we follow it whether it is Torah-law or rabbinic. This requires analysis, and will be explained there with help from Heaven.
Some say, etc. This is the view of Terumas HaDeshen, siman 121, and the Rav's words in this are astonishing. For this is what appears in Terumas HaDeshen: the question was that they eat all of the fourth hour and no longer; in a leap year, when the day is longer in our regions, at the end of the fourth hour there are still about three hours until midday. Until when is it permitted to eat chametz? Is it always until two hours before midday, or perhaps when four hours from the beginning of the day are completed, it is always forbidden to eat chametz? He answered: it appears from the halachah that it is always permitted to eat until two hours before midday. Although Rava concludes that the reason is because of a cloudy day, and the fourth hour is mealtime for everyone, and in this a person does not err, according to that answer one could say that even on long days the mealtime is always at the fourth hour from the beginning of the day, and after four hours mealtime is over and there is reason to decree because of a cloudy day. Nevertheless, since according to the first answer a person errs by two hours, one should never forbid except two hours before midday, as is proven there, etc., end quote. It is implied from his words that he means two of our hours, meaning that we calculate our hour as one twenty-fourth of the day and night. So too it appears from his words in siman 167 regarding the measure for salting meat, where he compares our hours to the hours in the Talmud. This is difficult, for if so, that which we learned that one may eat all of the fourth hour would also mean our ordinary hours. But are hours written in the Torah, that we should follow them? Rather, certainly the hours are proportional, as written in the first opinion, and this is the view of the Rambam. This is very strongly proven, for the prohibition of chametz and the slaughter of the Pesach offering are connected to one another, as the verse says, 'You shall not slaughter the blood of My offering upon chametz,' and the slaughter of the Pesach offering depends on the nature of the day, as stated at the beginning of the chapter Tamid Nishchat. There we say that on erev Pesach the tamid is slaughtered at seven and a half hours, and Rava explains that the time of the mitzvah of the tamid is from when the evening shadows incline, when the sun begins toward evening, etc. It is therefore explicit that we follow the day. Since midday according to the sun is called six hours, for we calculate every day, whether long or short, as twelve hours, those are the hours about which the Sages said that one may eat all of the fourth. Also, what the Sages said, that the fourth hour is everyone's mealtime, likewise means according to the nature of the day, for a person is accustomed to eat his meal at a third of the day, each day according to what it is, as is people's practice. Certainly the mealtime on a long day is not the same as on a short day, but depends on the nature of the day. If so, there is no question at all, for when the Gemara says a person errs by two hours, it means according to the hours of that day, as is explicit where it says: if so, let us also eat in the sixth hour; Rav Ada bar Ahavah said: in the sixth hour the sun stands at the horns, meaning in the middle of the sky, etc. Our ordinary hours have no relevance to the position of the sun. Therefore, also according to the first answer, that a person errs by two hours, it means proportional hours. When on a long day we calculate two proportional hours before midday, that is exactly a third of the day, and both are one and the same. The words of Terumas HaDeshen are therefore very astonishing, since he made the prohibition depend on our ordinary hours, and this certainly is not so. The Beis Yosef in siman 431 wrote that the words of Terumas HaDeshen accord with the Rambam's view. If so, he should have explained that the two hours mentioned by Terumas HaDeshen are proportional hours, but this certainly is not so, for then there would not have been two sides to his doubt, and Terumas HaDeshen would not have needed to analyze it. The Rema understood, as written in Terumas HaDeshen, that he was speaking of our ordinary hours; therefore he made from this two opinions here. But the truth is that the words of Terumas HaDeshen in this matter are not clear, for according to his view the words of the Tanna would depend on our ordinary hours. What relevance is there for us to make the prohibition depend on two of our hours before midday? Why did the Tanna give the measure of eating until four hours and no more? He should also have said until two hours before midday, which would be the same in all seasons. Rather, this is certainly a void matter, and all the hours are proportional. Heaven forbid that we should eat after a third of the day from dawn. So too my father-in-law, the Bach, ruled in siman 431, that one should be stringent like the first view here. In my humble opinion, the second view has no support at all, and the essential law follows the first view.
