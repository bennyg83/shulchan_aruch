He should hold it back. It seems to me that if it is something for which purchasers will not readily be found in the fifth hour, it is permitted to sell it earlier. However, if it is a place where he can sell it to a non-Jew whom he knows will return it to him after Pesach, it is forbidden to sell it as an absolute sale.
He is not liable for its responsibility. Even though he himself does not transgress regarding it, nevertheless he should destroy it so that the depositor will not transgress regarding it. And even though perhaps the depositor sold it to a non-Jew in a manner such that he would not transgress regarding it, nevertheless a doubt does not remove a certainty, and perhaps he did not sell it; therefore he is obligated to destroy it, as the Bach writes. It appears that the bailee is obligated to pay the value of the deposit to the depositor, for an unpaid guardian who could have saved the item with shepherds and sticks and did not save it is liable; here too, he should have sold it before the time of prohibition. One cannot say that the depositor was negligent, for he should have sold it to a non-Jew wherever it was, since we rule that the bailee is not permitted to rely on that and is obligated to sell it. If so, he should have sold it. However, if the chametz is still intact, he can say to him: here is yours before you. So too it appears in Bava Kamma folio 45. However, if it was a pledge in his hand, it requires analysis whether he can say to him: here is yours before you, and the borrower is obligated to pay him his money; or perhaps, since the lender was negligent, for he should have sold it, he has lost his money. At first glance it appears that since one who lends on a pledge has the law of a guardian, and regarding a guardian he can say to him: here is yours before you, so too with a pledge, and the borrower must pay him. This requires analysis.
