Even though the chametz is not his, nevertheless it is incumbent upon him to destroy it so that the depositor will not transgress concerning it, if we do not know that he sold it; a doubt does not remove a certainty. Bach. The Magen Avraham wrote: it appears that the bailee is obligated to pay the value of the deposit to the depositor, for he should have sold it before the time of its prohibition. One cannot say that the depositor was negligent, for he should have sold it to a non-Jew wherever it was, because we rule that the bailee is not permitted to rely on this, since a doubt does not remove a certainty and he is obligated to destroy it; and he is obligated to sell it. If so, he was negligent by not selling it. However, if the chametz is intact, he can say to him: here is yours before you. So too it is implied in Bava Kamma folio 45, end quote of the Magen Avraham. Regarding the law of 'here is yours before you,' this is correct, as is explicit there in the Shach and on folio 98. Although I saw in Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim, siman 28, that he is uncertain about this law because Maharshal in Yam Shel Shlomo, chapter HaGozel Kama, siman 4, distinguishes between one who stole chametz and a guardian, and concludes there that in all benefit-prohibitions in the world we say 'here is yours before you' regarding a robber but not regarding a guardian, end quote of Maharshal. However, the Shach to Choshen Mishpat siman 363 disagreed with him; see there, and in Sha'ar Ephraim he discusses the matter at length to decide between the views. In truth, however, this is a great error and he labored over it for nothing, for Maharshal did not write there to decide that regarding a guardian we do not say 'here is yours before you' as a general halachah against the Rabbis. He said it only regarding an ox whose judgment was completed, where one could have sent it to the marshes; but regarding chametz on Pesach, where the prohibition comes automatically, everyone agrees that we say 'here is yours before you.' Otherwise they should have disputed regarding chametz on Pesach, as is explicit in the Shas there in two places. This is clear to one who studies Maharshal's words carefully. However, regarding the main law that the Magen Avraham wrote, that if the chametz is not intact, such as where he destroyed it during its time of prohibition, the bailee must pay, and as is also implied there in Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim siman 28, his words are not correct. The Rambam and Choshen Mishpat siman 292 wrote that the bailee is obligated to sell it only because of returning a lost object to its owners. So too it is somewhat implied from Rashi in Bava Kamma folio 98, who explains that since the Gemara disputes concerning an ox and a guardian, it should also have disputed concerning one who stole chametz and Pesach passed over it. Since, regarding chametz, it mentioned theft and did not mention a guardian, this implies that a guardian is certainly exempt even without saying 'here is yours before you.' In a responsum to one scholar I explained this law at greater length, and I will copy it nearby. See in the words of the later authorities that the same applies if a Jew pledged a pledge with another Jew; regarding this matter its law is like that of a guardian. See the Rosh and the rulings of the Rosh in chapter HaGozel Etzim: although one who stole chametz and Pesach passed over it can say 'here is yours before you,' nevertheless if he swore falsely about it, he does not achieve atonement until he pays him money value for it. This requires analysis, because this law is not mentioned in Choshen Mishpat siman 363 regarding theft; see there in Yam Shel Shlomo. I was asked as follows: an incident occurred in a country near Hamburg, where one Jew deposited his chametz, worth three hundred, which was actual chametz, with another Jew who lived in another city. The chametz remained with him into Pesach, and the bailee did not sell that chametz to a non-Jew. Then that chametz was stolen from him during Pesach without negligence on his part, and we were uncertain upon whom the loss falls. According to what the Magen Avraham wrote at the end of siman 443, that specifically if it is intact he can say 'here is yours before you,' but if it was stolen and he cannot say 'here is yours before you,' the bailee must pay the depositor. But it seems obvious to me that even if the failure to sell the chametz is called negligence, nevertheless when it was stolen during Pesach he is exempt, since for not selling the chametz he is exempt because it is damage that is not visible, and for the theft he is exempt because he was not negligent in the theft and he is an unpaid guardian. Furthermore, the very law written by the Magen Avraham, to obligate him for not selling because it is negligence for which an unpaid guardian is liable, requires analysis. From Rashi in Bava Kamma folio 98, s.v. 'since he disputed regarding an ox,' it sounds that with a guardian there is no basis to obligate him at all for chametz because he was negligent and did not sell it. The Rambam and Choshen Mishpat siman 292 se'if 17 wrote: one who deposits chametz with another and erev Pesach arrives after the fifth hour, he goes out and sells it in the market because of returning a lost object to the owners. Thus he is not obligated under the law of guardianship, but under the law of returning a lost object to the owners; and one is not obligated with a lost object to involve himself so extensively in a sale. I later saw in Responsa Divrei Rivos, siman 123, regarding a guardian of orphans who was negligent by not selling the orphans' property and thereby caused a loss, that this is not called negligence, for it is merely indirect causation, end quote. Although the questioner wrote at length, in my humble opinion most of it is not relevant to this case, as I answered him at length. Therefore I abbreviated here and copied only what pertains to the main law. This is what I answered: if the bailee were being claimed during Pesach, when he could not say 'here is yours before you,' since everyone is commanded to destroy it then it is as though it is already burnt, so he is not returning anything. For this reason the Shas and poskim specifically spoke of chametz over which Pesach had passed, where he says 'here is yours before you,' unlike during Pesach. As stated in Bava Kamma folio 98, if one stole chametz before Pesach and during the festival another came and burned it, he is exempt because everyone is commanded to destroy it. If so, even if it was stolen or became an unavoidable loss during the festival, it could be considered beginning in negligence and ending in unavoidable loss, for which one is liable, as stated in siman 291 se'if 6; see the Sema there. Here too we could connect the unavoidable loss to the negligence, for had he sold it to a non-Jew outside the house, it would have been better guarded by him, and all the more so if he could have sold it directly to a non-Jew and guarded the money. But what was written to reject the Magen Avraham's view, that this is not called negligence, from Rashi in Bava Kamma and from the Rambam and Choshen Mishpat siman 292, in this his lips clearly spoke, and this too is what I intended in my work in siman 243 s.k. 7, in the law though not in his reasoning. Still I must clarify: from his words it sounds that he challenges the Magen Avraham only because an unpaid guardian should not be liable for this negligence, but if he were a paid guardian he would be liable; so too it is implied in Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim siman 28. If so, the difficulty from Rashi returns: Rashi explains that the Gemara should have disputed regarding theft, so why did it not also explain it regarding a paid guardian? In truth, the main proof from Rashi can be questioned, because Rashi may have explained theft because the Mishnah discusses theft, and from the Mishnah it is proven that they do not dispute, as Rashi explicitly explains in Bava Kamma folio 45, s.v. 'they should dispute,' etc.; therefore I was precise in my language in my work, and it is only somewhat implied from Rashi, not a complete proof. Further, what is clear from his words, that since the bailee is obligated to sell only because of returning a lost object, he is not obligated regarding a lost object to involve himself so extensively in selling it, is not clear to me at all. According to the Rambam in the laws of lost objects, one who guards a lost object is a paid guardian. If he sees the lost object going to loss, he must make an arrangement so that it should not be lost, since he has already acquired responsibility for the lost object; if he does not do so, even though it may not be called negligence, it certainly is not unavoidable, and he must pay according to the Rambam, like a paid guardian. According to those who disagree, he is an unpaid guardian, and it is a doubt in law, as explained in the Beis Yosef, siman 72. If so, the same applies in our case: even though he was an unpaid guardian, when the time close to six arrived and he became obligated because of returning a lost object, he became like a paid guardian at that hour, since the deposit was in his hand and he was then like one guarding a lost object; according to the Rambam, he is like a paid guardian because of Rav Yosef's perutah, as I wrote in my work Toras HaShelamim, question 15; see there. Therefore according to the Rambam there is at first glance reason to obligate him as a paid guardian at that time for not selling. Do not answer from what the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch, Choshen Mishpat at the end of siman 292, wrote, that if he sold the deposit by instruction of beis din he is permitted to use the money and therefore is a paid guardian for it, which implies that if he were not permitted to use the money he would not be a paid guardian. Why do we not say he is like a paid guardian because he became a guardian of a lost object at the time of sale, since it was going to be lost? That is no refutation, for after he has already sold the deposit he has fulfilled the return of the lost object in that regard, and afterward he is only over the money as he was initially over the deposit, an unpaid guardian unless he is permitted to use it. This is unlike the very hour when it is going to be lost, when he is an actual guardian of a lost object. However, there is another reason to exempt him, namely what the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch write at the end of siman 292: this applies to a deposit whose owners went overseas, but if the owner is with him in that land, he should not touch it even though it will be lost, because he caused the loss to himself and it is like knowingly abandoning it. If so, the same applies here. Although the language of the Rosh and Tur says specifically that he is with him in that city, the Sema already wrote there that city is not literal; see there. And even if you say city is literal, that is only with items going to loss, becoming wormy, or becoming chametz, where if the depositor is not in the city and would not know that his fruit is going to loss, the bailee is obligated to return his lost object. But with chametz on Pesach, since the depositor knew that his chametz was in the bailee's hand and that its time of prohibition would arrive, he should have sold it in his own place. Even if he was far from the chametz, he could have made arrangements regarding the sale, as explained in Responsa Maharash Levi, siman 12, and above in siman 448. Although the bailee could not rely on this and is nevertheless obligated to destroy it, as the Magen Avraham wrote, the depositor should have notified the bailee in writing or through an agent. If he did not sell it and did not notify the bailee, he caused his own loss and it is a knowing loss. Nevertheless, the bailee is obligated to destroy it so that the depositor should not transgress bal yera'eh. However, if the depositor was overseas in a manner that he could not notify, and even if he sold it in his own place the bailee would still have to destroy it, then the bailee is obligated to sell it because of returning a lost object. It is possible to obligate him if he did not sell it according to the Rambam, as we wrote; nevertheless it is certainly not negligence, as we proved. An administrator who did not sell chametz certainly cannot be obligated, since he is liable only for negligence, as explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 290; see there. This seems clear.
