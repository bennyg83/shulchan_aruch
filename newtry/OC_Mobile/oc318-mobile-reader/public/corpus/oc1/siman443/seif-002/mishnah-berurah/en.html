(10) He should hold it back until the fifth hour. That is to say, until its beginning; and he should not sell it earlier, lest its owner come and take it.
(11) He should sell it to a non-Jew. For this is like returning a lost object, since afterward it will be forbidden in benefit. If he is uncertain whether in the fifth hour he will perhaps find no buyers at all, it is permitted to sell it earlier; so Mekor Chaim explained the words of the Magen Avraham, and see Biur Halacha. The Magen Avraham and Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah further wrote that in a place where he can sell it to a non-Jew and the non-Jew will return it to him after Pesach, as in our customary sale of chametz, it is forbidden to sell it as an absolute sale.
(12) He is obligated to destroy it, etc. The view of the Magen Avraham is that if it was possible for him to sell it and he was lazy and did not sell it until he had to destroy it, he is afterward obligated to pay the value of the deposit to the depositor, even though he is an unpaid guardian, for this is negligence, since he should have sold it before the time of its prohibition. Chok Yaakov distinguishes in this matter between an unpaid guardian and a paid guardian, but most later authorities agree that whether he is an unpaid guardian or a paid guardian, he is exempt, for this was not included in the guardianship: he accepted upon himself only to guard the object so that it should remain in its owner's domain and that he should return it to him intact, but he did not accept upon himself to sell it. On the contrary, through this sale he removes it from its owner's domain. He must sell it only because of the mitzvah of returning a lost object, and we have not found that one who does not return a lost object is obligated to pay. However, if the chametz was pledged with him, and the fifth hour passed and he did not sell it, and afterward when the time of its prohibition arrived he was forced to burn it, in this case it stands to reason that he has lost his debt in any event, as the Magen Avraham holds. For the understanding of anyone who takes a pledge is certainly that as long as the lender does not return the pledge to him, he cannot claim his debt from him, unless the lender was forced by circumstances beyond his control in the loss of the pledge, such as if bandits stole it from him, or the like among other unavoidable circumstances. But here, where it was possible for the lender to sell it before Pesach and he did not sell it, his laziness caused the loss of the pledge, which became forbidden in benefit, and this is not an unavoidable circumstance. It is obvious that if he sold it on erev Pesach, even though he sold it cheaply, there is no claim against him, and the loss is upon the borrower, as the Taz writes. However, if the pledge is still intact, such as where the lender did not destroy it after the time of destruction and kept it until after Pesach, perhaps he can say to him, 'Here is yours before you,' even though it is now worth nothing, for this is a deficiency that is not visible, and he has not lost his debt.
(13) At the time of its prohibition. In the sixth hour. Although it is possible that the owner of the chametz sold it to a non-Jew wherever it is, through one of the methods of acquisition, nevertheless, since here there is definite chametz and a doubt whether its owner sold it to a non-Jew and a doubt whether he did not sell it, a doubt does not remove a certainty, and he is obligated to destroy it.
(14) Even if he is not liable for its responsibility. Even if he did not accept any guardianship upon himself at all, as the later authorities write. Although he himself does not transgress regarding it, nevertheless he must destroy it so that the depositor will not transgress regarding it, for all Jews are responsible for one another, as the Bach and Magen Avraham write. The view of the Gra is that he has an obligation according to the Torah to destroy it, even though the chametz is not his, since the chametz is in his house and it belongs to a Jew. This is also the view of the Tzlach and Beis Meir; see there.
