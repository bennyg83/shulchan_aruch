However, if he is in a place where he can sell it to a non-Jew whom he knows will return it after Pesach, he may not sell it to him as an absolute sale. Magen Avraham. To remove it. Although the custodian himself does not violate the prohibition of possessing the chametz, he must remove it so that the owner does not violate it. Although it is possible that the owner sold the chametz to a non-Jew in a manner that avoids the prohibition, a doubt cannot displace a certainty. Perhaps the owner did not sell it, and the custodian must therefore remove it. Bach. Magen Avraham writes that the custodian must compensate the owner for the value of the deposit, because he should have sold it before the time of prohibition. One cannot say that the owner was negligent because he should have sold it to a non-Jew in the place where he was located. Since we rule that the custodian may not rely upon that possibility and is himself obligated to sell it, he was negligent by failing to do so. If the chametz remains intact, the custodian may say, “Your property is before you.” If the chametz was held as collateral, however, it requires study whether he may say, “Your property is before you,” and the borrower must repay the debt, or whether the custodian lost his money because he was negligent. See there. Taz writes simply that the loss of the chametz falls upon the borrower. Chok Yaakov disagrees with Magen Avraham and rules that the custodian is not obligated to compensate the owner for the value of the deposit. See there and Responsa Yad Eliyahu, siman 17. Yad Aharon writes that the custodian may say, “I follow the opinion that exempts me.” Even HaOzer likewise disagrees with Magen Avraham and rules that the custodian need not compensate the owner. See the Proops edition of Magen Avraham.
