(There, seif 7 in the gloss) Anyone who is exempt from the sukkah and does not leave from there receives no reward and is only a commoner. Note: See Magen Avraham, siman 472 in the laws of Pesach, what he wrote in the name of the Taz, and that one must consider the matter, etc. In truth, it is explicit in the Mishnah regarding R. Tzadok, "bring them up to the sukkah," because he was stringent upon himself; if so, it is proven that one may be stringent upon himself. At first glance one might distinguish: where the person is in any case one who is subject to the obligation, such as R. Tzadok, who was obligated in sukkah and the exemption was only from another side, namely that this food is exempt from sukkah, since in any case the person is subject to the obligation he may be stringent upon himself. But in the Rema's case, when rain fell, he himself is exempt; therefore it is not proper to be stringent and he is called a commoner. However, one who examines the Yerushalmi in chapter 2 of Sukkah, in the Mishnah that agents of a mitzvah are exempt from sukkah, where it says that Rav Huna went to Ein Tav, etc., until he entered the sukkah of R. Yochanan the scribe, etc., should see there at length. There he was an agent of a mitzvah, and there was also water; so he was exempt and the water was exempt, and nevertheless he was stringent upon himself. It is also difficult: how do women recite blessings over positive time-bound commandments? If it is an act of commoners, how can they bless, and would this not be a blessing in vain? Perforce, one may be stringent. Perforce one must say that only when rain falls, since it is like a cup poured in his face and they have shown him from heaven that they do not want his service, he should not be stringent if they do not want him; but generally it is permitted and proper to be stringent. This is clear in my opinion. I also see proof for the words of the Magen Avraham in siman 472 mentioned above, that one should not be stringent in a matter that is permitted, from what is stated in the Yerushalmi, Sukkah 2:10: Rabban Gamliel entered and left all night. R. Eliezer, one student from R. Mana, ruled for one of the women's relatives: he left; we do not burden him to return. Did he not hear that Rabban Gamliel entered and left, that R. Eliezer entered and left all night? End quote. The Korban HaEdah explained there that the student who ruled that we do not burden him did not know of the incident of Rabban Gamliel, etc. At first glance this is difficult: from where do we know he did not know? Perhaps he knew, but Rabban Gamliel and R. Eliezer were stringent upon themselves. We find similarly in the Mishnah there above, in the incident where they brought Rabban Gamliel a bucket of water and two dates and he said, "bring them up to the sukkah"; the Ran wrote that this teaches that if one wishes to be stringent upon himself he may be stringent and there is no arrogance; see there. Thus Rabban Gamliel was stringent upon himself. If so, perhaps here too he was stringent upon himself. Perforce, if one is exempt he may not be stringent. The case of bringing a bucket of water up to the sukkah is when the person is obligated and subject to sukkah, and the impediment is only from the item, that this item has no sukkah obligation; in such a case he may be stringent, for it is as though he says: for me this is considered like bread and like something obligated in sukkah, and for stringency his considering it significant is effective, and he is not called a commoner. But when rain falls, he himself is exempt from sukkah; and likewise there with reclining, he himself is totally exempt. In that case, considering it significant is irrelevant, and therefore he is called a commoner. This is well proven from Rabban Gamliel and R. Eliezer entering and leaving all night. This is a correct principle in my humble opinion; examine it. The Yerushalmi did not bring the baraita cited in our Talmud, which states explicitly that if he was sleeping, we do not burden him to go up. It appears that from this there is proof for the Magen Avraham's view, that specifically if he had already lain down, but otherwise he is obligated to go up. Therefore it is well: the baraita speaks where he had already lain down and slept, and therefore we do not burden him; but the incident of Rabban Gamliel and R. Eliezer was that they had not yet lain down, but only went out, and therefore they entered and left all night. That student, however, ruled even before he had lain down that we do not burden him; therefore it said: he had not heard that Rabban Gamliel entered and left all night. If so, this law of the Magen Avraham is explicit in the Yerushalmi; examine it. Now we learned in tractate Yoma, chapter 8: the large date they spoke of is more than an egg's bulk, etc.; see there to the end. From this sugya one may learn what I was uncertain about: since for sukkah we require a measure, and with less than the measure one does not recite "to sit in the sukkah," if he intends to eat the measure but does so with a delay longer than the time of eating a peras, and all the more so if he delayed between one eating and the next the time of eating a peras, is he obligated to recite "to sit in the sukkah"? All the more so if he changed his mind: at first he intended to eat less than the measure and did not recite "to sit in the sukkah," and afterward changed his mind to eat more up to the measure. If it is within the time of eating a peras, he certainly must recite "to sit in the sukkah"; but if he did not delay the time of eating a peras, is he obligated or not? At first glance it would appear simple that whenever he delayed the time of eating a peras, it is not considered one eating, and certainly he does not bless. But from the Talmud the opposite appears proven. It is difficult: what does the Talmud ask according to the one who says a date is more than an egg's bulk, from what is stated regarding Rabban Gamliel, that it was not because the law was so but because he wanted to be stringent upon himself? What is the question? Say that the intent is this: in Pesachim, chapter 3, we say that our women bake kav by kav for Pesach, and Rav Yosef said to him: What is your intent, stringency? It is a stringency that leads to leniency, for it exempts it from challah, etc. If so, it implies that one may not make devices to exempt oneself from a mitzvah. In Chullin, chapter Kisui HaDam, we say the opposite: what should he do? He should slaughter by tearing or uprooting in order to exempt himself from covering, which proves that one may make devices to exempt oneself from a mitzvah. So too in Yoreh De'ah siman 25; I wrote elsewhere to resolve this. In any event, it is proven that there is a concept that one should not make devices to exempt himself from a mitzvah; so too in Menachot, chapter 3, regarding a linen garment in summer: what will become of tzitzit? Sometimes the opposite is true, that one may use a stratagem to exempt himself. If so, perhaps the Mishnah's intent is this: Rabban Gamliel certainly could have delayed between one eating and the next the time of eating a peras and thereby not need a sukkah. This is what it means, "not because the law was so": not that it is forbidden to use a device to remove oneself from a mitzvah, for by law he could use a device to exempt himself, but he wanted to be stringent upon himself. What then is the Talmud's question? There is an even greater difficulty: who says that what does not combine outside the time of eating a peras applies to a prohibited matter and the like, where there is no blessing to combine it and therefore it combines only within the time of eating a peras; but for a matter over which one recites a blessing, since he eats everything on the basis of the first blessing, perhaps the blessing combines it to be the manner of eating. With this it fits that in siman 475 the Mechaber wrote that one should not delay between one eating and another the time of eating a peras, while in the laws of Yom Kippur he wrote that from the beginning of eating until the end of eating should not be the time of eating a peras. Since on Yom Kippur there is no blessing, for it is prohibited eating, it must not be from the beginning of eating until the end the time of eating a peras. But with matzah, where there is a blessing, the concern is only that he not delay between one eating and the next the time of eating a peras. Examine this. Yet one may say that although the blessing combines it, blessings are rabbinic, and from the Torah there is no combining; therefore it is forbidden to interrupt in it. This is true with matzah, for otherwise from the Torah he has not fulfilled. But with sukkah it is the opposite: although from the Torah it is not one eating, rabbinically it is one eating, and therefore he is obligated in sukkah rabbinically. According to this, one could still say that Rabban Gamliel could have eaten less than the measure each time, blessed each time anew, and changed his mind only once, and if he delayed the time of eating a peras he would be exempt from sukkah. But if so, he would cause an unnecessary blessing, which is forbidden to cause, as the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 215; therefore he was forced to eat together. Yet one may say: if he goes to the sukkah he will also become obligated to bless over the sukkah, which is also an unnecessary blessing, since he could eat less than the measure and be exempt without it. If so, either way he causes an unnecessary blessing; he could have eaten less than the measure. This is what it means that they brought them up to the sukkah and did not eat less than the measure each time: not because the law was so, because causing an unnecessary blessing is forbidden, for this too would be an unnecessary blessing if they brought them up to the sukkah when it was possible to be exempt without it; rather, he wanted to be stringent upon himself. What then is the Talmud's question? Perforce, it is proven that sukkah does not depend on delay; rather, whenever he eats the measure, even with a great interruption, he is obligated in sukkah. Examine carefully.
(There, in the gloss) And when he leaves the sukkah because of rain, he should not kick and leave, but should leave submissively, like a servant who pours a cup for his master and his master pours it in his face. Note: In the Talmud this was a question: who poured for whom? It resolves there: like a servant who poured a cup for his master, and his master poured a pitcher in his face. At first glance it would appear that there is a practical legal difference from this question, whether he may be stringent upon himself or not. If we say it is like the servant poured the cup in his master's face, then although by law he is exempt because he is distressed, nevertheless if he wants to be stringent upon himself and reflect in repentance, he may sit in the sukkah. But according to the wording that the master poured a pitcher in the servant's face, since they show him that they do not want his service, it is not proper to serve against the master's will, and it is forbidden to be stringent upon oneself. However, according to those who hold that one who is exempt and nevertheless performs is called a commoner, one may say there is another practical difference: if rain fell on the first night. According to the view that it is as though the servant poured in his master's face, then on the first night, when he is specifically obligated in sukkah, even in rain he must eat in the sukkah, for since one who is distressed is obligated then, he is obligated even in rain. But according to the wording that the master poured in his face, then they are showing him that they do not want his service, and it is not proper to serve against the master's will; even on the first night he is exempt from sukkah. And the practical difference we say, that on the first night he is obligated in sukkah, will be explained according to the other commentators, that on other Yom Tov days there is no obligation to eat bread, or that the practical difference is that an olive's bulk suffices. Those who hold that the practical difference is when rain fell will hold that one may be stringent upon himself in other mitzvot, and we do not say that one who is exempt and performs is called a commoner. Therefore the practical difference from this is that here he certainly may not be stringent, since it is as though his master poured a pitcher in his face. There are practical differences for both sides. So it appeared at first glance to say, but it still requires examination. Perhaps Hashem will grant me merit at another time to explain this further; examine carefully.
