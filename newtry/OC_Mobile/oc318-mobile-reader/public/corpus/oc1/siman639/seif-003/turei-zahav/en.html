On the first night of Yom Tov it is obligatory. For R. Yochanan said in the name of R. Shimon ben Yehotzadak: "the fifteenth" is stated here, and "the fifteenth" is stated regarding the festival of matzah. Just as there the first night is obligatory and from then onward optional, so too here the first night is obligatory and from then onward optional. The Semag and Rosh explained that this refers to when rain fell, for otherwise one is obligated on Yom Tov to eat bread, and automatically obligated in sukkah even from then onward. The Semak and Or Zarua prove from this that there is no obligation on Yom Tov to eat bread, since it says that from then onward it is optional, and they do not explain that it refers to when rain fell. In Piskei Maharai, siman 160, he ruled that on the second night of Sukkot one need not eat an olive's bulk in the sukkah if rain fell, for we rely on the Semak and Or Zarua and not on the Ashiri, who holds that for us the second Yom Tov is like the first; rather, we are expert in the fixing of the month, etc. The student of Maharai concludes there: However, I heard that he expounded in the synagogue that on the first and second nights one must eat an olive's bulk in the sukkah if rain fell.
Even if he ate an olive's bulk. The Tur wrote: although the measure of eating without a sukkah is an egg's bulk, the first night is different, for it is more obligatory, and even if he wishes to eat only an olive's bulk, it is forbidden to eat it outside the sukkah; therefore he also fulfills the sukkah obligation with it. End quote. Thus, in the Tur's view, the first night is more stringent than all the days of Sukkot also in this respect, that even an olive's bulk, which is less than an egg's bulk, requires a sukkah. The Beit Yosef objected: My heart hesitates, for he says "even if he wishes"; is it dependent on his wishing? He should have said "he cannot" rather than "he does not wish." Further, how does he know this very point, that the first night differs from other nights? He should rather have stated the reason that since it is derived from the festival of matzah, its measure is an olive's bulk like Pesach. End quote. I did not understand the first objection at all, for he certainly said well "if he wishes," meaning that there is a stringency on the first night, that even an olive's bulk requires a sukkah. The second objection is not an objection, for our teacher the Tur learns it from what the Rosh brings from the Yerushalmi: The colleagues asked: perhaps just as the festival of matzah requires that one enter the mitzvah while hungry, so too sukkah? R. Zeira asked: perhaps just as there, only until he eats an olive's bulk of grain? He explains that R. Zeira was uncertain whether the obligation of sukkah on the first night extends to an olive's bulk and exempts only less than an olive's bulk, and is more stringent than other nights. We rule stringently that an olive's bulk requires a sukkah. From this our teacher learned that on the first night an olive's bulk requires a sukkah even if rain fell, and that is why an olive's bulk suffices.
And he should not eat at night, etc. This too we learn from the festival of matzah, for it is written, "in the evening you shall eat matzot." Likewise, what he writes afterward, before midnight, is also learned from matzah on Pesach, for that is its law. So too what he writes afterward, that one should not eat during the day from midday, etc. What he concludes, "like the eating of matzah," refers to everything he wrote before this.
