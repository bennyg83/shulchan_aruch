The custom is that we do not recite the blessing, etc. By the law, we rule like Rabbah bar bar Chanah in the name of R. Yochanan, that a sukkah all seven days requires a blessing. The Rif, the Rosh, and the Tur wrote that one should bless each time he enters it. So too Tosafot in chapter Lulav VeAravah wrote that whenever one enters it to eat, drink, and sleep, even ten times in a day, he blesses over each time, just as with tefillin whenever he puts them on. The reason with sukkah is that if a meal happens to come to him, he cannot eat it outside the sukkah, even though he already ate that day in the sukkah. End quote. The Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Maggid: I saw written that this applies specifically when he left in a complete departure to attend to his affairs and not to return immediately, but if he left only to speak with his fellow or to bring something to the sukkah for that moment, it is not considered a departure to obligate him in another blessing when he returns; and this is a correct matter. End quote. The Tur concluded: Rabbeinu Tam explained that since the main fixed act that a person does inside the sukkah is eating, he blesses over the eating and exempts all things, even sleeping, etc.; and our rabbis established the practice like Rabbeinu Tam. In Tosafot at the beginning of Berachot 11, Tosafot wrote: If you ask, how is sukkah different, where one must bless over each and every meal "to sit in the sukkah," whereas for the blessing over Torah one time suffices? One may answer that Torah is different, for he does not remove his mind from it, since every hour a person is obligated to learn, as it is written, "you shall meditate on it day and night," and it is like sitting all day without interruption; but eating in the sukkah has a fixed time. End quote. This implies that even if he intended today to eat twice in the sukkah, he must bless a second time, for otherwise what was Tosafot's question? With Torah he certainly intends to learn afterward. Rather, it is clear that with sukkah he must bless again in every case, and because of this they had to distinguish between them. It is difficult for me: how is this different from a perfume shop, where in siman 217 it is stated that one who enters and exits recites the fragrance blessing each time? The Tur wrote there in the name of the Ramah that this is specifically when he did not intend to return, but if he intended to return he does not bless, for he did not divert his mind. There we are not particular whether he leaves for an hour or not, but even for a long time, as long as he intends to return that same day; whereas here we require that he left only to speak with his fellow. And according to Rabbeinu Tam, who ruled that the matter depends on eating, even if he intended to return and eat afterward he must bless a second time. How is this different from the fragrance blessing? Why should we not say that just as the fragrance blessing helps for what he will smell all day, even if he left in between for many hours, so too it should help with sukkah? It appears that the answer is that specifically with sukkah there is a fixed time, namely at the time of eating, and afterward he has permission to leave. Regarding this we say that whenever he delayed for a long time, according to the essential law, between departure and entry, and according to Rabbeinu Tam even if he did not go out of the sukkah but immediately after reciting Birkat HaMazon finished the first meal, he must bless again over the second meal, even if he originally intended it. This is like a night intervening, where it is obvious that his intention today does not help for what he will eat tomorrow, for tomorrow is another matter; so too on the same day after completing the first eating. This is unlike the fragrance blessing, where there is no completion of an act and removal of the first act; if he did not leave the shop all day and all night until tomorrow, he would not need to bless again. Therefore his intent to return that day is effective, as appears to my humble opinion. And it is written in the Levush regarding those who establish themselves to drink after Birkat HaMazon, and after drinking for some time they return and eat a second meal without interruption, and recite "to sit in the sukkah" a second time: It seems to me that they are making a complete error, for they did not divert their minds from sitting in the sukkah, over which they already blessed at the first meal. What they practiced, to fix the blessing "to sit in the sukkah" over eating, is because they divert their minds from eating, leave the sukkah several times, and return to it; but here, where they sat inside it all day, why should they bless over it another time? End quote. I say that the practice is correct, and I am very astonished at the author of the Levush, who ruled from his own reasoning and did not seek to examine the source of the law at all. First, the Rosh wrote in chapter Lulav VeAravah: "to sit in the sukkah" means to remain in the sukkah, and for this reason he disagrees with the Rambam, who wrote that one should recite kiddush standing, etc. If so, there is no practical difference whether they remained there or left and returned, for the main obligation is because of the fixedness of eating. Since the first fixed eating ended, he must bless again over the second fixed eating, since if he wanted to go outside the sukkah for that eating it would be forbidden to him, and the obligation of sitting in the sukkah applies to him then as well; he must bless over the second obligation, and the first blessing already ended with Birkat HaMazon. How is this different from the blessing of hamotzi, where he must bless a second time and the first blessing before the first meal does not help? The Levush wrote to distinguish that there they diverted their minds from eating, but why should he bless "to sit in the sukkah," since he did not divert his mind from it? This is nothing. Even if you say that drinking requires a sukkah, nevertheless it is subordinate to eating, as the Rosh wrote in chapter Lulav VeAravah: Rabbeinu Tam says that since the primary fixed act that a person does in the sukkah is eating, the other strolling and sleeping that he does in the sukkah are subordinate to eating and it exempts them. End quote. Now it is a fortiori: strolling and sleeping are explicit in the Gemara as obligations of sukkah, and nevertheless we consider them subordinate to eating; all the more so drinking, which is not mentioned in the Gemara, and on the contrary, it is explicit in the decisors we mentioned above that it does not require a sukkah. Although we are stringent and require a sukkah for them, nevertheless it is obvious that they are subordinate to eating. By contrast, eating in a different meal is certainly and obviously not considered subordinate to the first. Furthermore, for drinking in between we do not require a sukkah blessing even if he went out to the market and returned to drink, because eating exempts it; but for eating, it is clear that if he goes out and returns and enters to eat, he requires a sukkah blessing. If so, how can you say that there is no obligation on the one who returns to eat because he already occupied himself with drinking in between? Drinking has no significance at all in relation to eating. One should not err from the language of Tosafot that I cited, where they wrote: But with sukkah, whenever he enters it in order to eat, drink, and sleep, even ten times, etc.; and mistakenly say that this is only when he enters from outside in order to eat, etc., but not when he was already there. That is complete error. Did Tosafot speak of one who sits and drinks in between, and who forced them into that case? Rather, plainly they stated the normal manner: he enters to eat, because a person does not sit in the sukkah from one eating to the next. On the contrary, they wrote afterward that if a meal happens to come to him, he cannot eat outside the sukkah, even though he already ate that day in the sukkah; and likewise tefillin all day, etc. End quote. This implies even if he did not leave in between, for they wrote without qualification. Further, what special force is there in drinking in between? If he sits idle in between, he is also engaged in sukkah, for we rule that strolling also requires a sukkah. If so, how could he bless afterward over the eating? Rather, eating is a separate matter, as appears to me clearly and simply, and many practice so, and the law is with them. I found that my father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote so plainly. I also saw fit to mention a simple matter: everything that one drinks before eating, such as spirits in a fixed manner, does not require a sukkah blessing, for the eating over which he will bless afterward exempts even what preceded it. The proof is from what the Rosh wrote in chapter Lulav VeAravah: since the mitzvah of sukkah is eating and strolling, if he blesses after he has sat, it is proper and is called "before performing it." He brings proof from tzitzit, where we say that after one has wrapped himself he says "to wrap in tzitzit"; thus, although he has already wrapped himself, he blesses because the mitzvah continues as long as he is wrapped and standing. So too with sukkah, etc. End quote. Thus even the sitting, strolling, and sleeping before eating are exempted by the force of the eating afterward; all the more so drinking. All this appears simple in my humble opinion, and one who wishes to bless over drinking must be concerned for a blessing in vain, without doubt. It seems to me that one who intends not to eat bread all day, and certainly one who fasts a dream-fast on Sukkot, must bless over the sukkah each time he enters it that day to stroll, since on that day there is no eating of bread. We return to the Talmudic law, that one blesses each time he enters to sit there, for the main reason is that eating bread is primary and exempts what is subordinate.
