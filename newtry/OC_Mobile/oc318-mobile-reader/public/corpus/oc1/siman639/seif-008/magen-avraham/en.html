At the time of eating. Hagahos Semak writes: “Those who recite the blessing but do not eat there, and merely sit there for an hour, are mistaken. The requirement is to dwell as one ordinarily resides, and it is close to being a blessing in vain.” See what I wrote in seif 2. Rosh and Tur at the end of siman 643 imply that even one who enters to sit in the sukkah before eating does not recite the blessing. It is nevertheless proper to eat a little in the morning and recite the blessing, thereby covering everything else. Without eating, he should not recite the blessing, as written above, contrary to Shelah. Shelah also writes that if he recited the blessing in the morning, he is exempt until the following morning. This requires study. Since he sleeps in the sukkah, why should the night constitute an interruption? A clear proof is found in the chapter HaTecheles: according to the opinion that nighttime is a time for tzitzis, one who slept in his tallis need not recite a new blessing in the morning. This is certainly so according to the ruling at the end of siman 8 that even according to our practice he need not recite a new blessing. If Shelah means that after leaving the synagogue in the morning he must recite a blessing because the synagogue constitutes an interruption, then going to the synagogue at night should likewise constitute an interruption. The principal ruling appears to be that going to the synagogue constitutes an interruption, as stated in siman 8 and by Rambam. This applies specifically when he leaves completely to attend to his affairs and does not intend to return immediately. If he leaves merely to speak with his fellow or to bring something promptly to the sukkah, he need not recite another blessing upon returning. The same applies when he leaves to relieve himself. Even if he recited Birkas HaMazon, when he eats again he need not repeat “leishev basukkah,” since he did not divert his attention from the sukkah, as Levush and Shelah write. Bach at the end of siman 643 writes that Maharil and other later authorities imply that when he returns and eats he recites the blessing again. Bach explains that a person ordinarily intends his blessing to cover him only until the next time he returns and eats, at which point he intends to recite another blessing. His words are astonishing. Could one wearing tzitzis intend that his blessing cover him only until midday and then recite another blessing at midday? Certainly not. I have also found no such implication in the later authorities. One should therefore not recite the blessing again, because of the concern for a blessing in vain. Tosafos, Berachos 11b, appears likewise. If one goes during his meal to his fellow’s sukkah, it requires study whether he must recite another blessing. At first glance the gloss to siman 8, seif 12, implies that he must. Even according to what is written there that he need not recite another blessing, that is because at the time of the first blessing he had the other tallis in mind and there was no interruption in between. Here, however, walking to the other sukkah constitutes an interruption, as explained there. It therefore appears to me that he must recite “leishev basukkah” again. See siman 47, seif 12, and this is also written in the name of Beis Hillel. If one forgot to recite “leishev basukkah” before eating, it appears to me that he should recite it afterward over what he still intends to eat. Even if he has already eaten, he may still recite it, because sitting itself is part of the mitzvah. See siman 621, seif 5, and siman 432, seif 1. The reason no blessing is recited over matzah throughout all seven days is that eating matzah is not itself a mitzvah; one merely refrains from eating chametz. Sukkah is different, as Maharil writes. See Levush and Mateh Moshe.
