There, seif 5. "If rain fell, he enters the house, etc." NB: I was asked about a time when rain is falling, but in one sukkah rain does not fall into it, whether one should recite the beracha of leishev basukkah or not. I answered that one should not recite the beracha. There is proof from what the poskim wrote, that even if he has no porridge before him he is exempt. Since the Sages gave the measure "from when the porridge would spoil," this implies that this is the reason for the exemption; nevertheless, even where he has no porridge before him he is exempt, because if porridge were before him he would be exempt, and also because another person who has porridge is exempt. So too here. At first glance one could bring proof to the contrary from what the Magen Avraham wrote at the end of siman 629, that if rain drips from the s'chach one should tie up a sheet, but should not recite the beracha because of the rule that doubtful berachot are treated leniently, since some hold that it is forbidden to spread a sheet under the s'chach. This implies that were it not for that doubt, he would recite the beracha because rain is not falling into his sukkah. But this is no proof, for one may say that he speaks where the rain has already stopped and no rain is falling from the sky anymore. If so, it is a time of obligation in sukkah, only he could not eat because the s'chach was dripping and he was distressed; if he made a remedy so it would not drip, he is obligated to recite the beracha. Therefore the Magen Avraham exempted him only for another reason. But if rain is falling from the sky in a manner that is a time of exemption, then even one into whose sukkah the rain does not fall is exempt from reciting the beracha, for it is a time of exemption. On the contrary, from the above sugya our view is proven; otherwise, according to those in siman 629 who permit spreading a sheet under the sukkah, why do the Gemara and poskim state that when rain falls one may leave? Why leave? Let him spread a sheet over himself so that the rain not fall. It is forced to say they speak where he has no sheet, but it is difficult that neither the Gemara nor all the poskim mention such advice. Perforce, this does not help, and there is no fulfillment of the mitzvah of sukkah in it. Yet they hold that the sheet does not nullify the s'chach. Perforce, because it is a time when rain is falling, the fact that the rain does not reach him does not help. One may explain the reason: the Levush and the Bach hold that where rain cannot fall into it, it is invalid. Even according to those who validate it, their reason appears to be based on the rule that anything fit for mixing does not require actual mixing; since it is possible to remove the extra covering and the rain would fall into it while there would still remain more shade than sun, it is fit for mixing, and lack of actual mixing does not prevent validity. But if rain is actually falling from the sky and here it cannot fall in, then either way he does not fulfill sukkah: if we follow the present state, it is invalid according to the Levush and the Bach; and if according to those who validate it because it is fit for mixing, then when he removes the extra covering rain will fall and he will be exempt because of the rain. Therefore either way one should not recite leishev basukkah. Especially since in any event the above-mentioned view is that one should not recite a beracha when rain cannot fall into it; certainly in such a case one should not recite the beracha. This is clear and correct in my opinion. See my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5607, Hilchot Sukkah siman 629, what I wrote in a responsum to the holy community of Zharovna. Examine very carefully.
There, seif 5, in the gloss. "All this is specifically on the other days or nights of Sukkot, but on the first night he must eat in the sukkah even if rain is falling, etc." NB: From the words of the Taz it is clear that the custom is to recite leishev basukkah even when rain is falling, for we hold like the Semag, etc. They showed me in the later authorities in the name of the Peri Megadim that one should not recite the beracha because it is a doubtful beracha. In my humble opinion this does not appear correct. Besides the fact that the custom is to recite the beracha on the first night, and the language of the Taz indicates that this was the practice, the language of the Rama also indicates it, for he wrote plainly that one is obligated to eat, implying according to the law, with a beracha; otherwise he should have explained. Beyond this, it appears that this is proper and correct, and there is no genuine doubtful dispute here. Even those who answer Tosafot's question in another way and not because of rain nevertheless agree to the law here. For one must understand: from where does the Mishnah know that when rain falls or one is distressed he is exempt from sukkah? One cannot say it is from "you shall dwell" in the manner of dwelling, because a person's way is to leave his house in such a case; if so, the Mishnah should have made the measure depend on whether, if this happened in his house, he would leave. Why does it measure by the point at which the porridge would spoil? And even if it is from "you shall dwell" in the manner of dwelling, one may say that this is the reason of the verse: since eating on the other days is optional, if he wished he would not eat and would have no obligation of sukkah; the Torah decreed only that if he wants to eat or sleep, then he must do so in a sukkah. Therefore, if the eating or sleeping is pleasurable, it is considered eating for benefit, and where one wishes to benefit he needs a sukkah. But when he is distressed or rain falls to the point that the porridge would spoil, this eating or sleeping is not pleasure for him but distress; wherever it is distress, it is not considered eating or sleeping, and it is as though he did not eat or sleep, so he is exempt from sukkah. This is well for the other days, where eating is optional. But on the first night, when eating is obligatory, the matter does not depend on his pleasure at all; even if he does not enjoy it he is obligated in sukkah, for even if he has no appetite he must eat in the sukkah. What difference does it make that he is distressed by this eating? In the end he is obligated in sukkah. One may also say that since it is obligatory for him to eat, the mitzvah itself is pleasure to him, for he fulfills the mitzvah, and therefore he is obligated in sukkah. Although mitzvot were not given for benefit, where bodily benefit accompanies the benefit of the mitzvah, we say that mitzvot are given for benefit; at least his distress is offset by the pleasure of fulfilling the mitzvah, and he is not considered distressed. The reason Tosafot did not answer their question this way is that, on the contrary, their difficulty was that for rain one should not need the gezerah shavah, since in any case he is obligated because of Yom Tov and therefore requires sukkah, as this eating is not dependent on pleasure but is obligatory. The gezerah shavah would not be needed for that. But in the law itself they agree that even when rain falls, he is obligated in sukkah, and there is no doubtful beracha. Another reason appears: although the Rama wrote in the name of Hagahot Maimoniyot that one who is exempt and acts nevertheless is called a common person, this is only on the other days. We see that there are several views, and the custom is that women recite berachot on positive time-bound mitzvot. Why did they not write to prevent them because they are common people? Everyone agrees that if they perform the mitzvah without a beracha we do not protest. Why do we not say it is the act of common people? Perforce, with other positive time-bound mitzvot, since for men they are obligatory, one who is not obligated but performs them is not called a common person. But with sukkah, since even for every person without rain it is not an absolute obligation but optional, for if he wishes he need not eat, therefore when rain falls, if he performs it he is a common person. If so, on the first night, where without rain there is an obligation to eat in the sukkah, even where rain falls he is no worse than women, and he may fulfill it as one who is not commanded; at any rate he is not called a common person. If so, he may recite the beracha just as women recite berachot on positive time-bound mitzvot, and there is no beracha levatalah. Thus there is a double doubt toward leniency: perhaps the halacha follows those who say the gezerah shavah teaches the obligation of sukkah when rain falls, and if not, perhaps the halacha follows those who say women may recite berachot on positive time-bound mitzvot, and this case is similar. Therefore the main practice is like the custom of Israel and like the Taz, to recite the beracha on the sukkah on the first night. So we acted in practice. May our words be accepted before the Master of all, amen. Examine carefully.
