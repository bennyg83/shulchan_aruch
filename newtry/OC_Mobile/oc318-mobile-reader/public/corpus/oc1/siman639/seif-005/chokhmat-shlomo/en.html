(There, seif 5) If rain fell, he enters the house, etc. Note: I was asked about a time when rain is falling, but in one sukkah no rain falls into it: should one recite the blessing "to sit in the sukkah" or not? I answered that one should not recite the blessing. The proof is from what the decisors wrote, that even if there is no congealed dish before him he is also exempt. Since the sages gave the measure as "from when the congealed dish would spoil," this implies that this is the reason for the exemption; nevertheless, even if no congealed dish is before him he is exempt. This is because if such a dish were before him he would be exempt, and because another person has such a dish and is exempt, he too is exempt. So too here. At first glance one might bring proof to the opposite from what the Magen Avraham wrote at the end of siman 629, that if rain drips from the roofing one should tie up a sheet, but should not recite the blessing because of the rule that doubtful blessings are treated leniently; this implies that were it not for that doubt, he would bless because no rain is falling into his sukkah. But this is no proof, for one can say that he speaks where the rain has already stopped and rain is no longer falling from heaven. If so, the time is one of sukkah obligation, except that he could not eat because the roofing drips and one who is distressed is exempt; if he made a device so that it will not drip, he is obligated to bless. Therefore the Magen Avraham exempted him only for another reason. But if rain is falling from heaven in a manner that is an exempt time, then even one into whose sukkah the rain does not fall is exempt from blessing, for it is an exempt time. On the contrary, from the above sugya our words are proven: otherwise, according to those in siman 629 who hold that it is permitted to spread a sheet under the sukkah roofing, why is it explained in the Talmud and decisors that when rain falls one may leave? Why should he leave? Let him spread a sheet over himself so the rain will not fall. It is forced to say that they speak where he has no sheet, for the Talmud and all the decisors would not have failed to give this counsel. Rather, perforce, this does not help, and there is no fulfillment of the mitzvah of sukkah in this. Yet they hold that the sheet does not invalidate the roofing. Perforce, because it is a time when rain is falling, it does not help that the rain does not reach him. One can explain the reason: the Levush and the Bach hold that if rain cannot fall into it, it is invalid. If so, even those who validate it appear to do so because whatever is fit for mixing is not held back by the absence of actual mixing. Since it is possible to remove the roofing and have the rain fall into it, and nevertheless its shade would remain greater than its sun, it is fit for mixing, and the lack of mixing does not prevent it. But if rain is actually falling from heaven, and in this sukkah the rain cannot fall, either way he does not fulfill sukkah: if we follow its present state, it is invalid according to the Levush and Bach; and if we follow those who validate because it is fit for mixing, then when the extra covering is removed rain will fall and he is exempt because of the rain. Therefore either way one should not recite "to sit in the sukkah." Especially since, even without this, according to the above view one does not bless when rain cannot fall into it; therefore in such a case one certainly should not bless. Examine carefully, for this is clear and correct in my opinion. See my work on Orach Chayim from the year 607, in the laws of sukkah, siman 629, what I wrote in a responsum to the holy community of Zravna, and examine well.
(There, seif 5 in the gloss) All this applies specifically on the other days or nights of Sukkot, but on the first night one must eat in the sukkah even if rain is falling, etc. Note: From the words of the Taz it is clear that the custom is to recite "to sit in the sukkah" even when rain fell, for we rule like the Semag, etc. They showed me later authorities in the name of the Peri Megadim, that one should not bless because it is a doubtful blessing. In my humble opinion this does not appear correct. Besides the fact that the custom is to bless on the first night, the language of the Taz indicates that this is the practice, and the language of the Rema also indicates it, for he wrote simply that one is obligated to eat, implying according to its law, with a blessing; otherwise he should have explained. Beyond this, it appears that this is proper and correct, and there is no doubtful dispute here at all. Even those who answer Tosafot's question differently, and not because of rain, nevertheless appear to agree to the law in this case. For one must understand: from where does the Mishnah know that when rain falls or one is distressed he is exempt from sukkah? One cannot say it is from "you shall dwell" in the manner that you live, because in a dwelling one also leaves in such circumstances. If so, the Mishnah should have made the measure depend on this: if such a thing happened in his house, would he leave? Why did it measure from when the congealed dish spoils, and why did it make it depend on that? Even if it is because of "you shall dwell" in the manner that you live, one may say that the reason of the verse is also this: since eating on the other days is optional, if he wished he would not eat and would not be obligated in sukkah; the Torah decreed only that if he wants to eat or sleep, then he must do so in the sukkah. This is understandable when the eating or sleeping is pleasurable for him, for then the eating is considered benefit, and where he wishes to benefit we require sukkah. But when he is distressed, or when rain fell to the measure that the congealed dish would spoil, this eating or sleeping is not pleasure for him but distress; wherever it is distress, it is not considered eating or sleeping, and it is as though he did not eat or sleep, so he is automatically exempt from sukkah. This works for the other days, which are optional. But on the first night, when eating is obligatory, the matter does not depend on his enjoyment at all; even if he does not enjoy it, he is obligated in sukkah, just as one who has no appetite for eating is still obligated to eat in the sukkah. Therefore what does it matter that he is distressed by this eating? In the end he is obligated in sukkah. One can also say that since it is an obligation upon him to eat, the mitzvah itself is a benefit to him, because he fulfills the mitzvah; therefore he is obligated in sukkah. Although mitzvot were not given for benefit, where bodily benefit accompanies the benefit of a mitzvah, we say mitzvot were given for benefit; in any event, his distress is offset by the pleasure of fulfilling the mitzvah, and he is not considered distressed. Why did they not answer Tosafot's question this way? On the contrary, their difficulty was that because of rain the verbal analogy should not have been needed, since even without it he is obligated because of Yom Tov, and automatically requires a sukkah. Since this eating is considered distress and also does not depend on his enjoyment, but is an obligation upon him, he is automatically obligated in sukkah, and we do not need the verbal analogy for this. But regarding the law, they agree that even if rain fell he is obligated in sukkah, and there is no doubtful blessing. Another reason appears: although the Rema wrote in the name of Hagahot Maimoni that one who is exempt and does it is called a commoner, this appears to apply only on the other days. We see that there are several opinions, and so is the practice, that women recite blessings over positive time-bound commandments. Why did they not write to prevent them because they are commoners, and that everyone agrees they may perform them without a blessing and we do not protest? Why do we not say this is an act of commoners? Perforce, with other positive time-bound commandments, since they are at least obligatory for men, even one who is not obligated is not called a commoner if he performs them. But with sukkah, even for every person without rain it is not an obligation but optional, for if he wants he does not eat; and since it is only optional, when rain falls too, if he does so he is a commoner. If so, on the first night, where in a place without rain there is an obligation to eat in the sukkah, then even where rain is falling it is no worse than women; he can perform it as one who is not commanded, and in any event is not called a commoner. If so, he can also bless, just as women bless over positive time-bound commandments, and there is no blessing in vain. Thus there is again a double doubt to be lenient: perhaps the law follows those who hold that the verbal analogy applies to the obligation of sukkah when rain fell; and even if the law follows the other decisors, perhaps the halachah follows those who hold that women may bless over positive time-bound commandments, and the same applies here. Therefore the primary law is like the custom of Israel and like the view of the Turei Zahav, to bless over the sukkah on the first night in this case. So we acted in practice. May our words be accepted before the Master of all, amen. Examine carefully.
