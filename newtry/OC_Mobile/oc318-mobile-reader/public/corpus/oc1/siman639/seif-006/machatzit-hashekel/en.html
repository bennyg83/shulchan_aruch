(Small paragraph 6) And even if he established, etc. From what the Rosh wrote regarding the words of Maharam of Rothenburg, who was careful not to eat fruit outside the sukkah, because he held it proper to be stringent according to that formulation in Yoma 79, that fruit requires a sukkah. About this the Rosh wrote: however, it appears that it is proven there in Yoma, etc.
And so we rule, meaning according to the one who says fruit does not require a sukkah. For otherwise it would be equal to bread. Meaning: if "established" that the Rosh wrote means that he ate the measure of fixed eating, namely more than an egg's bulk, even if he did not establish himself over it, then according to the one who says fruit requires a sukkah, fruit would be equal to bread. If so, why did the Rosh need to bring proof from tractate Yoma that even according to the one who says fruit requires a sukkah, we require fixedness, meaning that he eat more than an egg's bulk? That is obvious; would fruit, according to the one who says it requires a sukkah, be better and stricter than bread?
From what we say in Yoma, etc. I will briefly set out the words of the Talmud there that are needed for this paragraph. It concludes there that the measure of eating for liability on Yom Kippur is a large date with its pit. They said there that this is more than an egg's bulk, and it objects from the teaching: one may eat temporary eating outside the sukkah; and there was an incident where they brought R. Yochanan ben Zakkai a cooked dish to taste, and Rabban Gamliel two dates, and they said: bring them up to the sukkah, not because the halachah is so, but because they wanted to be stringent upon themselves. The initial assumption was that the reason it was only a stringency was that it was less than the measure; and the measure of fixed eating to obligate sukkah is slightly more than an egg's bulk. If so, Rabban Gamliel, who ate two dates without pits and said that eating them in the sukkah was only a stringency, must have eaten less than more than an egg's bulk.
If so, how is it possible that one date with its pit is more than an egg's bulk? Rava answers: that which it says that two dates are exempt from sukkah by law is not because the measure is lacking, but because they are fruit, and fruit does not require sukkah. It objects from a baraita: Rabbi said, when we studied Torah before R. Elazar ben Shammua, they brought before us figs and grapes, and we ate them as temporary eating outside the sukkah. The Talmud infers: temporary eating, yes; fixed eating, no; apparently fruit requires sukkah. It answers; see there. After this it concludes: let us say this supports the view that fruit does not require sukkah, for we learned that R. Eliezer says one must eat fourteen meals in the sukkah, and one who did not eat on the first Yom Tov completes it on the last Yom Tov, meaning on the eighth day. It was taught in a baraita: if he completed with kinds of targima, he fulfilled his obligation, because we require that he complete with something that requires eating in the sukkah; he holds that kinds of targima require sukkah. But if it enters your mind that fruit requires sukkah, it should have taught a greater novelty, that he completes even with fruit. It rejects this; see there. Now I come to explain the words of the Magen Avraham: what the Magen Avraham wrote, "and he learned so from what we say: temporary eating, yes, etc.," means from the fact that it taught, "and we ate them as temporary eating," which implies that they ate them in a temporary manner and not in a fixed manner. For if one would think that the meaning is that they ate a measure of temporary eating, namely less than an egg's bulk, it should have said, "we ate from them a temporary eating." About this the Talmud inferred: temporary eating, yes; fixed eating, no. Therefore the intent is specifically that in a temporary manner, yes, but in a fixed manner, no. Thus even if you say fruit requires sukkah, he is obligated in sukkah only if he eats them in a fixed manner, for now, according to this inference, it wants to infer that fruit requires sukkah.
But perhaps Rabban Gamliel ate them in a temporary manner. Meaning: granted, the questioner did not conclude in his mind that fruit does not require sukkah, but rather his initial assumption was that fruit also requires sukkah; nevertheless, that is only if he established himself over them, as is proven by the questioner's later words from the statement of Rabbi, as above. If so, perhaps Rabban Gamliel ate them in a temporary manner, which according to all opinions does not require sukkah; and then it fits that it said, "not because the halachah is so," etc.
Similar to tasting a cooked dish. He comes to explain how one can say that the reason it was not by law is because he ate them in a temporary manner, when nothing of this was mentioned in the Mishnah. The Mishnah wanted to bring proof that if one wished to be stringent even with a temporary eating, he may do so, and it brought the incidents of R. Yochanan ben Zakkai and Rabban Gamliel. If so, the essential point is missing, namely that Rabban Gamliel ate them in a temporary manner. It is understandable if the initial assumption of the questioner is correct, that two dates without pits are less than more than an egg's bulk and fall short of the measure, for that was well mentioned, since it taught "two dates." Likewise according to Rava's answer, that fruit does not require sukkah, this too is clear in the Mishnah. But if the reason is that he ate them in a temporary manner, the Mishnah should have explained so, for that is the main proof. The Magen Avraham wrote the reason for this: it is similar to R. Yochanan ben Zakkai tasting a cooked dish, which is in a temporary manner. If so, the Mishnah need not explain that he ate them in a temporary manner, since these two incidents of R. Yochanan ben Zakkai and Rabban Gamliel are taught together without qualification and are similar to each other, and in both they were eating in a temporary manner.
And even if he ate much and established himself over them, and so it appears in the Gemara. The Magen Avraham's intent is not from the fact that it taught, "and we ate them as temporary eating," etc., that according to the one who says fruit requires sukkah, one must establish himself over them, and automatically according to the one who says fruit does not require sukkah, even if he ate them in a fixed manner he does not require sukkah. That cannot be, for the Magen Avraham disagrees with the Rosh and holds that according to the one who says fruit requires sukkah, this means even if he ate them in a temporary manner. If so, perforce the Magen Avraham did not make an inference from the wording "and we ate them," but rather means from the support brought from the teaching: if he completed with kinds of targima, he fulfilled his obligation, and it did not teach: if he completed with fruit. Therefore fruit does not require sukkah, as above. And it is difficult: according to your reasoning, even if you say fruit does not require sukkah, if you think that when he ate them in a fixed manner he admits that he is obligated in sukkah, it should still be difficult: let it teach that he completes with fruit, eating them in a fixed manner. The plain assumption is also so, for it is the completion of a meal. Rather, perforce, according to the one who says fruit does not require sukkah, even if he ate much and in a fixed manner, nevertheless it does not require sukkah. This is when he ate an egg's bulk, not precisely, but meaning slightly more than an egg's bulk, and even if he ate it casually, it is no worse than fruit according to the one who says fruit does not require sukkah, as the Magen Avraham wrote, unlike the Rosh. Know that "kinds of targima" means a cooked dish from the five species of grain, as the Rema wrote.
