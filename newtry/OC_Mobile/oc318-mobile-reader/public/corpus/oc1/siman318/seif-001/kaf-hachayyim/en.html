1) [Seif 1] One who cooks on Shabbos, etc. Tur writes that one who cooks on Shabbos is liable. Beis Yosef writes that it is explicit in the chapter Klal Gadol, Shabbos 73a, that cooking is one of the primary labors. It appears that the Shulchan Aruch did not state that one is liable because this is obvious.
2) There: One who cooks on Shabbos, etc. Baking bread, cooking food or dyes, and heating water are all one category of labor. The measure for heating water is enough to wash a small limb, and the measure for cooking dyes is enough for the purpose for which they are cooked. Rambam, chapter 9, halachah 1. Similarly, one who melts any quantity of a metal, or heats metal until it glows like an ember, performs a derivative of cooking. One who heats wax, fat, pitch, asphalt, sulfur, or similar substances likewise performs a derivative of cooking and is liable. One who fires an earthen vessel until it becomes pottery is liable for cooking. The general rule is that whether one softens a hard substance with fire or hardens a soft substance, he is liable for cooking. Rambam there, halachah 6; Magen Avraham, paragraph 10.
3) If one placed meat upon coals and the volume of a dried fig was roasted, which is one third of an egg as stated below in siman 368, seif 3, he is liable even if this amount was distributed over two or three places. If less than a dried fig was roasted but the entire piece was half cooked, he is liable. See above, siman 253, paragraph 28. If it was half cooked on only one side, he is exempt until he turns it and it becomes half cooked on both sides. Rambam there, halachah 5; Magen Avraham there. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 9, has already challenged what Magen Avraham cites there in the name of Raavad and writes that Raavad also agrees with Rambam on this point; see there. All this concerns liability to bring a sin offering. The act is forbidden in every case, because we rule that half the prescribed measure is forbidden by the Torah, as stated in Shabbos 74a and Rashi there. Yoma 74a records the dispute of Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish, and we rule like Rabbi Yochanan. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, Yoreh De'ah, siman 98, paragraph 67, likewise proves from several passages that the law follows Rabbi Yochanan, who holds that half the prescribed measure is forbidden by the Torah; see there.
4) There: One who cooks on Shabbos, etc. If a pot was on the fire and would not have cooked for another hour or two, and one stirred the coals so that it cooked sooner, Ritva writes that it is possible he is liable. Pis'chei Olam, paragraph 1. It appears that he means liability for cooking in addition to the prohibitions of extinguishing and kindling.
5) There: One who cooks on Shabbos, etc. Whenever a dispute creates doubt whether an act constitutes cooking, the doubt concerns a rabbinic matter and is resolved leniently. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 10. This means that the prohibitions imposed upon one who deliberately or inadvertently cooked, or performed other labor, are penalties and therefore rabbinic. A doubt is consequently resolved leniently. See below, paragraph 8.
6) There, gloss: Or performed one of the other labors. See Nesiv Chaim, who writes that this gloss is an unnecessary teaching. Tur wrote the distinction according to Rabbi Meir, but since we rule like Rabbi Yehudah, it is obvious that no distinction exists between cooking and other labors; see there. Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 1, writes the same.
7) There, gloss: Or performed one of the other labors. Regarding rabbinic labor, Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 1, writes that the result is forbidden that day even when the act was performed inadvertently; see there. In his commentary, Gra proves at length that according to all opinions, if rabbinic labor was performed inadvertently, one may benefit from it after the fact that same day; see there. Chayei Adam, klal 9, paragraph 11, writes the same. If it was performed deliberately, it is forbidden even to others until immediately after Shabbos. Chayei Adam there. If, however, produce was taken beyond the techum and returned, even deliberately, it may be eaten that day, including by the person who returned it, because the prohibition was not performed upon the produce itself and it remains unchanged, as explained below in siman 405, seif 9; see there.
8) There: If done deliberately, it is forbidden to him forever. This means that the Sages penalized him by forbidding benefit from the labor itself. He may benefit, however, from the money received for it, as stated in Bava Kamma 71a and Kesubos 34a. The Gemara likewise treats it as obvious at the beginning of Chullin 115, and Rashi explains there that this is only a penalty; see there. Levush, who writes that when done deliberately it is forbidden to him by the Torah, therefore requires examination. See Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2, and Pischei HaDevir, paragraph 1, and examine carefully. The permission to benefit from its value appears to mean that he may sell it only for the amount the item was worth before the labor was performed, so that he does not benefit from labor performed on Shabbos. Otherwise, what purpose would the Sages' penalty serve?
9) There: If done deliberately, it is forbidden to him forever. Responsa Rashba, siman 175, implies that the pot is also forbidden, since with respect to him it absorbed a forbidden substance. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1; Machatzis HaShekel; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 1. Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 1, however, disagrees with Magen Avraham and permits the pot. Although Erekh HaShulchan, paragraph 1, challenges Mateh Yehudah, he nevertheless agrees in practice that the pot is permitted for another reason; see there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1, rules stringently like Magen Avraham. Ben Ish Chai, Parashas Bo, paragraph 1, likewise rules this way, as do the later authorities. Kashering is nevertheless effective even for an earthenware utensil. If one cooked in it without kashering, sixty times the absorbed substance is required, because this is more stringent than food cooked by a non-Jew. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1; Likkutei Rema on Nezirus Shimshon, siman 6, paragraph 2; Ben Ish Chai there. Although kashering is effective here for earthenware, it must nevertheless be performed three times, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, siman 113, seif 16; see there.
10) Nevertheless, if a Jew cooked for a dangerously ill person, all opinions agree that the pot is permitted. Rashba there; Mateh Yehudah there; Yad Aharon, Hagahos HaTur. Even if the pot was new because the physicians instructed that the food be prepared in a new utensil, the pot is nevertheless permitted. Responsa Shevus Yaakov, volume 2, siman 21; see there.
11) There: If done deliberately, it is forbidden to him forever. The same applies to members of his household. Nehar Shalom, paragraph 1. If he dies, his son is penalized after him. Magen Avraham at the end of siman 538; Tosefes Shabbos here, paragraph 1.
12) There: It is forbidden to him forever. A person for whom the food was cooked has the same status as any other person, since the food is not his. We are not concerned that he will instruct another Jew to cook it, because a person does not sin without personal benefit. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1. Nehar Shalom, at the end of paragraph 2, however, writes that it is proper to forbid it also to the person for whom it was cooked; see there. All this applies when someone cooked incidentally. If a Jewish innkeeper regularly cooks every Shabbos, it is certainly forbidden to a person for whom he cooked with that person's knowledge and consent. Moreover, even one for whom it was not cooked at all may not purchase it from him, because of placing a stumbling block before the blind and assisting transgressors. Responsa Ksav Sofer, Orach Chaim, siman 50; see there.
13) There: Others may partake immediately after Shabbos. One must wait the time needed to perform the labor only when a non-Jew performed it for a Jew, because the matter appears lenient to him and he might arrange for it again. When a Jew performs an act directly, there is certainly no concern that he will openly cook again. Beis Yosef; Taz, paragraph 1; Magen Avraham there. If a non-Jew heated water on Shabbos and it subsequently cooled, so that one derives no benefit from the Shabbos labor, one can say that after Shabbos no waiting period is required. Even according to the reason that he might tell the non-Jew to do it, he is now drinking cold water. That same day, however, all opinions forbid it because a prohibition was performed. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 2. Nevertheless, in a place where people drink well water only after boiling it to prevent harm and then cooling it, one must wait after Shabbos the time required to perform the labor, because the non-Jew's act provided benefit.
14) There: It is permitted immediately after Shabbos. If Yom Tov begins immediately after Shabbos, it is forbidden, because Shabbos may not prepare for Yom Tov. Likkutei Mahariv, siman 2; Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur; Magen Avraham there; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 2; Erekh HaShulchan, paragraph 4. Some say that preparation does not apply here, but one should account for the first view. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 3. Similarly, if one cooked for an ill person on Shabbos and Yom Tov begins immediately afterward, a healthy person may not eat it, specifically when the food cannot be eaten raw. Eshel Avraham there. If Sunday is the first day of Yom Tov, it is permitted on the second day, as stated in siman 513, seif 5, in the gloss; see there. If water heated on Shabbos cooled after Shabbos when it was Yom Tov, preparation certainly does not apply. Eshel Avraham there. For those who drink water only after boiling it, as stated in the preceding paragraph, preparation appears to apply, and they may not drink it when Yom Tov begins immediately after Shabbos.
15) There: It is permitted immediately after Shabbos. Rema writes in Yoreh De'ah at the end of siman 102 that if the forbidden food became mixed with other food, it is nullified. We do not say that it is an item that will later become permitted and therefore cannot be nullified because others may eat it after Shabbos. Since it remains forbidden forever to the person who cooked it, it is not considered an item that will later become permitted and is nullified. Kreisi U'Pleisi writes there that this applies only to the cook. For others, it is not nullified even in a mixture, because for them it is an item that will later become permitted. Chavos Daas there writes the same; see there. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, however, writes that even for the cook it is not nullified if mixed that day, since for others it is an item that will later become permitted after Shabbos. Only if it became mixed after Shabbos is it nullified and permitted even to the cook; see there. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 4, write the same, and the latter states that nullification is by a majority; see there. Responsa Rav Pe'alim, volume 1, siman 17, discusses this extensively and concludes stringently like Magen Avraham, agreeing with his ruling but not his reasoning; see there. He writes the same in Ben Ish Chai, Parashas Bo, year 1, paragraph 2; see there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes that if it became mixed on Shabbos, the mixture is forbidden forever to one who deliberately cooked it. According to the reasons stated in Rav Pe'alim, however, it appears that it should be forbidden to the cook only for the same period as to others. This appears primary, since Rema and several later authorities permit the mixture to the cook even that same day. It is sufficient for us to be stringent only that day; after Shabbos, the mixture is permitted to him as it is to others. I afterward saw that Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger likewise writes that it is permitted after Shabbos; see there.
15) If Yom Tov begins immediately after Shabbos and the food will spoil if one waits until after Yom Tov, one can say that even if the act was inadvertent, the food is permitted that day if it became nullified. Eshel Avraham there.
16) There: If done inadvertently, it is forbidden that day, etc. Since he committed a Torah prohibition, the Sages imposed a penalty that day like the penalty for a deliberate act, forbidding it even to others, lest one cook deliberately and claim that he acted inadvertently. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1.
17) There: If done inadvertently, it is forbidden that day, etc. Anything performed on the instruction of a halachic authority who ruled it permitted is considered inadvertent, and the person is not subject to punishment. Responsa Radakh, House 26, volume 2; Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur; Magen Avraham, paragraph 3; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 5; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 3; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 2.
18) Forgetfulness is considered inadvertent. Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 28; Knesses HaGedolah there; Magen Avraham there; Eliyah Rabbah there; Tosefes Shabbos there; Shulchan Aruch HaRav there; Erekh HaShulchan, paragraph 3. The latter writes the same in his work on Yoreh De'ah, siman 99, paragraph 10; see there. This applies whether one forgot that it was Shabbos or forgot that the act was a prohibited labor. Shulchan Aruch HaRav there.
19) Similarly, one who says that he believed the act was permitted is considered inadvertent. Magen Avraham, siman 104, paragraph 6. Pischei HaDevir there, paragraph 3, proves this from several passages in the Gemara and the halachic authorities; see there. Erekh HaShulchan there writes the same. Although he cites a dispute, in Yoreh De'ah there he cites several authorities who hold this way and writes that it is primary; see there. See above, siman 253, paragraph 21.
