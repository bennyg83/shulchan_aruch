(א) Even after they removed it, etc. Beit Yosef wrote: it is written in the Gemara that it is close to say it is not called a kli rishon except when it leaves the fire immediately; but if he left it to stand to stop boiling — it is a kli sheini; and to me it is implied from what is stated in the chapter Kirah regarding placing a pitcher of water opposite the bonfire that as long as yad soledes bo it is considered a kli rishon, even though in a kli sheini yad soledes bo does not have the status of a kli rishon, for a kli rishon heated by fire itself is different, as Tosafos wrote, etc., end of his words; and I do not know whence Beit Yosef learned from the Gemara he brought for this, for there it does not deal at all with removing it from the fire, only with yad soledes bo; but I wondered greatly, for there is an explicit Mishnah in the chapter Kirah: the pot and kettle that one removed boiling — one may not put spices into them; Rashi explained: boiling at bein hashemashos — one may not put spices into them after dark, for a kli rishon as long as it is boiling cooks — behold it is clear not like the Gemara:

(ב) Salty meat. In Asheri it is written: it is implied that ox meat does not cook in a kli rishon and permitted to put raw meat in a kli rishon that was removed from the fire; nevertheless if it is old salt perhaps forbidden, for it quickly cooks as we said above; and even if salted anew — even though the meat does not cook, the moisture, blood, and salt in it cook and are absorbed in it and forbidden, end of his words. And it is implied from this that salt causes it to cook quickly — salt softens meat when it stays long in salting as they salt meat after slaughtering from its blood; and this is when it stays there; but from new salting it does not yet soften the meat, only gives it another taste for Shabbat prohibition, since salt and moisture, etc., cook; and if so, even without salt there is prohibition for Shabbat due to cooking the moisture; nevertheless Tur here mentioned only moisture and not salt. Rosh mentioned salt to include also prohibition not for Shabbat cooking but even on a weekday due to blood; and this is Rama's intent when he wrote "and it appears" regarding without salting, etc. — Rosh mentioned salty meaning Rosh mentioned salty because otherwise, etc.; and it appears Shulchan Aruch can also hold thus — for moisture plainly cooks; only if there is no moisture, such as if dry — nevertheless since there is salt on it that then dried on it, it cooks; and therefore Rama did not write the language of dispute and "it appears," etc., but "and it appears," etc., for there is no dispute since Tur explains thus for moisture alone; and so he wrote further on this regarding blood prohibition:

(ג) And some forbid putting salt. In the Gemara there are two versions on this: the latter version — salt for leniency, for it is difficult to cook like ox meat — nevertheless permitted even in a kli rishon when removed from fire; and the first version — salt stringently, for even in a kli sheini it cooks; and they ruled like the latter version; except Rama wrote according to Tosafos that one who is stringent like the first version — praiseworthy; and we already mentioned in seif 4 regarding onions that the law is the same, since they are sharp and quickly cook; and our eyes see that when put in a hot kli sheini they sweeten from bitterness after being left there a while:

(ד) Nullified on the food. And it is not that we permit eating after Shabbat, for even in a thousand it is not nullified; because here it was nullified before the prohibition was created — for salt does not cook except like ox meat as above, and we do not forbid due to taste, for it is not made for taste and not nullified like chametz and spices; since presumably it was also salted erev Shabbat but not completely salted — this and that cause it and it is permitted like terumah chametz and non-sacred chametz that fell into dough and combined and leavened — we rule zeh v'zeh gorem permitted; therefore in Beit Yosef in the name of Shiltei Gibborim in the name of R' Shimon. And in my humble opinion it is very astonishing, for we do not find zeh v'zeh gorem only when at the time the prohibition causes its effect it also causes the permitted thing's effect at the same time, like the chametz we mentioned where both chametz at once; and so in all zeh v'zeh gorem mentioned in Yoreh De'ah as I wrote there siman 142; unlike here where it first cooked in permitted salt and already did its effect, only it is not enough for taste, and afterward through prohibited salt it gets good taste — this is not zeh v'zeh gorem, rather the prohibition causes giving good taste; and the difference is clear to us — it is like a dish that has spices but they are insufficient and he put prohibited spices and improved the dish — shall this be permitted? There is no greater eating of prohibition than this; and zeh v'zeh gorem applies only if they mixed before giving taste — namely before they came to the pot — then they give taste together; unlike this case, which is like first putting spices on Shabbat in a kli rishon — here too they plainly taste the prohibited taste added to the permitted, as appears to me to distinguish; and one who comes to refute this distinction — the proof is upon him; and it appears one may not permit eating this on Shabbat but should wait until after Shabbat, as stated:
