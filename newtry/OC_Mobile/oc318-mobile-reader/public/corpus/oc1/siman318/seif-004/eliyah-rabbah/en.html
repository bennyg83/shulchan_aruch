[10] [Levush] It may afterward, etc. Some say this applies specifically when further reduction worsens the food (Rama). Levush's omission apparently follows Bach, who rejects that opinion and holds that even food worsened by further cooking is prohibited. One may also say Levush relied on what he writes in seif 8 regarding food improved by reduction. Taz and Magen Avraham appear to agree, and Minchat Kohen likewise implies this, though he concludes it is proper to be stringent because we are not expert regarding which foods improve or worsen through reduction. One should be lenient only when clearly known to worsen, such as turnips, dates, porridge, and similar foods, and water after it has been fully cooked. This applies near a fire, but directly by the flame even food worsened by reduction is prohibited (Taz). See the gloss to seif 6: if the hand is not soledes bo it is considered cooled even though still warm. See the end of siman 15.
[11] That was already cooked, etc. Fully cooked; see seif 15. Regarding "and it is dry," Mas'at Binyamin explains that the majority must be solid. If the majority is liquid it is treated as a liquid. This requires study.
[12] To soak it in hot water even in a kli rishon, etc. Malbushei Yom Tov and Magen Avraham rule this way, unlike Bach, and this is primary. Tosafot, Ran, Rabbenu Yerucham, and Mordechai in chapter Kirah likewise rule so. Bach's stringency is based on the doubt of Hagahot Maimoniyot, but that is an isolated view. Even the major authorities cited above express doubt only when the item was not cooked before Shabbos but merely soaked in hot water. Magen Avraham writes that Shulchan Aruch implies that if an item was merely soaked in hot water before Shabbos and not cooked, it is prohibited to soak it in hot water on Shabbos, and it is proper to be stringent when the water is yad soledes bo. Levush's wording does not imply this, but the authorities support Magen Avraham. In a kli sheni, however, it appears one may be lenient and rely on Rambam.
[13] One may rinse, etc. By pouring from a kli sheni. It is possible that soaking in a kli sheni is likewise permitted, and the text says rinsing only because of the final clause (Malbushei Yom Tov). This follows one answer in Tosafot, Kirah 39a. Magen Avraham and Bach, however, follow Tosafot's other answer that soaking in a kli sheni is prohibited because it appears like cooking, except regarding spices in seif 9. Rabbenu Yerucham and Hagahot Mordechai likewise imply this.
[14] [Levush] Even to rinse them, etc. See Beit Yosef, who says Pardes's wording is difficult. Derishah explains it as follows: some interpret old salted fish as a kind of fish edible raw even without hot rinsing, merely with cold rinsing. Pardes rejects the claim that hot water is needed for its preparation, because if cold water suffices then cold rinsing is obviously permitted, and since it is already edible through cold water, hot water is likewise permitted. When Pardes says some prohibited eating that fish, the meaning is that those who prohibit it would effectively prohibit even rinsing cups, which is untenable. From this it follows that if an item cannot be eaten even after cold rinsing and becomes edible only through hot water, hot rinsing is prohibited, though cold rinsing remains permitted. But anything that can be prepared with cold water may be rinsed even with hot water.
[15] Without soaking, etc. Likewise, if food is not fit without rinsing, it appears to me that rinsing should be prohibited (Malbushei Yom Tov). Magen Avraham also implies this. At first glance this is exactly the law of old salted fish mentioned above.
[16] [Levush] Because it does not cook. This is surprising, for Rashi on 145b and 39a explains that it does cook. Rambam in his commentary to the Mishnah, chapter Chaviyot, likewise writes that since it renders the food fit to eat it is cooking, and he writes similarly in chapter 9, as does Ran.
[17] Because of cooking, etc. The same applies that there is roasting after cooking. Therefore, one may not place a cooked item next to the fire without liquid (Piskei Recanati in the name of Rosh and Semag; Magen Avraham). Shiltei Gibborim, chapter Chaviyot, however, writes that Tur holds there is no roasting after cooking even though there is cooking after roasting. This surprised me, because Sefer Yere'im, p. 38, and the authorities cited above indicate the two are equivalent, and it is also explicit there that there is baking after cooking, such as baking after scalding. I found some support for Shiltei Gibborim in Kol Bo, p. 24, though Kol Bo there requires study. I also found Mas'at Binyamin ruling this way, although he lengthens the discussion and apparently overlooked Shiltei Gibborim and Kol Bo. Shiltei Gibborim further writes that there is no baking after baking, roasting after roasting, roasting after baking, or baking after roasting. Magen Avraham writes that according to all opinions there is no roasting after roasting, so something already roasted may be placed near the fire even after cooling, and the same applies to something baked. This requires study in light of Levush's statement in the preceding seif that warming it directly over the fire is rabbinically prohibited. It is strained to say that Levush there discusses warming with liquid, which is worse, though the wording may imply so. If without liquid, the matter is precisely roasting after cooking. Alternatively Levush may follow Shiltei Gibborim. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 26. One may answer that Magen Avraham permits placing it near the fire where yad soledes bo, but agrees that directly upon the fire is prohibited.
