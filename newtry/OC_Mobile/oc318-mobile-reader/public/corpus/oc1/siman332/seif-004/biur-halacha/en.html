And if it is doubtful, etc. The source of this matter is Pesachim 11 concerning bloodletting for a bekhor in a place that does not make a blemish. Rabbi Yehudah forbids, because since a person is anxious over his money, if you permit it where it does not make a blemish he may come to do it where it does make a blemish. The Rabbis permit in a place that does not make a blemish, because if you do not permit it, all the more he will come to do it in a prohibited way. Rashi explains that because he is anxious, if you do not permit the non-blemishing place he will transgress your words and act, and then will no longer distinguish between a blemishing and non-blemishing place. On this basis the Mordekhai in Bameh Behemah writes that one may tell a non-Jew to let blood for an animal in a case where, if he does not let its blood, it will die, because if we decree against amirah le-nokhri, which is a shevut, he may come to do it himself, since one is anxious over his money and cannot restrain himself; he will then no longer distinguish between a Torah prohibition and a rabbinic one. This follows the Rabbis, not Rabbi Yehudah, because the law follows the many, and the Shulchan Aruch rules this way without dissent. However, Tosafot in Shabbat 43a imply that they do not agree with the Mordekhai and Shulchan Aruch. They write that although regarding money we say the opposite, that if you permit him he may come to extinguish, that is because a person is not as distressed over his money as over his dead relative; if you permit him while he is preoccupied with saving, he may forget that it is Shabbat and extinguish inadvertently, whereas over his dead relative he is greatly distressed and if you do not permit him he may extinguish deliberately. Thus they explicitly hold that regarding money we do not say that if you do not permit him he will deliberately transgress, but the opposite, that because he is anxious we must fence him in lest he transgress inadvertently, like Rabbi Yehudah. So too write Ramban, Rashba, Ran, and Ritva. This is very surprising: how did the sugya in Pesachim, where the Rabbis say this even regarding money, elude all these Rishonim, and the law follows the many? Perhaps they hold that although in other prohibitions the law follows the Rabbis, with Shabbat, which is very severe and punishable by stoning, he will not transgress deliberately; rather, because he is anxious we decree lest he transgress inadvertently. But this too is difficult, for at the beginning of Mi Shehechshikh we say that the Rabbis permitted giving one's purse to a non-Jew because they knew a person cannot restrain himself over his money, and if not permitted he would carry four amot in the public domain. One may answer that there he would actively lose the purse, and one cannot restrain himself from actively losing his money even regarding Shabbat; but where the loss comes by itself, as with a bekhor or fire, there is a distinction between Shabbat and other prohibitions. Ramban states a similar principle, cited by Ran and Rashba there. Yet the truth appears primary like the Mordekhai and Shulchan Aruch, and one should not distinguish between Shabbat and other prohibitions. The Rishonim were pressed by the sugya in Kol Kitvei, where if a fire fell on Shabbat night one may save food for three meals, and the Gemara asks why not save more since he is occupied permissibly; Rava answers that because a person is anxious over his money, if you permit him he may come to extinguish. From there they inferred that regarding money we do not say "if you do not permit him he will deliberately transgress." In my humble opinion there is no difficulty from that sugya. In Pesachim and Mi Shehechshikh, if we permit the lesser act, it is not likely he will come to a Torah prohibition: with the bekhor, once he lets blood in a non-blemishing place he need not do it in a blemishing place; with the purse, once the non-Jew carries it he will not carry it himself. But in a fire, even if we permit him to save whatever he can without extinguishing, there remains concern that he will extinguish items surrounded by fire and impossible to save otherwise. Therefore the Sages did not permit saving beyond the limit even items that do not require extinguishing. As to why they did not permit telling a non-Jew to extinguish, as in Mi Shehechshikh, see Ran there. It seems to me that fire is different because extinguishing requires many people, and each extinguishing act saves property; if we permit telling a non-Jew to extinguish, one may show the non-Jew how to extinguish and then extinguish himself. They permitted only saying, "Whoever extinguishes will not lose," which is not a command but the non-Jew acting on his own, and that too only because a person is anxious over his money. In Mi Shehechshikh, once he gives the purse to the non-Jew he does not need to assist him. Likewise in the Mordekhai's case, telling a non-Jew to let blood will not lead him to assist the non-Jew as with a fire. See Shulchan Aruch siman 332, where Rabbi Akiva Eiger leaves this Mordekhai unresolved, asking why it differs from fire; according to what we wrote, the Mordekhai's words are correct. I later found a similar distinction in Hagahot Mordekhai, Bameh Tomnin.
Now let us return and see whether the Rif inclines to the Mordekhai's view. The Rif writes in Bameh Behemah that since we rule like Rabbi Yoshiyah, who is lenient because of animal suffering and does not decree because of grinding medicines, it follows that the baraita saying that an animal seized by blood should not be placed in water to cool it, lest one grind medicines, is not halakhah, because a person is not so anxious over healing his animal that we should suspect grinding medicines. Since he gives the reason that a person is not anxious, it implies that if he were anxious over healing his animal, one would have to suspect grinding medicines. According to the Mordekhai it should be the opposite: if he were anxious over its healing, we should not suspect grinding, for if we do not permit other remedies he will be more likely to grind medicines because he is anxious over his money. After reflection, however, the Rif too can be read like the Mordekhai. The Rif's statement that a person is not anxious over healing his animal is itself difficult, since Pesachim 11 and Shabbat regarding fire and many other places state that a person is anxious over his money. Therefore the Rif must be explaining the law of an animal seized by blood, and an animal that ate too many vetches, as cases without fear that it may die, but only ordinary suffering. He holds the case parallels a person, where it certainly does not involve danger, for if there were danger, even grinding medicines would be permitted and all the more other remedies. Thus the Rif says that one is not so anxious over an animal's mere discomfort, as Pesachim also says that a person is not anxious over the suffering of his animal. But where there is concern it may die, he is certainly anxious, and although the decree of grinding medicines exists, it is permitted for another reason: he is extremely anxious, and if no remedy is permitted he may more likely grind medicines, as in Pesachim regarding a bekhor. The Rif's language is somewhat forced, but we must explain it so that it not contradict an explicit Tosefta, cited by Eliyah Rabbah in siman 332, that an animal that ate too many vetches may be run in the courtyard so it not die. It is explicit that where the animal is endangered, one may run it and we do not decree lest he grind medicines, although there he is certainly anxious, as many sugyot state that a person is anxious over his money. This Tosefta seems at first to contradict our Bavli, for our sugya implies that even where there is only animal suffering one may perform other remedies, and the phrase "do we decree for an animal?" implies that the decree of grinding medicines does not apply to animals at all. It appears that our sugya concerns mere suffering, and that is the dispute of Rabbi Yoshiyah and the Rabbis; but where there is doubt that it may die, even the Rabbis agree that other remedies are permitted and we do not apply the decree of grinding medicines, because a person is anxious over his money, and if we do not permit other remedies he may grind medicines, as stated above according to the Mordekhai. The Tosefta, which permits running it in the courtyard so it not die, follows the Rabbis, who permit animal remedies only where there is doubt it may die. A similar approach was written by Peri Megadim to resolve the Taz, though he did not mention that it also resolves the Tosefta. In truth this sugya is a great proof for the Mordekhai. Still, the Mordekhai's law appears true for another reason as well, and all the Rishonim cited would agree in his actual ruling: even if they hold that regarding Shabbat we do not say that if you do not permit him he will deliberately transgress, they would agree that one may tell a non-Jew to let blood for an animal on Shabbat because tza'ar ba'alei chayim is a Torah concern and overrides amirah le-nokhri, which is rabbinic, as the poskim write regarding telling a non-Jew to milk an animal on Shabbat, above in siman 305:20.
