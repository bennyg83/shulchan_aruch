Already, see Taz regarding his statement that with blessings over benefit each person must bless, yet one may still fulfill his obligation through another's blessing. His statement that one must answer amen is qualified by siman 213, seif 2: after the fact, one fulfills even without answering amen. Regarding what he cites in the name of Bach, Bach's intent is inferred from Rashi on Berachos 40a, who explains, “the one who breaks the bread, before he broke it,” rather than speaking generally. Even among participants, if one says to the person near him, “Take and bless,” it is not an interruption. This shows that Rashi refers specifically to the one breaking the bread. Once he has broken and tasted, another participant's speech, even about something unrelated to the meal, is not an interruption. As for the comparison to putting on tefillin, one may distinguish between an obligatory mitzvah, which rests personally upon him and therefore constitutes an interruption, and optional eating with a blessing of praise and thanksgiving, where one person praises the Master of all on behalf of everyone, since a person's agent is like himself. Once the one breaking bread has eaten, the others' speech is no longer an interruption. Regarding eating matzah on the first night of Pesach, however, if the leader tasted and the others interrupted, they must repeat the blessing, because it resembles a blessing over mitzvos. Taz proposes a safeguard: if the participants interrupted after hearing HaMotzi, the one breaking bread should intend to discharge only those who will not interrupt between hearing and eating. Such a person is discharged in every event, and no blessing is in vain even according to the Rokeach, because in that case one who interrupted was never included in the shared intent. See Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 12: according to Magen Avraham, if either the one breaking bread or one participant tasted, all are discharged. Levush holds that initially speech is forbidden but after the fact they are discharged. Rama in the gloss also agrees that initially one should not speak even after the one breaking bread has eaten.
And know that if the one breaking bread spoke between the blessing and eating, while the others then ate without speaking, it may be that they fulfilled their obligation through his blessing because they themselves did not interrupt. I derive this from Rama, siman 209, seif 2, in the gloss: since he discharged others through his blessing, the blessing remains valid even though he had not intended to drink further wine and the cup before him contained water. See also siman 213, Magen Avraham, seif katan 7, that through an inadvertent blessing he may discharge others even though he himself did not benefit. The same may apply here when he intentionally spoke. His blessing was initially valid and he did not violate the Sages' enactment when reciting it, because he intended both to bless and to benefit, but only afterward interrupted. The others may therefore have fulfilled through his blessing. We have thus returned to the dispute between the Rokeach and Beit Yosef discussed above.
If one recited Kiddush over bread, blessed HaMotzi, completed Kiddush, and wine then became available so that he blesses over it, this requires study. It may not constitute an interruption, since the primary mitzvah of Kiddush is over wine. With Hashem's help, this will be explained in siman 271; we also discussed it in our novellae.
