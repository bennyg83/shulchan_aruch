And he should not speak until he has swallowed at least a small amount, as I wrote in Mishnah Berurah. One must be very careful about this, for Magen Avraham and Eliyah Rabbah hold that chewing is not yet considered eating; if he speaks while chewing, he has interrupted between the blessing and the eating. Eliyah Rabbah cites Bircas Avraham as agreeing with Magen Avraham. Many later authorities, however, write that although one must certainly be careful initially, if he spoke while the food was still being chewed in his mouth he need not repeat the blessing, because chewing is itself the beginning of eating. [Gloss: Magen Avraham's proof is from siman 210, where one who chews and spits out does not require a blessing. In truth, according to the later authorities there, that ruling is because his intent was merely to taste. It therefore provides no proof for our case, where his chewing was intended for eating. Bigdei Yesha, Magen Giborim, and Rabbi Yaakov Emden in his siddur all reject Magen Avraham's ruling on this basis. It also appears to me that although Eliyah Rabbah here states Magen Avraham's view without qualification, in siman 210, seif katan 3, where he cites Olas Tamid, it is explicit that chewing food with the intent to eat, rather than merely to taste, is considered eating. Nevertheless, Magen Avraham's view might be explained according to what he initially wrote in siman 210 following the Rokeach, that even when one's intent is to eat, it is still called tasting. Although that case concerns actual eating, Magen Avraham may hold that regarding chewing alone one should certainly follow that view and regard it as mere tasting. The later authorities cited above did not accept this for the law after the fact. In my humble opinion, this remains very difficult. According to Rambam, who understands the sugya concerning blessings as referring to swallowing, it is possible that without swallowing, mere chewing is still called tasting even when intended for eating. Practically, it appears that one should follow Chayei Adam's ruling. It is also possible that even Magen Avraham agrees that if he swallowed the flavor extracted in his mouth by chewing, he need not repeat the blessing, because the blessing thereby took effect.] Chayei Adam likewise agrees with this practical ruling, though his words imply that this applies specifically where he first swallowed at least the flavor extracted in his mouth through chewing. Nevertheless, initially he should not interrupt at all, even to say, “Give food to the animal.” This is implied by Kol Bo, and Shiyurei Berachah cites Sefer HaOrah to this effect; it is likewise evident from Behag and Sefer HaEshkol. Magen Avraham cites Responsa Maharam in siman 271 that eating before one's animal is a Torah prohibition, which implies that he regards the exposition as a full derashah and not merely an asmachta. According to that view, even after reciting HaMotzi one would plainly have to interrupt. The authorities cited above, however, evidently do not hold this way. Know further that Rama's unqualified wording implies that even where the bread is not clean, although initially one should certainly bring salt before reciting the blessing, if he already recited HaMotzi he may not interrupt before swallowing some of the piece of bread. Peri Megadim writes that Rama refers only to clean bread, but this does not appear correct. I later found, with Hashem's help, that Behag and Sefer HaEshkol explicitly support what we have written.
But afterward, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, where I cited the later authorities who agree with Beit Yosef. In truth, Rama's source is the Rokeach and Or Zarua. One who studies the beginning of Or Zarua's discussion will see that it does not address Rama's law of listeners who interrupted before eating. Rather, Or Zarua debates whether those discharged by another's blessing are specifically obligated to taste from the bread of the one who broke it, or whether each may eat from the loaf before him, since the leader discharged them only from the blessing and did not obligate them to eat from his loaf. Although someone must certainly taste from the loaf over which HaMotzi was recited, for otherwise the leader's blessing would be void and consequently the listeners would not be discharged, the obligation does not rest upon every listener. It is enough if the one who broke the bread, or even one other participant, tastes. The others may then eat from their own loaves. His proof is from Eruvin, where the Gemara says to give it to a child to taste, showing that not every listener must taste from the cup and that even if the one who blessed does not taste, the tasting of one listener suffices. This is the substance of Or Zarua's words, and the other Rishonim in this sugya also appear to debate this point. Nevertheless, each listener is forbidden to interrupt before eating from his own loaf, and if he does so, he loses the blessing. The Rokeach cited by Beit Yosef can also be read this way, with a minor textual correction: instead of “they spoke,” it should read “he spoke.” The meaning is that after the one who blessed ate from his loaf, even if he subsequently spoke, everyone was discharged by his blessing and may then eat from their own loaves. If, however, he spoke before eating, he lost his blessing, it became a blessing in vain, and the others likewise may not rely upon it, as explained below in siman 213, seif 2. This is unlike Peri Megadim, who wished to say that in such a case all agree that the listeners are discharged. The same interpretation may be given to Recanati, siman 74, cited by Eliyah Rabbah. Accordingly, he too may agree with Beit Yosef. It follows that neither the Rokeach nor Or Zarua provides a source for Rama's ruling, and the law therefore remains as Beit Yosef and the later authorities ruled.
