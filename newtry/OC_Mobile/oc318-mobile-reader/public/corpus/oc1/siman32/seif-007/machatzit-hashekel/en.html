(Seif katan 7) A line, etc. That it is forbidden. For we hold that only a mezuzah, and likewise a Torah scroll according to Rashi, and so we rule in Yoreh De'ah, requires scoring. But tefillin do not require scoring. This means only that one need not score every line as in a mezuzah, and a Torah scroll according to our practice, because of the scriptural decree. Nevertheless, at least the upper line must be scored, because it is forbidden to write a verse from the Torah without scoring in order to straighten the writing. For this, the upper line suffices, since once the first line before him is written straight, he knows how to align the second line beneath it even without scoring, for the upper line serves like scoring for the second, the second for the third, and so for all of them.
And Be'er Heitev asked, etc. At the end of chapter 12 of Yevamos, Abaye said that when beis din writes a chalitzah document for the yevamah who performed chalitzah before them, they should write in it: They had him read from 'my yavam refuses' until 'my yavam refused,' meaning they should write the whole verse, from the beginning and end. They also had him read until 'to take her,' for the yavam says the verse, 'I do not desire to take her.' They had her read from 'so' until 'the removed shoe,' but not the entire verse. The Gemara concludes that Mar Zutra scored, etc., for the entire section. Be'er Heitev understood that he scored all the lines and wrote all the above verses as they appear in the Torah because it is forbidden to write without scoring, and on this he objected. However, the wording of Magen Avraham's question in the name of Be'er Heitev is not precise, for it implies that Be'er Heitev objected only to his scoring the whole section, when it would have sufficed to score the upper line alone, and to that Magen Avraham's answer fits. In truth, Be'er Heitev asked a stronger question, and therefore Magen Avraham's answer is brief and somewhat obscure. He quotes the Rosh in Megillah chapter 2 and Tur Yoreh De'ah, that where the upper line was written without scoring, such as where one wrote ordinary words or a letter of greeting in the upper line, from there onward the upper line is like scoring. This is like what is said here, that if he scored the upper line he no longer needs to score. It is not reasonable to distinguish and say that here is different because the first line was scored and therefore written straight, for ordinary people know how to train their hands to write the entire line straight without scoring. On this Be'er Heitev wrote that it requires study: why did Mar Zutra need to score the chalitzah document, since before reaching the reading text they had already written several lines mentioned in the chapter HaCholeitz and copied by the Rosh at the end of the chapter Mitzvas Chalitzah? Thus he asked that there should have been no need to score at all, not even the upper line, since several lines were already written in the chalitzah document before they wrote that they had read the verses. Accordingly, Magen Avraham's second answer, that Mar Zutra in fact scored only the upper line, does not fully resolve Be'er Heitev's question, since he asked that even the upper line should not have required scoring. It must be that Magen Avraham meant that the reason is explained in the Rosh: even if the upper line is not scored, one need not score the second line because ordinary people know how to train their hands to write all the lines straight without scoring. Mar Zutra, however, did not know how to write the upper line straight without scoring; but if the first line was scored, he could align the lines below it. Therefore he scored only the first line and afterward wrote the document. According to the first answer, however, even if the first line was scored Mar Zutra still could not align the lines below it, and therefore he had to score the entire section that he wrote. I did not find in the Rosh the phrase 'ordinary people, etc.' except regarding the upper line, as I cited above; perhaps Magen Avraham inferred by reasoning that this is also the reason for the lines below the upper one. This excludes the view that the whole section may not be written, namely Abaye as above. Afterward it says there that Mar bar Idi challenged: But it was not given to be written, which Rashi explains to mean that one may not write a single section alone. For this reason the Gemara says that Mar Zutra wrote the entire section, excluding the view of Mar bar Idi.
And he further asked, etc. The Rosh asked, etc. Be'er Heitev understood that for this reason Abaye did not write all the verses, because he did not want to score due to the effort. On this the Rosh objected: nevertheless, regarding 'they had him read, I do not desire to take her,' he could have written the verse as it is written even without scoring, since the verse contains only three words, and three words may be written without scoring. Be'er Heitev objected that although a baraisa teaches that three words may be written without scoring and four require scoring, Rabbi Yitzchak disagrees and holds that only two words may be written without scoring, but not three. The Rosh ruled like Rabbi Yitzchak. If so, Abaye acted properly.
But to me it does not appear so, etc. The text should read that he holds like Mar bar Idi. Meaning, Abaye would not have acted this way merely because of the slight effort of scoring a little. Rather, it must be that he holds like Mar bar Idi, that even with scoring it is forbidden because it was not given to be written, as above. Three words may be written with scoring; for according to Rabbi Yitzchak, two words may be written and three may not be written. Just as 'two may be written' means without scoring, so too 'three may not be written' means without scoring, but with scoring it is certainly permitted. Similarly, according to the baraisa, this applies to four words. Only writing a section by itself is forbidden, but certain words are permitted. If so, why did Abaye not write at least all three words, 'I do not desire to take her,' with scoring? However, regarding the two verses that they had him read, which are long verses, the Rosh found no proof that Mar bar Idi would permit them; therefore he objected only concerning 'I do not desire to take her.'
