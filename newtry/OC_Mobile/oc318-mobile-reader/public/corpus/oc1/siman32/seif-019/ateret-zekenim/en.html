At the beginning of the writing, he should state verbally that he is writing it for the sake of the sanctity of the Divine Name. Some say that it is sufficient for him to have in mind that he writes the Divine Names for their sake. The following is the intention to have when writing the Divine Names, from the writings of the pious Rabbi David, a student of Rabbi Chaim, of blessed memory, author of Divrei Chaim. He transmitted it in manuscript to the worthy student Rabbi Tevel, of blessed memory, a scribe of Torah scrolls, tefillin, and mezuzos, and I copied it from the manuscript of that pious man: When forming the upper point of the letter yod, he should direct his intention toward the crown associated with the Divine Name Ehyeh, and toward the Cause of all causes bound to it. From there the light is drawn down and clothed within the body of the yod. When he comes to write the first hei, he should intend that the light is extended and clothed within the hei; from the hei into the vav; and from the vav into the final hei. In the final hei the supernal Emanator completes the structure. He must further intend that every letter is itself comprised of ten. Regarding the yod, he should have in mind that its three points correspond to keter, chochmah, and binah within chochmah. The upper point alludes to the Infinite and also to keter within chochmah; the body of the yod corresponds to chochmah; and the lower point corresponds to binah. He should further contemplate the full spelling of yod, namely yod, vav, dalet. The vav contains the six directions within chochmah, and the dalet is the tenth, making ten in all. All ten are enclosed and concealed within it. When writing the first hei, he should first form a yod, intending that Abba comes to unite with Imma and to place within her what was concealed within him, namely the numerical value seven. He should then form the upper line, extending it slightly from the yod to indicate that it receives from the yod. This is the mystery of “a river issues from Eden.” He should extend the right leg of the hei, intending that it is the vav that emerges from between Abba and Imma. It includes the six directions, while the dalet likewise contains them in concealment, because the upper roof, which is the river, serves both sides. The inner, suspended leg of the hei corresponds to tiferes and yesod, which together encompass the six directions. It should be made somewhat thick, indicating two vavs, one corresponding to tiferes and the other to malchus. This represents the children emerging from Imma while they were previously concealed within her as she carried them, and then proceeding toward birth. When writing the vav, he should intend that it emerges from Imma. At its head he should intend the three highest attributes, keter, chochmah, and binah, together with daas. These are the mochin within its head. See Pardes, gate 3, sections 7 and 8. As he extends the vav, before reaching its midpoint, he should intend chesed, gevurah, and tiferes, corresponding to the Divine Names Kel, Elokim, and the Tetragrammaton. These Names correspond to the arms and torso. In the Tetragrammaton he should intend that the first yod and hei derive from Abba and Imma, the vav is its own essential aspect, and the final hei is its female counterpart, the two having entered the world as a dual configuration. As he continues extending the vav, he should intend netzach, hod, and yesod, corresponding to the two legs and the reproductive organ, together with its Shechinah, as explained in the Tikkunim concerning the letters hei and vav. When writing the final hei, he should first write its right leg, intending that it is tiferes coming to bestow upon the Shechinah that which was concealed within it. He should then write the upper roof, intending that it represents the attribute of Adonai, which is nourished from the right leg, corresponding to the Tetragrammaton. In the small inner leg he should intend the unification of the two Divine Names together, in the combined form customarily represented by their interlaced letters. At the beginning of the writing he must make the declaration stated above. He need not repeat it at the beginning of every passage, unlike the writing of a Divine Name, which possesses an additional sanctity and requires a declaration for each Name. Some say that he must make the declaration at the beginning of every passage. Beit Yosef cites this in the name of Ran. The first view is primary. Nevertheless, if he failed to make the declaration at the beginning of the writing, the writing is invalid. Likewise, if he failed to do so when writing any Divine Name, that Name is invalid. This follows Rabbeinu Yonah in the name of Rosh and Orchos Chaim.
