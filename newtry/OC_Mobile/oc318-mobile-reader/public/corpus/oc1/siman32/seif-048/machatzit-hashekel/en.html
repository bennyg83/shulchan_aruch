(Note 48) And in these countries, etc., and it is difficult, etc. — know: if indeed what I will write here is explained thus in the book of the Taz, nevertheless it comes at length, and I will come briefly with some additions of explanation. Know: an open parsha is one that is open to itself and is not drawn upward; and a closed parsha is one that is drawn to the matter above, but space is given between them so that Moses may discern between parsha and parsha, and as Rashi wrote. And we hold that between parsha and parsha, whether it be open or closed, one must leave space to write upon it nine letters. But the Tur here wrote space for three words of three letters each; and the Rambam chapter 7 of Laws of Tefillin wrote nine letters like asher asher asher; and the Bach siman 7 seder katan 4 wrote, and this is his language: And one asked: why did our teacher write to leave space for three words, etc., and not write briefly to leave space for nine letters? And one can say that one must also leave full space for two small letters besides the nine letters — for when writing three words of three letters each, one must leave a full small letter between word and word, as above seif 32; and with this measure one must leave space — end of his words. According to his words it must be said: why did they not write a measure of eleven letters? Or because these nine letters are the measure of large letters like asher asher asher, and the two letters between word and word that the Bach wrote suffice with small letters, as explained in seif 32. Also, it is explained in seif 32 that between letter and letter one must leave a little like a hair's breadth; if so, besides the nine letters one must also leave the space between letter and letter. But those two letters that the Bach added according to his explanation do not require space; and if it were taught a measure of eleven letters, one would say that one also needs the measure of ten hairs between the letters — but that is not so. And all this is per the Bach. But in the Shulchan Aruch here, and likewise in Shulchan Aruch Yoreh De'ah, and likewise in the Tur Yoreh De'ah, three words were not mentioned, only nine letters — implying that he holds that nine letters alone suffice, unlike the Bach.

And the matter of an open or closed form is a dispute between the Rambam and the Rosh. Per the Rambam, every open parsha must begin at the beginning of the line; therefore if he finished the parsha in the middle of a line and there is still empty space until the end of the line the measure of nine letters, he leaves space and begins at the head of the line beneath it another parsha, and that parsha that began at the head of a line is called open — and in this the Rosh also agrees that it is called open. But if from the end of the parsha that he finished until the end of the line there is space more than nine letters — as the measure of one word — and he left space the measure of nine letters and afterward wrote the first word of the parsha that follows it (and likewise more than one word per the place of the space) — this is called closed per the Rambam, since it did not begin at the beginning of the line; and in this the Rosh also agrees that it is called closed. However, there are two other forms in which the Rambam and the Rosh disagree, and they are opposite views: (1) If he finished at the end of a line and left the line beneath it entirely empty, and in the line beneath the empty line he began another parsha — per the Rambam it is called open, since it begins at the beginning of a line; but per the Rosh it is called closed, for we consider all three lines — the line where parsha one ended, the empty line, and the line beneath it where the parsha began — as if they are one line, and the empty space is in the middle of the line, which is called closed, as above. (2) If he finished the parsha at the end of a line and in the line beneath it he left at its beginning space the measure of nine letters and afterward began another parsha — per the Rambam it is called closed, since it does not begin at the beginning of a line; and per the Rosh, in such a case it is called open. And the Rabbi wrote in Yoreh De'ah siman 275 that ab initio one should fulfill the view of all and write in that form which is open according to all where it should be open, and likewise where it must be closed he should make the form that is closed according to all; and the Rama wrote: but if he cannot arrange it thus, he should not deviate from the words of the Rambam, whose words are primary in this. And in tefillin one must make the parsha as they are in the Torah: the first three parshiyot are open in the Torah, and parsha four V'haya im shamo'a is closed in the Torah; therefore parsha Kadesh, which is the first, must be open, even though it is not evident at its beginning whether it is open or closed, since there is no space before it at all — nevertheless it suffices to call it open, like the beginnings of the five books of the Torah, which the Rambam did not include among open and closed parshiyot because of their being beginnings; and likewise the other four books have in this sufficient indication that they are the beginning of a book — and likewise the beginning of Kadesh, which is the beginning of the tefillin. However, to recognize that parsha V'haya ki y'vi'acha is open, one must leave at the end of Kadesh space the measure of nine letters; and what begins again at the head of the parchment V'haya ki y'vi'acha, and there is no space before it — it is as if he began in the line beneath the end of Kadesh, which is open according to all; and if he began at the head of the line, as above. And likewise one should do at the end of V'haya ki y'vi'acha, whereby there will be indication that parsha Shema is open according to all. But V'haya im shamo'a — to make a closed form according to all is impossible, for if so he would need in the last line of Shema to leave space of nine letters and begin parsha V'haya im shamo'a in that same line, as above; and this is impossible — not to mention in the head tefillin, since each parsha is on a separate parchment, but even in the arm tefillin, at least each parsha must begin at the head of the parchment. And since the words of the Rambam on this are primary, therefore the Rabbi wrote that one should not leave at the end of Shema space of eight letters, but at the head of the parchment where V'haya im shamo'a begins he should leave space at the head of the line of nine letters — and it is closed per the Rambam and open per the Rosh, as above. However, in this the Rabbi disagrees in the name of the Rambam with those who validate: per the Rambam, if he made V'haya im shamo'a open, it is invalid, since in the Torah it is closed — even though in the Torah the reason it is closed is because it has connection to the parsha before it in the Torah, not so in tefillin, where V'haya im shamo'a has no connection to parsha Shema before it, since Shema is written in parshat Va'etchanan and parsha V'haya im shamo'a is written in parshat Eikev. If so, it follows that ab initio it is fitting for the scribe to write in the form written in the Torah — but why, if he did not do so, should it be invalidated? The Rabbi answered that the Rambam holds that in the three parshiyot before it, if he changed from what is written in the Torah they are invalid — the same law applies to parsha V'haya im shamo'a; and those who validate do not need this reasoning, and therefore they validate at least b'dieved if he made V'haya im shamo'a open.

And the Bach wrote, and also they finish parsha V'haya im shamo'a at the end, etc., as should read. And it appears to me that he needed to teach us this, that it is proper to finish in the middle of a line, and as the Mordechai wrote, similar to a sefer Torah — for we hold that one must finish before the eyes of all Israel in the middle of a line; he teaches us that it is not comparable, for specifically a sefer Torah requires this to show that here the Torah ends and to it one may not add; therefore the ending in the middle of the line shows that it is not because of lack of parchment that no more is written — for behold, there is still space before him, the remaining half line — and nevertheless nothing is written in it, but because here is the end of the Torah from the mouth of God, and one may not add more; and this reason does not apply in tefillin. And see responsum of the Maharshal siman 37 at its end — a little of this is explained.

If he made it closed — that is, per the view and custom of the Rabbi, unlike our custom.

Even according to our custom. That is, certainly per the custom of the Rabbi to make parsha four closed, certainly one should not leave space at the end of V'haya im shamo'a — for by finishing at the end of a line it appears more like closed, even though the indication of open and closed is at the beginning of the parsha; nevertheless this is preferable — that he finish the end of the parsha at the end of a line — also because there is greater indication; and so too the Maharshal in responsum 57 at the end of the responsum, see there. But even according to our custom, where we also make parsha four open, nevertheless one should finish V'haya im shamo'a at the end of a line — and that is like the words of the Bach above.

And if my strength were sufficient, etc. — that they are valid per the Levush — that is, according to our custom where we also make parsha four open; if so, they are invalid per the Rambam, who holds that if he changed and made parsha four open, it is invalid.

And it appears to us that our custom, etc. — and the Taz wrote: a picture to fulfill according to all, according to what the Rambam wrote — picture 3 for a closed parsha — that is, if he finished the parsha in the middle of a line and there is not enough space to leave space for three words and write the first word of the other parsha, whereby it would be closed according to all; and he must write the parsha after it so that it will be closed — how shall he do it? For if he begins at the beginning of the second line it will be open per the Rambam; therefore he must leave a little space at the beginning of the second line, and afterward the parsha after it is closed per the Rambam, since it does not begin at the beginning of a line. And according to this, even if they would leave at the end of Shema space for nine letters and at the beginning of V'haya im shamo'a they would leave a little space — then per the Rambam V'haya im shamo'a would be called closed, as above, since it does not begin at the head of a line. But per the Rosh it would be such a case of open, since at the end of Shema in any case there is space of nine letters — for per the Rosh, even if he does not leave space at the end of Shema but leaves space at the beginning of V'haya im shamo'a for nine letters and begins V'haya im shamo'a in the middle of a line, nevertheless it is called open; only at the end of Shema he should leave a little space, but not the measure of nine letters; and likewise before V'haya im shamo'a he should leave a little space, but not the measure of nine letters. But in any case, if one combines together the two empty places — end of Shema and beginning of V'haya im shamo'a — there will be the measure of nine letters, for in any case we require space between parsha and parsha the measure of nine letters; and in such a case it is closed according to all — not only per the Rambam, for according to him even if there were at the end of Shema space the measure of nine letters, nevertheless since he leaves at the beginning of V'haya im shamo'a and does not begin at the head of a line, it is called closed per the Rambam — all the more so in such a case where there is not at the end of Shema space the measure of nine letters; but even per the Rosh he agrees that in such a case it is closed, since in one place there is not the measure of nine letters, only in combination — at present. And I in my case was perplexed: how is it possible to do such a thing to fulfill according to all? Behold, the Rabbi wrote in Yoreh De'ah siman 275, and so too the Shach there seder katan 4 in his name — that ab initio one requires between parsha and parsha the measure of nine large letters; nevertheless b'dieved it suffices if there is space the measure of nine small letters — that is, nine yods suffice; and according to my view, three times asher, which is more than eighteen yods. If so, how shall he do it? If he divides the measure of nine yods and places some of them as space at the end of Shema and some at the beginning of V'haya im shamo'a — behold, ab initio one requires that there be space the measure of three times asher; and if he places some of them from three times asher as space at the end of Shema and the remainder from three times asher he places as space at the beginning of V'haya im shamo'a — in any case there is the measure of nine yods in one place, and it is at least b'dieved the measure of a parsha; and it is open per the Rosh.
