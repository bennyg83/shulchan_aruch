That he should scrape the snout, etc. An open mem is formed through two writings, meaning first like a nun and afterward like a vav. The same applies to any letter written in two acts, such as a gimel and the like; if it became invalid in the last writing, he need scrape away only that part, as the Taz writes, though this requires study in Beis Yosef. It is obvious that all this speaks where he did not write afterward, for otherwise it would be out of order. This is the language of the Levush: a reish that he wrote like a dalet, and likewise a kaf that he made like a beis, is not sufficiently corrected by scraping only the leg or the roof. However, this is specifically where he wrote the roof with a point and a tag behind it like a dalet, and afterward made the leg below, as is the custom of some scribes. But if he wrote a reish and afterward they erred and made a tag behind it, it is enough to scrape the roof or the leg together with the tag.
If one letter adhered to another, etc. See below, siman 143, s.k. 8. The Bach wrote that if the entire length of the letter is attached to its fellow, scraping does not help.
And if he scraped, etc. In the responsum of Menachem Azaryah he ruled that separation does not help where the letters adhered before the letter was completed. Olas Tamid wrote that this is specifically above, but if it adhered below it is valid, end quote. Even above, it seems to me that one may rely on the authors of the Shulchan Aruch and Levush to validate. Olas Tamid wrote in the name of Responsa Ramban, siman 236, that if a reish adhered to a beis, one must scrape away the entire reish, but the beis need not be scraped, end quote. His words are puzzling, for this is out of order. I examined the responsum of the Ramban itself and saw that it refers to a Torah scroll, where writing out of order is valid. It also seems to me that the Ramban follows his own view that scraping the adhesion does not help, but according to us, where correction by scraping the adhesion alone does help. However, the Radach wrote that if the form of the letter changed through the adhesion, it is like a mem that became closed.
If the leg of the heh touched, etc. Even by a very fine hairbreadth, and even though a child knows that it is a heh, so wrote the Radach. Olas Tamid wrote: if he wrote an ayin instead of a vav, then if he first made the vav and afterward the circle around it, he scrapes away the circle and the vav remains; but if the reverse occurred, he must scrape away the whole thing, end quote. It seems to me that this resembles what the Levush wrote in this se'if, where he first wrote the roof and the leg, etc., and afterward erred, etc. Magen Avraham wrote: it seems to me that this is specifically where the yud itself touched the alef, but if the left point of the yud touches the alef, it is valid, as written in Even HaEzer siman 125, se'if 16, for even without the points it is still called a yud, end quote. From the fact that the Levush wrote that he should scrape the entire yud, what Magen Avraham wrote requires study, namely that he should scrape all that he wrote in that letter after he made the contact. I was asked about a Torah scroll in which the upper yud was found touching the alef of the word for God, whether it is permitted to scrape the yud. Response: one can say that it resembles what the Rama wrote in Yoreh De'ah siman 276 regarding the heh of the Divine Name, whose leg touches its roof slightly, that there is doubt in the matter. The reason in Beis Yosef and Shach is that even though it is invalid, nevertheless since people read it as a heh, it appears like erasing the Name. If so, the same law applies here with an alef; even though the yud actually touches, nevertheless people read it as an alef. Yet I found another remedy: he should add ink above in the yud until what is below is considered the leg of the yud, for scribes say that sometimes a yud is made very thick. There is proof from the Taz, s.k. 16, that there is a remedy by adding ink; see there.
And the peh, etc. The same law applies to a doubled peh, Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 117.
