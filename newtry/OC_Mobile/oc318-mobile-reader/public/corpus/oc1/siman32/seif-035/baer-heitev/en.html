The author of Be'er Sheva wrote in a responsum at the end of his book, folio 52b, that since the wording is doubled, it implies that even after the fact it is invalid, even regarding the other Names that may not be erased, and even an appended element following the Name is invalid if it protrudes outside the line. This, he says, appears correct as halachah, though not for practical ruling. The Magen Avraham objected, for the Shach in Yoreh Deah, siman 276, seif 6, wrote that some authorities validate where part of the Name was suspended above the line; see there, seif katan 5. At first glance this implies all the more so where it merely protruded outside the line, for the prohibition of suspending is mentioned in the Gemara, whereas this prohibition is not stated explicitly in the Gemara, but only derives from Rabbeinu Tam. How, then, do we know to invalidate after the fact? And in Mishnas Binyamin, siman 57, he wrote that if one wrote 'our God' lacking the yod, he may suspend the yod, since it is not part of the Name itself. If so, certainly where an appended element of the Name protruded outside the line it is valid. The gaon Rabbi Yitzchak, av beis din of the holy community of Pozna, ruled that in a Torah scroll one should erase all the upper lines and extend them so that they align with the Name, since where it is possible to repair it, it is not considered after the fact. But if there is a Name in the upper lines which may not be erased, it is valid after the fact. This is the language of the Magen Avraham. The same applies to tefillin, where it is forbidden to erase it because that would make it out of sequence: after the fact it is valid. The author of Halachah Berurah made a great error in writing, 'I am astonished, for this is out of sequence'; see there, seif katan 41. See Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah, siman 276, who cites a responsum of the Rambam and writes there in this language: Regarding a Torah scroll in which a heh was found written partly within the line and partly outside the line, what seems proper to me is to replace that sheet and place it in genizah, and after replacing it they may read from it publicly even ab initio. It appears that the Rambam holds it invalid even after the fact. The author of Yad Aharon discussed this at length and concluded that for practical ruling the matter remains uncertain. See Responsa Ginas Veradim, Orach Chaim, klal 2, siman 29, and the book Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 5 of Hilchos Tefillin.
