Magen Avraham, seif katan 18. He emends the text to read, “the first leg.” According to this, the word “or” must be deleted, as Peri Megadim and Machatzis HaShekel also write. Yet according to their explanation, the phrase is superfluous, and the expression “the form of a leg” does not readily support it. See Taz, who writes that one should not emend the text to refer to the letter hei, because regarding a hei the law is that a small letter’s measure must remain. In my humble opinion, one may say that the requirement that a small letter’s measure remain is because otherwise the reading of a child does not help, as the poskim write. Nevertheless, even where a small letter’s measure remains, the letter is valid only if an unlearned child reads it correctly. Otherwise, since the writing is not legible to such a child, there is reason to invalidate it, because this is not the perfect writing required by the Torah, namely writing that is recognizable to everyone from the writing itself. If an unlearned child cannot read it, and only a learned person recognizes it through intellectual inference, that does not help, since the recognition does not arise from the writing itself. Thus, both are required: the measure of a small letter must remain, and the child must read it correctly. In the Gemara’s case where the hei was split by a hole, it appears to me that it was obvious that a child would still read it as a hei, since there was only a thin interruption caused by a hole that formed after the writing. Because the hole was narrow, the lower portion could still be joined to the upper portion, as Beit Yosef writes in the name of Meiri and as Eliyah Rabbah explains. By contrast, where there is a complete break, even a hei must be examined through a child. If the leg of a dalet was severed but a small letter’s measure remains above, it likewise requires examination by a child, just as with a hei. The same law applies where the leg of a chet was severed. Although a small letter’s measure, namely a yud, remains on each side of the chet, that merely preserves the designation of a leg. For it to retain the legal status of writing, however, the child must read it correctly. Now that we have reached this conclusion, it appears to me that Magen Avraham may contain a scribal error, and should read: “hei, chet, dalet.” Rama would then be explaining that when he wrote, “one of the letters was severed,” this includes simple letters, where the break is in the body of the letter, such as a vav or zayin, to which the term “leg” is less applicable. He then says that even in non-simple letters, such as hei, chet, and dalet, if one of their legs is severed, a child’s reading is required. The order hei and chet before dalet may serve as a mnemonic, forming the word “echad.” The phrase “and the like” would include a final kaf. A comparable case also exists among non-simple letters, such as a bet or kaf whose upper or lower side is severed, where one must determine whether the form of a bet or kaf still remains. Since Magen Avraham was very concise regarding the essential ruling, I do not definitively adopt this emendation. Yet if this was indeed his intention, it is a remarkable explanation. The primary question, whether the measure of a small letter suffices without a child’s reading, still requires study. See what I wrote on this matter in Responsa Beit Ephraim, Orach Chaim.
