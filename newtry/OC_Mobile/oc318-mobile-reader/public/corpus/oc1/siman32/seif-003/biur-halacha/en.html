He should write them in black ink. See Rambam, chapter 1 of Hilchos Tefillin, who writes that the halachah transmitted to Moshe at Sinai that they be written in ink was stated only to exclude other kinds of colors, such as red, green, and the like. This implies that anything from which one makes a black appearance like ink is valid, provided only that it endures. Similarly, in Hilchos Sefer Torah he wrote that one of the twenty things that disqualify a Sefer Torah is if it was written without enduring black. If the appearance is similar to the appearance of blue, called blau in German, it seems to my humble opinion that one should be stringent in this. There is proof from Chullin 47b: if it is like blue pigment it is kosher, but if like ink it is tereifah. Although from the Rambam it appears that the halachah was stated only that there be a black appearance, and we clarified below in siman 33 that straps, which also have a halachah transmitted to Moshe at Sinai that they must be black, are sufficient with a blackness like blue, here it is different, since the halachah was that he should write with ink. Even though according to the Rambam ink is not precise, and the same applies to any black appearance, nevertheless its appearance must at least be black like ink. All the more according to the explanation of the Rosh and other poskim, who maintain that actual ink is required, a blue appearance is certainly not included in ink. Yet this requires some study, for if so, if the appearance of the ink resembles the appearance of a raven, it too is not included in ink, as is evident from Niddah 20a that the appearance of ink is blacker than the appearance of a raven, for it says there that black like ink is tamei, but like a raven is included in the thereafter category and is tahor. If so, why does the Midrash say, black like a raven, these are the letters? However, we do not derive halachah from aggados. Alternatively, one may say with difficulty that black like a raven is also black like ink, but like wet ink, and that too is tahor, as is evident there in the Gemara. Even if its nature is to become entirely black after the writing, this requires much examination whether it is valid after the fact, for the halachah was stated that they should write with ink, as appears in the Yerushalmi, and it was not stated that the sacred texts should be written with ink. The wording of the Shulchan Aruch also implies that at the time of writing the ink must be black. One may further explain that the intent of the Shulchan Aruch is any black substance, even gall water and vitriol, unlike Rabbeinu Tam, as the Beis Yosef explained. This requires study.
Whether it has gall water. The Mechaber spoke generally and did not specify from what to make it, because even if it is made from vitriol and gall water, or from gum and gall water, it is valid, since two kinds together are called ink, unlike Rabbeinu Tam, who prohibits this, as the Beis Yosef explained. The Gra explained this below in siman 691, and also in this siman he alluded to it, for therefore the Shulchan Aruch wrote only that it has gall water, and did not write, even if the ink is from gall-nuts alone. With this the difficulty of the Pri Megadim is resolved. Therefore the Rama came and added that ab initio one should be stringent not to make it mixed with vitriol or gum, because that is writing that cannot be erased, as was explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 271, seif 6, that only after the fact, if they wrote with gall water and vitriol, it is valid. Gum too is writing that cannot be erased, as appears in the Mishnah in Sotah 17. This is also evident in Rambam, chapter 1 of Hilchos Tefillin, halachah 4; see there. The intent of the Rama is not to say that ab initio it must specifically be from smoke and not from the trees themselves, and see Magen Avraham and Eliyah Rabbah, for in Yoreh Deah, siman 271, the Rama himself wrote that ab initio it is better to make it from something that comes from a tree. Afterward, with Hashem’s help, I found in Olas Tamid that he explained it this way. Alternatively, one may say that the Rama’s intent is to exclude what the Beis Yosef wrote generally, black ink, which implies any black substance, since if it endures and is recognizable on the parchment it is ink, as he wrote in the Beis Yosef in the name of Rabbeinu Simchah. Therefore the Rama came and added that ab initio one should be stringent like Rabbeinu Tam, that it must specifically come from a tree.
Whether it does not, etc. See below in siman 691 and in Yoreh Deah, siman 271, where the Shulchan Aruch wrote that if one wrote with gall water and vitriol, it is valid. The Pri Megadim wrote that since he did not mention gum, this implies that even if he did not put gum in them, it is valid. See further in his words, where he is uncertain in practice whether this is specifically through both of them together, since there is no gum in them, or even with each one alone one can make ink, since it is black. The Gra, in his commentary in this siman and in siman 691, rules that from gall water alone or from vitriol alone it is invalid even after the fact, for it is not called ink except from two kinds together, gall water and gum, or gall water and vitriol. Regarding gum and vitriol together without a mixture of gall water, or smoke of wood and oils, the opinion of the Gra is not clear to me. The Gaon Rabbi Mordechai Banet, in his commentary on the Mordechai, is stringent ab initio, and with vitriol alone or gum alone he is stringent even after the fact, like the opinion of the Gra. In Responsa Mishkenos Yaakov, siman 37, he is stringent even with gall water and vitriol together without a mixture of gum; see there. Although after the fact one certainly should not be stringent to invalidate gall water and vitriol together against the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch in siman 691 and in Yoreh Deah, siman 271, nevertheless ab initio one certainly should be careful according to his words, so that if he makes ink from gall water, even if he mixes it with vitriol, he should not make it without a mixture of gum. The same applies that gall water and gum alone is also perfectly acceptable. See there in Responsa Mishkenos Yaakov, who brings many poskim as support for his words. He also wrote there in the responsum that ab initio one should be precise to make it through cooking, according to the opinion of Ramban and the Ran, since it is possible.

If it is the first letter, or if he wrote all of it this way and afterward wants, in tefillin or mezuzah, to pass a quill over them in order, with ink, it requires study whether this helps. See Pri Megadim, Mishbetzos Zahav, letter 15. The Chasam Sofer in Yoreh Deah, siman 256, ruled that because of doubt it is invalid, for this is included in ink over red pigment. See Gittin 19a. The law is with the Eliyah Rabbah, not like the Pri Megadim, who questions this; see there.
Gold dust. The same applies to other kinds of colors placed upon the ink, for this disqualifies it from being written, and here the remedy of passing ink over it is not relevant. Even if afterward he wants to pass ink over the colors and the gold, the Olas Tamid and the Eliyah Rabbah ruled that it does not help. This is what he wrote afterward, that for a letter of a Divine Name there is no remedy, meaning that even if he wants to cover it with ink from above it is invalid. The Pri Megadim rejected this proof; see there. In truth, in my humble opinion, this requires examination, whether there is even doubtful invalidity here like ink over color, since the lower writing and also the upper writing are writing in ink. This is not the place to elaborate.
He removes the gold. The Perishah wrote: see Yoreh Deah, siman 276, where I wrote in the name of Rivash that one should be concerned for speckled writing if there are many letters like this on several leaves. So too wrote the Lechem Chamudos. The Eliyah Rabbah challenged them, for the Rivash is not at all referring to the words of the Nimukei Yosef when one threw gold onto the ink, since in that case the issue of speckled writing does not apply, because the writing underneath remains. Rather, the Rivash refers to the basic law where one wrote with gold or with colors themselves, then scraped them off and wrote them with ink; in that case the issue of speckled writing applies. He elaborated on this. See Birkei Yosef, siman 276, who agreed with the words of the Eliyah Rabbah, and so too the Bnei Yonah.
