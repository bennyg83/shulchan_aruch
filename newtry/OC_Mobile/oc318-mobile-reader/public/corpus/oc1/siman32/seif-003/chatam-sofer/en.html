There, in Magen Avraham, se'if katan 24: 'It is permitted to scrape it.' Note: See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Orach Chayyim, siman 178. He was asked about a Sefer Torah containing an error: a final kaf was written in place of a hei. The scribe scraped away part of the final kaf until it became a dalet, then added a left leg inside it to form a hei. The location of this correction could no longer be found, and it was unknown where it was. The questions were whether this was invalid as chok tochot (forming a letter by scraping around it rather than writing it) and, if invalid, whether, since the letter's location was lost, the Sefer Torah could nevertheless be read publicly despite effectively lacking that letter. Regarding the first question he concludes: 'In our case it may be invalid according to all opinions, because the only element corrected through writing was the hei's left leg, which is not one body with the rest of the hei. Even if we say that regarding an open mem, writing only the projecting stroke suffices for the entire mem, although two letters had visibly existed, and the correction of one helps the other, that is because they are at least one body. A hei and kuf, however, consist of two bodies. Perhaps one must correct through writing the same body that was formed improperly, and correcting the leg cannot remedy the dalet that was formed by scraping. This requires further study.' Regarding the second question he discusses at length whether the unidentified letter is nullified, and concludes: 'At least according to the essential Torah law it is nullified and the invalidity returns to validity; only rabbinically does an important item not become nullified. We may therefore combine Rambam's responsum and the reported practice that the geonim read from a Sefer Torah containing one invalid letter with Rashba and Ran's view that an error invalidates only that chumash, etc. Here, since the letter is already nullified under the basic law and the scroll has returned to validity, we can combine these two views. To avoid consigning the scroll to genizah, it appears to me that one may rely upon the foregoing, read from it publicly, and recite a blessing over it.' Examine the responsum itself carefully, and see Chatam Sofer, Yoreh De'ah, siman 277.
