All its passages. Meaning the passages of the hand tefillin, since they are written on one piece of parchment and the concepts of open and closed passages apply to them. Thus, if he leaves a blank space of nine letters at the end of the last line of the first passage and begins the following passage at the beginning of the line on the second column, the following passage is called open, just as in a Torah scroll a passage beginning at the start of the line is called open according to all opinions when there is a nine-letter space before it in the preceding line or at the end of the last line of the previous column. But in the head tefillin, where the passages are written on four parchments, one need not be particular about open and closed passages. Nevertheless, the later authorities write that the custom is ab initio to be particular about this. See Biur Halacha for what he wrote in the name of Pri Megadim regarding this.
Open, except etc. Because the first three passages, Kadesh, Vehayah Ki Yeviacha, and Shema, are open in the Torah, while the passage Vehayah Im Shamoa is closed. Therefore, in tefillin too one must write them that way: one leaves a blank space of nine letters at the end of the last line of the passage Kadesh, so that the passage Vehayah Ki Yeviacha, which begins at the start of the first line on the second column, will be open as it is in the Torah. Likewise after the passage Vehayah Ki Yeviacha, so that Shema will be open. But the passage Vehayah Im Shamoa should be made closed, and the manner of closing it will be explained below. The passage Kadesh, though in the Torah it is open because a nine-letter space precedes it on the line before it, and here this cannot apply because it is the first passage, is nevertheless called open, since it begins at the head of a line and nothing written before it closes it.
And if he changed. Whether he made a closed passage open or an open passage closed.
Some validate. Meaning after the fact. Their reason is that although in the Torah the passage Vehayah Im Shamoa is closed, nevertheless, since in the Torah it is not adjacent to the passage Shema at all, it is not considered a change if the space between it and Shema is open rather than closed as the space before it is closed in the Torah, since in the Torah too there is a large open space between it and the Torah’s passage of Shema. The later authorities agreed with this.
All of them open. Conversely, if one made any of the first three passages closed, it is invalid according to all opinions; so the later authorities wrote. They further wrote that specifically if one made the passage Vehayah Im Shamoa open, it is valid according to this view. But if there is no space at all before it, neither on its own column nor on the column of the passage Shema, meaning that he began it at the start of the top line of the column and left no space before it on that line or at the end of the line of the passage Shema, or he left space but not the amount of a passage break, namely enough to write nine letters, this is considered a complete change from the way it is written in the Torah and is invalid according to all opinions, even for this passage. At the end of the passage Vehayah Im Shamoa no space need be left, and the custom is to finish the words “upon the land” at the end of the bottom line.
And in these countries, etc. Although ab initio it is a mitzvah to make it closed even according to those who validate, so that this custom is not proper, nevertheless the Rama copied it because the main law is that after the fact it is valid in every manner, and it is impossible to make it closed according to all opinions; therefore one should uphold this custom so as not to cast aspersions on earlier authorities. So Magen Avraham wrote in the name of Lechem Chamudos. But from the words of Levush and the Gra it appears that it is better to do as written in the Shulchan Aruch.
The custom is also, etc. Likewise, in Rabbeinu Tam tefillin one should also make all of them open. Although he writes Vehayah Im Shamoa after Shema, as explained below in siman 34, Mishnah Berurah seif katan 3, he leaves the measure of nine letters after the words “upon the land,” and when writing the passage Shema he begins at the beginning of the column at the head of the line.
One begins at the head, etc. For this makes it open according to all opinions, when he left a blank space of nine letters at the end of the bottom line of the preceding column. If he did not begin at the head of the line, but left a small space, even less than nine letters, this passage is called closed according to the Rambam and is invalid even after the fact. Pri Megadim, letter 26, wrote that even if at the beginning of Kadesh, Vehayah Ki Yeviacha, or Shema he did not move inward by more than one or two letters, one could say that it is invalid, but this requires study. In Maamar Mordechai it seems that one need not be stringent after the fact. In any event, ab initio one must be very careful about this. If he left the space of the word “asher,” it seems to me that one should be stringent even after the fact, for this measure certainly contains six small letters or more, as Machatzis HaShekel wrote, and according to the Taz it legally joins, according to all opinions, to be called closed even if the upper and lower spaces together are only the measure of nine small letters, as Pri Megadim wrote. Thus, the Rambam’s phrase that one should leave a little space means a very small measure. Therefore, if one sees that the passage began even one letter inward from the place originally scored, he should arrange all the other lines in this manner as well.
Nine letters. The later authorities wrote that ab initio one must leave the measure of three instances of the word “asher.” Therefore, one must also leave a full space of two small letters in addition to the nine letters, for between each word one must leave the space of a small letter. After the fact, one may be lenient when he left only the space of nine small letters, namely yuds, as implied by the Shulchan Aruch here. Pri Megadim wrote that the nine-letter space is measured according to that script. This entire measure applies both to an open passage and to a closed passage.
Closed according to the Rambam. Although according to the Rosh it is open, nevertheless we do this because in tefillin it is impossible to make a closed passage in a form that satisfies all opinions. The closed passage we customarily make in a Torah scroll, namely that one finishes the preceding passage in the middle of a line, leaves a space of nine letters, and then begins the following passage on that same line, is called closed according to all opinions, because it is closed before and after. But this cannot be done here in tefillin, where each passage is on a separate column. Similarly, if one wanted to leave one blank line at the beginning of Vehayah Im Shamoa and begin it at the head of the second line, this is also not closed according to all opinions; it is only closed according to the Rosh, not according to the Rambam, for according to him it is open. Since it cannot be made closed according to all opinions, the custom followed the Rambam, for that is the main rule even in a Torah scroll when one cannot satisfy all opinions, as written in Yoreh Deah, siman 275. The Taz devised a way to make it closed according to all opinions: in the passage Vehayah Ki Yeviacha he should do everything as written in the Shulchan Aruch, except that at the end of the passage Shema he should leave less than nine small letters, and likewise leave less than nine small letters at the beginning of Vehayah Im Shamoa. Since there are not nine letters in one place, but only by combining the two spaces, it is called closed according to all opinions. So too wrote Responsa Rama MiPano and the Biur of the Gra, and Pri Megadim also implies that he practiced this way. Know that after the fact, even if one left a space of nine large letters on each side, it is also valid, for according to the Rambam it is closed and according to the Rosh it is open, and we wrote above in seif katan 158, in the name of the later authorities, that they agreed that the halachah follows those who validate. Some great authorities practiced this way ab initio; each river follows its own course. See Biur Halacha.
