136 — He shall make, etc. As they are in the Torah, as written regarding mezuzah, 31–32.

And if he changed — invalid. As written in Shabbat 103b and in Mishnat Sofrim, chapter 1, halachah 14: an open one he made closed, a closed one he made open — it shall be stored away.

And some validate, etc. As written regarding mezuzah there — Ran says it is a mitzvah, etc., and the reason as written there: since they are not adjacent, etc.; if so, the same here. And meaning: bedi'eved, as written there. The Rambam's and Shulchan Arukh's words are difficult; also the rav who wrote "in countries," etc., is difficult. And Orach Meir: two reasons — because it is impossible to do closed according to all, and his words are not understood.

And therefore, etc. This is the Rambam's language, chapter 8: An open parashah has two forms: if he finished in the middle of a line and space remains as required, he leaves it and begins at the start of a second line; and if space remains, or if he finished at the end of a line, he leaves a blank line and begins at the start of a third line. A closed parashah has three forms: if he finished in the middle of a line and space remains as required and enough to write one word at the end of the line so the space is in the middle — he leaves it; if the above space did not remain, he leaves all blank and leaves a small space at the start of the second line and begins from the middle of the line; if he finished at the end of a line, he leaves at the start of the second line space as required and begins from the middle of the line. Thus an open parashah always begins at the start of a line, and a closed one always from the middle; and the space, whether in open or closed, is always nine letters. In chapter 6 he wrote "like three asher," except there he wrote that for the mitzvah; if he changed, he does not disqualify. Beit Yosef in Yoreh De'ah, siman 275, explained: specifically three asher we do not require, but nine small letters we certainly require even for validity; so too Sefer HaTerumah and Mordechai — the measure is explicit for closed and open: nine letters; so in Mishnat Sofrim: space between parashah and parashah — three words of three letters each. All the Rambam's words above are built on Mishnat Sofrim at the end of chapter 1, where the precise text that Beit Yosef brought there from Ri'ak appears: Which is open? Whatever began at the head of the line — how much he leaves at the end and begins at the head of the line — is called open, enough to write three words of three letters; if the parashah ended at the end of the line, he leaves one line; if at the end of the page, he leaves a line at the start of the page; if a line remained at the end of the page, he begins from its start. Which is closed? Whatever began from the middle of the line — how much space in the middle — is called closed, enough for three words. There is a textual error in Beit Yosef where he wrote and explained Rashi "closed," etc.; it should be as written, and in our Mishnat Sofrim it is confused, as Mordechai wrote. Tosafot 32a, s.v. vehaidna, and the Rosh there, and the Tur, siman 275 — disagree with the Rambam per ancient arrangement; they copied Mishnat Sofrim as before us. They agree with the Rambam on the first form of open and first form of closed; on the other forms they argue: the second form of open for them is closed, and the other two forms of closed for them are open. They brought proof from Yerushalmi, chapter 1 of Megillah: open from its head — open; from its end — open; from here and here — closed; and they strained themselves on the end "all the more from here and here," that it is open, and explained there. But the Rambam explains: open from its head means open from head to end; now Yerushalmi's words are per their plain sense, and they are themselves the two open forms the Rambam wrote, and likewise the Rambam's second closed, and certainly the other two which are closed, as Ri'ak wrote there: the rule for the Rambam — whatever begins at the start of the line is open, in the middle closed. For the Rosh, whatever is open on one side, and certainly from here and here, is open; whatever has its gap in the middle, or a whole blank line which is like the middle — for him is closed. They also disagree on the measure of space; in this the poskim agreed with the Rambam, as Beit Yosef and Shulchan Arukh wrote there. One who fears Heaven should fulfill both and make the first form of open and of closed, on which there is no dispute. Darkhei Moshe and Hagahah there: if he can aim the space — good; if not, he should not deviate from the Rambam's words, for so in old books and in Sefer Ezra. Here for open it is possible; for closed it is not — therefore he wrote here "and therefore the custom," etc. So too they practiced for mezuzah if the Shema begins in the middle of a line; Mordechai, siman 690, in the name of Rabbi Yehudah — this is closed from what they write in mezuzah; thus Yerushalmi is explained another way, which he extended; at the end he wrote in Semag's explanation of Yerushalmi: open from here and here, closed — meaning he did not leave in one place the full measure but combined both as measure; thus one need not strain what Tosafot strained above; so too the Rosh in laws of mezuzah: why does it say "now the world practices in closed," etc. — he copied the Yerushalmi above; Rav ruled to make open as Tosafot copied Yerushalmi "there are tannaim," etc.; the Rosh wrote "and so the world practices" — meaning according to him open at the head is open. He wrote it is good not to leave the measure at the end of the line nor at the head, to fulfill "now the world," etc. The Rambam also admits one need not leave space before it except if he finished at the end of a line — thus one fulfills all; so too Taz.
