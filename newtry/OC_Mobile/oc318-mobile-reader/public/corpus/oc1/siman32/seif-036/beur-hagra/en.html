And if he changed it, it is invalid. As written in Shabbos 103b and in Maseches Sofrim, chapter 1, halachah 14: an open parsha that he made closed, or a closed parsha that he made open, must be placed in genizah.
And there are those who validate, etc. As written regarding mezuzah there: Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak said, it is a mitzvah, etc.; and the reason is as stated there, since they are not adjacent, etc. If so, the same applies here. Meaning, this is after the fact, as written there and there. The words of the Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch require study, and also the words of the Rav, who wrote, and in these countries, etc., require study. See Magen Avraham, who answered that it is because it is impossible to make a closed parsha according to all opinions; but his words are not understood.
Therefore, etc. This is the language of the Rambam at the beginning of chapter 8: An open parsha has two forms. If he finished in the middle of the line and there remained a space of the required measure, he leaves it and begins at the start of the second line. If the required measure did not remain, or if he finished at the end of the line, he leaves one line without writing and begins at the start of the third line. A closed parsha has three forms. If he finished in the middle of the line and there remained a space of the required measure, and enough to write one word at the end of the line so that the space is in the middle, he leaves it. If the above measure did not remain, he leaves the entire rest of the line empty, leaves a small space at the beginning of the second line, and begins from the middle of the line. If he finished at the end of the line, he leaves a space of the required measure at the beginning of the second line and begins from the middle of the line. It follows that an open parsha always begins at the beginning of the line, and a closed parsha always begins from the middle of the line; and the space, whether in an open parsha or a closed parsha, is always the measure of nine letters. In chapter 6 he wrote: like three occurrences of the word asher, except that there he wrote this as the ideal manner, and that if one changed it, it is not invalid. The Beit Yosef explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 275, that specifically three occurrences of asher are not required, but nine small letters are certainly required even indispensably. So too wrote the Sefer HaTerumah and the Mordechai: the measure is explicit, both for a closed parsha and for an open parsha, nine letters. So too it is in Maseches Sofrim: the space between one parsha and another is three words of three letters each. All the above words of the Rambam are built on Maseches Sofrim, for at the end of chapter 1 appears the precise version that the Beit Yosef brought there in the name of Rabbi Yosef ibn Chaviv: Which is an open parsha? Any parsha that begins at the head of the line. How much should he leave at the end and begin at the head of the line, so that it is called open? Enough to write three words of three letters from a name. If he completed the parsha at the end of the line, he leaves one line. If he completed at the end of the column, he leaves a line at the beginning of the column. If one line remained at the end of the column, he begins at its beginning. And which is a closed parsha? Any parsha that begins from the middle of the line. How much should he leave in the middle of the line, a space, so that it is called closed? Enough to write three words. There is a scribal error there in the Beit Yosef, where he wrote, and Rashi explained closed, etc.; it should read as I wrote. In our Maseches Sofrim it is written in a corrupted form, as the Mordechai wrote; see there. Tosafos, 32a, s.v. and nowadays, the Rosh there, and the Tur at the beginning of siman 275, disagree with the Rambam on the basis of the arrangement of the early authorities, and they copied Maseches Sofrim as it appears before us. They agree with the Rambam regarding the first form of an open parsha and the first form of a closed parsha, but regarding the other forms they disagree: the second form of an open parsha according to them is closed, and the last two forms of a closed parsha according to them are open. They brought proof from the Yerushalmi, first chapter of Megillah: open at its beginning is open; open at its end is open; open on both sides is closed. They pressed themselves regarding the last clause, for all the more so if it is open on both sides it should be open, and they explained it there; see there. But the Rambam explains: open at its beginning is open, meaning that it is open from its beginning to its end; and now the words of the Yerushalmi are according to their plain sense. These themselves are the two open forms that the Rambam wrote, and so too the Rambam’s second closed form, and all the more so the other two forms, are closed. As Rabbi Yosef ibn Chaviv wrote there, the rule according to the Rambam is: anything that begins at the beginning of the line is open, and anything that begins in the middle is closed. According to the Rosh, anything open on one side, and certainly on both sides, is open; and anything whose space is in its middle, or a whole line, which for him is like the middle, is closed. They also disagree with him regarding the measure of the space; and in this the poskim agreed with the Rambam. The Beit Yosef and Shulchan Aruch wrote there that a G-d-fearing person should fulfill both opinions and make the first form of an open parsha and the first form of a closed parsha, for in this there is no disagreement. The Darchei Moshe and the gloss there wrote: if he can align it, that is good, but if not, he should not move from the words of the Rambam, for so it is in the ancient scrolls and also in the scroll of Ezra; see there. Now here, regarding an open parsha, it is possible to do so, but regarding a closed parsha it is impossible; therefore he wrote here: therefore the practice is, etc. So too is the practice in a mezuzah, that the parsha of vehayah im shamoa begins in the middle of the line. The Mordechai, siman 960, likewise proved in the name of Rabbeinu Yitzchak that this is a closed parsha from the fact that we write a mezuzah this way, and therefore he explained the Yerushalmi in another way; see there, where he elaborated. At the end he wrote that the Semag explained what is written in the Yerushalmi, open on both sides is closed, to mean that he did not leave the required measure in one place, but only by combining the two spaces to the required measure. According to this, there is no need for the forced explanation that Tosafos gave above. So too wrote the Rosh in Hilchos Mezuzah there, regarding what it says that nowadays the world practices with closed parshiyos, etc. He copied the words of the above Yerushalmi, that Rav ruled to make it open, as the above Tosafos copied the Yerushalmi’s words, there are Tannaim, etc. The Rosh wrote: and so the world practices, meaning according to his view, that open at its beginning is open, as above. He wrote that it is good not to leave the required measure at the end of the line nor at the beginning of the line, in order to fulfill what was stated, and nowadays, etc. Now the Rambam too would agree to this, for he only said that the required measure must precede it if one finished at the end of the line. Through this, one fulfills all opinions. So too wrote the Taz.
