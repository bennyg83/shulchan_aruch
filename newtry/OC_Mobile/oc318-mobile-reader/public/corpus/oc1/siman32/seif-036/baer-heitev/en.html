Invalid. See the Taz, who explains the Rambam's view from the chapter HaKometz and proves that even after the fact a closed passage is required. His words are somewhat difficult, but their substance is this: perhaps the inference, 'we may not lower them,' but if one did lower them they may be made, refers to tefillin; therefore, he says, it is certain that in tefillin too, if the passages are open, it is invalid. In my humble opinion this does not seem correct. For if so, regarding the other question too let us say the same, where the Gemara shortly thereafter asks: 'But if one lowered them, they may be made,' yet they require ruling lines. According to his view, what is difficult? The Rambam ruled in chapter 7 that a Torah scroll requires ruling lines, so let us likewise say that the reason for 'we may not lower them' refers to a Torah scroll. Rather, one must say that the Talmud does not accept this reasoning, for if so, why mention tefillin at all? If so, here too, if it referred to tefillin alone, why would the Mishnah mention a Torah scroll at all? This is the proof from 'shall we say this supports.' With this Rashi there is resolved, for in his comment there, 'here closed in a Torah scroll, here open in a mezuzah,' why did he first mention a Torah scroll and not tefillin? It must be that regarding tefillin one may say that open passages are valid after the fact, so the difficulty is not from tefillin; the difficulty is only from a Torah scroll. But regarding ruling lines, Rashi wrote, 'and tefillin do not require ruling lines,' mentioning tefillin alone, to indicate that the question is why tefillin were mentioned. Examine this carefully.
Kadesh. I will explain before you the source of the dispute so that it will be clear. We have already mentioned that three passages must be made open, namely Kadesh, Vehayah Ki Yevi'acha, and Shema, while the fourth passage, Vehayah Im Shamoa, must be closed. The dispute concerns the form of an open and a closed passage. According to the Rosh, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 275, whenever blank space is left on a line and one begins writing in the middle of the line, and likewise where one writes and leaves blank space at the end of a line and begins the next passage at the start of the next line, this is an open passage; the measure of the blank space is enough to write nine letters. A closed passage is when one leaves blank space in the middle of a line in that measure and begins writing at the end of that same line. The same applies if he finished the passage at the end of a line: he leaves one blank line and begins on the third line. The Rambam wrote as follows: An open passage has two forms. If he finished in the middle of the line, he leaves the rest of the line blank in the above measure and begins the open passage at the start of the second line. This is like the Rosh. If there is not enough blank space remaining, or he finished at the end of the line, he leaves the next line blank and begins the open passage at the start of the third line. According to the Rosh, this is closed. A closed passage has three forms. If he finished in the middle, he leaves the required space and begins at the end of that same line with one word. This is like the Rosh. If there is not enough space left for nine letters and to write one word at the end, he leaves the rest empty, leaves a small space at the beginning of the second line, and begins writing the closed passage from the middle of the second line. It is possible that the Rosh also agrees to this. If he finished at the end of the line, he leaves at the beginning of the second line the required measure of space and begins writing the closed passage from the middle of the line. According to the Rosh, this is open. It follows that, according to the Rambam, an open passage always begins at the beginning of a line, and a closed passage always begins from the middle of a line. The Beit Yosef wrote: How should one make tefillin so as to satisfy all opinions? If one makes the three passages open by leaving the required space at their beginning and beginning the passage in the middle of the line, he has not satisfied the Rambam, who holds that beginning in the middle of a line is called closed. And if he leaves blank space at the end of every passage, then even the last passage is open, for whenever there is blank space at the end of the passage, it makes the following passage open. Therefore the practice in Eretz Yisrael, where they rule like the Rambam, is as follows: the first two passages begin at the head of the column and finish at the end of the column in the middle of a line. The third passage, Shema, begins at the head of the column and ends at its end, and the fourth passage begins in the middle of a line. Thus the first three passages are open according to the Rambam. The first, even though there is no space before it, is called open, as Bereshis is plainly open according to the Rambam. The two passages after the first are made open by the required blank space before them, meaning the space at the end of the prior passage makes the following passage open, and it begins at the head of the line; this works both according to the Rambam and according to the Rosh. The only issue is that beginning the fourth passage in the middle of a line is called open according to the Rosh, whereas we need it closed; but according to the Rambam it is closed. With this the words of the Mechaber are explained. The Taz wrote that there is another way before us to satisfy both the Rambam and the Rosh. First one must introduce what the Rambam wrote in the second form of a closed passage: if not enough of the line remains to leave the required space and to write one word at the end of the line, he leaves it all empty, leaves a small space at the beginning of the second line, and so on. I wrote there that it is possible the Rosh also agrees to this. Therefore one who wishes to do so should make it this way: the first and second passages begin at the head of the column and leave at the end blank space sufficient for nine letters. The third passage also begins at the head of the column, but leaves less than nine letters at the end. The fourth passage should also leave some space at the start of its column, but less than nine letters. The fourth passage is then closed according to both the Rambam and the Rosh: according to the Rambam it is called beginning in the middle, and according to the Rosh there is no open passage here, since there is no one place with the required measure of blank space. One cannot say that he should leave no space at all at the end of the third passage and leave less than the required measure at the beginning of the fourth, for in any case we need a space of nine letters between one passage and another, whether closed or open. Here, however, it cannot be made in one place, for then it would be open; therefore we divide the measure into two parts. This is the language of the Taz. So too wrote the Rama of Fano, siman 106. See Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah, glosses to Beit Yosef, seif 26. One should not, because of this doubt, make two pairs of tefillin. The Beit Yosef wrote that one great authority invalidated all the tefillin in his country that were made according to the Rosh, because their Torah scrolls had their passages arranged according to the Rambam; if so, one who ascends to the Torah with tefillin on his head is self-contradictory. See Magen Avraham. I said I would seek wisdom to know what should be done in Rabbeinu Tam tefillin regarding open and closed passages, since the fourth passage precedes the third. According to the Beit Yosef, this is clear: the passages Kadesh and Vehayah Ki Yevi'acha begin at the head of the line. At the end of Kadesh Li one leaves blank space of nine letters; at the end of Vehayah Ki Yevi'acha one leaves no space, or if one leaves space, it is less than nine letters. The passage Vehayah Im Shamoa begins in the middle of the upper line, with blank space of nine letters before it, and at the end of Vehayah Im Shamoa one leaves blank space of nine letters. The passage Shema begins at the head of the line. Thus Kadesh Li, Vehayah Ki Yevi'acha, and Shema are open according to both the Rambam and the Rosh, and Vehayah Im Shamoa is closed according to the Rambam. According to the Taz, Kadesh Li and Vehayah Ki Yevi'acha begin at the head of the line; at the end of Kadesh Li one leaves blank space of nine letters. At the end of Vehayah Ki Yevi'acha one leaves blank space of less than nine letters. For Vehayah Im Shamoa one leaves at its beginning a space of less than nine letters, and at its end leaves blank space of nine letters; the passage Shema begins at the head of the line. This is straightforward. However, this requires study in light of what the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 34, seif katan 2: nevertheless, they must be written in order, as written in siman 32, seif 1; therefore one must leave space for the passage Vehayah Im Shamoa, write the passage Shema at the edge of the parchment, and afterward write Vehayah Im Shamoa in the middle. If so, when according to the Taz he leaves at the end of Vehayah Ki Yevi'acha less than nine letters, or according to the Beit Yosef he leaves no space at all, and afterward he begins to write Shema at the edge of the parchment at the head of the line, this is open according to the Rambam, but closed according to the Rosh. See the Rambam's second form of open passages. After further consideration I saw that this is nothing, for once Vehayah Im Shamoa is later placed above it, it will then be corrected and become open; and this helps, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 275, seif 1 in the gloss. See the Rama of Fano, siman 37, and see Olas Tamid and Responsa Rama, siman 25.
