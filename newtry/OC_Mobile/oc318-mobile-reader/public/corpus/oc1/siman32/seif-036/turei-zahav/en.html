Although the reason it is closed in the Torah is that it relates to what precedes it, unlike in tefillin, nevertheless he should write it as it is in the Torah. The Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Rambam that if he made a closed parsha open, or the reverse, they are invalid, and that the first three parshiyos in tefillin are all open and the last parsha is closed. He raised a difficulty: in HaKometz 32, regarding the parshiyos of mezuzos, we make them closed, but if he made them open they are valid, because in the Torah they are not adjacent. If so here with tefillin, granted Kadesh and VeHaya Ki Yevi’acha, which are written in the order in which they are written in the Torah, must also here be open. But Shema and VeHaya Im Shamoa are not adjacent in the Torah, so why should it be invalid if he made VeHaya Im Shamoa an open parsha? The Beit Yosef answered that since with some parshiyos of tefillin, if he changed them they are invalid, we do not distinguish among them. This is forced, for from where does the Rambam know this? And the Maharshal in responsum siman 37 explained that when the Rambam says “invalid,” he means that he invalidated them from their essential proper arrangement initially. It appears to me to explain the Rambam’s view, that he proved this from an explicit Talmudic passage. Necessarily there is a difference between tefillin and mezuzos regarding the parsha of VeHaya Im Shamoa, for in tefillin we require it to be closed even after the fact. This is from HaKometz, where Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak said: It is a mitzvah for a mezuzah to be with closed parshiyos, but if one made them open, this is acceptable. The Gemara suggests: Shall we say this supports him, for similarly, worn-out sifrei Torah and tefillin may not be made into a mezuzah, because one may not lower from a severe sanctity to a lighter one. This implies that if one were allowed to lower, one could make them into a mezuzah. But why? Here they are closed, and here, in a mezuzah, they are open. Rather, necessarily, even a mezuzah is with closed parshiyos. The Rambam inferred that this proof, “one may not lower,” must also refer to tefillin, for if it referred only to a sefer Torah, the Gemara could answer that indeed one may not make a mezuzah from a sefer Torah because in a sefer Torah they are closed and in a mezuzah they must be open, while the reason given, that one may not lower, refers to tefillin, that one may not make a mezuzah from them even though it would also be valid with open parshiyos. Therefore it gave one reason for both, namely that one may not lower. Necessarily, then, tefillin too require closed parshiyos as in a sefer Torah and mezuzah. And if you say that with open parshiyos it is valid after the fact, why did the Gemara not say: Shall we say there is a support from tefillin, that the reason is that one may not lower, but if one may lower then one could make them; but why, tefillin are closed and a mezuzah is open? This would have supported that after the fact it is valid when open. Since it did not answer so, but gave a forced answer there, we learn that in tefillin it is invalid if open even after the fact. This appears correct according to the Rambam.
And some validate, etc. I will explain before you the source of the dispute so that the practical halacha may become clear, with the help of Heaven. We have already mentioned that one must make the first three parshiyos open, namely Kadesh, VeHaya Ki Yevi’acha, and Shema, and the fourth parsha, VeHaya Im Shamoa, closed. However, there is a dispute over the form of an open and closed parsha. According to the Rosh, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 275, whenever there is blank space in the line and one begins writing in the middle of the line, and likewise when one writes and leaves blank space at the end of the line and begins the following parsha at the beginning of the second line, this is open. The measure of the blank space is enough to write nine letters. Closed means wherever he leaves blank space in the middle of the line to that measure and begins to write at the end of that very line; the same applies if he finished the parsha at the end of the line, he leaves one blank line and begins on the second line. The Rambam wrote as follows: An open parsha has two forms. If he finished in the middle of the line, he leaves the rest of the line blank to the above measure and begins the open parsha at the beginning of the second line. This is like the Rosh. If there did not remain blank space to the required measure, or he finished at the end of the line, he leaves the following line blank and begins the open parsha at the beginning of the third line. According to the Rosh this is closed. A closed parsha has three forms. If he finished in the middle, he leaves a space to the required measure and begins one word at the end of the line. This is like the Rosh. If there did not remain enough for nine letters and to write one word at the end, he leaves it all blank, leaves a small space from the beginning of the second line, and begins writing the closed parsha from the middle of the second line. Possibly the Rosh also agrees to this. If he finished at the end of the line, he leaves at the beginning of the second line the required space and begins writing from the middle of the line a closed parsha. According to the Rosh this is open. Thus you find that an open parsha always begins at the beginning of a line, and a closed parsha always begins from the middle of a line. End quote. The Beit Yosef wrote that if the tefillin are made as the Tur wrote, namely that he leaves at the end of the line a blank space equal to three words of three letters, or at their beginning, except that the final parsha should be closed, then we have fulfilled the view of the Rosh, who holds that beginning in the middle of a line is called open, but not the view of the Rambam, who holds that beginning in the middle of a line is called closed. If so, if he leaves space at their beginning, all of them are closed, so how could our teacher the Tur write “or at their beginning”? Necessarily, the only validation is if he leaves blank space at the end of each parsha. But then even the last parsha is open, for whenever there is blank space at the end of a parsha, it makes the following parsha open. Therefore throughout the lands of Yishmael, where they follow the Rambam in all their ways, they do as follows: the first two parshiyos begin at the top of the page and end at the end of the page in the middle of a line; the third parsha, Shema, begins at the top of the page and ends at its end; and the fourth parsha begins in the middle of the line. Thus the first three parshiyos, according to the Rambam, are open, for the first, although there is no blank space before it, is called open like Bereishis, which is plainly open according to the Rambam, as the decisors wrote from his words. The two parshiyos after the first are made open by the measure of blank space before them, namely the space at the end of the preceding parsha, which makes the following parsha open, and it begins at the head of the line; this is so both according to the Rambam and the Rosh. Only the fourth parsha, which begins in the middle of a line, is called open according to the Rosh, and we require it to be closed. But according to the Rambam this is proper, for in this manner it is called closed. In these countries the custom is that all three parshiyos begin at the head of the column and end in the middle of the page, and the fourth parsha also begins at the head of the page and ends at the end of the last line. This is difficult, for it follows no view. According to the Rambam, the fourth parsha is not closed, since it begins at the head of the page. Even according to the Rosh, where we find a closed parsha at the head of a page, namely if he leaves the first line blank and begins from the second line, nevertheless in this manner, where he begins at the head of the page and already left blank space at the end of the third parsha, the fourth parsha is certainly open. One cannot say that the space at the end of the third parsha helps only to make that parsha itself open, but has no effect on the following parsha. Maharam Padua wrote this reasoning in responsum siman 77 in the name of Orchot Chaim, but did not agree to it except to validate tefillin that had already been written and had spread in practice, for the Rambam’s words do not prove this. The Maharshal too, in responsum siman 37, raised many difficulties against this reasoning, and there he nevertheless justified our form on the ground that the Baal HaItur holds that all four parshiyos in tefillin must be open. In this way we also fulfill the view of the Rosh, since after the fact he agrees that even the fourth parsha is valid if open; and even the Rambam, who wrote that it is invalid if the fourth was made open, invalidated it only initially. But according to what I wrote above from the Talmud, that even after the fact the fourth parsha is invalid if open, like the view of the Rambam, it is not proper that we should do this initially and recite a blessing on them every day. The Maharshal also raised a further difficulty, that the words of the Baal HaItur do not appear correct at all in what he wrote, that even the fourth parsha should be open, for this is against the Talmud and the custom we follow with a mezuzah; this view is nullified against all the early and later decisors. Now we have before us another way to satisfy both the Rambam and the Rosh. We must first introduce that which the Rambam wrote in the second form of a closed parsha: if there did not remain in the line enough to leave the required space and write one word at the end of the line, he leaves it all blank, etc. The same applies if this was done at the end of the page, where he leaves a blank space less than the measure of space between parshiyos, namely nine letters and enough to write one word, and begins at the head of the next page, where he also leaves a small blank space less than the above measure. This too is called closed. According to the Rambam, the reason for closed in this case is that since there is not a full measure of blank space in one place at the end of the line, even if you combine it with the additional space before the second parsha, we say it is as though the beginning of the second parsha was written at the end of the line that contains the blank space at the end of the first parsha, and therefore it is fully closed. By contrast, if there is at the end a full measure of space, then the following parsha is a matter unto itself and is open whenever it begins at the head of the line. The same applies at the end of a page together with the space at the head of the following page, for the reason is one. If so, one who wishes to do it in the most correct manner should do as follows: the first and second parshiyos should begin at the head of the page and end at the end with blank space equal to nine letters. But the third parsha should also begin at the head of the page and end with less than the measure, namely less than nine letters, and the fourth parsha should also have at the beginning of the page some blank space, in any case less than the measure. It emerges that the fourth parsha is closed according to everyone: according to the Rambam, this too is called beginning in the middle, and according to the Rosh, there is no open parsha here, since there is no full measure of blank space in one place. But one should not say that he should leave at the end of the third parsha a full measure of space, and nevertheless it is not called a measure regarding the closed parsha that follows because he did not also leave enough blank space to write the first word of the following parsha, as the Rambam wrote explicitly regarding a closed parsha, that there must be space at the end of the parsha to write nine letters and also to write one word. If we did so, we would not fulfill the view of the Rosh, who holds that whenever one leaves the measure of nine letters alone, the following parsha is called open, even though it begins at the very head of the page, and if so the fourth parsha would be open. Nor should one say that he should leave no space at all at the end of the third parsha, and at the beginning of the fourth parsha leave less than the measure at the head of the line, for in any case we require space between one parsha and another equal to nine letters, whether closed or open. Only here it is impossible to make it in one place, for then it would be open; therefore we divide the measure into two parts, as we mentioned, and in this way one satisfies both the Rambam and the Rosh. All the above I have written according to my humble opinion as correct. Although one should not, Heaven forbid, cast doubt on any custom, nevertheless in this matter the Maharshal already wrote that one should not conduct oneself this way from now on. He wrote one form that seemed to him to satisfy also Maseches Soferim and the order of the early authorities brought by the Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah, siman 275. In my humble opinion, one need be concerned only to satisfy the Rambam and the Rosh, who saw all the baraisos and interpreted them according to their view, and it is enough for us to satisfy all. This is exactly like our custom, except that we needed to distance the beginning of the fourth parsha slightly, unlike the form written by the Maharshal, which is an entirely different arrangement. The form I mentioned is also cited in Responsa Maharam Padua there, and he praised it. He wrote that although our Ashkenazic custom that I mentioned above can be justified, nevertheless from now on they should not continue to do so. The Levush wrote: If my strength were sufficient, I would abolish it and institute writing according to the custom of the land of Yishmael. I say that since there is a mitzvah here to change, why should we do according to the way of the land of Yishmael, through which we do not satisfy the view of the Rosh? Therefore one should make a slight change as I wrote, and thereby satisfy all views, as it appears to me.
