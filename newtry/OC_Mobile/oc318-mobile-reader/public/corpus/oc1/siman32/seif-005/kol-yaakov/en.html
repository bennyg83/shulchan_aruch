Se'if 5, he must write with his right hand, etc. And if he wrote with his left hand it is invalid, etc.; this refers to one whose dominant hand is the right. But one who controls both hands, if he wrote with his left hand, it is valid. Magen Avraham, s.k. 4; Eliyah Rabbah, s.k. 10; Halachah Berurah, letter 4; Yeshuos Yaakov, letter 6; GraZ, letter 7; Gra, letter 15; Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger; Keset HaSofer, siman 3, letter 7. Nevertheless, a person of spiritual sensitivity should be concerned for himself, since he lays tefillin on the left arm and therefore should specifically write with the right hand, as I wrote in the holy work Kaf HaChaim, siman 27, letter 31; see there. See further below, siman 271, letter 21.
There, if it is possible to find others, etc. This implies that if it is impossible to find others written with the right hand, he should lay these that were written with the left hand. Regarding the blessing, Beis Shmuel in Even HaEzer, siman 123, s.k. 4, wrote that he should not recite a blessing over them. So too wrote Eliyah Rabbah, letter 10; Yad Ephraim; Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger; Machatzis HaShekel, s.k. 4; Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, s.k. 4; and Keset HaSofer, siman 3, letter 7, unlike Levush.
There, and a left-handed person's left hand is his right. If he wrote with his right hand, it is invalid. Magen Avraham, s.k. 5; Eliyah Rabbah, letter 10; Halachah Berurah, letter 5; GraZ, letter 7; Keset HaSofer, siman 3, letter 7; Imrei Shefer, rule 2, letter 5. This is where it is possible to find others. But if it is impossible to find others, he should lay those that were written with his own right hand, GraZ there, and this too is without a blessing, as written in the previous letter. See the holy work Kaf HaChaim there, where I wrote that even if one writes with his left hand and does all his work with his left hand, and he wrote tefillin with his left hand, which according to the law is permitted, nevertheless a person of spiritual sensitivity should be concerned for himself and should buy tefillin only from one who writes with the right hand and also does all his work with the right hand; see there.
And if he writes with his right hand but does his other work with his left. Or if he writes with his left hand but does his other work with his right, he should not be accepted as a scribe. After the fact, if he wrote, it is valid, like one who controls both hands. Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, s.k. 5; Keset HaSofer, siman 3, letter 7; and Imrei Shefer, rule 2, letter 5, wrote likewise. But in the name of Shenot Chaim he wrote that even at the outset he may be accepted as a scribe, and so it appears in Yeshuos Yaakov, letter 6. Nevertheless, he must write with the right hand, as stated above, letter 22.
Rema MiFano wrote. In siman 38, he reports an incident involving one whose hand was amputated, who held the quill with his lips and wrote Torah scrolls, tefillin, and mezuzos, and he invalidated them because this is not the normal way of writing. Olas Tamid, letter 17, cites this and writes that the same applies to anything similar, for it is not the way of writing; even if there are no others, one should not recite a blessing over tefillin written in this manner. This implies specifically that one does not recite a blessing over them, but one may lay them without a blessing if it is impossible to find others. But Magen Avraham, s.k. 5, wrote in the name of Rema MiFano that he invalidated them even if it is impossible to find others. So too wrote Halachah Berurah, letter 5; GraZ, letter 7; and Keset HaSofer, siman 3, letter 7. Their words imply that if it is impossible to find others, he should not lay them even without a blessing. See Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger, who wrote concerning Magen Avraham's words: I did not find this there, but rather Rema MiFano learned this law from the law of writing with the left hand; since we maintain that if one wrote with the left hand and there are no other tefillin, they are laid without a blessing, one can say that the same applies to writing with the mouth; see there.
