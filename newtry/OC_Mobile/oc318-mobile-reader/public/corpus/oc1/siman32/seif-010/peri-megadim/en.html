If. See Taz. The word “severed” can be understood in two ways: perforated, and this is intelligible only if it became perforated afterward; or the ink was split, or there was a thin scratch, or the scribe initially wrote a broken vav, in which case a child’s reading helps. The Taz wrote that the lower part does not combine, and the upper part alone must have enough that one can reasonably be uncertain, and then a child’s reading helps. But if we recognize that the upper part does not have the measure of a vav, how can the lower part’s joining help? It is like the yuds of the alef and the like in seif 25, where in any case correction is required. I agree that the same is true for a vav: wherever their separation is not plainly recognizable, a child’s reading helps to join them and to permit correction, and this is not out of order, as we will say below in seif 25 with the help of Heaven. Here, however, the case is where it is distant and recognizable, and without correction the lower part does not join. One who examines the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe sees that the matter depends on a dispute. The Meiri wrote that the same applies to a heh and to all bent letters, that a child’s reading helps join what is below. The Mordechai wrote plainly that it helps only in a doubtful case whether there is the required measure, but not with bent letters; consequently in straight letters the lower parts do not join. The Darkei Moshe wrote that the Tur holds like the Meiri. In Darkei Moshe he wrote that the Tur distinguishes between a hole and a split, not between straight and bent letters. His intent is that with a hole we require that it occurred after the writing, for otherwise it lacks surrounding parchment, unlike a split without a hole, which is valid through a child’s reading even from the outset. In the Shulchan Aruch, the Rav added “straight,” but not bent, for they do not combine, like the Mordechai. The Bach explained the Tur’s position, that since he wrote “severed” without qualification, it implies even a bent letter, like the Meiri, and specifically after it was written; but if it was initially split without a hole, a child’s reading does not help, for it refers to the immediately preceding case, “if after it was severed”; see there and Eliyah Rabbah, letter 25, and understand. But according to the ruling of the Rav, who added “straight,” there is no distinction even before writing: if it was split but not perforated, a child’s reading helps. The proof is that regarding letter 9 they discussed an alef and the like, where it does not help because it lacks the form of a letter. Thus, for a straight letter it helps even from the outset without a hole, but for a bent letter, even afterward the lower part does not join for us. This is where the separation is plainly recognizable or where no correction has been made, as I wrote. See Magen Avraham 21.
