One should not score lines. This means because tefillin do not require scoring, and one who is exempt from something yet does it is called a commoner. In some books a gloss is written here: it is not necessary to score lines. The Bach also wrote that Rama emended the word 'must,' and he disagreed with him and wrote that one should not score lines.
The upper line. This is because it is forbidden to write a verse without scoring, and when he scores the upper line that is sufficient. The author of Birkas HaZevach asked in Menachos, folio 32: if so, why did Mar Zutra score all of the passage, as stated at the end of chapter 12 of Yevamos? End quote. It seems to me that the Tur and the Rosh guarded against this, for they wrote that ordinary people know how to train their hand after writing the upper line, whereas Mar Zutra was not expert in training his hand after the upper line, and therefore he scored it all. Furthermore, the language of the Talmud can be explained as follows: Mar Zutra would score and write the whole passage. One may say that he scored only the upper line and then wrote the whole passage, to exclude the view that we do not write the whole passage. He further challenged what the Rosh wrote there in Yevamos regarding what Abaye wrote in the chalitzah document, 'And we read to him from lo until lekachtah; I do not know why, for we maintain that three words may be written,' end quote. He asked that the Rosh himself wrote in Megillah that two words may be written without scoring, but three may not be written, etc.; see there. It seems to me that the Rosh holds that Abaye did not write the entire text because he holds like Mar bar Rav Ashi, that it may not be written even with scoring; see the Gemara there. On this the Rosh asked, for we maintain that three words may at least be written with scoring, and if so why did Abaye not write 'lo chafatzti lekachtah'?
And if he does not know, etc. The Yerushalmi said that one who is exempt from something yet does it is called a commoner only when he does so as a stringency. Here, however, he does not intend that, since this is not a matter of prohibition to which stringency applies; he scores only in order to write more straightly, and he is not a commoner; Beis Yosef. See siman 639, se'if 7. In truth this ruling requires consideration, for we find many stringencies that we accept upon ourselves and we do not say that one is called a commoner. The book Beis Shmuel also wondered about this. See Yam Shel Shlomo, chapter 8 of Chullin, regarding eating meat after cheese, where he wrote in the name of Maharam that it appears like disagreeing with the Talmud unless one does it as a fence and separation.
