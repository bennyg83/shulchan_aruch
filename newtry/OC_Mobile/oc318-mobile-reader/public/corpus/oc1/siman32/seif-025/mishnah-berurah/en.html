Every letter, etc.. This seif will explain the law of writing out of order, and its matters are lengthy. I will include the law briefly. There are three details in it. First: if there is a defect in some letter, whether it occurred at the time of writing or after writing, as Pri Megadim writes in the introduction, if it is visible to all that its form is not upon it, such as a yud missing its right leg, or a shin missing one yud, and similar cases where some part is missing from the letter so that its form is not upon it, even if because of this it does not resemble another letter, and he wrote afterward, no correction helps. Even if it happened that a child read it as the letter, it is nevertheless invalid because it was written out of order. All the more so if through its defect and lack it resembles another letter, such as a dalet resembling a reish, or a beis resembling a kaf, or a ches whose left leg has a break between the leg and the roof and appears like a hei, or a tzadi that has a break at the place where the yud joins its body and appears to all as a yud and nun; in all such cases we do not rely on the child and correction does not help. Second: if through its defect and lack it did not completely lose its form, such as where there is a thin break in the middle of some letter and the essential form is still upon it, or where the yuds on alefs and shins, or the legs of tavs, did not touch the body of the letter, then if a child who is neither wise nor foolish recognizes them, although the letter is not thereby rendered valid because its form is not upon it completely, nevertheless this helps, for its essential form remains upon it, so that it is permitted to correct it and it is not called out of order. There is also a third case: although its essential form is upon it and a child reads it as a letter, nevertheless no correction helps, such as a letter invalidated by something whose correction is to scrape part of it and write it anew, for example the leg of a hei or kuf that touched above, even with a thin contact like a hairbreadth, whose correction is to scrape the whole leg and write it anew, since mere separation does not help because of chak tochos; or likewise where the leg of the alef touched the roof of the alef, and similar cases, as above in seif 18. If, before correcting it, he wrote afterward, no correction can be devised, for he must scrape everything written invalidly, and if so, what he corrects afterward is out of order. Now, with the help of God, we will begin to explain the seif: “Every letter that is written,” etc.; the same applies if it became ruined afterward.
And its form is not upon it. Meaning that it is visible to all that its form is not upon it at all. Although where the leg of the alef touched the roof of the alef, or the face of the alef touched its roof, or the leg of a hei or kuf touched above, the essential form of the letter is still recognizable and a child would read it as the letter, nevertheless, since this invalidity can be corrected only by scraping everything that was made invalidly, as above in seif 18, its form is automatically considered entirely nullified; if he corrects it afterward, it is out of order.
The leg of the alef. The same applies to all those cases that the Rama wrote in the gloss at the end of seif 18 above.
Touching. Even a contact as thin as a hairbreadth.
A tzadi that he wrote. Meaning that he separated the part of the yud from the nun so much that it appears like two letters, and the same applies to all the others. Therefore, even if it happened that a child read it properly, he cannot correct them afterward, for since our eyes see that they have the form of other letters, when he corrects them afterward this is new writing and is out of order. The same applies even if they do not have the form of other letters, but it is visible to all that the letter lacks its essential form, such as a yud for which he did not make the right leg, or an alef that is missing the upper yud, and all similar cases; he cannot correct them afterward. But if he did not make the left leg of the yud, although if it remains so it is invalid, for it is not a proper yud according to most poskim, as below in siman 36, nevertheless since it has its essential form, for even without the small point the name yud is upon it, he may correct it and there is no concern of writing out of order.
Ayin, yud. The same applies to an open mem that he wrote as kaf-vav, without drawing the tag between them.
Or a ches as two zayins. Even if he made a hump over them, but they do not touch one another above and their separation is clearly recognizable, it has lost its essential form, for it appears like two zayins.
The yuds on the alefs. The same applies if there was a break in the middle of any letter, as Pri Megadim writes in Eshel Avraham in the introduction.
And a child, etc.. Meaning that therefore, even if their separation is clearly recognizable, that is, immediately when he sees them, he may correct them, for the letter has not yet lost its essential form, since the child read it as a letter. So wrote Rabbi Akiva Eiger in his novellae, unlike Pri Megadim, who was stringent in this; see Biur Halacha. But if their separation is not clearly recognizable until one looks at it carefully, there is no need to show it to a child. Nevertheless, if it happened that he showed it to a child and the child did not read it as a letter, we follow the child stringently and he cannot correct it, as Pri Megadim wrote.
