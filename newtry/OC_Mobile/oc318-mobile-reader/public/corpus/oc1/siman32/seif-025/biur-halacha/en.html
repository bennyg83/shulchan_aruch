One letter, etc. I have seen some people invalidate in this case when there is a very fine break, so fine that it is not recognizable until one looks at it carefully. Their source is the Pri Megadim in Eshel Avraham, who wrote that in all such cases repair does not help even if their separation is not plainly recognizable; see also there under letter 36. In my humble opinion, as a matter of law one should not be stringent about this, and I will clarify it, with Hashem's help, through several proofs. First, from the words of Rabbi Yitzchak Aksandari, from whom this law originates. His language in the Beis Yosef is that if one wrote in tefillin or mezuzos one letter divided into two letters, for example a tzadi written as a yud and a nun, etc., and similarly the Rosh wrote about a hei and a kuf that became attached in tefillin, that they have no remedy through correction, etc. See Magen Avraham, s.k. 36, who wrote that in all these cases, even if a child can read it, it is invalid, since it looks like two letters; and see the Pri Megadim there. According to his words, Rabbi Yitzchak Aksandari and the Magen Avraham are speaking even where their separation is not plainly recognizable. Besides this being very forced in the words of the Magen Avraham, as I will clarify at the end, it creates a great difficulty in Rabbi Yitzchak Aksandari's words. If so, what proof did he bring from kuf? The reason repair does not help for kuf, while for other letters that are not touching but have not lost their essential form repair helps, as Rabbi Yitzchak Aksandari himself wrote, must be as the Beis Yosef first wrote in the name of Terumas HaDeshen, that its repair is through erasure, and therefore its essential form will be cancelled before everyone's eyes. If so, that is no proof to a tzadi written as yud and nun where the separation was not plainly recognizable until one looked closely, and a child read it as a letter. Although one could force a distinction between tzadi and a yud and shin that are not touching, since until they are joined they are intrinsically two other letters, at any rate there is no proof from kuf, and certainly no a fortiori proof. Rather, Rabbi Yitzchak Aksandari meant to invalidate specifically when it was divided until it was recognizable and appeared to the eye as two letters. Then he properly brings proof from kuf: at the time of writing it did not resemble another letter because of its attachment, and even at the time of repair, when it must first be erased until its form is cancelled, it still will not thereby resemble another letter, yet it is invalid to repair. All the more so a tzadi written as yud and nun, which appears to all eyes as two letters and certainly does not yet have the form of a tzadi, may not be joined afterward. This is also the case addressed by the Magen Avraham: a child's reading does not help where it appears to all eyes as two letters, for the tzadi has lost its essential form and is like an alef missing one yud. But if the separation is not recognizable until one looks carefully, it may be repaired, for its essential form remains and a child reads it as a letter, like a yud and shin that are not touching, where the child's reading helps. This also resolves his wording, "in all these cases, even a child," because it looks like two letters; he did not write, "because they are two letters." The implication is that when the separation is not plainly recognizable and the child reads it as a letter, it is still called retaining its essential form and may be repaired. The language of Rabbi Yitzchak Aksandari and the Shulchan Aruch also implies this, for they speak of a tzadi written as yud and nun, not merely of a break at the point where the yud joins the nun. Olas Tamid also implies that the case is one where it appears to everyone as two letters; see there. Know also that Rabbi Yitzchak Aksandari's explanation, that before repair they still had the name of that letter and not another name because a child neither wise nor foolish could read them, is one reason: the second clause clarifies the first, for it proves that the name of the letter is still upon it. This is unlike a tzadi written as yud and nun, where the separation is plainly recognizable; presumably a child will not read it as a tzadi, since it looks like two letters and its essential form has been cancelled. As proof that this is correct, I copy the language of the long Darchei Moshe: Rabbi Yitzchak Aksandari wrote that if he wrote one letter and divided it into two letters, etc.; but if the break was in other letters where the break does not make them resemble another letter, such as an alef or tav whose parts were not joined, it is permitted to repair them, since without repair a child neither wise nor foolish can read them. Thus the main reason to validate a yud and shin that did not touch is that a child can read it, unlike a tzadi written as yud and nun, where presumably the child could not read it because it appears to all as two letters. The Magen Avraham's statement means that even if it happened that a child read it as a tzadi, it is still invalid because our eyes see that its form is not upon it at all. Although the Taz in s.k. 21 explains the case as one where he did not make its roof, implying that the Mechaber's case of a ches made of two zayins applies even where the separation is not plainly recognizable, for it is parallel to the latter case, and there it speaks where it is not plainly recognizable except that there is a roof, therefore repair helps there and not here, nevertheless the words of the Taz themselves are very difficult. First, the Mechaber's language implies that at the beginning the invalidity of the ches is because its separation is plainly recognizable, since at the end he says, "but it is not recognizable," implying that first he speaks of where it is plainly recognizable. Second, one who studies the Beis Yosef at the end of siman 36, under "I found written, if the roof," will see explicitly that it is invalid even when it has a roof, and his language there implies that even a child would not help, because it has no name of a letter at all, since its separation is plainly recognizable and it looks like two zayins. If so, this itself is proof to our words: in all the earlier cases the Mechaber also speaks specifically when their separation is plainly recognizable, for because of this the letter has lost its essential form and appears like two other letters. But where the separation is not plainly recognizable, its name still remains upon it. This is the reason of the opinion that is lenient regarding the roof of a ches whose separation was not recognizable. One may also say, as a matter of law, that regarding a tzadi and similar cases, even the Taz agrees to all we have proven, that where the separation is not plainly recognizable it can be repaired, for its name remains upon it. Only regarding ches does the Taz hold that since it has no roof it is evidently two zayins, and therefore even if they were made so close that the separation was not plainly recognizable, it lost its name. Terumas HaDeshen in siman 48 also somewhat implies this. He writes that the Rosh's ruling about a leg of a hei or kuf that touched above in tefillin does not prove that the Rosh disagrees with Maharam, who validates repairing the yuds on alefs that touch the body of the letter, for those cases are different since they cannot be repaired by erasure alone, etc. He further writes that perhaps the Rosh also refers to what he wrote afterward, that the yuds on alefs and shins must be attached, meaning that those too cannot be repaired in tefillin and the Rosh disagrees with Maharam. The Beis Yosef writes that one who studies his words will see that Terumas HaDeshen found the first answer more reasonable, that the Rosh does not disagree with Maharam and was not referring to what follows. If so, we see that the Rosh later included in this law also the yuds behind the tzadi, writing that the point on the alef shaped like a yud must touch it, and likewise the yud within the shin and ayin and behind the tzadi; if it does not touch, the form of the letter is altered. The plain language of Terumas HaDeshen and the Beis Yosef implies that the Rosh's view that there is no remedy does not apply at all to what he wrote afterward. Terumas HaDeshen mentioned only yuds on alefs and shins because he was not listing every item; proof is that he also omitted yuds on ayins and legs of tavs. Hashem enlightened my eyes and I found this still more explicit in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 230, where he is uncertain about a get in which the yud in an alef, the yud in a shin, and the yud behind a tzadi were not touching, but were so close that a child read them properly. It is explicit there that in all these cases the name of the letter is upon it, since the letter is recognizable even to a child; they are invalid only for tefillin and mezuzos, and he is uncertain whether to invalidate them even in a get because the letters must be written as given to Moshe at Sinai, each letter as one connected body except for hei and kuf. Accordingly, it seems clear that since we rule regarding the rule of writing in order for tefillin and mezuzos that it is permitted to repair yuds of shin and ayin that are not touching, because once a child reads it as a letter the name of the letter is upon it and its essential form remains, the same applies to the yud behind a tzadi. Returning to our matter, all this proves explicitly that if the break was fine, so that the separation was not plainly recognizable until one looked carefully, and a child reads it properly, even if such a case occurred in a tzadi between the yud behind it and its nun, or the like, it may be repaired, for it is not called a letter divided into two letters. Perhaps in such a case one need not even show it to a child, for he will certainly read it properly, like the ches about which the Shulchan Aruch wrote that seeing a child is not indispensable; although initially it is certainly proper to show it to a child. I have also seen some later authorities who incline to leniency where the separation is not plainly recognizable and the child reads it as a letter. Therefore, in pressing circumstances it appears to me that one may rely on all this to be lenient and permit repair, not like the Pri Megadim. Know further that if the break is extremely fine, visible only against the sun, it is valid even according to the Pri Megadim and needs no repair at all. Certainly a break is no worse than such a hole in that place, and regarding a hole we rule that a hole over which the ink passes is not considered a hole, even if visible against the sun, as written above in the Mishnah Berurah, s.k. 32. Certainly even if this occurred in a tzadi between the yud and nun, or the like, it is not considered a hole; otherwise, how could they say in Shabbos 108a that tefillin may be written on the skin of a kosher bird, even though the skin is perforated, because it is only a hole over which the ink passes? We should be concerned that the hole might occur in a tzadi between the yud and nun, or in a shin where it divides it into an ayin and zayin, or the like. Rather, such a hole is not considered a hole at all. One who wishes to be stringent about such a fine break should be stringent only to require repair, but Heaven forbid to invalidate the tefillin because of this.
Recognizes them, even though, etc. From the plain wording of the Shulchan Aruch it is proven that it speaks even where the separation is plainly recognizable, as Rabbi Akiva Eiger proved in his novellae, unlike the Pri Megadim in Eshel Avraham at the end of the forms of the letters. As for the Pri Megadim's proof from what the Beis Yosef wrote at the end of siman 36, under "I found written, etc.," that if it were certainly plainly separated until it looked like two zayins, etc., one who studies it will see that there is no proof at all. There the reason is that since it looks like two zayins, its essential form is cancelled; it is the earlier Shulchan Aruch case of a letter divided into two letters, and we do not say that the roof proves its identity. This is not so here, where even though the separation is plainly recognizable, its essential form has not yet been lost, so long as it is not so divided that the child no longer recognizes its form. Also according to what we proved above, that a letter divided into two letters refers specifically to where the separation is plainly recognizable, it follows that the latter clause also speaks in such a case, where even if the separation is plainly recognizable, a child's reading helps for repair. In any event, in pressing circumstances it appears one may rely on this leniently, that a child helps, as Rabbi Akiva Eiger wrote in his novellae that he ruled so in practice in pressing circumstances. What he wrote at the end, "and this requires study as law," appears to be copied from the Pri Megadim's language in the introduction at the end of his words, for otherwise he would contradict himself after writing that he ruled so in practice. Nevertheless, it seems obvious to me that Rabbi Akiva Eiger also agrees that where the gap is so large that it is recognizable and visible to every eye from a distance, then we see that its form has been entirely lost, and it is no better than a tzadi written as yud and nun, for which a child does not help. I saw something similar in later authorities, who cite Responsa Panim Meiros, part 2, siman 16: if from a distance one recognizes that there is a space and it does not reach the body of the letter, according to the Beis Yosef in siman 36 at a distance of four cubits, and according to the Bach at a distance of one cubit, certainly one should not rely on a child. But if, at each authority's relevant distance, we see that it is an alef or shin, and only when we bring our eyes close do we see a space, and a child reads it as an alef or shin, we can properly rely on him.
