And some. See Taz. See Tosafot, Menachos 32a, s.v. “and now,” who cite the arrangement of the early authorities, and what is written in the Yerushalmi. The general rule is that between one parsha and another there must be the measure of nine letters; less than this is invalid, whether open or closed. There are many disputes regarding the form of an open and closed parsha, and we will record only the dispute of the Rambam and the Rosh. According to both, there are two forms of an open parsha and three forms of a closed parsha. According to the Rambam, laws of Torah scroll 8:1, an open parsha must begin at the start of the line, and a closed parsha is always in the middle of a line; this is the distinguishing sign. Thus there are two forms of open parsha: one leaves blank space of nine letters at the end of the previous parsha and begins the next at the start of the next line, or he ends the previous parsha at the end of a line, leaves a blank line, meaning nine letters, and begins the open parsha at the start of the third line. The forms of a closed parsha are three: first, blank space of nine letters in the middle of a line, and he writes one word of that closed parsha on that line; second, if there is not enough room for nine letters and one word, he begins writing the closed parsha somewhat in the middle of a line; third, he leaves blank space of nine letters and begins in the middle of a line. He states them in a “not only this but even this” manner. According to the Rosh, there are two forms of an open parsha: one leaves blank space at the end, nine letters, and begins the open parsha at the start of the next line; second, he leaves nine letters and begins in the middle of a line. There are three closed forms: two like the Rambam’s illustrations, and the third, if he leaves an entire blank line, even if he begins afterward at the start of the third line, it is closed. If so, regarding hand tefillin, whereas in head tefillin see Magen Avraham 49, since one must write specifically in four columns and not two in one column, as in seif 47, how shall one act in order to satisfy both the Rambam and the Rosh? He concludes that one should make the second form of closed parsha, to which the Rosh agrees, for wherever there is not a blank space of nine letters in one place but only through combination, the Rosh agrees that it is closed. When the Rosh says that beginning in the middle of a line is open, that is where the blank space of nine letters precedes it in one place, not such a case through combination. What he wrote that according to the Rambam it is closed because there is not a full measure of blank space in one place is difficult, for according to the Rambam, whenever it does not begin at the head of the line, even if there is more than nine letters in one place, it is closed. Also, what he wrote according to the Rambam regarding a closed parsha when he left only nine letters of blank space and not enough to write one word, that since this is the measure of closure, therefore when he leaves a little blank on the second line it is through combination, I do not understand, for it seems that the Rambam’s reason is that whenever it does not begin at the start of a line it is closed. In any case, the practice that he wrote is correct, for now Kadesh is recognizable as being at the beginning with none before it, like Bereishis, though one who examines the Rambam sees that he did not count Bereishis as either open or closed; Ve-hayah ki yeviacha and Shema are recognizable from the nine-letter blank and begin at the head of the line; and at the end of Shema there is less than nine letters, and Ve-hayah im shamoa also has less than nine letters, so one satisfies everyone. Perhaps even according to the Rosh, although one leaves below a blank space of nine letters, as long as there is not at its beginning nine letters in one place, it is considered closed. Still, one should not do this, as the Taz wrote. At the end of Ve-hayah im shamoa one should not leave a blank space of nine letters, because some say this is the sign of an open parsha before it; see Maharam Padua, who elaborated on this. See Yoreh Deah 275 in Taz 5. This requires study.
Now. Regarding a mezuzah, see Yoreh Deah 288 and the Shach, letter 10. There too one can say like the Taz: at the end of Shema he should leave less than nine letters, and Ve-hayah im shamoa should begin on the seventh line somewhat distant from the margin, so that together they combine to nine letters, thereby satisfying both views. In a mezuzah one must necessarily begin the seventh line in the middle so that it is initially closed; if so, let it be through combination and thereby satisfy all, as stated. I later examined Beit Yosef, Yoreh Deah 288, and found this according to the Rosh, who holds that three letters are the measure of a parsha according to Maseches Sofrim: one should divide the measure of nine letters. The same applies for us: one should divide the nine-letter measure. Now, in Menachos 32 it says that one makes its parshiyos open, etc., and then concludes even open. One can say that it is a dispute there on 31b regarding “upon the earth”: some say it is written at the beginning of a line and some say at the end of a line; see there. According to Maharam Padua, also cited by the Tur, a blank of nine letters indicates the parsha before it, so indeed specifically open. But we rule that “upon the earth” is at the beginning of a line, and nevertheless it is closed, because the blank space indicates what follows it. One must also consider that according to the view that “upon the earth” is at the end of a line, there will sometimes be blank space of nine letters where there is no parsha, and it is invalid; see Magen Avraham, letter 33.
And in tefillin. Of Rabbeinu Tam, where one must write them in order, see siman 34 and Magen Avraham, letter 2. If so, he should write Kadesh and Ve-hayah ki yeviacha according to the law, so that they are open according to all. He should leave blank space in the hand tefillin at the end of Ve-hayah ki yeviacha of less than nine letters, and leave the space for the parsha of Ve-hayah im shamoa. He should write Shema at the top of the column, at the beginning of the line, and afterward write Ve-hayah im shamoa somewhat in the middle of the line, less than nine letters from the beginning. At the end of Ve-hayah im shamoa he should leave a complete blank space of nine letters, thereby satisfying everyone. According to Maharam Padua, this does not work well in Rabbeinu Tam tefillin, but we rule that the blank spaces indicate the parsha that follows them.
And know. There is a configuration in which one leaves an entire blank line at the beginning, and starts Ve-hayah im shamoa somewhat in the middle of the line beneath it. Either way, according to the Rambam it is closed because it begins in the middle, for an open parsha is specifically at the beginning; and according to the Rosh, a full line indicates its closure. With this one can explain somewhat the Taz’s words above. However, one should not make contradictory forms, and regarding this configuration one can say that it has neither the form of an open nor closed parsha, as I wrote; understand. See Magen Avraham, letter 48, where with the help of Heaven this will be further explained, and letter 49 there. I return further to what I wrote above: according to the Rosh, whenever one leaves nine letters of blank space at the end of a parsha, even if he begins the second parsha in the middle less than nine letters from the start, it is open, as is proven in Yoreh Deah 275 from the Yerushalmi, “open from its end”; see there in Beit Yosef. From the Tur there it implies that combination does not help according to the Rosh to make it closed, for he wrote that if there is not enough blank to write words there, he should leave a blank line and begin on the third line. Why should he not begin on the next line less than nine letters from the start by means of combination? This requires study; see there.

An explanation of what is written in the Shulchan Aruch, seif 33, that he should make the lines equal and be careful that there should not be three letters, though after the fact it is not invalid. Sometimes one or two letters may also invalidate, for at the beginning of Kadesh, Ve-hayah ki yeviacha, and Shema, according to the Rambam this form appears closed whenever it does not begin at the head of the line. This requires study. One should also ask: if he left blank space of nine letters and mice or worms ate the place of the blank so that there is no blank parchment there at all, is it valid in such a case or not? In hand tefillin, such a case is especially doubtful. At present I have not found a clear source for this law.
