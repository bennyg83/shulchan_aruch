That he is obligated in one blessing, etc. In Tur likewise, in the name of Rabbenu Yonah, and these are his words: but if he already fulfilled, we have no [issue] with it; but from the words of Rav Cohen Tzedek it appears that even if he already fulfilled he should not answer orphaned amen, for he wrote: the congregation must be silent and hear each and every blessing from the mouth of the cantor, and afterward they answer amen — for one who answers amen and did not hear the blessing is orphaned amen — and this is most reasonable. Nevertheless, if he knows which blessing he finished, even though he did not hear it from his mouth he may answer amen, as proved in Sukkah: in the great synagogue of Alexandria they would wave scarves to announce that the prayer leader finished a blessing, and they would answer amen — thus they answered even though they did not hear from his mouth — and it must be said that they had kavana in counting the blessings to know which blessing he was saying, end quote. Behold, the distinction that Rabbenu Yonah wrote is also from the gemara’s difficulty in Sukkah, chapter Hilul — therefore Beit Yosef wondered, in the name of Mahari Abuhav, why the Tur did not bring first the case of Sukkah. It seems that regarding other blessings that a person blesses for himself, there is no prohibition at all to answer amen even if he did not hear and does not know at all — there is no dispute except in repetition of the prayer leader’s eighteen [blessings], in which one is obligated to answer amen. Even Rabbenu Yonah certainly holds that one should not do so ab initio — that is, in repetition of the prayer leader’s eighteen: even though he already prayed, he should not wish to hear the blessing and answer amen — for that is surely not so; for then why would the prayer leader repeat the eighteen [blessings] if there is no need to hear him, and what relevance is there to answering amen? Therefore one need not bring proof from reasoning that it was not said thus; rather, surely Rabbenu Yonah did not exclude that he is not obligated to bless, but that there is no name of orphaned amen there. The case in chapter Hilul — they were forced to do so even ab initio, since it was a very large synagogue and it was impossible otherwise, per the words of Rabbenu Yonah — and it was not necessary to bring that case of Hilul, as it has no relevance from this. From the fundamental law there are three distinctions: if he is obligated in the blessing, the language of “orphaned” applies; if he is not obligated in the blessing but only obligated to answer amen in repetition of the prayer leader’s prayer — there is no orphaned amen, but a general prohibition; in other blessings, such as blessings that a person blesses for himself — there is no prohibition at all to answer amen even if he knows nothing. But Rav Cohen Tzedek, who wrote that one who answers amen and did not hear the blessing is orphaned amen — meaning, even if he is not obligated in the blessing, for in the lack of hearing he based the matter that answering amen is improper in this — therefore you must distinguish whether he knows which blessing he finished; then the case of Hilul, even ab initio and not in a time of pressing need, is permitted. Therefore the Tur brings there to teach us that there is a distinction between knowing or not, which is not proved to Rabbenu Yonah — and thus there is leniency and stringency for Rav Cohen Tzedek: leniency, that if he knows which blessing he may answer amen even ab initio, unlike the first opinion that forbids in all cases ab initio and there is no advantage from knowing which blessing; and just as without knowing at all there is reason to say there is prohibition to answer amen even though there is no name of orphaned amen, so too with knowing — for Rabbenu Yonah this does not decide; but for Rav Cohen Tzedek, since there is advantage from knowing which blessing, we can say: although from reasoning it is forbidden without knowing at all, nevertheless with knowing it is permitted even ab initio, and this suffices — since there is advantage in this that he removes himself from orphaned amen, and without that there is orphaned amen even though he is not obligated in that blessing. Therefore the Tur brings the advantage of knowing, after the words of Rav Cohen Tzedek specifically; but for Rabbenu Yonah there is no advantage in knowing at all. And stringency: that even if he is not obligated in that blessing, it is orphaned amen without hearing from the cantor, for all who do not know which blessing specifically — and therefore it is fitting for everyone to adopt the stringency of both of them: that is, if he does not know which blessing, even though he is not obligated, it is even orphaned amen and it does not suffice with prohibition alone; and in all cases there is prohibition ab initio, even if he knows which blessing and he is not obligated in it — as Rama wrote, “some are stringent,” etc. — and this is the view of Rav Cohen Tzedek. Rama did not cite the leniency that flows from this view, that with knowing which blessing it is permitted ab initio, but rather one must hear the blessing expressly. What Rama wrote, “if he does not know,” etc. — not to infer from this that with knowing it is permitted ab initio, but that it is not orphaned amen, as Rama concludes: this too is called orphaned amen, meaning that in this alone there is a distinction, but ab initio it is forbidden in all cases, whenever it is possible for him to hear, per my humble opinion in this. Yet one may challenge: why did the Tur write the first opinion in the name of Rabbenu Yonah — and it is the Jerusalem Talmud that the Rif and Rosh bring in chapter 3 she’akal, and these are its words: “What is orphaned amen? Rabbi Abba: one who is obligated for a blessing and does not know what he is answering amen for,” end quote — and all the harder on Rav Cohen Tzedek: how did the Tur write in his name that even if he already fulfilled he should not answer? Does he have power to dispute the Jerusalem Talmud? Our Talmud does not disagree with it. It seems Rav Cohen Tzedek explains the Jerusalem Talmud: he is obligated to hear the blessing or to know which blessing, in order to know upon what he answers amen — and this one did not know upon what he answers, and therefore it is orphaned amen. This is explicit in the words of Rav Cohen Tzedek, who wrote “they must hear,” etc. — and this includes also knowing the place, to avoid orphaned amen; and this is what is intended in the Jerusalem explanation we mentioned. Thus, even for Rav Cohen Tzedek there is no prohibition in all blessings, but he speaks of the eighteen [blessings] of the prayer leader: even though he is exempt, having already prayed, nevertheless he is obligated to hear the blessing from the prayer leader and to answer amen — in this they said orphaned amen applies; and this is the intent of the Jerusalem Talmud, when he does not know even which blessing — therefore he said “upon what he answers,” implying that if he knows upon what he answers, it suffices. But Rabbenu Yonah, who is the first opinion, explains the Jerusalem Talmud regarding whether he is obligated to bless that blessing, that he has not yet prayed — and for practical halacha, likewise what is fit to rule in this.
