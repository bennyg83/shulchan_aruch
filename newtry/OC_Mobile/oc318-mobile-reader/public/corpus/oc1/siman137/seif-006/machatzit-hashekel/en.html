Seif katan 6: And two with him, etc. He called the maftir and read “On the second day.” On the first day of Chol HaMoed, the maftir must read both “On the second day,” because perhaps the first day of Yom Tov was actually a weekday and the first day of Chol HaMoed is the second day, and “On the third day,” because perhaps the first day of Yom Tov was truly Yom Tov and the first day of Chol HaMoed is the third day, as explained below at the end of siman 663.
In the Shulchan Aruch: We do not recite the blessing, etc. Although we sit in the sukkah, we do not recite the blessing “to dwell in the sukkah,” because perhaps it is the eighth day and no longer Sukkos. This shows that we do not recite a blessing because of a doubt. Yet on Shabbos Parashas Parah we do recite a blessing when repeating the reading, because the Sages instituted that the entire Parashas Parah be read. If so, here too, since the Sages instituted reading “On the third day” on the first day of Chol HaMoed, even though the reason is a doubt, it is nevertheless a Rabbinic enactment and should require a blessing. Parashas Parah is different, because there is an opinion that it is D'Oraisa, unlike our case, which is merely a doubt concerning the day. These are the words of the responsum. This implies that even though we rule like the opinion that Parashas Parah is D'Rabbanan, it is nevertheless treated more stringently than a doubt concerning the day, because at least one opinion holds it is D'Oraisa. See what is written in siman 685, meaning the discussion there whether Parashas Parah is D'Oraisa.
Although we are expert, etc. We know that the first day is the principal day, and therefore the first day of Chol HaMoed is the third day and it is proper to read “On the third day.” Nevertheless, they were not stringent regarding the festival readings, because those portions were already read on their respective Shabbosos, as stated in the Shulchan Aruch.
Regarding the other festival portions. Meaning, the portion in which five people are called.
But the Mussaf offerings, namely what the maftir reads, are obligations of the day, based on the Midrash cited by Tosafos and the Rosh in Megillah 31b: Avraham said, “Master of the universe, if my children sin, what will become of them?” Hashem answered him, “That suffices while the Beis HaMikdash exists. What will they do after the destruction?” The Holy One, blessed is He, replied, “I have already established for them the order of the offerings; whenever they read them,” etc.
And the rabbi did not see, etc., since he was uncertain whether, if one omitted a passage on Shabbos, he must return and recite a blessing.
Regarding the festival Mussaf portions, however, there remains room for doubt. We now see that on Shabbos one recites a blessing, while for the festival portions one does not, as Terumas HaDeshen and the Shulchan Aruch here write. Yet the Mussaf portions are obligations of the day, and one may question whether they resemble Shabbos or the other festival portions, as Maharam was uncertain above.
But on the seventh day of Pesach, when the Song at the Sea is read, and on Shavuos, when the Ten Commandments are read, there is not so much concern. Meaning, even if the congregation reads the Song and the Ten Commandments from chumashim and thereby does not listen to the chazzan's reading from the Sefer Torah.
Rather, this was stated incidentally. Meaning, since even if one omitted a passage on the festivals it is of no consequence, the same is true if the congregation did not hear it from the chazzan reading from the Sefer Torah. The case there concerns the Song and the Ten Commandments, which are read for the five aliyos. Their practice was to read these from chumashim. This does not apply to the sacrificial portions read by the maftir, which they did not customarily recite by heart. Therefore, there is no basis to distinguish between festival portions and sacrificial portions.
Nor is there any inference to be drawn. Accordingly, the question remains whether a blessing should be recited when repeating the Mussaf portion.
And Tosafos, etc. “The last person who read previously is considered as though he did not read,” etc. It is possible that Tosafos holds like the opinion that one may not add aliyos on Yom Tov. Since the full prescribed number already read and another person will now read the obligation of the day, there would be one extra person beyond the prescribed number. Tosafos therefore writes that the final reader is considered nonexistent, so the one reading the obligation of the day is not an addition. Since Tosafos had to say that the final reader is considered nonexistent, they necessarily hold that the person who repeats the obligation of the day recites a blessing; otherwise, there would be no addition. On the contrary, it must be that the final reader is counted, for otherwise one reader would be missing from the prescribed number. This applies not only on Sukkos, where Megillah explicitly states that the maftir reads the day's offerings as we do, but even on the other festivals. The Mishnah and Gemara specify only the portion read by the five aliyos, while the maftir's reading of the sacrificial offerings is not found in the Talmud. It appears only in the words of Rabbi Akiva. The Rosh writes that it must have been instituted in the days of the Savoraim after the sealing of the Talmud, or in the days of the Geonim, based on the Midrash concerning Hashem's response to Avraham.
For it does not cause, etc. Even if they had read it immediately, another blessing would still have been required.
All the more so regarding the Mussaf portions. Although they concern offerings that atone, unlike the general festival portions, Magen Avraham nevertheless holds that with respect to omitting a single verse they are no better than the festival portions mentioned in the Gemara. Since the principal sacrificial portion was read, omitting one verse does not require repetition.
It is as though he did not read at all. Meaning, although Magen Avraham wrote that omitting a single verse even from the sacrificial portion does not require a blessing, forgetting the entire reading of “On the third day” is considered as though he did not read at all. Tosafos cited above proves that in such a case a blessing is required. See Tosafos, Sukkah 44b, who write that although we are expert in determining the calendar and observe the second day only because of ancestral custom, it is nevertheless understandable that we recite blessings, since those blessings do not contain the phrase “and commanded us,” which would be inappropriate for a mere custom.
In Shabbos 23a, Tosafos write that this is why we recite blessings on the second day of Yom Tov: if people saw that no blessings were recited and the day was not treated as a full Yom Tov, they might belittle it and say that it is not Yom Tov at all.
So that he not violate bal tosif. We rule that outside the mitzvah's proper time one violates bal tosif only when intending to add. Therefore, by merely sitting in the sukkah he does not violate it, because his intention to add is not evident. Perhaps the sukkah is pleasant to him and he sits there for comfort, not for the mitzvah. But if he recites a blessing, it is openly apparent that he sits there for the mitzvah of sukkah, and he violates bal tosif.
As Tosafos write regarding lulav. They ask why lulav differs from sukkah. The Sages should have instituted that one take the lulav on the eighth day without a blessing, just as one sits in the sukkah. They answer that taking the lulav involves moving a muktzeh object. People would say that since moving muktzeh was permitted, it must be the seventh day and only Chol HaMoed, and they would belittle Shemini Atzeres. Sitting in the sukkah does not lead to such belittlement, because even though it is the eighth day, Shemini Atzeres, one may sit in the sukkah because it is pleasant to him, as explained above.
So write Tosafos, Sukkah 47a, s.v. masiv, etc. For this reason, if one recited a blessing over sitting in the sukkah, people might belittle Shemini Atzeres just as with lulav, because it would then be evident that he sits there for the mitzvah.
