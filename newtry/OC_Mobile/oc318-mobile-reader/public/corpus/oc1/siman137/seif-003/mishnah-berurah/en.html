(5) And they read ten verses. This refers to a case where each person read three verses and the total was ten. If, because of the omission, one person read fewer than three verses, even after the fact they must return and read, as explained below in seif 4.
(6) He does not return. For they read ten verses in accordance with the enactment of the Sages, the omitted verse is not an obligation of the day, and it will be read on the following Shabbos.
(7) And if not, he returns. Meaning, only nine verses were read altogether. Although seif 4 below explains that after the fact nine verses suffice, here there are two defects: they read fewer than ten verses and also omitted a verse in the middle. This is worse, and they must return. “He returns” means that if he remembered only after reciting the concluding blessing, he recites the opening blessing again, reads that verse together with two additional verses, since fewer than three verses may not be read from the Torah, and recites the concluding blessing.
(8) One verse. Even if he omitted part of a verse, or even a single word, he must return and read it. He should begin from the start of that verse and read it together with two others, as required for a complete verse.
(9) He returns and reads. This applies even if seven people read without the omitted verse. “He returns” means that he recites a blessing before and after. Later authorities.
(10) And after saying Kaddish, he returns and reads. It appears that this applies specifically if the omission occurred in the earlier aliyos. If it occurred in the seventh aliyah, and certainly in aliyos added beyond the prescribed seven, there is no need to return and recite a blessing specifically because of the omitted verse, since the maftir will in any event be called to the Torah. The maftir should begin from the omitted verse and read through the end of the portion. A still stronger case is found below in siman 282, seif 6; see there and Biur Halachah there, s.v. dekayma lan.
(11) Their law is like, etc. This refers to a case where the five readers each read at least three verses, as required. Otherwise, he certainly returns and reads. Magen Avraham writes that the same applies if one omitted a verse from those read from the second Sefer Torah by the maftir as the obligation of the day, namely the verses of the Mussaf offering. He does not return unless the essential body of those verses was entirely omitted. For example, on Shabbos that is the first day of Chol HaMoed Sukkos, when the maftir reads “On the second day” and “On the third day,” if he omitted “On the third day,” then although he read “On the second day,” it is considered as though he did not read the obligation of the day at all, since we are expert in determining the calendar. However, if he read “On the third day” but omitted “On the second day,” he does not return and read with a blessing. Rather, he opens the Sefer Torah and reads those verses before the congregation without a blessing. Eliyah Rabbah adds that sometimes even omitting a single verse requires repetition with a blessing, namely when that verse contains the principal obligation of the day. Examples are: if on the first day of Pesach he omitted from “You shall offer” through “to atone for you,” since what follows is not as directly related to the day's obligation; if on the first day of Sukkos he omitted any verse from “You shall offer” through the end of the portion; or if on the first day of Chol HaMoed that falls on Shabbos he omitted any verse from the section “On the third day.” Shaarei Efraim agrees.
