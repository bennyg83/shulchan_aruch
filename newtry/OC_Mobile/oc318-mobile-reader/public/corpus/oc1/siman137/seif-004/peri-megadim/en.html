He must. See Taz. His view is that so long as the sefer Torah was not completely rolled up, meaning to return it to the aron kodesh, even though the reader already recited the concluding blessing, the initial blessing is not lost. The case of Parashas Parah in seif 5 is different, because there he recited the blessing and rolled it up completely, as explained in seif katan 5, and the two verses require a blessing. Accordingly, weekday and Shabbos are alike. If another person was not yet called, the first reader should remain and complete a third verse, and he recites only the concluding blessing, thereby avoiding one unnecessary blessing. If another person was already called, however, a different person should read because of possible discredit to the first. He holds that this is unlike siman 135, seif 6, where the reader remains in place. See Eliyah Rabbah here, seif katan 7. According to this, Taz apparently disagrees with Magen Avraham in three respects. First, according to Magen Avraham the initial blessing is also lost. Second, the next reader begins from the point where the first stopped. Third, on Shabbos another person reads. It is possible that the last point is only optional, or that another may read if desired, since in any event the first reader gains nothing with respect to the initial blessing. It is preferable, however, to minimize the disagreement and say that they differ in only one point: according to Magen Avraham the initial blessing is lost, yet the next reader nevertheless begins again from an earlier point. Thus, on a weekday, when the same person must read again, he reads the two earlier verses and a third verse, and that suffices. On Shabbos, another person may read, since the first gains nothing. Unlike Taz, these two matters are interdependent. Accordingly, the second reader should properly read three verses from the beginning, going back as Taz requires, or else read three additional verses from that point onward, as in seif 6, where it is proper, for greater assurance, to add three verses. A proof is that otherwise, if he began exactly where the first reader stopped, it would be obvious, according to Eliyah Rabbah’s understanding of Magen Avraham, that he must recite the initial blessing. There is no need to elaborate.
Regarding practical law, I will copy from my short booklet bound with the siddur. We certainly rule that in a doubt concerning blessings one is lenient. Therefore, if the same person reads, he should begin only with the third verse, because if he goes back it appears that the initial blessing was in error, and he recites only the concluding blessing. If another person was already called, perhaps the second should remain, as in siman 135, seif 6, and as Eliyah Rabbah writes here. For if this occurred with a kohen who read only two verses and a Levi was then called, the Levi must necessarily remain, since a Levi is not called first where a kohen is present, and no distinction was made. Similarly, if this occurred with the final aliyah, the second person should remain and serve as maftir. If it occurred with maftir and he read only two verses, then if no one else was called, he himself should read a third verse and recite only the concluding blessing. If they mistakenly called another person for maftir, another must read because of embarrassment. The same applies to the third aliyah on a weekday. See Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 7. He also writes that a maftir who read only two verses has the same law as any other reader, whether the maftir of an ordinary Shabbos or, all the more so, the maftir of a festival. Nevertheless, if only two verses remained until the end of the sidrah, Taz agrees that the initial blessing was recited in error, since only two verses remained, and he must recite the initial blessing again. Likewise, if the sixth reader read until the end of the sidrah and only two verses remained for the seventh, the seventh repeats one verse from above.
If on Pesach or Shavuos they called the fourth reader for the entire reading and only two verses remained for the fifth, or similarly on Yom Kippur for the final reader, he should repeat one verse from above and should not read below, because the verses below do not discuss the festival. Regarding the festival offerings, if the Levi on the fourth day read too far, the Yisrael should read the fifth day, because all of it concerns the festival. See Tosafos, Megillah 22a, s.v. shani; Maharsha there; and Eliyah Rabbah 138:1.
Know also that if the kohen read only two verses and the Levi and Yisrael each read three or even four verses on a weekday, one may argue that the Yisrael should read another three verses from that point onward, while the first two verses, over which no proper reading was completed, are treated like a total omission. This is acceptable on a weekday. On Shabbos it requires study whether the Yisrael should read the two verses and an additional third verse. Going back is not inherently problematic, as Levush writes in seif 5 that the Torah was given section by section. Yet there is concern that those entering will say that a Yisrael is reading at the beginning of the sidrah while kohanim are present in the synagogue. The concern applies immediately to those entering and leaving. If a kohen reads, there is also concern because he reads after a Yisrael. Perhaps an actual omission must be corrected on Shabbos, whereas two verses over which no proper reading was completed need not be repeated after the fact. Only initially, wherever correction is possible, do we correct it. This is copied from my short booklet there. See what I wrote in the following seif katan.
