[1] One should have concentration, etc. The chazzan should be careful to emphasize the gimel of "yisgadal," lest it sound like "yiskadal," for kadal is the Aramaic word for the back of the neck. He should not emphasize the beis of "yisbarach," lest it sound like "yisparach," meaning that He should crumble. He should also articulate the hei of "yis'halal," lest, God forbid, it sound like "veyischalal," meaning that He should be profaned. Shelah, folio 252. I say that one should protest against a chazzan who pauses between "ba'agala uvizman kariv" and "ve'imru amen." Rather, he should say them in one breath, for otherwise the congregation begins "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba" before the chazzan says "ve'imru amen," which is improper. I likewise found in Sefer HaManhig: The shaliach tzibbur says "ve'imru amen," and the congregation then responds "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba," as it is stated, "Declare the greatness of Hashem with me." It is therefore improper to precede the chazzan. Responsa HaRosh, rule 4, states that one should not recite Kaddish together with the chazzan, because Kaddish is recited only with ten; rather, he must listen to the chazzan and then answer "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba." Shibbolei HaLeket writes that the expressions "yisgadal veyiskadash Shemei rabba, yisbarach, veyishtabach, veyispa'er, veyisromam, veyisnasei, veyis'hadar, veyis'aleh, veyis'halal" correspond to the ten utterances through which the world was created; this is why we say "be'alma di vera chir'usei." Since the Ten Commandments are included within the ten utterances, and there was a division between the first two commandments, which were spoken by the Almighty, and the other eight, which were spoken by Moshe, Kaddish similarly separates the two praises "yisgadal veyiskadash" from the final eight. Kol Bo writes that the eight forms of praise beginning with "yisbarach" correspond to the seven heavens, with the eighth corresponding to the firmament above the heads of the Chayos. One should prolong and draw out the praises so as to enumerate them. There is another explanation of "Shemei rabba": reversed, the word bara alludes to the Name through which Hashem fashioned the worlds. We say "in your lifetimes and in your days" because only one from a city and two from a family will remain; Israel is therefore blessed that it occur in their lifetimes. Recanati explains this as a prayer for the leaders of Israel, that they not die because of others. Kol Bo writes that one must draw out "rabba" with all his strength, so that he may think in his heart and, while drawing it out, have all the intentions described above. I found in Ra'avan, siman 12: I was asked why fifteen praises were instituted in Kaddish; from "yisgadal" and "yiskadash" through "veyis'halal, shirasa, tushbechasa, venechemasa." It appears to me that they correspond to the fifteen Songs of Ascents. Yishtabach likewise contains fifteen praises, and Emes Veyatziv contains fifteen vavs through "tov veyafeh"; "emes" is not counted, because it belongs to the preceding words, "Hashem Elokeichem emes." It appears that these correspond to the Name formed by yud and hei. Kol Bo writes that "kedushasa" is spelled with a vav because kodesh has the numerical value 410, the number of years the First Temple stood. In it the Name was blessed, and the Kohanim pronounced it as written; now we therefore require consolation above. "Tushbechasa venechemasa" complete four forms of praise corresponding to the four letters of the Name. You can now understand the expression "nechemasa." See Tur.
[2] Aloud, etc. This is its language: when one raises his voice, his concentration is aroused. Nevertheless, he should not raise his voice above that of the person reciting the blessing. See simanim 101 and 124, and Lechem Chamudos, folio 24.
[3] One should stand, etc. Maharil would remain standing until he had finished answering “Amen, yehei shemei rabba.” It appears to me, however, that one should remain standing through the Amen following “yisbarach,” as will be explained in siman 66, seif 4.
[4] "Ve'atah yigdal," etc. Maharil writes that "Ve'atah yigdal" should not be said at all. Piskei Tosafos concludes that it should not be said during the Kaddish between Yishtabach and Yotzer or during the Kaddish before Maariv, but it is said during other Kaddish recitations. Shelah rules likewise on folio 254. Magen Avraham, paragraph 6, writes that one should say "Ve'atah yigdal" only before the chazzan begins; while the chazzan says "Yisgadal," one should not say "Ve'atah yigdal," unless he says it quietly. This is also implied by Kol Bo, who adds that one should also say "Hashem chafetz," etc.
[5] Levush: Its explanation is that we, etc. I have already written another reason. Tur gives a further explanation: the words are literal, and "Shemei rabba" is the Aramaic translation of "His great Name." Accordingly, one must say "Shemei rabba mevarach" in one breath, without pausing. Rema, Lechem Chamudos on folio 24, and Bach rule likewise. Bach adds that, to satisfy also the interpretation that "Shemei" means the Name Yah, one should clearly pronounce its final hei. Shelah writes that one should pause slightly between Amen and "yehei Shemei rabba," because Amen refers to "Yisgadal." Similarly, one should pause between Amen and Modim because Amen refers to Retzei. He further writes that "mevarach" should be vocalized with a kamatz, because it is Aramaic, whereas "mevorach" is Hebrew. I also received the tradition that "le'alam" is vocalized with a sheva under the first lamed, a kamatz under the ayin, and a patach under the final lamed, like the Aramaic word in Daniel. Kitzur Shelah writes that one should say "di vera" as two words. It appears to me, however, that "devara" can also bear this meaning. We similarly find in the Targum to Chukas that "asher ein" is rendered "deleit," though the Hebrew consists of two words, and there are many similar cases, including "asher ta'aseh" and "avodeihem te'abedun." This is also explained in Beis Yosef, Even HaEzer, siman 126, regarding wording in a get such as "deyasva, detehevein," and "detzivein." Because of the stringency of a get, some adopted the stricter practice there, but that concern does not apply here. I found in Sefer HaManhig that Michael, Gavriel, and Raphael, who are appointed over Israel, understand Aramaic. See below, siman 101. Sefer Tanya explains that the enemies in their time decreed that Kaddish not be recited, so it was instituted in Aramaic in order that they not recognize it. Even after the decree was annulled, the earlier practice was not restored, so that the miracles and wonders would not be forgotten. Kol Bo explains that it is so the angels will not prosecute Israel for praying for the Redemption, when the righteous will be greater than the angels. Angels by nature can do only good and therefore deserve no particular credit for not sinning, whereas the righteous overcome their evil inclination. Abudraham writes that one should not question why "yisbarach, veyishtabach, veyispa'er," and the following praises were instituted in Hebrew, for these praises could not be rendered into Aramaic. See there also regarding the Kaddish after burial. Knesses HaGedolah cites Rabbeinu Yonah: if one must choose between answering "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba" and Kedushah, he should answer "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba." Nevertheless, if two synagogues are before him, one approaching Kedushah and the other the Kaddish after Shemoneh Esrei, he should go answer Kedushah, because there he will also hear Kaddish after the prayer. Magen Avraham.
