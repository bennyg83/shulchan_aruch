1) [Se'if 1] One should concentrate when answering Kaddish, etc., and answer it aloud, etc. Tractate Shabbos, in the chapter Kol Kisvei, folio 119b, states in the name of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi: Whoever answers "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba mevarach" with all his strength has an adverse decree against him torn up. Rabbeinu Yonah, in the chapter Mi Shemeiso, explains that this means with the full strength of his concentration. Since some people's concentration is aroused only through vocal force, the Sages said "with all his strength"; however, he need not shout so loudly that people ridicule him. Rashi in Kol Kisvei likewise explains "with all his strength" to mean with all his concentration. Tosafos, citing Ri, writes that Pesikta relates in the account of Rabbi Yishmael ben Elisha: when Israel enters the synagogues and says "yehei Shemei rabba mevarach" aloud, harsh decrees are annulled. The holy Zohar in Parashas Terumah, folio 129b, similarly says to answer "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba mevarach" with mighty force. Beis Yosef cites all this and concludes that it should be answered aloud. The Zohar there states: Come and see; this Kedushah is unlike the other Kedushah that we repeat three times. This Kedushah ascends on every side, above and below and in every aspect of faith, and breaks iron locks and seals and evil shells, so that the glory of the Holy One, blessed be He, may rise above all. We must say it in the language of the Other Side and answer with mighty force, "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba mevarach," so that the power of the Other Side will be broken and the Holy One, blessed be He, will rise in glory above all. When the power of the Other Side is broken through this Kedushah, the Holy One, blessed be He, rises in His glory and remembers His children and His Name. Since the Holy One, blessed be He, rises in glory through this Kedushah, it is recited only with ten. Through this language the Other Side is forcibly subdued and its power is broken; the glory of the Holy One, blessed be He, rises, breaking locks, seals, mighty chains, and evil shells, and the Holy One, blessed be He, remembers His Name and His children. Fortunate are the holy people to whom the Holy One, blessed be He, gave the holy Torah, through which to merit the World to Come. Beis Yosef writes that from this one can appreciate how much concentration Kaddish requires and how diligently one should answer it. Maran therefore rules in Shulchan Aruch that both are required: concentration while answering Kaddish and answering it aloud. The holy Zohar in Parashas Bereishis, folio 38b, further states: Within this chamber stand all those who sanctified their Master's Name with all their strength and answered "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba mevarach" with all their strength. They stand in the innermost part of this chamber, and the light containing all colors shines upon them. Through that illumination they stand and behold other lights that unite yet are not absorbed within them. Above them is Mashiach, who enters and stands among them; he descends to them, takes from this chamber, and enters. You have now been shown the great distinction of one who answers Kaddish with concentration and aloud. Nevertheless, he need not shout so loudly that people ridicule him; rather, he should raise his voice somewhat and concentrate fully. See Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 3; Moreh Be'Etzba, siman 2, paragraph 34; Siddur Beis Oved, laws of answering Kaddish, paragraph 3; and below, paragraph 15.
2) There: One should concentrate when answering Kaddish, etc. Sefer Chasidim relates that one pious man saw another pious man after the latter's death, and his face was green. He asked, "Why is your face green?" The deceased answered, "Because I would speak while the shaliach tzibbur recited Vayechulu, the blessing Magen Avos, and Yisgadal." Mateh Moshe, siman 411, cites a Midrash in which a sage appeared to his student in a dream. The student saw a stain on his forehead and asked why this had happened to him. He answered that it was because he had not guarded himself from speaking while the chazzan recited Kaddish. Knesses HaGedolah, paragraph 1; Olas Tamid, paragraph 1; Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 1; Kaf HaChaim by Rabbi Chaim Palagi, siman 13, paragraph 9. The latter writes that this applies not only to the Kaddish recitations within the prayers, but to every Kaddish; see there.
3) There: One should concentrate when answering Kaddish. If one hears Kaddish and has the opportunity to answer either "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba mevarach" or Kedushah, but answering Kedushah will prevent him from answering Kaddish and answering Kaddish will prevent him from answering Kedushah, he should answer "Amen, yehei Shemei rabba," etc., and not Kedushah. Rabbi Yonah, siman 9; Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos on the Tur; Olas Tamid, paragraph 2. Magen Avraham, paragraph 1, similarly cites Be'er Sheva, folio 100; Pri Chadash, siman 125; Yad Aharon, Hagahos on the Tur; Soles Belulah, paragraph 1. The latter writes that this accords with the Zohar's statement that this Kedushah is unlike the other Kedushah that we repeat three times. Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 4; Kesher Gudal, siman 9, paragraph 18, citing Shai LaMora, siman 70; Shaliach Tzibbur, folio 87b; Siddur Beis Oved, paragraph 5; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1; Chayei Adam, rule 30, paragraph 6; Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 5; Ben Ish Chai, Parashas Vayechi, paragraph 11.
4) If two synagogues are before him, one approaching Kedushah and the other the Kaddish after Shemoneh Esrei, he should go answer Kedushah, because there he will also hear Kaddish after the prayer. Magen Avraham there; Yad Aharon there, who writes that this is obvious; Halachah Berurah, paragraph 1; Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav there; Chayei Adam there. Siddur Beis Oved, paragraph 6, writes that this applies specifically when both synagogues are equally before him, so that whichever one he enters he will not pass over the other. If, however, he will encounter one synagogue first and would have to pass it in order to reach the other, he should answer in the nearer one that he encountered first, even if there he will answer only the Kaddish after Shemoneh Esrei, because one does not pass over a mitzvah. It appears to me, however, that the rule against passing over a mitzvah applies when both are equal, not when they are unequal, for here one place offers one mitzvah while the other offers two; Kedushah and Kaddish. Consider this carefully. See Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 4, who writes that if he can first go where Kaddish is being recited and afterward go where Kedushah and Kaddish are being recited, that is preferable. Shaliach Tzibbur, folio 87b, cites him.
5) If he has already heard Kaddish but has not answered Kedushah, he should give priority to answering Kedushah, so that he will have fulfilled both Kaddish and Kedushah, for both together are good. Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 4; Shaliach Tzibbur, folio 87c; Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 5; Ben Ish Chai there. It appears to me that this applies when he will not be able to answer Kedushah later and thereby fulfill his obligation. If he will later have another opportunity to answer Kedushah, Kaddish takes priority even though he has not yet answered Kedushah. It further appears to me that the rule giving Kaddish priority over Kedushah concerns a Kedushah through which he is not presently obligated to fulfill his duty; for example, if he has not yet prayed Shemoneh Esrei, or if he has already prayed both the silent Amidah and the repetition and answered Kedushah. If, however, he is presently obligated to answer that Kedushah; for example, he prayed Shemoneh Esrei, the shaliach tzibbur is repeating it aloud, and upon reaching Kedushah he hears Kaddish from elsewhere and cannot answer and complete Kaddish before answering Kedushah; Kedushah takes priority. A mitzvah at its proper time is preferable, and this is Kedushah's proper place, with a mystical reason explained in Shaar HaKavanos, discourse 2 concerning the repetition of the Amidah; see there.
