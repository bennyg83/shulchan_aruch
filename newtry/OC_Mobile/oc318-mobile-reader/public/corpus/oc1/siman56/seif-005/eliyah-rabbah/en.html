The Tur gives a further explanation, namely that the words are to be understood literally: “Shemei rabba” is the Aramaic translation of “His great Name.” According to this, one must say “shemei rabba mevarach” in one breath, without pausing between them. Rama, Lechem Chamudos on folio 24, and Bach rule likewise. Bach nevertheless writes that, in order also to satisfy the interpretation that “shemei” means the Name Yah, one should pronounce the final hei of “shemei.” The Shelah writes that one must pause slightly between Amen and “yehei shemei rabba,” because the Amen refers back to “Yisgadal.” Similarly, one should pause between Amen and Modim, because the Amen refers back to Retzei. He further writes that “mevarach” must be pronounced with a kamatz, for it is Aramaic, whereas “mevorach” is Hebrew. I also received the tradition that the word “lealam” is vocalized with a sheva under the first lamed, a kamatz under the ayin, and a patach under the final lamed, like the Aramaic word “lealam” in Daniel. Kitzur Shelah writes that one must say “di bera” as two words; see there. It appears to me, however, that “devara” may likewise bear this meaning. We find similarly in the Targum to Chukas that “asher ein” is rendered “deleit,” although “asher ein” consists of two words, and this occurs in many places. The same is true of “asher taaseh,” and we likewise find “you shall destroy their ruins.” This is also explained in Beit Yosef, Even HaEzer siman 126, regarding the wording of a get, such as “deyasva,” “detehevein,” and “detzivein.” Because of the stringency of a get, some adopted the stricter practice there; see there. That concern does not apply here. I found in Sefer HaManhig that Michael, Gavriel, and Raphael, who are appointed over Israel, understand Aramaic. See below, siman 101. Sefer Tanya explains that in their days the enemies decreed that Kaddish not be recited, so they instituted its recital in Aramaic in order that the enemies not recognize it. Even after the decree was annulled, they did not restore the earlier practice, so that the miracles and wonders would not be forgotten. Kol Bo explains that the reason is so that the angels will not prosecute Israel for praying for the redemption, since in the future the righteous will be greater than the angels. The reason is that angels are naturally capable only of doing good, and therefore deserve no special credit for not sinning, whereas the righteous overcome their evil inclination. Abudraham writes: do not find it difficult that they instituted “yisbarach, veyishtabach, veyispaer,” etc., in Hebrew, because the words of praise could not be rendered into Aramaic. See there also regarding the Kaddish recited after burial. Knesses HaGedolah cites Rabbeinu Yonah that if one has the opportunity to answer either “Amen, yehei shemei rabba” or Kedushah, but cannot answer both, he should answer “Amen, yehei shemei rabba.” Nevertheless, when two synagogues stand before him, and in one they are approaching Kedushah while in the other they are approaching the Kaddish after Shemoneh Esrei, he should go and answer Kedushah, because there he will also hear the Kaddish after the prayer. Magen Avraham writes likewise.
