(Seif katan 5, siman 124) At the end of that siman, the gloss states that if one hears most of the congregation answering Amen, although he did not hear the blessing at all, but knows to which blessing the Amen refers, he answers Amen together with them; see there. The same applies here. When he hears Amen from most of the congregation and knows that this Amen is in response to Kaddish, it is as though he too were saying Amen. This is what Magen Avraham means when he writes that if one can direct his attention so that he knows, etc. In other words, if he paid attention and knew that the chazzan was reciting Kaddish, then even though he did not hear the chazzan’s words, he should say Amen together with most of the congregation. By contrast, if when they said Amen he did not know to what it referred, then even if he afterward hears “yehei shemei rabba” and understands that Kaddish had been recited, he should say only “yehei shemei rabba,” without Amen. Although such an Amen would not constitute an orphaned Amen, since he now knows that it was recited in response to Kaddish, the congregation has already finished saying Amen, for they are already saying “yehei shemei rabba.” It is forbidden for him to say Amen then, because Amen must be said within the time required to utter a brief greeting after the blessing or Kaddish, or while most of the congregation is answering, as explained there in seif katan 4. Alternatively, one may say that this case also involves an orphaned Amen, since when the congregation said Amen he did not know to what it referred. Therefore, even though he afterward hears “yehei shemei rabba,” it is an orphaned Amen. This is clear.
