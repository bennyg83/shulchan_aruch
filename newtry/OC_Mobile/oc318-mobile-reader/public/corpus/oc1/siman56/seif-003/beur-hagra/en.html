Seif 3: Those who answer, etc., because, etc. The Midrash relates that Rabbi Elazar son of Rabbi Yose once said: I was traveling on the road and encountered Eliyahu, of blessed memory, accompanied by four thousand camels carrying burdens. I asked him, “What are these carrying?” He replied, “Wrath and fury.” “For what purpose?” “To exact vengeance with wrath and fury from one who speaks between Kaddish and Barchu, between one blessing and another, between one section and another, or between ‘Amen, yehei shemei rabba’ and ‘yisbarach.’” Ri Migash likewise writes that one should rely on the mystical tradition. All the halachic authorities, however, write that one should answer only through “almaya,” which contains twenty eight letters. The Geonim write likewise, as does Rambam in his order of prayer. The Midrash itself proves the opposite, in support of their view, because it classifies this interval together with the interval between sections, between Kaddish and Barchu, and between one blessing and another. It forbids only speaking during those intervals. The individual answers through that point, listens from there onward, and remains silent, whereas ordinary people are accustomed to speak. The same applies between Kaddish and Barchu, etc. The Midrash therefore did not say that punishment comes upon one who speaks before “almaya” or in the middle of a blessing. As for Ri Migash's statement based on the mystical tradition, this does not appear correct to me. It is no stronger than the intervals between sections, which according to the mystical tradition are closely connected, as is known to one who understands, for the sections are interwoven and there is no greater severance than interrupting there. Yet the Midrash cited above also forbids speech there, even though the sections cannot be recited as one. Such an interruption is nevertheless less severe than an interruption in the middle of a section, as stated in the Mishnah. Still, Rav Amram's order and Rambam's text, which instruct one to answer Amen after “yisbarach,” imply that the matter concludes there. The Tur therefore writes “yishtabach” without a prefixed vav. Even so, Rambam and the Tur both write that one should answer only through “almaya,” and this is the primary ruling.
