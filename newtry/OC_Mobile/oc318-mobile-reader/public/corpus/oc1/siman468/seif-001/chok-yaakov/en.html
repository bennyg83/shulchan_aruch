From midday and onward. The reason is in the Yerushalmi: since that is the time for bringing the korban, and when an individual brings a korban he is forbidden to perform labor, unlike the daily offering, which is a communal korban, and this is clear, not like the Eitz Yosef. Even nowadays, when there is no korban, the prohibition remains in its place, as the reason is explained by the Rosh and the Ran in chapter Kol Sha'ah. All the poskim adopted this reason. However, Rashi wrote another reason at the beginning of chapter Makom Shenahagu, that he should not be occupied with labor and forget the destruction of his chametz, the slaughter of the Pesach offering, and the preparation of the mitzvah matzah, for one must trouble himself while it is still day, end quote. What the Maggid Mishneh asks against Rashi's explanation is not difficult at all, as written by the Nachalas Tzvi, Lechem Mishneh, and Tosafos Yom Tov. It appears to me that a practical difference between the reasons is that according to Rashi, if Erev Pesach falls on Shabbos, it would be forbidden to perform labor on Friday from midday and onward, since that is then the time for destroying chametz and preparing the matzah. But according to the other poskim, whose reason is because of the korban, since the Korban Pesach overrides Shabbos it is permitted to perform labor on Friday until Minchah, as above in siman 251. So wrote Maharil in Hilchos Shabbos HaGadol: If Erev Pesach falls on Shabbos, one who wishes to be stringent not to perform labor on Friday may be stringent because of the principle that no distinction is made, but there is no prohibition, since the prohibition is because of the korban and they are offered on Shabbos, so it is not relevant to decree, end quote. In truth, it seems to my humble opinion that there is no dispute at all between Rashi's reason and the Yerushalmi, despite what is implied by some poskim. Certainly the Yerushalmi was not hidden from Rashi. Rather, Rashi explicitly explained his reason regarding the place where they were accustomed to prohibit labor the entire day, and Rashi explained that reason there. But the fact that from midday it is forbidden according to all, and that one is placed under excommunication, is necessarily because of the reason of the Yerushalmi. Therefore one who performs labor is placed under excommunication, and this is clear.
They place him under excommunication. The Rambam, in chapter 7 of Hilchos Yom Tov, wrote: and it goes without saying that they administer lashes of rebellion to him. The Maggid Mishneh wrote that the reason is that excommunication is more severe than lashes, as explained in the first chapter. So too wrote Tosafos in Kesubos, folio 25, s.v. Rav Huna, and in Rashi's commentary in Kiddushin, folio 70b. See Even HaEzer siman 26, 119, and 134, and see Be'er Sheva and Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim, siman 64.
They place him under excommunication. The implication of the Gemara and the poskim is that he also never sees a sign of blessing, meaning that what he earns from the labor he will lose from elsewhere. This applies specifically on Erev Pesach, unlike other eves of Yom Tov and Erev Shabbos, where one who performs labor from Minchah and onward is not placed under excommunication, but he does not see a sign of blessing, and he has committed a prohibited act, as stated in the Rambam, chapter 8 of Hilchos Yom Tov. So too wrote the Shiltei Gibborim, and so is explicitly proven from the sugya at the beginning of chapter Makom Shenahagu. This is simple and clear, unlike the Taz in Yoreh De'ah siman 270, who understood that since they do not place him under excommunication there is also no prohibition, and therefore raised a difficulty there against the Beis Yosef. In truth, this is not difficult at all. As for what the Taz further asks in Orach Chaim siman 585, and the Shiltei Gibborim in this siman, that the words of the Beis Yosef contradict each other, for in Yoreh De'ah siman 270 he wrote: one who sells a Torah scroll, and it implies from the fact that it says that they do not see a sign of blessing that it is forbidden to sell it, end quote. But in Orach Chaim siman 585 he wrote regarding that which the Rabbis taught, four perutos do not have a sign of blessing, one of them being the wages of a translator: if there were any aspect of prohibition in the matter, it should not have said that they do not have a sign of blessing, which implies that there is no prohibition in them, end quote. They left this as requiring study. In my humble opinion, this is not difficult at all. Certainly regarding those four perutos, necessarily they do not have a sign of blessing, but there is no prohibition. Proof is that it lists there the wages of those who write Torah scrolls. What is the reason? The Men of the Great Assembly sat in twenty-four fasts over those who write Torah scrolls, that they should not become wealthy, for if they became wealthy they would no longer write. If so, it follows that it is permitted to take payment; for otherwise they would still refrain from writing, since it would be forbidden to take payment, and this is clear. Since all of them are included in one clause, where it says that they do not have a sign of blessing, it follows that there is no prohibition in any of them. Similarly, one who takes orphans' money as wages, which is listed there, certainly has no prohibition at all. It is also possible that this is the intent of the Beis Yosef, who was precise in the wording: regarding these four perutos it says that they do not have a sign of blessing, implying that the perutos themselves do not have blessing, and it did not also say that one does not see in them a sign of blessing, as with one who sells a Torah scroll, which would imply that it speaks of the person engaged in it, that he is committing a prohibition. Thus the words of the Beis Yosef are correct without any question.
And so is the custom. Maharshal wrote that one may take a haircut after midday through a non-Jew, but not through a Jew. And the Magen Avraham in s.k. 3 wrote: As for barbers, even through a non-Jew it is forbidden to take a haircut after midday, citing Matteh Moshe. It appears to me that the reason is because he bends his head and assists somewhat, end quote. This reason is no reason at all, for what of it if he assists? If he can cut his own hair, he is permitted, for this is included in what they said, that he may repair his utensils for Yom Tov, as written in Responsa Mahari Mintz, siman 4. Although this law is not clear, for perhaps only repairing his utensils, which is mere repair, is permitted, but not actual labor, as the Beis Yosef wrote in explaining the view of the Raavyah, and as implied by the Mechaber and the Rav in the gloss in seif 2. Mahari Mintz must be understood as explaining the view of the Raavyah that even actual labor for one's own need for Yom Tov is permitted, as the Nachalas Tzvi also explained, disagreeing with the Beis Yosef. Nevertheless, regarding a haircut, where he bends his head and assists somewhat, this certainly is not actual labor, and assistance has no substance; it is permitted according to all opinions. And Mahari Weil and Maharil wrote that nail cutting is also forbidden after midday. My brother-in-law the Rav, in his work Olas Shabbos, permits nail cutting if one forgot to cut them before midday. Regarding the law of a mourner whose Erev Pesach falls within his thirty days, see Yoreh De'ah siman 399. And the Magen Avraham wrote that whatever is permitted on Chol HaMoed is all the more so permitted on Erev Pesach.
