One who goes from a place where they perform labor. I have already written this principle in my work Minchas Yaakov, klal 78, s.k. 7, and there I supported the words of the Shach in Yoreh De'ah siman 214, and disagreed there with the Nachalas Tzvi and others, who did not understand it that way. Now I have seen that the Magen Avraham also left the words of the Shach in Yoreh De'ah siman 14 as requiring study, and also mixed the opinions together. To clarify the matter, I had to clarify the opinions and the reason for their dispute as it appears to me. This entire seif copied by the Mechaber is the precise language of the Rambam in chapter 8 of Hilchos Yom Tov. From his wording, when he writes, 'and similarly, one whose intention is to return,' it is clear that the first part of the matter discusses one whose intention is not to return. He holds that one who goes from a place where they perform labor to a place where they do not perform labor may not perform it only because of dispute specifically; even if he wants to perform it privately, nevertheless, since he intends to settle there, we are concerned that once they may notice that he performs it, and this will lead to dispute. However, if his intention is to return, he may perform it privately, since he is there only incidentally and we are not concerned for dispute. But in the wilderness we are not concerned for dispute, and it is permitted to perform it even if his intention is not to return. So too wrote the Maggid Mishneh, that this is the Rambam's view, though some disagree, and this applies specifically outside the city's techum, as stated in the Gemara in Chullin, folio 110a. With respect to the stringencies of the place from which he left, even if his intention is not to return, he must follow the stringency of the place from which he left, unlike what is implied by the sugya in the first chapter of Chullin. Perhaps he holds that the sugya in chapter Makom Shenahagu disagrees with the case of Rabbi Zeira, and he does not hold like Tosafos's explanation there, that the teaching was stated in different contexts. All this follows the Rambam's approach. The reason, it appears to me, is that he holds that the primary custom takes effect upon the person from the place from which he left, while the stringencies of the place to which he went are only because of dispute, as written. However, from the words of the Rosh and the Ran in chapter Makom Shenahagu, and of the Rivash, siman 44, it is clear that they hold that the primary custom takes effect upon a person based on the place where he intends to be; the custom takes effect by law, for each city accepts its own customs upon itself and upon all who join them. That which they impose upon him the stringencies of the place from which he left applies when his intention is to return. But if his intention is not to return, he must uphold the custom of the place to which he went; even privately he may not perform it, and even in the wilderness, since the custom of the place where he now is takes effect upon him by law, and not because of dispute. However, if he has not yet once reached the city's techum, he is not obligated to act according to their custom. So wrote the Kol Bo in Hilchos Pesach in the name of Toras HaMinhagim. And if his intention is to return, he is not legally obligated to follow the stringencies of the place to which he went; he should be concerned to be stringent publicly only because of dispute, while privately everything is permitted, except for labor, which is a public matter, and therefore it is forbidden to perform it privately. All this is simple and clear as the reason for their dispute for one who studies their words carefully. It follows that the Mechaber, who copied here the language of the Rambam, appears to have ruled like him. So too it appears from his words below in siman 496, although the later authorities, following their own approach, distinguish there in several ways; see there.
