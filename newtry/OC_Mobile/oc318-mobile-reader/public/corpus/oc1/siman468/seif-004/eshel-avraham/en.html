To a place. People of the Diaspora who come to Eretz Yisrael: whether it is permitted for people of Eretz Yisrael to do melacha for them in private; see Teshuvos Zera Avraham, siman 9 and siman 10. And likewise whether a kohen from Eretz Yisrael may ascend the duchan for them on the second day of Yom Tov; see there, siman 11.
That he went there. A person of the Diaspora who came to dwell in Eretz Yisrael and was not permanently settled, and before one Festival arrived his intention was to return: is he obligated to observe a second day of Yom Tov? See there in Teshuvos Zera Avraham, siman 12; and Maharival, part 3, siman 93; and Yoreh De'ah siman 214. And the Ra'em wondered, from whichever way you turn: if they err and think it is forbidden, we hold that we tell them "you are permitted"; and if the prohibition is because of a fence, why specify erev Pesach? Even all year; see there that it is like a vow; see Yoreh De'ah siman 214. And it works out for him when they know it is permitted; rather the practical difference is for those who come from other places, who must practice the stringency of the place they went to; and Peri Chadash holds of a forced explanation; see there. Nevertheless there is a resolution: a custom they practiced as a fence, such as here, that their children are not permitted to change their custom, while on other days of the year they are permitted to change; and see Tosafos, Pesachim folio 51. And regarding this reasoning it appeared to me, the compiler, to resolve the difficulties of Peri Chadash, who found the words of Shulchan Aruch contradictory to one another: for in Yoreh De'ah siman 228 he wrote that if it was with an agreement as a fence for the public or a safeguard, etc., one may not permit, etc.; and he further wrote: but a known permitted matter in which he was stringent as a safeguard, one does not permit of itself; and he explains that through regret they certainly permit; see there. However, truth guides its way: after we find in the aforementioned Gemara, in Beishan they practiced not to go from Tyre to Sidon on erev Shabbos; they came before him; he said to them: your fathers were able, we, etc.; he said to them: your fathers already accepted "Hear, my son, the discipline of your father," etc. And so the Ran wrote explicitly: a matter that the fathers practiced as prohibition, and their children thought it was forbidden by law, it is forbidden to reveal to them that it is only because of a safeguard. And according to this, he wrote precisely in the past tense: if it was with agreement as a fence for the public or a safeguard; and that which the fathers accepted upon themselves, now their children stand in their place for prohibition, "do not forsake," etc., and one may not permit at all; which is not so regarding a known permitted matter in which he himself was stringent as a safeguard, meaning he alone was stringent, etc.: that is a vow, and they permit through an opening and regret; and it is easily understood.
