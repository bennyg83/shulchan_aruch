1) [Se'if 1] One who places his eruv in a cemetery, etc. The discussion applies even to an ordinary Jewish person, as explained in Beis Yosef. Rosh and Tur, however, maintain that it applies specifically to a kohen because he cannot reach the eruv during bein hashmashos, whereas an ordinary Jewish person can acquire an eruv in a cemetery. Bach and Derishah also hold this opinion. See there. Korban Nesanel on Rosh, chapter 3, likewise holds this view. Olas Shabbos cites several authorities who agree and concludes that one who is lenient does not lose, for these authorities are worthy of reliance even outside pressing circumstances, since Ashkenazim follow their rulings. See there. It nevertheless appears that Sephardim, who follow the Shulchan Aruch, should permit this only when there is a need and pressing circumstances.
2) There. Because a cemetery is forbidden for benefit. This discusses a built grave. If the eruv was placed on the natural ground of a grave, that ground is not forbidden, as stated at the beginning of Yoreh De'ah, siman 364. Magen Avraham, subsection 1; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1; Tosefes Shabbos, subsection 1. What is considered a built grave depends upon the dispute between the Tur and Rabbeinu Yeshayah there. According to Rabbeinu Yeshayah, even earth removed from the grave and subsequently replaced over it to cover the coffin has the law of a built grave, since it was detached and later reattached. Only a niche hewn into rock is called natural ground, because the rock above the niche was never detached, and the sides of the grave certainly have that status. According to the Tur, however, even earth that was detached and subsequently reattached has the status of natural ground because it remains earth; only an actual built grave is forbidden. See there. Perhaps one may rule leniently regarding an eruv in accordance with the Tur, particularly since the primary ruling there also appears to follow the Tur. Machatzis HaShekel. Tosefes Shabbos there, however, writes that Rema rules that earth placed over a corpse becomes forbidden like a built structure, and the same applies here. See there. Shach there, subsection 2, appears to agree with the Tur. It therefore appears that regarding an eruv one may be lenient, as Machatzis HaShekel writes.
3) There. After it takes effect, etc. At the moment the eruv takes effect, one establishes it for a mitzvah purpose, as stated at the beginning of siman 415, and mitzvos were not given for personal benefit. Magen Avraham, subsection 2; Tosefes Shabbos, subsection 2. Since one is pleased that it remains guarded there after it takes effect, however, one derives benefit unrelated to the mitzvah. Ma'amar Mordechai, subsection 2.
4) There. If he placed it in a beis haperas, etc. This is a field in which a grave was plowed. Its tumah is Rabbinic because of concern that a bone the size of a barley kernel lies there. Taz, subsection 1; Magen Avraham, subsection 3; Tosefes Shabbos, subsection 3. A beis haperas measures one hundred by one hundred amos, for the Sages determined that a plow does not carry bones farther than this. Although a bone the size of a barley kernel does not convey tumah through a tent, it conveys tumah through touch and carrying. A kohen may not walk there lest his foot move a bone, which would constitute carrying it. Machatzis HaShekel.
5) There. Even if he was a kohen, etc. Although the eruv became tamei and kohanim ordinarily eat terumah in a state of purity, so that it is unfit for him, it is nevertheless fit for an ordinary Jewish person, as stated in siman 386, se'if 8. Magen Avraham, subsection 4; Tosefes Shabbos, subsection 4; Eshel Avraham, subsection 4.
6) There. In a mobile tower. This means a large box measuring one amah by one amah and three amos high that is carried by animals. He can retrieve the eruv with a flat wooden utensil less than one handbreadth wide and bring it to himself. Magen Avraham, subsection 5. The Magen Avraham's statement that he can retrieve it must be understood as an independent case and not as referring to a beis haperas. A beis haperas does not convey tumah through a tent, so there would be no concern even if the utensil were one handbreadth wide. Rather, it refers to an isolated grave that is not constructed, which is permitted, as stated above in subsection 2. He may approach it by means of a carriage, box, or tower. Even according to the authority who maintains that a moving tent is not considered a tent, it is nevertheless permitted at an isolated grave, for there is no greater distancing measure than this, as Tosafos write. The wooden utensil must therefore be less than one handbreadth wide so that it does not contract tumah through a tent. Tosefes Shabbos, subsection 5. Eshel Avraham, subsection 5, agrees with this explanation. See Machatzis HaShekel.
7) A kohen who has an agent place terumah in a beis haperas upon a flat wooden utensil does not have a valid eruv. Eshel Avraham, subsection 6.
8) There. Or he should blow with his mouth. If a bone the size of a barley kernel is present, he will move it by blowing. If it is larger, he will see it. Magen Avraham, subsection 6, based on the emendation of Yad Efrayim and Machatzis HaShekel.
