Se'if 1: One must juxtapose redemption to prayer, etc. The reason is stated explicitly in the Yerushalmi cited by Tur: This is comparable to the king's friend who knocked at his door. The king came out to learn what he sought, but found that the friend had departed; the king then departed as well. On this basis one can explain the Sages' exposition of the verse, 'And I have done what is good in Your eyes.' What does this mean? That he juxtaposed redemption to prayer. Many have wondered how the Sages knew that this particular deed was intended; I have already discussed this elsewhere. It now appears as follows. The phrase 'good in Your eyes' implies: good in Your eyes, but not in my own eyes, just as the Sages expounded 'The matter was good in my eyes' to mean, as Rashi explains, but not in the eyes of the Omnipresent. Here the meaning is the reverse: good in Your eyes but not in human eyes. Scripture is careful about this point. In Parashas Shoftim it repeatedly says to do what is upright and good in Hashem's eyes, using both 'upright' and 'good.' Yalkut explains: good in the eyes of Heaven and upright in human eyes; another opinion reverses them. See the dispute between R' Yishmael and R' Akiva there. In any event, Scripture specifies that the act is also proper in human eyes, so it should not appear, God forbid, that it is not good in their eyes. Here too, then, the verse should have said, 'What is good and upright in Your eyes I have done.' Why does it mention only 'good'? It therefore refers to juxtaposing redemption to prayer. The need for this juxtaposition follows the Yerushalmi's parable: The king immediately comes personally to meet the beloved friend who knocks. This reason applies only to one who is truly beloved and esteemed by the king. For one who is not beloved, the king does not immediately come when he knocks, but waits until he cries out and pleads; even when responding, the king may send a messenger. The same applies, as it were, to Hashem. Unique exalted individuals such as R' Chiya and his sons merited an immediate response while still reciting praise: when they said, 'Who causes the wind to blow,' the wind blew. The Sages similarly said of Shmuel HaKatan, 'How did the congregation's praise begin? He said, Who causes the wind to blow, and the wind blew, etc.' Such exceptional people merit that while they are still mentioning His praise, Hashem Himself immediately comes to meet them. The general public does not merit His coming immediately before prayer; even afterward, may their prayer at least be accepted through an angel. For them, the Yerushalmi's reason for juxtaposing redemption to prayer seemingly does not apply. Moreover, even the greatest tzaddikim require exceptional humility; Moshe Rabbeinu proves this. How could they regard themselves as Hashem's beloved at such a lofty level, and how could they avoid the concern of arrogance when juxtaposing redemption to prayer? Were it not Hashem's desire, they could not intentionally do so. But Hashem desires it: He regards all Israel as children of kings and as His beloved, and asks them to juxtapose redemption to prayer. Thus the verse says, 'I have done what is good in Your eyes': It is good in Your eyes, though not in my own, for I know that I have not attained this level. Since it is good in Your eyes, however, I have done so. The verse must therefore refer to juxtaposing redemption to prayer. This resolves the matter.
