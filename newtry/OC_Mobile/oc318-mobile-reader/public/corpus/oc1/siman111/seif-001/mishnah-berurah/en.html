(1) Must place immediately before. At Mussaf and Minchah, however, one may recite verses before “Hashem, open my lips,” but not afterward, because this verse is part of the prayer. Indeed, for this reason it may be recited between redemption and prayer. Since the Sages incorporated it into the prayer, it has the status of prayer, and one therefore may not interrupt between it and the Amidah.
(2) Between them. Even a silent pause longer than the time needed to speak should preferably be avoided. The time needed to speak is the time required for a student to greet his teacher.
(3) Even with Amen, etc. The same applies to Kaddish and Kedushah, to which one may not respond, as stated above in siman 66, seif 9. The Mechaber mentions Amen because the series of blessings of Krias Shema ends there, and one might have thought that he may answer Amen even after his own blessing, as discussed below in siman 215, seif 1. He therefore teaches that it is forbidden because it constitutes an interruption, and one should not even answer Amen after the chazzan.
(4) Nor with any verse. Even at Maariv, before the Amidah, one should be careful regarding this.
(5) Amen. However, he does not interrupt for Yehei Shmei Rabba or Kedushah, because Amen relates to the blessing and therefore is not considered an interruption, unlike those responses.
(6) And this is the custom. This means after the chazzan, but not after one’s own blessing. See above, siman 66, in the gloss and in the Mishnah Berurah there.
(7) And Shabbos, etc. On Yom Tov, since that day would ordinarily be a day of distress on other days, the verse “May Hashem answer you,” also applies. It is recited after the verse “May the words of my mouth be acceptable,” which speaks of redemption.
(8) Is not a time of distress. Beit Yosef disagrees and maintains that the verse is merely an allusion. The principal obligation to place redemption immediately before prayer is a Rabbinic enactment. The Sages compared reciting the eighteen blessings after the blessing of redemption to a beloved friend of the king who came and knocked at the palace gate. The king came out to meet him, but if the king then saw that the friend had withdrawn and departed, the king too would withdraw and would not readily draw near when the friend returned and knocked again. So too, this applies to one who interrupts between redemption and prayer. Rama therefore writes that it is proper to be stringent.
(9) When there is a need. This means that on Shabbos, even at Shacharis, it is permitted to answer Yehei Shmei Rabba, Kedushah, and Barechu between redemption and prayer. Eliyah Rabbah, Peri Megadim, and Shalmei Tzibbur rule this way. See Biur Halachah.
