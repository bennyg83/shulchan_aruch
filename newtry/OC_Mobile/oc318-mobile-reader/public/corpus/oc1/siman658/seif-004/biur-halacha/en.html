Even if he gave him its value. See Mishnah Berurah; this is the language of the poskim. According to this, if he has another lulav with which to fulfill the obligation, it is reasonable that giving money helps, at least where the object no longer exists. See Choshen Mishpat siman 241:7 in the Rema, and Ketzos HaChoshen and Nesivos there. This is like the case of brothers who bought, etc., if he can eat it, etc.; see there in chapter Yesh Nochalin, daf 137. So too it appears that if he returns to him another valid and beautiful lulav, it helps, for then there is no longer an assessment of intent. Although one could say that a person wants his own, nevertheless, since in any case there are poskim who hold that if it was burned or lost and the like, he gives its value and fulfills the obligation, see Kol Bo, and the view of Rabbeinu Yeshayah is also brought in the Rosh in this sugya, from which it is even more evident that where unavoidable accident occurred he is entirely exempt and fulfills the obligation, for the giver intended 'on condition to return' only when the object is intact. Although the Mechaber did not rely on them, and it is implied from him that even money does not help, even in a case of unavoidable accident, as we wrote in Mishnah Berurah, nevertheless, where he returns to him another valid and beautiful lulav, perhaps everyone agrees. In the book Sdei Chemed he brought in the name of the book Shem Aryeh that even in this case he has not fulfilled the obligation; I have written what appears to my humble mind.
After the time of its mitzvah. See Mishnah Berurah regarding where he said 'on condition that you return it' and did not say 'to me.' So it is in Choshen Mishpat siman 241:6 in the gloss there, in the name of the Rashba in responsum, siman 1000. Seemingly there is a contradiction to this from seif 5, where even if he gave it to him without specifying, we say that he certainly gave it to him on condition that he return it in order to fulfill the obligation with it; and this is no worse than if he had said nothing. I found that the Eliyah Rabbah already raised this in the name of Responsa Beis Yaakov. Therefore we must say that the Rema there holds that since he specified his words, 'on condition to return,' but did not specify 'to return it to me,' this is worse than an unspecified case. Nevertheless, in practice this requires investigation, for several Acharonim question this reasoning [see Korban Nesanel in this sugya, and Bikkurei Yaakov]. Their opinion is that the Baal HaItur, who holds that even when unspecified his intent is that it be returned at the time of its mitzvah, all the more so when he makes a condition 'on condition that you return it,' even though he did not say 'to me'; and he disagrees with the Rashba. Know that even when one says 'on condition that you return it to me,' where he is obligated to return it at the time of its mitzvah, there is also a distinction. If he has no other lulav, he is obligated to return it that day if he has not recited the berachah, or at least on the next day at the time of its mitzvah, as we wrote in Mishnah Berurah, because it is an evident assessment of intent. But if he has another valid lulav on which to recite the berachah, although he is not permitted to return it to him after the festival, since he said 'return it to me' and referred to something fit for him, nevertheless he is not obligated to return it that day or the next. Whenever he returns it to him during the festival, even on the seventh day, he has fulfilled his condition, for it still stands for its mitzvah, and he did not specify which day or how much it must be worth at the time of return. I gathered all this from the words of the above responsum of the Rashba; see there. Know further that it is close to say that even if the giver already recited the berachah, if his dependent sons have not yet recited it, and their usual way is always to bless on their father's lulav, he also has not fulfilled the obligation if he did not return it, for this too is an evident assessment of intent, since a person does not leave his sons without a mitzvah and give it to others. Afterward I found that Mor Uketziah also inclines this way.
And it is permitted to give it to him as a gift on condition that he not consecrate it. See the Rosh, Ran, Ritva, and other poskim, who wrote the reason for the matter: the rule that any gift that cannot be consecrated is not a gift, which we learned, comes only to exclude where it is mere evasion and he did not intend to give at all. But where there is no evasion, even if the gift is only for one thing, it also takes effect. So too Tosafos wrote in Sukkah 42; see there at length. This is also clearly implied in the sugya in Bava Basra 137a, which says there: rather here, if he does not fulfill the obligation with it, for what did he give it to him? See the Rashbam's explanation there. It is explained that even if he gives it to him for nothing except fulfilling the mitzvah of the festival, this too is called giving, since it is his for this matter. Consequently it appears that all the more so if he said to him, 'it is yours as a gift on condition that you not give it to anyone,' or even if he says to him, 'it is yours and you are not permitted to give it to anyone,' it is certainly his and he fulfills the obligation with it, even though he did not give him all his power in this esrog. So too it is implied in the Meiri, who brought that some rule that one who gives a gift on condition to return may not give it to many people except with permission of the first giver. Although there is one who permits this, as is evident from the Mechaber in seif 5, there the reason is that he did not make an explicit condition, but said only 'on condition that it be returned to me,' and it was returned to him. In any event, it is proven that where he explicitly gives a gift but says the recipient may not give it to another, nevertheless he fulfills the obligation with it. Now, the Gaon of Lissa in Derech HaChayim was stringent about this and wrote that whenever the recipient does not have the power to give it to another, it is not a gift at all. In my humble opinion this is not so, as we have proven. I found in Sdei Chemed that he already brought the view of several Geonim who disagree with him about this.
