(There, seif 9.) The practice in a place where esrogim are not common, etc. Note: One who examines Rashi's explanation in Sukkah 27b, s.v. 'kol ha'ezrach beYisrael,' etc., where he wrote that it is impossible to require a perutah's worth for each one, would seemingly infer from this that with lulav, although one fulfills the obligation with one owned by partners, nevertheless we require that each person have a perutah's worth. But in truth this is not so. Even if each one had a perutah's worth, still all of it is not his, and regarding esrog we require that all of it be his. Rather, the main reason one fulfills the obligation with it is that each one transfers his share to his fellow. If so, what difference does it make that he does not have a perutah's worth in it? His fellow transfers all of it to him, and it all becomes his. But regarding sukkah, where one cannot say that they intended to transfer the entire sukkah to each person so that when each one sits in it, all of it is his on condition to return, for then it would not be a sukkah fit for seven days, as I wrote in my work Sefer HaChayim, siman 637. Therefore one cannot reject the proof here by saying that it is a gift on condition to return, especially according to those who hold here that partners presumably intend a gift on condition to return. If so, what proof is there that a borrowed sukkah is valid? Perforce, because it would be a sukkah not fit for seven days. But with lulav, where we do not require that it be fit for seven days, we say that they intended a gift on condition to return. However, from this it would be proven that if the whole esrog is not worth a perutah, it does not help even though it is actually his; anything less than a perutah does not help and is not considered his. For if less than a perutah is also considered his, what is this that Rashi wrote, that it is impossible for each one to have a perutah's worth? What of it if there is no perutah's worth? In the end he has a share in it. It is proven that specifically with a perutah's worth it is considered his, and with less than a perutah it is not his. If so, with an esrog we require that it be worth a perutah. This is a novel law that the poskim did not explain. In truth, Rashi's explanation requires investigation, for it implies that specifically because it is impossible for each one to have a perutah's worth, he does not fulfill the obligation, but if each one did have a perutah's worth, he would fulfill it. What of it? In the end, if regarding sukkah we require that it be his, then even if he has a perutah's worth, still all of it is not his. And if with sukkah too it is applicable to say that they intend to transfer each one's share to his fellow, then even if it has no perutah's worth, in the end his fellow transfers all of it to him. But if with sukkah it is not applicable to say that he transfers all of it to him, then even if he had a perutah's worth, what would it help? In the end they are partners. Why with lulav does partnership not help even if each one has a perutah's worth, were it not for the reason that each one transfers his share to the other? What difference is there between sukkah and lulav, if with sukkah too we require that it be actually his? Why did Rashi need to prove from the fact that it is impossible for each one to have a perutah's worth? Seemingly it would appear that Rashi holds that one cannot say the case is where each one explicitly transferred his whole share to his fellow, for if so, what does the verse teach us? It is obvious that they fulfill the obligation with it, for it is actually his. The fact that a gift on condition to return is called a gift is learned from elsewhere in the entire Torah, that it is a gift, and Scripture did not need to write this here. Perforce, the main novelty is that Scripture teaches us that even unspecified, they also fulfill the obligation, for unspecified is like a gift on condition to return. This works if each one has a perutah's worth, for then he is considered a real partner, and to a partner one presumably transfers on condition to return. But where he does not have a perutah's worth, the name partner does not apply to him, and to such a partner his fellows do not transfer ownership unless he says so explicitly. This is similar to what we say generally, that partners are not particular with each other and waive to each other. Therefore, if he has a perutah's worth in it, he is considered a partner and his fellow transfers to him without specification; but if he does not have a perutah's worth in it, he is not considered a partner and is only like an ordinary person, and his fellow does not transfer to him without specification. According to this, it would appear that if the whole esrog is his, we do not require a perutah's worth, and even less than a perutah is his. Only if it is not entirely his do we require that each one have a perutah's worth in it. Therefore according to Rashi, it was necessary to say that the whole congregation cannot fulfill the obligation with one esrog, since it is impossible for each one to have a perutah's worth in it, and they are not considered partners, and it is not theirs unless each one explicitly transfers his share. This matter requires investigation regarding the poskim, who did not bring Rashi's view in this. Examine it well.
