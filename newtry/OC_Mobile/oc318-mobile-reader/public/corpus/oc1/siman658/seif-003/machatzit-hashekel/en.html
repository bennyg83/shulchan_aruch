(Seif katan 3) A person does not, etc. This requires investigation, for in Even HaEzer, etc. One may say that there it speaks where he does not know, meaning that he does not know that a woman cannot be betrothed with a borrowed item. The intent of the Magen Avraham in his answer is as follows: Granted, regarding betrothal, where 'yours' is not written, we do not require that the object belong to the husband. We require only that the woman receive something from the husband and that the object not be stolen in the woman's hand. If so, since he did not know that she cannot be betrothed with a borrowed item, we say that for this reason he used the language of borrowing out of lack of knowledge, for he thought that a borrowed item was also enough; and if he had known that she cannot be betrothed with a borrowed item, he would have given it as a gift. Therefore, although he used the language of borrowing, and we cannot say that he was imprecise in his wording and meant a gift, nevertheless she is betrothed. Although it still does not belong to the husband, for it was given to the husband as a loan and not as a gift, and the husband gave it to the woman as a gift, in any event it is not stolen in the woman's hand. For when the lender now learns that a woman cannot be betrothed with a borrowed item, he will give it as a gift, and therefore it is not stolen in the woman's hand, even though the husband and woman did not acquire it retroactively, but only from the time of the gift onward. Nevertheless this suffices, and it is not stolen in her hand. But with an esrog, where we require 'yours,' although in the end, when the lender learns that one does not fulfill the obligation with a borrowed item, he will give it as a gift, nevertheless at the time of taking it was not 'yours,' and he did not fulfill the obligation.
And in truth, one who knows, etc. Meaning, according to this, the language of borrowing that he used is precise, except that we say it was from lack of knowledge, as above, and if he had known, he would have used the language of a gift. If so, one who knows that she cannot be betrothed with a borrowed item and nevertheless used the language of borrowing, she is not betrothed, for it is stolen in her hand.
So too it is explicit in the Rosh, first chapter of Kiddushin. Meaning, he wrote the reason: not everyone is expert in the laws, that she cannot be betrothed with a borrowed item. This implies that if he knew the law, he agrees that she is not betrothed. See Beis Shmuel there.
And the same applies to an esrog. Meaning, since even when he knew, she is betrothed, necessarily the above reason does not apply. Rather, we must say that the language of borrowing that he used was not precise, and he was not exact in his wording. In any event, he intended a gift on condition to return, because he wants to fulfill the request of the one asking to betroth a woman, so the lender's intent is certainly in a way effective for betrothal. If so, the same applies to an esrog: in such a case it helps, for it is 'yours.'
So too it is implied above in siman 14, regarding one who borrows a fringed garment, that he recites a berachah over it, although regarding tzitzis too we require 'yours.' With an esrog, a gift on condition to return helps more, because it is a mitzvah from which one cannot exempt himself; he certainly gave it wholeheartedly as a gift on condition to return, at least. This is not so with tzitzis, for if one does not have a four-cornered garment he is exempt; perhaps he did not intend a gift, and his true intent is that he should not recite a berachah over it. See there, where he discusses this at length. Regarding this the Magen Avraham wrote: this requires investigation, for it is implied in the Talmud that in every place a gift on condition to return helps, except for betrothal, where it does not, because it resembles exchange, and a woman is not acquired through exchange, as stated in Kiddushin.
