A person does not fulfill his obligation, etc. If the owner is not at home and his wife gives the lulav as a gift to one of his friends or relatives, it appears that the recipient does not fulfill the obligation, because the owner may prefer to sell its use for one day. Selling it is permitted, because one may confer a benefit upon a person in his absence. If the owner is a distinguished person for whom selling the esrog would be demeaning, it is permitted, because we assess that he would prefer it to be given. See siman 367. This requires study, for Even HaEzer 28:19 rules that one who borrows an object from another and informs him that he wishes to betroth a woman with it effects valid kiddushin, because it is presumed to have been given as a gift on condition that it be returned. The same is written above in siman 14. Why should we not say the same here? One can answer that there the lender does not know the law, and therefore the item is not stolen, because we are certain that had he known, he would have transferred it as a gift. Here, however, the Torah requires 'yours,' meaning actual ownership. Indeed, one who knows that a borrowed item cannot be used for kiddushin does not transfer it merely through language of lending unless he explicitly uses language of gift, as is clear from Rosh, Kiddushin, chapter 1. In the chapter Yesh Nochalin, however, Rosh implies that even where the lender knew, the recipient fulfills his obligation. The same should apply to an esrog: if it was lent for the purpose of fulfilling the mitzvah, it is as though it was given as a gift. See there and what I wrote above in siman 14:3. Yam Shel Shlomo, Chullin, chapter 8, siman 53, implies that a gift on condition of return is more effective for an esrog than for tzitzis. His words require study.
