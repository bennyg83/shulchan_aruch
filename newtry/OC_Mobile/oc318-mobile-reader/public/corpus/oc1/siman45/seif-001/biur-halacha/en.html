It is forbidden to enter a cemetery, etc. Peri Megadim writes that there are three opinions in this matter. Bach holds that even in a cemetery the prohibition applies only within four amos of a grave. Beyond four amos from a grave it is permitted, even while standing in the middle of the cemetery, provided no grave is within four amos. Taz explains that by strict law the prohibition applies only within four amos of a grave. Tur nevertheless imposed a safeguard and prohibited standing anywhere within the cemetery, even beyond four amos from a grave. This means the area designated for burial. Outside the cemetery, however, Taz would impose no additional stringency, as Peri Megadim explains, because one does not make a safeguard around another safeguard. Magen Avraham explains that even within four amos of the cemetery itself it is forbidden. Peri Megadim asks why the Mechaber did not simply combine the clauses and write: “It is forbidden to enter within four amos of a cemetery or a dead person.” Machatzis HaShekel in siman 71, however, explains that Magen Avraham agrees with Taz that outside the boundary of the cemetery, meaning the land designated for burial, there is no prohibition even one amah beyond it, provided one remains four amos from the nearest grave and no partition intervenes. They disagree only inside the cemetery. Magen Avraham holds that once one stands within the cemetery boundary, whether the cemetery is surrounded by a wall or merely designated for burial, the prohibition applies by strict law even beyond four amos from the graves. Accordingly, when Magen Avraham writes here that even within four amos of the cemetery is forbidden, he refers to a case where the graves begin immediately at the cemetery boundary. This also removes Peri Megadim's question. The Mechaber mentions specifically entering a cemetery to add that from the point where the designated cemetery ground begins and inward, it is forbidden even beyond four amos from the graves. Know further that Biur HaGra in siman 23 holds that within four amos of a cemetery is forbidden and refers the reader to Magen Avraham here. This also implies that he understands Magen Avraham like Machatzis HaShekel and only in the specific manner explained above, since Gra cites it as a source for the Mechaber's ruling that within four amos of a grave is forbidden. This interpretation is not forced. Taz himself, who does not impose any added stringency outside a cemetery, writes in Yoreh Deah, siman 282, seif katan 3, that within four amos of a cemetery is forbidden. He too must be referring to the manner described by Machatzis HaShekel. The practical result is that outside land designated for burial there is certainly no prohibition at all, even one amah beyond the boundary, provided one remains four amos from a grave. Even regarding Magen Avraham's stringency within the cemetery itself beyond four amos from a grave, the matter requires consideration. Bach and Olas Tamid both rule leniently, and Taz agrees with them regarding the strict law. Peri Megadim also challenges Magen Avraham: if the entire area designated for burial is forbidden even very far from the graves, how did the custom arise to recite Kaddish in a cemetery? See Yoreh Deah, siman 376. Nahar Shalom likewise writes that it is an excessive stringency to forbid beyond four amos, because this would require prohibiting all land designated for burial, even very far away. Nevertheless, once one is inward from the actual graves, if he happens to be more than four amos from a particular grave, Nahar Shalom writes that there is room to be stringent.
