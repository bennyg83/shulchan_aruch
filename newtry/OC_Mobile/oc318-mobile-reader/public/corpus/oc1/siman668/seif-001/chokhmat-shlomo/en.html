Seif 1. "On the night of the eighth day one says in the prayer, etc." NB: Regarding the law if one said in the Shemoneh Esreh "Chag HaSukkot," whether he fulfilled, it now appears to me that there is proof he fulfilled by Torah law. In Parashat Re'eh, the Torah counts all the festivals and mentions "you shall make Chag HaSukkot," but does not mention Shemini Atzeret. Perforce it is included in Chag HaSukkot. Although it says "seven days," nevertheless the Sages expounded "and you shall be only joyful" to include the night of the last Yom Tov of the festival; if so, since the night of the last Yom Tov is hinted at, all the more the day, for the honor of the day is more stringent than the honor of the night. With this, the Sages' statement is well understood: "ach sameach" includes the night of the last Yom Tov. People ask that everywhere "ach" is a limitation, so why here is it an inclusion? They also ask how we know it refers to the night of the last Yom Tov; perhaps it refers to the day of the last Yom Tov. But one question answers the other. Regarding the night, "ach" is indeed a limitation: it should not have joy like the day. From this we automatically learn that by day he is obligated in joy, and it follows that the phrase refers to the night. Then the word "ach" is well understood as limiting, meaning to limit the joy from that of the day, and it is automatically proven that by day he is obligated in greater joy. But if we say that "and you shall be only joyful" includes the day of the last Yom Tov, it is difficult, for "ach" is a limitation and here it is an inclusion. Perforce "ach sameach" refers to the night. Since Shemini Atzeret is alluded to in the Torah and is not named separately, it is proven that Shemini Atzeret is included in Chag HaSukkot. Also, from the wording of the Gemara in Sukkah regarding Pazar Kashav, where Rashi explains that it is a separate regel for another matter and does not explain that it has a separate name, it is proven that by name it too is called Chag HaSukkot. This resolves it. Examine carefully. See Magen Avraham s.k. 2, that some do not sit in the sukkah at night and sit in it by day, and the Tur writes that this is not the custom. In my humble opinion this is proven from Rashi's explanation on Sukkah 48, s.v. ha lan livnei Bavel: "and he must sit in it tomorrow." Since he said "tomorrow" and did not say that he must eat in it that day and night, for it is Yom Tov and he must eat bread and make kiddush, this proves explicitly that Rashi holds that at night one does not eat there, only by day. Therefore, for the night he could have reduced it, but what will he do tomorrow? Therefore he cannot reduce it. It is explicit in Rashi like this view. Perforce the reduction would be before Yom Tov, for on Yom Tov it is forbidden to reduce it; and if before Yom Tov, Rashi should have explained that he must eat there both at night and tomorrow. Perforce it follows this view, that at night they do not eat there, only tomorrow. This is strong support from Rashi for that view. Examine carefully. Regarding the reason the Sages enacted that on Shemini Atzeret one sits in the sukkah because of doubt that it may be the seventh, but did not enact taking the lulav without a beracha for the same reason: one cannot say it is because lulav is rabbinic on the other days and therefore it is a rabbinic doubt treated leniently, for according to those who hold that where an issur has been established even rabbinic doubts are treated stringently, this is not so. Indeed, regarding Chanukah all the later authorities asked why they did not enact nine days because of doubt of the day, even though all of Chanukah is rabbinic; the Mordechai asked similarly regarding Purim. If so, the same question applies here. It appears resolved according to what I wrote in the derashah for Shemini Atzeret in my work on the Torah of this year, 5609. I concluded there that since Rabbi Eliezer holds that Shemini Atzeret can serve as compensation for one who did not eat on the first night of Yom Tov, although the Rabbis disagree, that is only where he intentionally nullified it; if he failed through compulsion, such as because of rain and the like, they agree that completion on Shemini Atzeret helps. See there, where I explained the reason. Therefore this is well resolved: the Sages enacted this regarding sukkah, even though we are expert in the calendar, so that perhaps if one missed the first night under compulsion it will serve as compensation. This applies to sukkah, since until Shemini Atzeret all the days are Torah law, and here, where nights do not interrupt days, it is like one long day, as stated in Beitzah; therefore Shemini Atzeret automatically serves as compensation. But with lulav, since by Torah law only the first day applies, the nights interrupt the days, and the days also interrupt between the first day and the eighth; therefore there is no compensation on the eighth. For doubt of the day alone, they did not enact it, lest Yom Tov be treated lightly. This resolves it. Examine carefully. I saw in the siddur printed by the gaon of Lissa, may the memory of the righteous be a blessing, an astonishing statement: he wanted to innovate that something over which one does not recite leishev basukkah, such as fruits and drink, is forbidden to eat in the sukkah on Shemini Atzeret, since no beracha is recited over it and there is no recognizable distinction, so it is like sleeping, for one does not sleep in the sukkah on Shemini Atzeret for this reason. His words are astonishing. Sleeping is different, for on Sukkot itself one is obligated to sleep in the sukkah; therefore if he sleeps there also on the eighth without any recognizable distinction, he appears to be adding, and it can also lead to treating Yom Tov lightly. But those items are foods, and yet one does not recite a beracha over them; perforce this is because they are exempt from sukkah. If even on Sukkot itself they are exempt from sukkah, the concern of bal tosif does not apply to them, as is proven in Rosh Hashanah, Sanhedrin, and several places. There is also no concern of belittling Yom Tov, since all year it is permitted to eat such things in a sukkah. The concern of belittling Yom Tov applies specifically to those things that are obligated in sukkah and may be done only in the sukkah; but things that are permitted to eat in a sukkah all year, since bal tosif does not apply to them, will not lead to treating Yom Tov lightly. Therefore there is no concern of issur at all; this is clear and correct in practical law. Examine carefully. See Taz s.k. 1, s.v. katav Maharil, until the end. His words prove explicitly that he holds that if one accepted Yom Tov while it is still day and is exempt by Torah law from the mitzvah of sukkah, his status is as if it were already night. It seems to me that I already explained elsewhere in our work that his words contradict what he wrote in siman 600 regarding shofar, where he wrote the opposite: even though they accepted Shabbat, the obligation of Shabbat takes effect upon them; see there. But now I find this astonishing from Yoma 46b, where it says that the sukkah is forbidden on the eighth because if a meal happened to him at bein hashmashot, he would need to eat in it. This speaks in the time of the Temple, when there were not two days of Yom Tov, as the Gemara says afterward, "and now we have two days." Especially if there were two days, the sukkah would in any case be forbidden on the eighth because it is obligated all day. Perforce it speaks where there was only one day of Yom Tov. If so, either way: if he did not pray and make kiddush for the day, in any case it is forbidden to eat at bein hashmashot before kiddush; snacks do not require a sukkah and bread is forbidden to eat. To say he would act in an issur certainly cannot make it muktzeh because of ho'il that he could act unlawfully; on the contrary, we should establish him in his presumption of fitness, that presumably he will not transgress. Why should it become muktzeh because of this? Perforce the meaning is that if he already prayed and made kiddush, like one who prayed the Shabbat prayer on erev Shabbat and sanctified the day, then he is permitted to eat and is obligated in sukkah. If so, it is proven that even if he prayed and made kiddush for the day, he is obligated in sukkah. Although by law it is forbidden on the eighth because of bal tosif, nevertheless he is obligated in sukkah because it is still day. This is a clear proof for the Maharshal. But if so, I find difficulty with the Gemara in Berachot, which says that Rav prayed the Shabbat prayer on erev Shabbat and then asks whether he says kiddush over the cup or not. Why not resolve it from here that one does say kiddush over the cup? Otherwise, how can it say "if a meal happened to him," since it is forbidden to eat before kiddush? This requires study. Examine carefully. Regarding whether one is obligated to sleep in the sukkah on the eighth, the poskim disagree. In my novellae long ago I proved that one should not sleep in the sukkah on the eighth. But now, in my humble opinion, it appears possible to prove that one is obligated to sleep in the sukkah. In the Gemara there, one opinion says the eighth is for the beracha and the seventh is for sukkah, and another says the eighth is for both. The Gemara says: regarding sitting, everyone agrees we sit; they disagree only regarding the beracha. According to the one who says the seventh is for sukkah, we also recite the beracha; according to the one who says the eighth is for both, we do not recite the beracha. This is difficult to me. According to the one who says we recite the beracha, it is obvious that one is also obligated to sleep, for the main reason of the Mordechai that one does not sleep is because it appears as adding; eating does not appear as adding because he does not recite the beracha. If so, according to the one who recites the beracha, he is certainly obligated to sleep; and according to the one who does not recite the beracha, he is not obligated to sleep according to the Mordechai. Thus, just as they disagree about the beracha, they also disagree about sukkah itself. Why did the Gemara frame their dispute regarding the beracha, when berachot do not prevent fulfillment? It should have said more directly that they disagree regarding sleep: according to the view that the eighth is for both, one does not sleep in the sukkah, and according to the view that the seventh is for sukkah, one is obligated even to sleep there; regarding sitting, everyone agrees we sit. Especially since afterward it wants to say that they disagree about whether we sit, it should have explained that one could also say they disagree about sleep: according to the view that the eighth is for both, one does not sleep in the sukkah; according to the view that the seventh is for sukkah, one sleeps in the sukkah. Even more astonishing is what it says there: Rav Yosef said, hold Rabbi Yochanan's ruling in your hand, for Rav Huna bar Bizna and all the great men of the generation once happened to be in a sukkah on the doubtful seventh; they sat but did not recite the beracha. The Gemara asks: perhaps they held like the view that once one recited the beracha on the first day he no longer recites it, and answers that we have a tradition that they came from the field. This is difficult: why introduce a new fact that they came from the field? According to the Mordechai, perforce according to the view that one does not recite the beracha, one does not sleep in the sukkah. Since the Gemara wants to prove from them that they hold one does not recite the beracha, perforce they also did not sleep in the sukkah. Since they did not sleep in the sukkah, it is automatically proven that they hold one does not recite the beracha, for eating has a recognizable distinction in that one does not recite the beracha, whereas sleep has no recognizable distinction; therefore they ate there but did not sleep there. But if they held that once one recited the beracha on the first day he no longer recites it, then eating has no recognizable distinction at all; if nevertheless they were not concerned for bal tosif, they should also have slept there. What distinction would there be between sleeping and eating? The Gemara should have answered that since they did not sleep there, it is proven that they hold one recites a beracha every day, and therefore there is a distinction between sleeping and eating; if nevertheless they did not recite the beracha that day, it is proven that one does not recite the beracha. Why did it need to answer that they came from the field? It should have answered that they did not sleep in the sukkah. From this, since it proves they did not recite the beracha, it seems plainly that there is no logic to distinguish eating from sleeping, and one is obligated to sleep just as one is obligated to eat. The words of the Mordechai require study. I later saw this last question in Responsa Noda BiYehudah, first edition; see there and examine very carefully.
There, in the same seif. "And outside Eretz Yisrael they eat in the sukkah at night and by day because it is possibly the seventh, and they do not recite the beracha over sitting in it, etc." NB: See Magen Avraham in the name of the Mordechai that one does not sleep in the sukkah. I saw in the novellae of the pious gaon R. Eliyahu of Vilna, may the memory of the righteous be a blessing, that he wrote that from Rashi's explanation in Eruvin 96, s.v. ela me'atah hayashen bashemini yilkeh, where Rashi explained "for we sit in it," it is proven that Rashi holds one even sleeps in the sukkah; see there. I say the opposite: from there it is proven otherwise. One should examine why the Gemara asks, "if so, one who sleeps in the sukkah on the eighth should receive lashes," while Rashi says "we sit in it" and does not write "we sleep in the sukkah." Another difficulty: why does the Gemara ask about one who sleeps on the eighth receiving lashes? It should have asked that one who eats in the sukkah or sits in the sukkah should receive lashes. Another difficulty: why ask specifically from the eighth? It should have asked that one who sleeps in a sukkah all year should receive lashes. Why ask specifically about sleeping on the eighth? Further, according to Rashi, who explained there that one sleeps on the eighth not for the sake of a mitzvah, there is a difficulty either way. If he sleeps for the sake of a mitzvah, what is the law? If we say that for the sake of a mitzvah he does not receive lashes, then Rashi's words contradict one another according to the view of the Gaon of Vilna, who understood Rashi in Eruvin to hold that what one does because of a rabbinic enactment receives lashes. And if his intent is that for the sake of a mitzvah he certainly receives lashes, then if in truth he receives lashes when sleeping for the sake of a mitzvah, perhaps even not for the sake of a mitzvah he receives lashes; what is the Gemara asking, "one who sleeps on the eighth should receive lashes"? Perhaps in truth he does receive lashes if he sleeps not for the sake of a mitzvah. Therefore it appears that Rashi's intent in Rosh Hashanah and Eruvin is one and the same. On the contrary, Rashi too holds like the Rashba and Ritva brought by that Gaon in his book, that where one acts because of a rabbinic enactment, bal tosif does not apply. But Rashi also holds like the Mordechai, that one eats on the eighth but does not sleep. The Gemara asks as follows: how could the Sages enact eating in the sukkah on the eighth, something that can lead to a Torah issur? Since all seven days one eats and sleeps in the sukkah, if the Sages enacted eating in the sukkah on the eighth, he may err and also sleep there, as he was accustomed throughout Sukkot, and think that since he eats there he should also sleep there; if he sleeps, he will violate bal tosif. How could the Sages enact something that can lead to a stumbling block? Therefore it does not ask from the whole year, for perhaps in truth he would receive lashes. Nor can it ask from eating on the eighth, for he does that because of a rabbinic enactment and bal tosif does not apply. It asks specifically from sleeping: how did the Sages enact something that can lead to a stumbling block, lest he come to sleep there too, where the Sages did not enact it and he would violate bal tosif? This is what Rashi means: "if so, one who sleeps on the eighth in the sukkah should receive lashes, and we sit in it"; how did the Sages enact something that may lead to a stumbling block? Rather, it must be that without intent for the mitzvah there is no bal tosif. If so, either way it is permitted to eat in the sukkah. And if he errs and thinks he is obligated even to sleep, nevertheless if he knew it was the eighth he would not sleep there; he does not intend for the mitzvah except because he thinks perhaps today is the seventh. If he knew it was the eighth, he would not sit or sleep in the sukkah, and therefore he does not violate bal tosif. So too is the intent in Rosh Hashanah: one who sleeps in a sukkah on the eighth not for the sake of the mitzvah should receive lashes. One cannot say that this is indeed true, for if so how did the Sages enact eating, since there is a concern for stumbling? Therefore it proves at the end that to violate outside its time one needs intent, and here he does not intend to add; if he knew it was not the seventh, he would not sleep there, and there is no concern of stumbling in bal tosif. What Rashi wrote in Eruvin, "therefore we permit," means that therefore it is permitted to eat and we do not decree lest he also sleep, because even if he does so there is no bal tosif. This resolves it. If so, on the contrary, from the Gemara and Rashi the opposite is proven. Therefore the main ruling is like the Mordechai, not to sleep in the sukkah on the eighth, especially since the matter is rabbinic, as appears to me. See what I wrote in my new work, siman 627, for another proof from chapter HaYashen that one should not sleep in the sukkah on the eighth. However, in my other work on this siman I brought proofs from the Gemara to the contrary, that one is obligated to sleep on the eighth in the sukkah just as one is obligated to eat there; see there and examine very carefully.
