1) [Se'if 1] It is forbidden to say, etc. If one said, 'This meat is for Pesach,' it is prohibited for consumption and even for benefit, and it must be buried. Bach; Ateres Zekeinim. Peri Chadash writes likewise and states that Sefer Damesek Eliezer, Chullin 77a, rules this way. See there. Magen Avraham, subsection 2, distinguishes: regarding a kid or lamb one should be stringent after the fact, but regarding other meat one need not be stringent. See there. Chok Yosef, subsection 3, inclines to this view, as does Chayei Adam, rule 130, subsection 8. Orchos Chaim, page 77a, subsection 11, however, writes in the name of Rabbeinu Shimshon that a purchaser should not say, 'This is for Pesach,' because it appears that one is consecrating it as a Pesach offering; if one did say so, however, it may be eaten. See there. Taz, subsection 1, likewise writes that if one transgressed and made the statement, there is no prohibition against eating it after the fact. See there. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1, writes likewise. This is also the opinion of Mikra'ei Kodesh, page 173b, which permits it after the fact. See there. Responsa Knesses Yechezkel, siman 18, discusses the issue at length and demonstrates that Taz and Shach permit consumption after the fact. See there. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1; Birkei Yosef, subsection 1; Ma'amar Mordechai, subsection 2; Nehar Shalom, subsection 1; Chukas HaPesach, subsection 1; Magen HaElef, subsection 2, agree. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2, likewise writes that after the fact, even if one said about a living kid or lamb, 'This meat is for Pesach,' it is permitted for consumption, even if the statement was made after midday on the eve of Pesach. A concern for appearances does not create an after-the-fact prohibition. These are his words. In the gloss there, however, he writes that regarding a kid or lamb it is proper to take the stringent authorities into account and prohibit consumption, provided that neither a substantial loss nor pressing circumstances are involved. See there. Moreover, the established law is that an unspecified vow is interpreted stringently but its stated explanation is interpreted leniently, and that a person's spoken words and inner intent must agree, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, simanim 208 and 210. Therefore, the person should be asked what was intended. If the person says that the intention was only that the meat was for the Yom Tov of Pesach, it may be permitted.
