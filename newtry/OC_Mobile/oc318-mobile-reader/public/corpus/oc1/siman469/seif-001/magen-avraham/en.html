This is an additional stringency, because according to strict law the prohibition applies only to an animal fit to be offered upon the altar. That he consecrated it while alive. The explanations given above appear contradictory. According to this reason, only a kid or lamb should be prohibited, because only they are fit for the Korban Pesach, as Tosafos and Rosh write. According to Rashi, however, who prohibits every animal, the concern must be that people will say he consecrated its monetary value for the Korban Pesach. It appears to me that Beit Yosef was troubled why Tur writes that according to Rashi every animal is forbidden. Tur should have said that every item is forbidden, because anything may be consecrated for the value of a Korban Pesach. One must therefore say that even if observers think he consecrated the item’s monetary value, they will not prohibit eating it, because they will assume he redeemed it. Monetary sanctity can be redeemed. If one consecrates an animal, however, Shevuos 11 teaches that a male animal consecrated for its monetary value acquires bodily sanctity and cannot be redeemed. Observers may therefore say that he is eating consecrated meat outside the Beis HaMikdash. Regarding wheat as well, an observer might think that it possesses bodily sanctity for a meal offering, although in truth it does not, as stated in the Gemara. There is nevertheless concern for appearances. It was therefore necessary to explain that regarding wheat people understand his statement to mean that it was guarded for Pesach. Bach writes that the Gemara challenges from the law of a roasted kid. What is the question? Perhaps a roasted kid is forbidden even after the fact. It follows that if one said, “This meat is for Pesach,” it is forbidden even after the fact. See Yoreh Deah, siman 5. Taz writes that it is permitted after the fact, and Shach writes similarly. This requires study, because Bach brings a proof from the Gemara that appears unanswered. It therefore seems to me that regarding a kid or lamb one should be stringent after the fact, while regarding other meat one need not be stringent. It further appears to me that the common Yiddish expression meaning, “this meat is for Pesach,” presents no concern. Had he intended to consecrate it as a Korban Pesach, he would have used the Hebrew formulation, “this meat is for Pesach.”
