One may distinguish that a whole roasted kid prepared in the manner of the Korban Pesach is forbidden only while it is eaten in that form. If it was roasted whole and afterward cut into limbs, it may be permitted, for it was not rendered forbidden merely because it had been roasted as a whole. The same may be true when one says, “This meat is for Pesach.” The declaration alone does not necessarily prohibit eating it after the fact. At the beginning of siman 476 and in several other places, however, it is stated that meat roasted whole may not be eaten. Even in a place where roasted meat is eaten on Pesach, a whole animal may not be placed on the table because of those who see it; rather, it must be cut into limbs before being placed there. Rambam, Hilchos Korban Pesach, chapter 10, halachah 11, appears to describe eating the Korban Pesach while it is still whole upon the table, cutting meat from it, and afterward, if desired, cutting the bones at their joints. Olas Shabbos explicitly permits a whole roasted kid that was cut into limbs after roasting. He explains the statement in Pesachim 74a, “What is a whole roasted kid that is forbidden to eat nowadays?” as referring to eating it whole, while cutting it into limbs permits it. This explanation is somewhat strained. Taz in siman 476 may instead mean that once it was roasted whole, it remains forbidden even if it is cut before being placed on the table. Yet his statement that a whole roasted kid is forbidden in eating because it appears consecrated may refer specifically to eating it while still whole. If so, after it is cut into limbs it would be permitted in a place where roasted meat is ordinarily eaten. Taz also writes that there is a prohibition in the very preparation of a whole roasted kid. The wording of Tur and Rambam indeed implies that preparing it in this manner is itself forbidden. If so, why does siman 476 speak only of eating meat roasted whole? It may be that the act of preparation is prohibited, but the food does not become intrinsically forbidden; similarly, merely saying “This meat is for Pesach” does not necessarily prohibit it after the fact. Alternatively, a whole roasted kid may involve two separate prohibitions: preparing it whole with its head and innards, and eating it whole. Beitzah 22b, “They would prepare a whole roasted kid, and the Sages forbade it,” supports a prohibition upon preparation itself. The relationship between this and Rambam’s wording still requires study. Taz also appears to imply that even if one took a living lamb fit for a Korban Pesach and said, “This is for Pesach,” it is permitted after the fact unless he expressly consecrated it. This too requires study. See Yoreh Deah, siman 5.
