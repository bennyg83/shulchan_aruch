An animal. Maharil forbids even poultry whenever the species requires shechitah. This is an excessive stringency, because according to strict law the prohibition applies only to something fit to be offered upon the altar. Bach; Magen Avraham. Eliyah Zuta writes that even fish and similar foods are forbidden, because perhaps he consecrated their monetary value. Since he said “for Pesach,” this concern arises. Wheat is different, because wheat requires guarding for Pesach, and we therefore interpret his statement as meaning guarded for Pesach. See there. After the fact, if he said, “This meat is for Pesach,” Bach writes that it is forbidden even for benefit. In truth, his own words contradict what he wrote in Yoreh Deah, siman 5. Erev Shabbos and Taz permit it even for eating, and Magen Avraham writes similarly in the name of Shach. Magen Avraham concludes that the matter requires study, because Bach brings a proof from the Gemara to which there is no response. He therefore rules that regarding a kid or lamb one should be stringent after the fact, but regarding other meat one need not be stringent. Chok Yaakov writes that the ruling of the other later authorities should be accepted and nothing should be forbidden after the fact, because unless a person consecrated in the ordinary language of those who consecrate, his words have no effect. See there, Pri Chadash, and Responsa Shevus Yaakov, part 2, siman 14. Magen Avraham writes that the common Yiddish expression meaning, “this meat is for Pesach,” causes no concern. Had he intended consecration, he would have used the Hebrew formulation “this meat is for Pesach.” Such is the custom, and people are not particular about it. Rokeach, siman 283, writes that one should not say, “How burdensome Pesach is.” The Jerusalem Talmud connects this with the wicked son’s statement, “What is this service to you,” meaning, “What is this burden?” Chok Yaakov defends the common practice of not taking care regarding this expression.
