He is then obligated in tzitzis. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote, “the adult who wears it.” This is the case discussed by the Shulchan Aruch. Regarding a four-cornered garment of a minor who has reached the age of education, whose father is obligated to place tzitzis upon it as explained below in siman 17, the measure is based upon the head and most of that particular minor's body. If he is six years old, the measure is his own head and most of his body, and his father is obligated D'Rabbanan to place tzitzis upon it. Peri Megadim in Mishbetzos Zahav. I further wrote in Mishnah Berurah that if the local practice is for adults to wear it, etc. Although Bach states simply that an adult wears it regularly, as cited by Magen Avraham, it appears obvious that Bach means where adults generally wear such a garment regularly. Otherwise, his individual practice is disregarded in relation to everyone else. The wording of the Gemara, “and an adult goes out wearing it,” from which Bach derives his ruling that it must be worn only occasionally and not regularly, likewise proves this. This removes the objections of Olas Tamid to Bach and Knesses HaGedolah. This also appears to be Magen Avraham's intent when he concludes without qualification that if a person goes out wearing a tallis that others do not wear, his opinion is disregarded. He does not limit this to occasional use, implying that it applies even when he wears it regularly. Nor does he indicate that he disagrees with Bach. This shows that he does not understand Bach as Eliyah Rabbah does, namely that a person's practice is not disregarded when he wears it regularly. It further appears obvious that even if a tallis can cover the head and most of a minor's body, if no adult ordinarily wears it in the marketplace even occasionally, then even if one particular adult does wear it occasionally, his opinion is disregarded. According to Magen Avraham, who rules like Rama's gloss, one should be stringent and not recite a blessing over it. Magen Avraham included this too in his concise wording, for his statement refers to the immediately preceding case of a tallis that covers the head and most of a minor's body. Peri Megadim labors greatly to interpret Magen Avraham differently, explaining that his final statement merely supports Bach and refers to a tallis that does not contain the measure of a minor's head and most of his body. His explanation is not convincing. Machatzis HaShekel likewise does not imply this, but treats it as an independent ruling concerning the immediately preceding case. Artzos HaChayim also proves this. Accordingly, it appears that even if this person wears the garment regularly, his personal practice is disregarded. Yet even if one were to say that because he wears it regularly in the marketplace his practice is not disregarded, since the garment is considered clothing for a minor when it can cover his head and most of his body, this would still not help where the garment cannot cover the head and most of a minor's body. His regular use cannot create the required measure. This appears correct, with Hashem's help. Know further that what we write below in Mishnah Berurah, that the garments commonly worn by Ashkenazim do not fulfill the mitzvah at all, is agreed upon by everyone. Artzos HaChayim writes that even according to Bach, who maintains that a garment is significant if an adult wears it regularly, this is so only when he wears it regularly outdoors as clothing without embarrassment. It does not apply to those who hide the corners of their garments so they will not be visible outside. In truth, they would be embarrassed to go out wearing such talleisim, which resemble amulets worn upon their garments. There is no basis for leniency. See Bach, Taz, and all the later authorities, who protest this practice emphatically.
To the marketplace. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote that the later authorities agree to be stringent. At first glance, this creates difficulty regarding our present practice: how do we recite a blessing over our talleisim ketanim even if they are sufficiently long, when no adult ordinarily goes out wearing them in the marketplace? In truth, this is no difficulty. In our context, “adult” means a boy of thirteen years, as is implied by Bach and Artzos HaChayim. The Shulchan Aruch's reference to the marketplace is not exact; the same applies to the entrance of his home facing the street, as Eliyah Rabbah writes. A thirteen-year-old boy would certainly not be embarrassed during the summer to go outside wearing it at the entrance to his home. It also appears obvious for another reason that the garment does not lose its status as clothing merely because of this. The Sages said only that if he is embarrassed to wear it because of its shortness, it is not considered a garment. Otherwise, even if a tallis katan could cover the head and most of an adult's body, and even reached his ankles, it would likewise be exempt from tzitzis merely because people do not go out wearing it over their clothing. If so, then in places where an adult is embarrassed to go into the marketplace wearing a tallis gadol, a tallis gadol should likewise be exempt. This is clearly impossible. The Gemara means specifically that where the people of that locale are embarrassed to wear it even occasionally in the marketplace because it is too short, it is not considered a garment. In our case, the embarrassment is only because this type of garment is ordinarily worn beneath one's clothing rather than over it. That does not cause it to lose the status of clothing. See further in Mishnah Berurah regarding the garment's size, where I wrote that three quarters of an amah is sufficient. At first glance, this measure works only if the tallis is woven or sewn in a square form with a neck opening in the middle. If one side of the tallis katan is in front and the other behind, with the two sides partly joined at one shoulder and temporarily fastened at the other by hooks when worn, it requires study whether this measure suffices. Perhaps the two sides combine for purposes of the required measure just as they combine to make it a four-cornered garment. If so, we would also be forced to say that the length and width of the garment combine to create the required measure for tzitzis. In truth, this law is not clear, because some maintain that the garment must contain enough to cover the head and most of the body both in length and in width. See Artzos HaChayim. Nevertheless, it appears to me that if the tallis katan measures one amah by one amah on each side, one may certainly rely upon it and recite a blessing. Even if the two sides do not combine for this purpose, several later authorities maintain that one amah by one amah alone is the measure of a garment obligated in tzitzis. The amah here is six tefachim; it need not be the larger amah commonly used today. Peri Megadim writes: If the garment can cover the head and most of the body, but the particular wearer considers it degrading clothing and does not go out wearing it even occasionally, while ordinary people do wear such a garment, then according to Bach, who requires both conditions, and according to Tur as Taz understands him, perhaps one should indeed not recite a blessing over it, because we follow this wearer's own practice. This requires study. At first glance this is astonishing. The Torah certainly did not establish a separate measure according to each person's subjective preference. Moreover, the baraisa does not say, “and he goes out wearing it occasionally,” but rather, “and an adult goes out wearing it.” Perhaps Peri Megadim's uncertainty arose from Bach's wording in this siman. In truth, Bach did not intend this. It appears more likely that the source of Peri Megadim's doubt was his own statement in Eshel Avraham that according to Bach, if this person wears it regularly, then even if it cannot cover the head and most of a minor's body and no one else wears it, it is nevertheless obligated because we follow his personal practice and do not disregard it. He therefore also remained uncertain in the reverse case. According to what we clarified at the outset, however, there is no doubt at all: the garment is obligated.
