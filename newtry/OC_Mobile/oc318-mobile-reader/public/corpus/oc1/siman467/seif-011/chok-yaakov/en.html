And so the practice is during Pesach. The Levush wrote at length against the Rama and held that dough should be permitted in every case where cold does not impart taste. The Magen Avraham, seif katan 19, wrote that he found written that the Gaon Maharal of Prague ruled to permit, but Maharam of Ostroh wrote to Maharam of Lublin to forbid, and so most of the world and Krakow practice; it depends on custom. In truth, although the Levush explained the Tur not to forbid cold, the Mordechai forbids soaked meat even if a wheat kernel was found after soaking. Perhaps he forbade because the meat has cracks, as written at the end of siman 465, and so the Beit Yosef writes here in the name of the Geonim and in siman 442 in the name of Orchos Chaim. Therefore meat with cracks is forbidden by law even cold, and the same applies where there is doubt whether it soaked for twenty-four hours. Still, this does not fully explain the matter, for if meat is forbidden because it has cracks, that means the wheat imparts even a minute taste in cold and it enters the cracks of the meat. If so, water or dough should all the more be forbidden, for they are more receptive than cracks in meat. This distinction applies only to another solid item such as meat without cracks, which is not soft and liquid-like. The Taz concludes that although for other cold cases one may permit like the Levush, with dough, because working the dough crushes the wheat and causes leavening, it forbids the entire dough during Pesach if it softened. The same stringency applies to chametz bread even cold, because of concern for crumbs. My brother-in-law in Eliyahu Zuta ruled this way, and this appears to be the conclusion of the Magen Avraham, the Bach, and Maharshal. Nevertheless, based on what I wrote above in seif katan 18, that if the kernel is not split there is room to permit benefit in a case of major loss, all the more so here in cold, where many authorities permit entirely. Perhaps even the Rama's statement that 'so is the practice' refers specifically to a split kernel, as the Mechaber mentioned, for otherwise he should have added here too that even an unsplit one is forbidden, as in seif 9. It must be that since it is cold, he did not intend to be stringent at all when it is not split, since there is another side for leniency. However, the Bach and other later authorities wrote that one forbids only when the kernel softened, implying that even without actual splitting there is a prohibition. Perhaps the Rama's gloss also means this and he relied on what he wrote in seif 9. Still, this is only to forbid eating, not benefit, as written above in seif katan 26. One who is lenient even for eating has not lost, for even with a split kernel there may be room for leniency in cold in a case of major loss, since the Rama's gloss may refer to the Mechaber's words, namely that there is leniency in major loss or pressing need. This appears primary.
Here it was found and here it was. The Magen Avraham wrote that if the wheat kernel is full of water and moist from side to side, it appears that it had been in the water, and with dough one must forbid the whole dough. Nevertheless, if one can attribute that it absorbed water elsewhere and fell into the matzah, it is permitted. Everything depends on the circumstances, for we attribute to what is common, whether to be lenient or stringent, and this is not called a doubt at all. See my Kuntres HaSefeikos. The Magen Avraham further wrote that the rule 'here it was found' because we do not establish a prohibition applies only from place to place or piece to piece. But from time to time within one piece, we do establish a prohibition, as the Pri Chadash also wrote in Yoreh De'ah, siman 41, seif katan 18. Thus, if he has dough in his hand, cut from it, and a piece remained in his hand and a wheat kernel was found on it, all are forbidden, for we say perhaps it was there earlier. Nor may we say it is a double doubt, perhaps it did not become chametz, because that is a doubt arising from lack of knowledge, as the Taz agrees in seif katan 14. See responsa Masas Binyamin, siman 69, and Tzemach Tzedek, siman 43, for distinctions in this rule. Maharam of Lublin, responsum 78, wrote of a case where one took cabbage from a barrel in a bowl, carried it elsewhere, and there found a wheat kernel upon it; he permitted the cabbage in the barrel, saying that here it was found and here it was. But if afterward a wheat kernel was found in the barrel itself, the law would differ according to the circumstances.
