(22) We do not establish a presumption of prohibition in it. To say it is forged with fine flour mixed into it: for we do not establish a presumption of prohibition; and further, even if they mixed, behold honey is fruit juice and does not make chametz.
(23) And they eat it on Pesach. Some wrote that this is specifically to eat the honey plain as it is, but to knead dough with it is forbidden, for it is common when removing the wax from the honey that water mixes there, and it is water with fruit juice and they make chametz.
(24) However there are those who are stringent. For they are accustomed to mix fine flour into it for soaking.
(25) Not to eat honey except, etc. Meaning that regarding this honey that they bring in a barrel from the hive with wax still mixed in it they practice to eat it even plain, and likewise to make from it a mead drink, because it is not the way to forge; and even to make from it a mead drink on Pesach is permitted for Yom Tov joy if he has no other drink; but what they bring in small vessels cleaned of wax: the non-Jew's way is to mix flour into them, and therefore it is forbidden to eat it and even to make a drink from it on Pesach. And some later authorities are stringent in this even before Pesach because it is common that they mix much flour into them until even in the water mixture there will not be sixty against the flour to nullify them; and likewise they are stringent not to eat honey plain on Pesach even from those that come from large barrels, lest they come to confuse and be lenient with all honey (except those they bring with the small combs whole as they rest in the hive that have not yet been crushed). And for practice regarding making a mead drink before Pesach it appears from the later authorities that now they practice to be lenient even from those that come in small vessels, for we attribute that the flour will be nullified in sixty (unless it is known that in those places the way is to mix much flour); and nevertheless one who is stringent in this not to make a mead drink except from honey that comes in a barrel with the wax, may blessing come upon him. And regarding eating honey plain on Pesach that comes from barrels it depends on the custom of the places; and in our land the custom is to be lenient. And nevertheless one must inquire and know the custom of the places, for there are places where they put bread in the hive at the time of extracting or other chametz things; and in a place where there is concern for this the honey is forbidden to eat plain on Pesach, but it is permitted to make a mead drink from it before Pesach (Chayei Adam).
(26) And dried figs. For there is concern that they scatter flour on them when drying them; and grapes they are accustomed to dry in an oven near bread or after they removed the bread from the oven; and there are places where they do not scatter flour on them and dry them in the sun; and that is what he wrote: it depends on the custom of the places.
(27) Not to eat any fruits, etc. That is for the reason above. And nevertheless they practice permission to make a drink from them on Pesach: that is, they soak them in water until their taste is absorbed into the water and drink the water on Pesach; for since there is only a mere concern we are not so stringent as to forbid even their soaking water; but we are not so lenient as to cook them on Pesach in order to drink their cooking water on Pesach, because through cooking their taste is absorbed in the water more than through soaking; and in some places they practice to be stringent regarding soaking water like cooking water (unless they soaked and cooked them before Pesach); and so in Shaarei Teshuvah in the name of the Noda BiYehuda he agrees that l'chatchila it is proper to be stringent; and therefore it is good to strain the sauce from the fruits on erev Pesach before nightfall: then even if a little flour stuck to the fruits and mixed into the sauce it is nullified in sixty.
(28) In a way that there is no concern for chametz. For example when it is known to him that it was dried in the sun or in an oven after he prepared it as kosher.
(29) And even to keep it is forbidden. For there is concern that they mix flour into it; and the later authorities agreed that what we call "hat sugar" made like a hat was clarified through investigation of the craftsmen that they do not mix flour into it; and nevertheless they practiced to sell it l'chatchila (and after the fact it is permitted after Pesach, and also on the last Yom Tov they eat it); and that which has a hechsher letter from the rabbi from the place of its making they practice to eat l'chatchila; but sugar made very fine has more concern of chametz from a flour mixture and it is forbidden to keep it; and nevertheless after the fact if he transgressed and kept them it appears from the later authorities that one should not forbid this either; and see Shaarei Teshuvah for manners of hechsher herein for eating it l'chatchila. And know that kinds of sugar coatings with which they coat fruits and spices, and all the more so those made like small cakes of sugar coatings: the later authorities agreed that certainly there is concern that there is a chametz mixture in them, and it is forbidden to keep by law, and all the more so to eat them even on the last Yom Tov of the Diaspora.
(30) And on the last Yom Tov. Of the Diaspora: since it is rabbinic we are not stringent regarding a matter that has only a mere concern.
(31) And candied sugar that has no, etc. This is not the cooked sugar cut into pieces that we call candied sugar; that has more concern of chametzing and it is forbidden to keep them and one may not eat them even on the last Yom Tov; rather it is sugar coming from the land of Candia, and it is like our "hat sugar," and it was clear to them that there is no concern of chametzing in it; and nevertheless on other days one may not eat them except through a hechsher so that they not come to be lenient also regarding sugar coatings. And the later authorities wrote that even l'chatchila one must certainly be careful not to keep our candied sugar and sugar coatings; nevertheless if he transgressed and kept them one should not forbid them after Pesach, for there are several doubts in this; see Chok Yaakov.
(32) And the custom in these lands is not to eat, etc. But to keep them is permitted.
(33) Saffron. For they forge them with fine flour to maintain their appearance, and likewise they place sourdough starter on it to restore its appearance; and the later authorities wrote that even saffron grown in his garden and guarded from chametz they practice prohibition regarding it because of appearance to the eye, for saffron is not common in these lands and they will think it is saffron coming from afar; which is not so regarding all other fruits dried by a Jew and guarded from chametz, for we are not concerned regarding them for appearance to the eye since they are common and all know that sometimes they dry them by a Jew. They further wrote that tobacco: the way in some places is to soak it in beer, and one must lock it in a room or make a partition before it; and likewise saffron in places where they practice prohibition regarding it. And Chayei Adam wrote that he inquired of the craftsmen who make sniff tobacco (that is, what they sniff in the nose): they moisten the tobacco packet with yayin nesech, and it is known that yayin nesech is forbidden for benefit by law; and if so it is fitting to be careful not to sniff that tobacco even all year. And behold all this Chayei Adam wrote in his time; but now one must inquire of the craftsmen whether they still do so.
(34) Or Negelikh [cloves]. Because they soak them in barley water before drying them (Darkei Moshe).
(35) However they do not forbid their mixtures. Since he stated without specification it implies even in less than sixty, and it refers to all the above in this gloss; and the reason is that we are not so stringent because of a mere concern; however regarding honey and sugar coatings some are stringent to forbid if there was not sixty in the cooked dish against it; and nevertheless in a place of major financial loss or deprivation of Yom Tov joy one may be lenient.
