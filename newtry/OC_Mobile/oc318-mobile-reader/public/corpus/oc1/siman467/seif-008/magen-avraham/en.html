Only that, etc., which they make, etc. Two conditions are required. A beverage may be made only from the honey kept in the barrel, because even if it contains a small amount of flour, it was nullified in sixty when cooked before Pesach. Honey brought in small vessels, however, may not even be used to make a beverage, and I have many times established that a large quantity of flour is mixed into it. Eating the honey itself is forbidden in every case, lest one confuse the cases. Mahari Weil and Maharshal write likewise. The beverage may not be prepared on Pesach. Bach writes this, and it is also implied by Darchei Moshe. Levush writes: Some say that only the beverage called mead is permitted, because it contains much water and the flour was presumably nullified before Pesach. Honey itself that was cooked before Pesach, however, even if care was taken to protect it from chametz and it was strained from the bees within it, may not be eaten or added to cooked food. It appears to me that one who is lenient has not lost, while one who is stringent may be stringent and will receive blessing. Nevertheless, honey brought directly from the hive is customarily permitted according to all opinions, since nothing is normally mixed into it when it is removed from the hive. Levush carefully writes ‘brought from the hive,’ and not ‘brought in a barrel from the hive,’ because the latter may not be eaten by itself and is permitted only when it arrives together with intact small pieces of honeycomb as it lay in the hive and has not yet been crushed. Some customarily cook honey-water until it becomes thick like honey and eat it. According to Bach this is forbidden, as explained above. Everything follows local custom.
And dried grapes. Mahari Weil writes in responsum 105 that dried grapes should not be eaten. If a person wishes to fulfill the phrase in Shir HaShirim, ‘the blossom has opened,’ he may do so with our grapes. In Maharil it is written that care must be taken with thin grapes because they are mixed with dried figs. Here in the holy community of Kalisz there is an ancient custom to permit purchasing small dried pears from non-Jews and making a beverage from them by pouring cold water over them and soaking them until they become a drink. They customarily forbid cooking the drink together with the pears, and they do not eat the pears themselves. They also customarily avoid using the vessels with which the drink is drawn for any other purpose. It appears to me that the reason is concern that a small amount of chametz may have adhered to the pears. They therefore do not eat them, but drink the liquid because the small amount is nullified in sixty. Although they continue soaking during Pesach, they did not act stringently in a case of doubt. They forbid cooking them because cooking extracts more flavor. Although some non-Jews dry the pears in an oven after bread was baked in it, this is a secondary imparting of permitted flavor. Rama in siman 447 is stringent regarding this, but here there is a doubt, since many were dried in the sun or after the oven was fired. They therefore customarily acted leniently. This appears to explain their practice, but in other places the custom is stringent.

Only small cakes made from a sugar mixture are forbidden, while ordinary sugar may be retained. I have likewise seen halachic authorities permit sugar that remained over Pesach. I found the same written elsewhere. Hagahos Minhagim states: Maharil wrote in responsum 25 that the custom is to be careful with sugar unless there is a great medicinal need, because peddlers mix various substances into it. Sugar made in the form of small cakes from a sugar mixture may not be retained, in the name of Maharil and Maharam Shapira. Terumas HaDeshen writes that one should be careful with all confections made from a sugar mixture, such as legumes and seeds coated with it. This implies that ordinary sugar may be retained, and Levush likewise appears to rule so. I also heard from the craftsman who prepares it that sugar made in the shape of a hat contains no flour. Olas Shabbos writes likewise. Nevertheless, the initial custom is to sell it to a non-Jew before Pesach; after the fact it is permitted. I also found written in a compilation of Mahari Bruna that the matter is exactly the reverse: white sugar raises no chametz concern, while candy sugar raises a concern. I heard that this is the practice in the holy community of Krakow. Maharil writes that Maharam of Znaim permitted his students on the last day of Yom Tov to eat sugar he had brought from Candia, because he knew with certainty that it contained no chametz concern.
Saffron. Rashba writes in responsum 133 that flour is mixed with water and sprinkled upon it. Levush writes that even saffron grown in one's own garden is forbidden by decree, in the name of Maharil. The reason is that saffron does not ordinarily grow in these countries, and people may assume that it is saffron brought from distant places. By contrast, fruit dried by a Jew is permitted and we do not fear confusion, because it is common, and the same applies to honey from the hive, as explained above. Thus I found written. Tobacco is ordinarily soaked in beer and must therefore be locked in a room or screened off by a partition. The same rule applies to saffron.
They do not prohibit their mixture. This implies that even if a large amount became mixed into a cooked dish, it is permitted, since the concern is remote. Regarding honey, however, Darchei Moshe writes that if a small amount became mixed into a cooked dish on Pesach, the dish should not be forbidden, because Rosh is sufficiently authoritative to rely upon in such a case, as written in siman 447. Regarding a D'Rabbanan prohibition that applies in the smallest amount, we do not presume a prohibition after the fact. This implies that if it became mixed in less than sixty, it is forbidden, and this is the primary ruling. With saffron and the other items, however, one should not be stringent even in less than sixty, because the chametz flavor has already dissipated, as written in siman 447 regarding dried meat and in Yoreh Deah siman 69 regarding blood.
