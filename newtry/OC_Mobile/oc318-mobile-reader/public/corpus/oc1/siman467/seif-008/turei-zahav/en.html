There are those who are stringent. This is the Mordechai, that we are concerned lest flour or fine flour was mixed in. And Beis Yosef wrote that there is no prohibition in this, for it is fruit juice and does not make chametz except with fine flour; and it is possible we are concerned that it is full chametz since they first soak the wheat. And the Rosh wrote in a responsum, klal 24, that he did not see a custom of prohibition regarding honey, for mixing flour is uncommon and also it was nullified before Pesach; and if we come to be concerned because of mixtures we should forbid it all year, for some say they put nevelah meat into it and it turns into honey and is permitted, for we follow the present state (end quote). And nevertheless the Rama concludes that one may not eat it on Pesach except what they bring from the hive, even though in the Mordechai he forbids this too because of confusion with other honey; and that is an excessive stringency. And I saw in many places that they eat it, and also in the community of Krakow there is no custom to forbid, unlike the Rama's words here who wrote not to eat honey except that which they bring from the hive from which they make a drink, etc.: meaning even to eat it plain is permitted, for they make a drink from it l'chatchila; but one should not say that specifically the drink made before Pesach is permitted, for he did not mention "before Pesach"; and further, he began with eating, implying that this is permitted even for eating, only that he mentioned the drink as proof that they practice permission. And based on this it appears to me there is full permission to cook on chol ha-mo'ed a drink called mead from honey that is recognizable as coming from the hive to people's eyes, whatever is fit for drinking on Yom Tov; even though my master and father-in-law of blessed memory wrote to forbid making the drink on the festival because of concern for mixtures, and wrote that so they practiced: nevertheless it appears to us that one who is lenient for the honor of Yom Tov in these lands where we have no other drink on Pesach has not lost; and no custom of prohibition applies here, for each prepares for himself before Pesach; but if one comes to cook on the festival and has no drink other than that, one should not protest him, and the praise of Yom Tov's honor is in his hand, as appears to me. And based on the Rosh's responsum that we mentioned in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah, it appears to teach merit regarding what is written in Shulchan Aruch Yoreh De'ah at the end of siman 114, that the Rashba was careful about saffron because they mix dry meat into it; and according to Rabbeinu Yonah's words there is permission, since the saffron dissolves in the food and the prohibition is nullified, and it is exactly like honey here all year.
And dried figs. For there is concern that they scatter flour on them; and even those that grow in his garden are forbidden because of an observer, because of other figs; and the prohibition of grapes is that they are placed near figs.
And candied sugar. This is not what we call candied sugar; that has more concern and is full chametz, for they cook white sugar in sour vats and it reddens; and even to keep it is forbidden because there is a mixture of chametz. Rather it is sugar of Candia that Maharil wrote the students ate on the last Yom Tov, and Moreinu HaRav Nosson gave them sugar he brought from Candia that was clear to them had no chametz. And it is difficult why they did not decree because of other sugar as with figs; and it appears that that sugar is a distinct species and its form is recognizable, and we do not decree on it; and it appears this is what we call knur or "hot sugar," which certainly has no chametz and no cooking was done to it: they bring it as its natural product; that is what they eat on the last Yom Tov, as appears to me.
Saffron that they call zafarin. And even that which they call "land zafarin," which is certainly without mixture, is forbidden because of an observer who will not know to distinguish; so I found in an old collection.
