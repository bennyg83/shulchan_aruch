1) [Se'if 1] Grain that was submerged in a river, etc. There is concern that some kernels became chametz, although this cannot be identified. Tur; Levush. This implies that if the chametz is identifiable, it may be sold, even in a large quantity to one non-Jew according to Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 4. We are not concerned that the non-Jew might mill and resell it, since flour is purchased in the marketplace only in pressing circumstances, as stated in siman 453. This is contrary to Olas Shabbos. Magen Avraham, at the beginning of this siman. Ran in the chapter Kol Sha'ah, however, writes that even when kernels split and the chametz is identifiable, they may not be sold to a non-Jew, lest the non-Jew mill and then sell them to a Jew. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1, writes likewise and questions Tur's implication that identifiable chametz may be sold. Magen Avraham therefore writes that his ruling is contrary to Olas Shabbos. Chok Yaakov, subsection 1, cites Ran and questions Magen Avraham: we should still be concerned that a Jew might purchase the flour before Pesach, retain it at home, and violate the prohibition against chametz being seen. He explains that Tur means that even though the chametz cannot be identified and one knows only that there is a concern of chametz, we nevertheless fear a mishap when it is sold. This concern certainly applies initially when the chametz is known and identifiable. See there. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1, resolves Chok Yaakov's objection by explaining that Magen Avraham holds that the Sages did not issue a decree on this account, since the Jew nullifies it by declaring all unseen chametz ownerless. See there. Mekor Chaim in the explanations likewise agrees with Magen Avraham. See there. Erech HaShulchan, subsection 1, permits selling it to a non-Jew because of a compound doubt: perhaps the non-Jew will sell it to another non-Jew, and even if it is sold to a Jew, perhaps it will be sold as visibly affected wheat. See there. Beis David, siman 232, however, agrees with Ran. See there. The matter is therefore disputed. Initially, if possible, one should sell a small amount to each buyer or sell it under an arrangement requiring its return after Pesach, as stated above in siman 448, se'if 3. If this is impossible and a substantial loss is involved, one may rely upon the permitting authorities. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 4, likewise writes that one may rely upon them where a substantial loss is involved.
2) There. It is likewise forbidden to retain it. If one transgressed, retained it, planted it in the ground, and it grew, the produce is permitted, as stated above at the end of siman 443. Chok Yaakov, subsection 2. See our words there in subsection 33.
3) There. And inform the buyer, etc. A public announcement does not help here as it does regarding meat in the chapter Gid HaNasheh. Shiltei HaGibborim, chapter 5 of Avodah Zarah; Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur; Magen Avraham, subsection 1; Chemed Moshe, subsection 1.
4) There. And inform the buyer, etc. This is Rambam's language in chapter 5. Beis Yosef writes that according to Rambam, selling one kav at a time applies only when selling to a non-Jew. When selling to a Jew without informing the buyer, even one kav at a time is prohibited, because the Jew might retain it to eat during Pesach. If the buyer is informed, even a large quantity may be sold. Maharshal, however, writes that even after informing the Jewish buyer, selling a large quantity is prohibited. Bach, Taz, subsection 2, Olas Shabbos, subsection 1, and Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 3, agree. Peri Chadash, however, agrees with Beis Yosef: without notification, even one kav at a time is prohibited lest the buyer retain it for Pesach; with notification, even a large quantity is permitted. Shulchan Gavoah, subsection 1; Chok Yaakov, subsection 3; Chok Yosef, subsection 2; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 3; Chayei Adam, rule 126, subsection 2; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 2, agree.
5) There. And inform the buyer, etc. If the kernels are visibly chametz, one need not inform a Jewish buyer, because their status is apparent. Chayei Adam, there. This implies even when selling a large quantity. Mishbetzos Zahav, subsection 1, however, writes that when selling a small amount to a Jew, it is obvious that no notification is required when the chametz is identifiable. See there. This implies that the law for a large quantity is not obvious. Therefore, initially one should be stringent and notify the buyer when selling a large quantity.
6) There. If one sells it to a non-Jew before Pesach, one sells a small amount, etc. Maharshal, however, writes that selling to a non-Jew is prohibited even one kav at a time. Bach and Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2, agree. Taz, subsection 2, however, agrees with the Shulchan Aruch, as do Peri Chadash; Chemed Moshe, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2; Chayei Adam, there.
7) There. One sells a small amount, etc. Only with chametz does selling small amounts help, because even if the non-Jew sells it to a Jew, the Jew will consume it before Pesach. With other prohibitions, selling small amounts does not help, because we remain concerned that the non-Jew might sell them to a Jew or give them as a gift to a Jewish friend. Magen Avraham, subsection 2; Chok Yaakov, subsection 4; Mekor Chaim, subsection 4; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 4. See, however, Zivchei Tzedek on Yoreh De'ah, siman 84, subsection 33, who cites a dispute whether other prohibited foods may be sold to a non-Jew in small amounts and concludes that one should be lenient only where a substantial loss is involved. See there.
8) There. So that it will be consumed before Pesach, etc. Even if the non-Jew sells that small amount to a Jew, the Jew will consume it before Pesach. This implies that during the fifth hour on the eve of Pesach, when it cannot be consumed before Pesach, it is forbidden to sell even small amounts to a non-Jew lest they be sold to a Jew. This also appears from the Talmud, Rambam, and the other authorities. Nevertheless, if a large amount of a Jew's grain became chametz on the eve of Pesach, it cannot be sold to non-Jews in quantities that will be consumed before Pesach, and a substantial loss is involved, one may sell it to a known non-Jew who will not touch it but will guard it until after Pesach. One may tell the buyer, 'Instead of buying for one maneh,' and so forth, as explained above in siman 448, se'ifim 3 and 4. There is then no concern. Chok Yaakov, there; Chok Yosef, subsection 3; Mekor Chaim, there; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 4; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 3.
9) There. So that it will be consumed before Pesach, etc. See Beis David, siman 237, who considers that if a long time remains before Pesach, even a large quantity may be sold to a non-Jew. See there. Zechor LeAvraham.
10) There. Lest the non-Jew resell it, etc. Although there is a compound doubt, one may not permit it on that basis because the compound doubt is deliberately created by human action. Responsa Maharashdam, Yoreh De'ah, siman 45; Responsa Maharit, Yoreh De'ah, volume 2, siman 2; Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur; Olas Shabbos, subsection 5; Magen Avraham, subsection 2; Zechor LeAvraham. Zivchei Tzedek on Yoreh De'ah, siman 110, laws of compound doubts, subsection 149, writes likewise in the name of several authorities and agrees as a matter of practical law. See there.
11) There. And sell it to a Jew. An incident occurred in which a Jew purchased wheat from a non-Jew. Another Jew later asked the non-Jew to sell wheat to him as well. The non-Jew said, 'I have no wheat. The Jew to whom I sold it is my friend, and I sold him the wheat that I had washed and intended to mill for my household, because of my affection for him.' It appears to me that this resembles the law in Yoreh De'ah, siman 16, se'if 11: after the non-Jew sold the wheat and it left the non-Jew's possession, the non-Jew is not believed. If the Jew personally considers the non-Jew trustworthy, however, it is prohibited. Magen Avraham, subsection 4; Chok Yaakov, subsection 5; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 3; Yad Aharon in Hagahos HaTur; Chok Yosef, subsection 4; Mekor Chaim, subsection 5. Chok Yaakov writes there that this implies that before the wheat is sold, while still in the non-Jew's possession, the non-Jew is believed. He rejects this, because the non-Jew may be speaking to improve the sale. See there. Chok Yosef there, however, challenges Chok Yaakov and writes in the name of several authorities that while the product remains in the non-Jew's possession, the non-Jew is believed to prohibit it even where the statement might have been made to improve the sale. See there. Zivchei Tzedek on Yoreh De'ah, siman 16, subsection 34, writes likewise in the name of several authorities. See there.
