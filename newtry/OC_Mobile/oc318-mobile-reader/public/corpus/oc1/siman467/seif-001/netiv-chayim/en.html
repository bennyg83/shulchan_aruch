(On Rama, seif 2) “Those kernels that became damp.” Note: I will copy for you the words of the Agur, from which Rama’s ruling is taken, and through them we will arrive at the explanation. Beis Yosef cites the Agur as follows: His father was asked about wheat that had been spread in an attic beneath the roof. Water passed through the roof and dripped upon the grain in several places, to the extent that in some places there was enough water to make an object wet enough to wet something else. He answered that the Ra’em and Rivah were stringent and said regarding wheat that, since wheat can split, if there is a large quantity of water the wheat is forbidden even if it did not actually split, for regarding wheat visible splitting is not required. Nevertheless, one may rely upon the leniency written by Raavyah and cited in the long Mordechai, namely, to eat some of it before Pesach or to sell it little by little to a non-Jew. The remainder is then permitted by a double doubt: perhaps water did not fall upon those kernels, and even if water did fall upon them, perhaps they did not split, meaning perhaps they did not become chametz. The wheat at the bottom, adjacent to the attic floor, almost certainly had water reach it to a depth of one finger. That portion must be discarded, while the remainder may be retained. It may not, however, be eaten on Pesach. Although for purposes of bal yera’eh the doubtful kernels are nullified in the majority, with respect to eating, where chametz forbids even in the smallest amount, we are not lenient. End of quotation. At first glance, these words are difficult to understand. It appears to me that the case was as follows. Water leaking through the roof dripped upon the grain in several places, and there was also a steady leak close to the spread grain, until water reached the attic floor beneath the pile of grain. The answer can now be understood. Rama writes that the kernels which became damp are forbidden, while the grain in the other places is permitted. This distinction is not explicit in the Agur. Rama was troubled that, if the Agur’s ruling referred only to kernels that were not known to have become damp, they would already be permitted through a double doubt even if some kernels had certainly become chametz. First, perhaps the forbidden kernels were among those already eaten. Even if they were not, perhaps one will now eat only permitted kernels. This resembles a ring of idolatry that became mixed with other rings and one ring fell into the sea, after which all the remaining rings may be used together. Every matzah contains far more than two pieces together, as Taz explains at length in seif katan 4. Rama therefore understood that the Agur’s instruction to eat some before Pesach or sell it little by little to a non-Jew refers to the particular places known to have become damp. The rest of the pile does not require that any portion be eaten or sold, and is permitted everywhere because of the doubt. This resolves Taz’s difficulty. Rama’s refusal to permit the grain for eating applies where there is no doubt available to permit it, and where the owner has other wheat that is certainly fit for the matzos of the mitzvah, as Kol Bo writes. If he has no other wheat, however, this wheat is permitted even for eating, because a double doubt is effective even regarding a Torah prohibition. Consider this carefully.
