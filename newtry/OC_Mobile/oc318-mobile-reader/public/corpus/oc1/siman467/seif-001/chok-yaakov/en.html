The Ran writes in Perek Kol Sha'ah that even if the kernels split and the leavening is recognizable, it is nevertheless forbidden to sell them to a non-Jew, lest he grind them and afterward sell the flour to a Jew. The Tur's wording, however, says that the chametz is not recognizable, which might imply that where it is recognizable there is no concern. This is difficult, for the Ran's concern still applies. The Olas Shabbos already noted this difficulty. The Magen Avraham answered that we are not concerned that he will grind them and sell them to a Jew, since flour is not bought from the market except in pressing circumstances, as written in siman 453, unlike the Olas Shabbos. In truth, this does not resolve the matter, for that restriction applies specifically to matzah of the mitzvah. Even according to our practice to guard all matzos, if a Jew buys that flour before Pesach and keeps it in his home, he violates bal yera'eh, since the chametz was recognizable in it. This is clearly the Ran's view. Therefore it appears that the Tur's main intent in saying that the chametz is not recognizable is by way of novelty, that even though the chametz is not recognizable and known, and there is only a general concern of leavening, nevertheless we are concerned for a mishap in the sale. All the more so where the chametz is known and recognizable, we are certainly concerned for a mishap.

And he informs him. This is the language of the Rambam. The Tur challenged him, for according to our text of the Gemara, it should be sold to a Jew kav by kav so that it will be consumed before Pesach, and since only a small amount is permitted, if informing suffices then even a large amount should be permitted. See the Beit Yosef and other later authorities who resolve the Rambam and the Tur and derive several laws from this. In truth, this very question was already asked of the Rashba in responsum 354, and he answered that it discusses a case where he informed him. It is not difficult that even a large amount should then be permitted, because a Jew will not buy much, since it is forbidden to keep it on Pesach. Why should he exchange one difficulty for another, becoming forced to sell it to a non-Jew little by little near Pesach, when not always and in every place is there a non-Jew who knows him and will return it after Pesach, as stated above in siman 448. This is like what is said in Nedarim, that where Jews may not benefit from him, he buys cheaply and sells dearly, yet we do not listen to him. The same applies here. Thus the Rambam's words are correct without question, and there is no need to emend our text as the Maggid Mishneh wrote. It is surprising that the Beit Yosef and the later authorities missed the Rashba's words. The halacha is that if he informed him, even much may be sold to a Jew, unlike the implication of some later authorities. Even if selling little by little to a Jew, he must inform him, and so the later authorities agree.
So that it will be consumed before Pesach. Meaning, even if that small amount will also be sold by the non-Jew to a Jew, nevertheless that Jew will eat it before Pesach. According to this, on Erev Pesach in the fifth hour, when it is impossible for it to be consumed before Pesach, it is forbidden to sell to a non-Jew even little by little, lest he sell it to a Jew. This is also implied by the Gemara, the Rambam, and the other authorities, even according to the Maggid Mishneh's understanding of the Rambam's text, and so the Magen Avraham agrees, unlike the Kesef Mishneh. Nevertheless, it appears to me that if a large quantity of grain became chametz in a Jew's possession on Erev Pesach, and he cannot sell it to a non-Jew in a way that it will be consumed before Pesach, and there is a major loss, he may sell it to a non-Jew whom he knows and trusts not to touch it at all, but only to guard it for him until after Pesach. He may say to him, 'Until you buy it,' etc., as explained above in siman 448, seifim 3 and 4, and then there is no concern. The Magen Avraham's statement that selling little by little is forbidden in other prohibitions and was permitted here only because chametz involves many doubts is correct, and accords with what I wrote in Minchas Yaakov, klal 46, seif katan 34, unlike the Shach and Taz there. The Pri Chadash in siman 84, seif katan 17, also reached my conclusion.

If the non-Jew told the Jew, even incidentally, after selling him wheat, that these wheat kernels had been soaked, this resembles what is written in Yoreh De'ah, siman 216, that a non-Jew is not believed regarding what he says after he has sold them and they have left his hand. However, if he is trusted, etc., it is forbidden. These are the words of the Magen Avraham, which imply that before he sells them, since they are still in his hand, he is believed. This is not so, for since one can say that he intends to improve the value of his merchandise, he is not believed, as appears in Yevamos 22 and as ruled in Yoreh De'ah, siman 16, by the Shach and Taz, and as I concluded in Minchas Yaakov, klal 59, seif katan 7. Perhaps this is not considered improving the sale, since it is actually more praiseworthy for wheat not to have been soaked and to be clean on its own. Therefore he may be believed while it is still in his hand. This requires study.
