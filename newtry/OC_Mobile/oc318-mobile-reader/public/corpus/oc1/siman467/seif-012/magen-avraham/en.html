The chicken is forbidden. We say that perhaps the wheat kernel was in the water when the chicken was scalded. The principle, ‘Here it was found, and here it had been,’ applies only from one place to another or from one piece to another, as written in seif 11. But where the question is within one location, such as scalding, and the doubt concerns time, perhaps the kernel had already been in the water, we do not say that it fell only now. This is found in the first chapter of Chullin and the first chapter of Niddah, where we do not say that the defect arose only now in the absence of a prior presumption. Beit Yosef cites Mordechai that if a wheat kernel is found in a bowl beside a chicken, it is permitted because of a double doubt, as Rama writes in his gloss, rather than because it was found there. One may answer that since wheat kernels are commonly found in chickens, which eat wheat, we say that perhaps it came from the chicken, as written in Yoreh Deah siman 57, where we attribute matters to the common cause. It is therefore permitted only by way of a double doubt. Masas Binyamin writes this in siman 69. This is difficult, for if the matter is common, it should not count even as one doubt, since we attribute to the common cause even leniently, as written in Yoreh Deah siman 57 and in Issur VeHeter. Rather, one must say that only something extremely common is not considered a doubt, but something somewhat common may form part of a double doubt, as written in siman 84. Masas Binyamin should therefore not have cited siman 57. See Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 6, that an uncommon possibility is not counted toward a double doubt. It appears to me that although wheat is commonly found in chickens, nothing ordinarily remains in them after they are washed, especially when prepared for Pesach, so this is not called a common doubt. Mordechai did not permit it because ‘Here it was found, and here it had been,’ and say that it fell only in the bowl, because Mordechai rules that a second vessel prohibits on Pesach, as written in siman 447:3. Regarding a doubt over time, we establish the prohibition retroactively. Thus, even if it was in the bowl when the chicken was poured into it, the chicken would be forbidden. He therefore permitted it through the double doubt that the food is now cold and does not prohibit, since the case contains no liquid.
And it does not contain enough to impart flavor. It appears to me that the meaning is that the chametz is small in quantity and does not impart flavor to the water because the water contains sixty against it. It is therefore permitted, since the prohibition of the smallest amount is D'Rabbanan and we are not stringent in cold food. Nevertheless, some forbid it. Levush writes at length to permit cold food and states that in every place through which he traveled, he saw great and elderly sages who did not prohibit cold food. I found written that Maharal of Prague ruled leniently. Maharam Schick of Ostroh, however, wrote to Maharam of Lublin to forbid it, and this is the practice of most people and in Krakow. It appears to me that the matter depends on local custom. Although Levush reconciles Tur so as not to prohibit cold food, Mordechai nevertheless forbids meat that was soaked even when the wheat was found after the soaking. Perhaps he forbade it because the meat had cracks, as written in siman 447, and Beit Yosef here cites the Geonim similarly, as does siman 442 in the name of Orchos Chaim. It therefore appears to me that meat with cracks is forbidden by law even while cold. The same applies if there is a possibility that it soaked for twenty-four hours, as written in Yoreh Deah siman 105. Other cold cases depend upon custom or upon the exigency of the circumstances. See what I wrote in seif 11, that one should be stringent regarding bread, and the same applies to dough, because the wheat causes the dough to ferment and imparts flavor throughout.
And there is one who permits, etc. His words appear to assume that Beit Yosef forbids a cold case even where the wheat was found after the meat was removed. This is not compelled.
For we say that perhaps afterward. The wording is imprecise, for how can we permit merely because of a doubt? Rather, the reason is that we maintain the prior presumption and say that it fell only now. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes so. Beit Yosef states: Rabbeinu Yerucham writes in the name of Rashba that if a wheat kernel is found in the vessel in which a chicken was washed, we say, ‘Here it was found, and here it had been,’ as with physical defects, and we do not say that it came from the chicken. He ruled this as practical halachah. This is difficult, for Rashba rules that if wheat is found in a matzah or dough, it prohibits the matzah or dough, as written in seif 11, and we do not say that it fell only now. Moreover, according to Rama's explanation, why does he write, ‘and we do not say that it came from the chicken’? He should have written, ‘and we do not say that it had previously been in the water.’ It is also unlike the law of physical defects. It therefore appears to me that the case concerns a chicken that was salted and then washed in cold water in a vessel, after which a wheat kernel was found in the vessel. There is concern that the kernel had been in the chicken and was salted together with it. Rashba therefore rules that we do not suspect this. His proof is from physical defects, concerning which Kesubos chapter 6 rules that when defects are found while a woman is in her father's domain, we presume that they were present before betrothal, while if they are found in her husband's domain, we say that they arose only now. Here too, since the wheat was found in the vessel, we do not say that it had been in the chicken, which would be moving the prohibition from place to place. A matzah or dough, however, presents a doubt from one time to another, as explained above. There is therefore no dispute at all. This appears to be the primary explanation.
By force of a double doubt. Perhaps it fell only now; and if it was previously in the chicken, perhaps it was digested. Those who disagree maintain that digestion helps only with ritual impurity and not with prohibition, as written at the end of Yoreh Deah siman 83. See Beit Yosef there, and the same is implied by Shulchan Aruch. Agudah in Bechoros writes that even according to the answer of Maharam of Pontoise one cannot permit a wheat kernel found in a chicken, because the chicken presumably swallowed it whole and this is not digestion. This implies that even if it later broke apart, it remains forbidden.
And chickens were soaked there. One must say that ‘chickens’ is not precise, since the case concerns a whole wheat kernel, to which digestion does not apply. There is therefore no difference between chickens and meat. If the wheat is found after the meat was removed, the meat is not prohibited.
That was soaked in water. Meaning, even though the wheat was found in the water while the meat was still soaking in it, this remains a doubt concerning a D'Rabbanan prohibition, because some permit cold food. Other cooked dishes may therefore be permitted.
Or was scalded. Meaning, the wheat was found afterward, so one may say that it fell only now. What was ruled above at the end of seif 2, that it may not be eaten despite a double doubt, refers to eating the wheat kernels themselves. The mixture, however, is permitted. Accordingly, if a matzah presents a double doubt, the matzah itself remains forbidden to eat. Bach writes that with a single doubt it is forbidden even to retain the item, even in a case of substantial loss. With a double doubt it may be retained, and certainly sold to a non-Jew, but may not be eaten, because the possibility that it was digested is not a complete doubt, since some authorities hold that digestion does not help regarding prohibition. This applies specifically to complete chametz, such as bread or dough. If a wheat kernel is found in water, however, a cooked dish prepared from that water beforehand is permitted because of a double doubt: perhaps the wheat fell only now, and if it fell earlier, perhaps it did not soften until now. If it did not soften, it does not prohibit at all, even according to our custom. His words imply that even in hot water one need not prohibit it unless it softened. See there. An incident occurred in which a person placed a piece of matzah and a piece of chicken in his mouth and ate them together. He felt a wheat kernel, removed the food, and saw a kernel, but could not determine whether it had been in the matzah or in the chicken. The chicken may be permitted through a double doubt: perhaps the kernel fell onto the chicken only now while cold, and if it had been there earlier, perhaps it was digested. If it had been in the matzah, however, it necessarily was there during baking, for had it fallen upon the cold matzah, it would have rolled off. Nevertheless, it appears to me that the matzah is also permitted, since the prohibition of the smallest amount is D'Rabbanan and we attribute the kernel leniently to the chicken, as in Yoreh Deah siman 111 regarding two pots, one of which contains sixty. We attribute the prohibition to the one containing sixty, and both are permitted. The amount requiring removal from the matzah at the place from which the piece was taken is nevertheless forbidden by law. It further appears to me that even if the other item was a piece of meat, both are permitted, because we do not establish a prohibition from one place to another and say that the kernel fell upon the meat only now while cold. The matzah is then also permitted. If, however, the meat lay in a bowl and he ate from it, the meat is forbidden, as explained above, because with a doubt from one time to another we establish the prohibition retroactively, perhaps the kernel was in the bowl when the meat was poured into it, and a second vessel prohibits on Pesach. One might suggest a double doubt: perhaps it was in the matzah, and if in the meat, perhaps it fell only now. This is not a valid doubt, because to say that it was in the matzah requires forbidding the matzah, and we do not create such a doubt even regarding a D'Rabbanan prohibition or even as part of a double doubt. Darchei Moshe writes so in siman 439. Accordingly, both the meat and matzah are forbidden. In most cases, however, one can recognize whether the wheat was cooked or baked. See Niddah 3b regarding a basket in which a creeping creature was found. This implies that if food was cooked in a pot, the pot was removed from the stove to another place and cooled, and a wheat kernel was then found upon it, we say that it fell only now, because we do not establish a prohibition from place to place.
