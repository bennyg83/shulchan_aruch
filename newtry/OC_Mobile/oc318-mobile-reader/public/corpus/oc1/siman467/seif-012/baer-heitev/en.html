In practice, if the liquid is hot enough that the hand recoils from it, the food is forbidden, because on Pesach chametz is forbidden even cold. Chok Yaakov. Split. Since Rama did not gloss here, as he did above in seif 9, to forbid even where the kernel was not split, perhaps the reason is that here there is an additional doubt, namely that it may have fallen afterward, and we are therefore not so stringent. Taz, however, writes that Rama follows his own view and forbids even where the kernel is not split, whenever it softened. Here, though, if it was not split, it may be sold to a non-Jew because there is a double doubt: perhaps it did not ferment, and perhaps it fell afterward. We are stringent only where there is a single doubt, as in seif 9. See there. Chok Yaakov is lenient even in the case of seif 9 and permits selling it to a non-Jew. See seif katan 18. The chicken. We say that perhaps the wheat was in the water at the time of scalding. We do not apply the principle “it was found here and was here” except from one place to another or from one piece to another, as explained in seif katan 21. Within a single place, such as during scalding, where the uncertainty concerns time and perhaps the wheat was already in the water, we do not say that it fell only now. See Chok Yaakov. This weakens the chicken’s presumption of permissibility, because it certainly was in fermented water. For this reason Rokeach forbids baked matzah in which a kernel was found when it is unknown whether the kernel fermented while the matzah was still dough. He permits the other matzos. See Taz there. Beit Yosef writes: Rabbeinu Yerucham states in the name of Rashba that if a kernel was found in the utensil in which the chicken was rinsed, we say, “It was found here and was here.” Magen Avraham asks from Rashba’s own ruling that if a kernel is found in matzah, the entire dough is forbidden, and we do not say that it fell only now. He therefore concludes that the case concerns a chicken that was salted and afterward rinsed in a cold utensil, and a kernel was then found in the utensil. There is concern that the kernel had been in the chicken while it was salted. Rashba teaches that we need not be concerned, because once the kernel is found in the utensil we do not presume that it had previously been in the chicken. We do not establish a prohibition from one place to another. Regarding matzah, by contrast, the uncertainty is from one time to another, as explained above. Accordingly, there is no disagreement at all, unlike Rama’s understanding. Magen Avraham. It must be that Magen Avraham’s statement, “regarding matzah the uncertainty is from one time to another,” refers to a case where some dough remained in his possession, as explained above in seif katan 21. Otherwise, it too would be a question from one place to another. Examine this carefully. A doubt. Perhaps it fell only now. Even if it had previously been in the chicken, perhaps it had already been digested. See Magen Avraham. The chickens. Magen Avraham writes that this is not limited to chickens. The same applies to meat, since the present case concerns a whole wheat kernel, where digestion is not relevant. Erev Shabbos writes that chickens were specified as the greater novelty. Although kernels are commonly found in them, the matter still remains doubtful, and we say that perhaps the kernel fell afterward. There is also another doubt, perhaps the halachah follows the opinion that cold contact imparts no flavor at all, making this comparable to a double doubt. Bach is stringent in these laws and challenges Rama. See Magen Avraham. The primary ruling follows Rama, and Eliyah Zuta and Taz agree. Especially where the kernel is not split, there is certainly no reason to be stringent, as explained above. If the kernel did not soften, one may be lenient in every case. Bach and the later authorities agree. Rabbinic. Rokeach writes: A piece of chametz was found in a bucket of water, and it was unknown whether the chametz had been there when water was taken from the bucket for cooking. We permitted the cooked food, but the water in the bucket was forbidden, because perhaps the non-Jew who brought the water placed the chametz there at that time. Taz writes that these words are unintelligible and apparently contain a scribal error. The text should read: “When water was taken from the well.” The meaning is that water was first drawn from the well for cooking, and afterward more water was drawn into the bucket, in which chametz was found. It was unknown whether chametz had been in the well when the water for cooking was drawn. Rokeach permitted the cooked food, while forbidding only the water remaining in the bucket, because the non-Jew who drew the later water may have placed the chametz into the bucket at that time, and the chametz had never been in the well. It is surprising that Beit Yosef copied this without noticing the error. In any event, the ruling is difficult, because it implies that if the chametz had been present before the cooking, the food would be forbidden, yet chametz in cold water should not prohibit. Perhaps Rokeach follows Rashba, who holds that even cold contact prohibits. We, however, follow Rosh and Tur, who hold that cold contact does not prohibit. It appears that if chametz soaked in cold water for twenty four hours, it is forbidden according to all opinions, because soaking for twenty four hours has the law of cooking. So writes Taz, and Bach and Maharshal agree. An incident occurred in which a person placed a piece of matzah and a piece of chicken together into his mouth. He sensed a wheat kernel, removed the food from his mouth, and saw the kernel. It was impossible to determine whether it had been in the matzah or in the chicken. The chicken may be permitted through a double doubt: perhaps the kernel fell into the cold chicken only now, and even if it had been there previously, perhaps it had been digested. If the kernel had been in the matzah, it must have been present during baking, because if it had fallen upon the cold matzah afterward, it would have rolled off. Nevertheless, it appears to me that the matzah is also permitted. Since the prohibition of any minute amount is Rabbinic, we attribute the kernel leniently to the chicken. Still, the measure of a netilah must be removed from the place in the matzah from which the piece was taken. It further appears to me that even if the other item was a piece of meat, it is permitted. We do not establish a prohibition from one place to another, and we say that the kernel fell upon the meat only now while it was cold. The matzah is therefore also permitted. If, however, the meat had been lying in a bowl and he ate from the bowl, the meat is forbidden. As explained above, we do establish a prohibition from one time to another, and perhaps the kernel was already in the bowl when the hot meat was poured into it. A kli sheni prohibits on Pesach. One might suggest a double doubt: perhaps the kernel was in the matzah, and even if it was in the meat, perhaps it fell only now. This is not a valid doubt, because if you say it was in the matzah, you must forbid the matzah. We do not apply such a doubt, even for a Rabbinic prohibition and even as part of a double doubt. Consequently, both the meat and the matzah are forbidden. Usually it is recognizable whether the kernel was cooked or baked together with the food. If the bowl had been moved from one place to another after cooling, however, we attribute the matter leniently, because we do not establish a prohibition from one place to another. Magen Avraham.
