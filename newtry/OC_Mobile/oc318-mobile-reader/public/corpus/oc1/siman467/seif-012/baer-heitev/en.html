Scalded. Ordinary scalding is done in a kli sheini. Rokeach. See siman 447, seif 3, in Rama. It is possible that here the prohibition is based upon pouring from a kli rishon, and Mordechai called it a kli sheini only because the food was in a bowl. As a matter of law, if the liquid is sufficiently hot to cook upon contact, it is prohibited, for on Pesach chametz is treated stringently even regarding cold contact. Chok Yaakov. Split. Since Rama did not gloss here, as he did above in seif 9, to prohibit even when the kernel is not split, perhaps here, because there is an additional doubt that it may have fallen afterward, we are not so stringent. Taz, however, writes that Rama follows his own position and prohibits even when it is not split whenever it softened. Here, however, where it was not split, it may be sold to a non-Jew because there is a double doubt: perhaps it did not become chametz, and perhaps it fell afterward. We are stringent only with a single doubt, as in seif 9. Chok Yaakov is lenient even in the case of seif 9 to permit sale to a non-Jew; see seif katan 18. The chicken. We say that perhaps the wheat kernel was in the water at the time of scalding. The principle "it was found here and therefore was here" is applied only when the issue is movement from place to place or from one piece to another, as stated in seif katan 21. Within a single place, such as the scalding water, where the uncertainty is one of time and perhaps the kernel was already present earlier, we do not simply say that it fell only now. Chok Yaakov. The chicken's prior permitted status is weakened because it certainly was in water that may have become chametz. For this reason Rokeach prohibits a baked matzah in which a wheat kernel was found when it is unknown whether the kernel fermented while the matzah was still dough, but permits the other matzot. Which prohibits. See what I wrote in seif katan 20. It fell. Beit Yosef cites Rabbeinu Yerucham in the name of Rashba: if a wheat kernel was found in the utensil in which a chicken was rinsed, we say that it was there where it was found. Magen Avraham asks from Rashba's own ruling that if a wheat kernel is found in matzah, the entire dough is prohibited and we do not say that it fell only now. He concludes that Rashba's case is where the chicken was salted and afterward rinsed in a cold utensil, and a wheat kernel was then found in that utensil. We might have feared that the kernel had been inside the chicken while it was salted. Rashba teaches that we do not establish a prohibition from one place to another. Matzah is different because there the doubt can be from one time to another. Accordingly, there is no disagreement at all, unlike Rama's understanding. The statement that matzah is a case of doubt from one time to another must refer to a case in which some dough remained in his possession; otherwise it too would be from one place to another. A doubt. Perhaps the kernel fell only now, and even if it had previously been in the chicken, perhaps it had already been digested. See Magen Avraham. The chickens. Magen Avraham writes that this is not limited to chickens; the same applies to meat, since here we are discussing a whole wheat kernel for which digestion is not relevant. Erev Shabbos writes that chickens were mentioned as the greater novelty: although kernels are commonly found in them, the case still remains doubtful and we say perhaps the kernel fell afterward. There is also another possible doubt, namely that perhaps the halacha follows the view that cold contact imparts no flavor at all. Bach is stringent in these laws and challenges Rama. The primary ruling follows Rama, as Eliyah Zuta and Taz agree. Especially when the kernel is not split, there is plainly no reason for stringency as stated above. If it did not soften, one may be lenient in every case, as Bach and the later authorities agree. Rabbinic. Rokeach writes that if a piece of chametz was found in a bucket of water and it was unknown whether the chametz was already present when water had earlier been taken for cooking, the cooked food was permitted while the water in the bucket was prohibited, because perhaps the non-Jew who brought the water placed the chametz there only then. Taz writes that the text evidently contains a scribal error and should read that the earlier water was drawn from a well. The meaning is that water was first drawn from the well for cooking, and afterward more water was drawn into the bucket and chametz was found in the bucket. It was unknown whether chametz had been in the well at the first drawing. The cooked food was permitted because the non-Jew may have placed the chametz into the bucket only during the later drawing. In any event, if chametz soaked in cold water for 24 hours, all agree that it prohibits because soaking for 24 hours is equivalent to cooking. Bach and Maharshal write likewise. An incident occurred in which a person placed a piece of matzah and a piece of chicken into his mouth and felt a wheat kernel. He removed the food and saw the kernel, but could not tell whether it had been in the matzah or the chicken. The chicken may be permitted through a double doubt: perhaps the kernel fell into the cold chicken only now, and even if it had been there earlier, perhaps it had been digested. If it had been in the matzah, it must have been there during baking, because if it fell on the cold matzah afterward it would roll off. Nevertheless, Magen Avraham inclines that the matzah too is permitted, because the prohibition of a minute amount on Pesach is rabbinic and we attribute the kernel leniently to the chicken. Still, a netilah must be removed from the place in the matzah from which the piece was taken. He further writes that even if the other food was meat, it may be permitted, because we do not establish a prohibition from one place to another and may say that the kernel fell onto the cold meat only now. If, however, the meat had been lying in a bowl and he ate from that bowl, the meat is prohibited, because from one time to another we do establish the possibility that the kernel was already in the bowl when hot meat was poured into it, and a kli sheini is stringent on Pesach. Usually one can recognize whether the wheat kernel was cooked or baked together with the food. If the bowl was moved from place to place after cooling, however, we again attribute the matter leniently, because we do not establish a prohibition from one place to another. Which was soaked.
