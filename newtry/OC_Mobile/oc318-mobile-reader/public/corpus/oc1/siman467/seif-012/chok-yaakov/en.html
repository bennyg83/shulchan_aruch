If they scalded the chicken. The Rokeach, siman 291, and the Mordechai in Perek Kol Sha'ah record an incident in which they scalded a chicken on Erev Pesach with its feathers in boiling water in a chametz bowl, which was a secondary vessel, and they forbade eating it, as we say in Zevachim regarding one who poured boiling liquid into it. Therefore the Taz wrote here that even in a secondary vessel there is room to forbid in this case.
A split wheat kernel. Perhaps since the Rama did not add here as he did above in seif 9, to forbid even one that is not split, it is because here, since there is an additional doubt that perhaps it fell in afterward, we are not so stringent. However, the Taz wrote that according to the Rama's own view, even one that is not split is forbidden once it softened, although it may be sold to a non-Jew.
The chicken is forbidden. The agreement of the early and later authorities is that with a chicken we do not say, 'Here it was found, here it was,' to say that it fell only now. The Magen Avraham distinguishes that we do not say it deteriorated only now where there is no prior presumption of permissibility, as appears in the first chapter of Chullin and in Niddah. The Taz likewise wrote that, on the contrary, all impurities are treated according to the time they are found.
And there is one who forbids. See what I wrote above in seif katan 31.
For we say, perhaps it fell there afterward. The Beit Yosef and Rabbeinu Yerucham wrote in the name of the Rashba that if a wheat kernel is found in a vessel in which the chicken was rinsed, we say 'here it was found and here it was,' as with blemishes, and do not say it came from the chicken. He so ruled in practice. See the Magen Avraham, who challenged this from the Rashba's own words, and see the distinctions written by the later authorities.
By force of a double doubt. See what I wrote above in seif katan 10.
Or with a whole wheat kernel. The Tur wrote here that his father the Rosh agreed that we do not derive prohibition from impurity, and it is considered digested. Yet in Yoreh De'ah, end of siman 83, he wrote that if a kosher fish swallowed a non-kosher fish, it is forbidden, for it is not considered digested as long as it remains whole. From there it follows that when it is not whole, the Tur holds it is considered digested. Here too the distinction depends on whether the kernel remains whole.
And chickens soaked there. The Magen Avraham wrote that this is not specifically chickens, but the same applies to meat, for here the discussion is of a whole wheat kernel, where digestion is not applicable. The Olas Shabbos wrote that chickens were mentioned as a novelty: even though wheat kernels are commonly found in them, nevertheless it does not leave the realm of doubt, and we say perhaps it fell in afterward. There is also the additional doubt whether it became chametz.
In doubts of a D'Rabbanan prohibition. See the Bach on this, and what I wrote nearby in seif katan 40. The Nimukei Yosef in Perek HaArel, and similarly the Shach in Yoreh De'ah at the beginning of siman 111, discuss the law of two pots, one permitted and one forbidden, with two pieces before him, one permitted and one forbidden, and one fell into one of the pots. If the prohibition is D'Rabbanan, the rule is lenient, and similar reasoning applies here according to the circumstances.
That soaked in water. The Magen Avraham explained that even though the wheat kernel was found in it while the meat was still soaking, this remains a D'Rabbanan doubt, since some authorities permit in cold. His words are not compelled as an explanation of the Rama. See seif katan 38. Or 'was scalded' means that afterward the wheat kernel was found, so we can say it fell only now. The reason the ruling differs in other cases depends on the precise circumstances and the relevant doubts.
