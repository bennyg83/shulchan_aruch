If they scalded a chicken, etc. Ordinary scalding is in a kli sheni; and so explicitly in Sefer Rokeach siman 211.
The chicken is forbidden. For perhaps the wheat kernel was in the water before the boiling and became chametz and absorbed from the water; so the Tur wrote in the name of the Ramban. And it is difficult: why do we not say "here it was found, here it was," and after the chicken was scalded it came there? And it appears that he derives prohibition from tumah, as we say in chapter "BaKol Me'arvin" (Eruvin folio 35): He touched one at night and does not know whether [the person was] alive or dead, and the next day he found him dead: the Sages declare tamei, for all tumahs are [judged] as at the time of their finding; and for this reason it is written in the Rokeach: a wheat kernel found in baked matzah, and it is unknown whether it became chametz while still dough or never became chametz: that matzah alone is forbidden, but other matzos we maintain in their presumptive status and are permitted; therefore here too we say the water retrospectively is as at the time of their finding now; and one should not say to maintain the chicken too in its presumptive status, for since the water was thus, it certainly makes chametz, and like the chicken that was certainly there its presumptive status was weakened; which is not so regarding the other matzos above, for even though we say retrospectively it is as now, that is regarding that matzah, but this is not compelling for the other matzos, as appears to me. And one should not say it is a double doubt: perhaps the wheat kernel was digested in the chicken, as the Rama wrote afterward: one can say that here it deals with a whole wheat kernel; and also one should not say "perhaps it did not become chametz," since one who is expert can distinguish: this is not called a doubt, as in Yoreh De'ah siman 84.
A cracked wheat kernel. Implying that if it is not cracked open there is no prohibition, for it is a double doubt: perhaps it did not become chametz, and even if it became chametz, perhaps it fell afterward; all the more so here where we see it is not cracked open, only that we are stringent from doubt; only that the Rama wrote nearby in se'if 9 that we forbid in any amount even if they did not crack open; and it appears that here, where there is a double doubt, we permit him to sell the chicken to a non-Jew, and we are stringent only following a single doubt like that of se'if 9.
If it is during Pesach. This he wrote according to his answer to the difficulty of the Tur's words against each other in se'if 11. But according to what I wrote, that there is no difficulty anyway, it is permitted here even on Pesach, for cold imparts no taste at all.
By force of a double doubt. My master and father-in-law of blessed memory raised a difficulty that this is not a double doubt, for the mind of fools is not knowledge, and it is possible that one who is expert knows to assess that it was not digested. And I do not know the difficulty, for this matter cannot be known: it is like bones in a full stomach; and all such cases are certainly a doubt, as written in Yoreh De'ah siman 81. And one can say: here in this siman se'if 1 regarding grain in an attic the Rama wrote that one may not permit for eating because of a double doubt; and one should not say here there is another leniency that there is only any amount, whereas regarding grain there is doubt in all the grain: this is not so, for above too he said "in some places," implying the majority is permitted; and so is that very law in the Rashba's responsum that Beis Yosef brings regarding a year when rains were abundant, which is what is written in Shulchan Aruch se'if 4, and there it is explained that the majority is permitted; and further, wherever a double doubt helps, it helps even regarding a Torah prohibition; and therefore it appears he was stringent there for eating only because there is a possibility without loss: regarding eating it is like something that has a way of becoming permitted; and as Kol Bo wrote on this law that I copied in se'if 2, concluding there: since there is no loss in keeping it, etc.; which is not so regarding food needed for Pesach, where we leave it according to the [basic] law, as appears to me. The Rokeach wrote: a piece of chametz was found in a bucket of water and it was unknown when they took the water from the bucket whether there was chametz in it or not, and they cooked in it and we overturned the cooked dish, but the water in the bucket is forbidden, for perhaps the non-Jew who brings the water put chametz in at that hour (end quote). And Beis Yosef brings these words and they have no explanation; and it appears there is a scribal error, and it should read: when they took water from the well; and the intent is that they first drew water from the well to cook, and afterward drew water in a bucket and chametz was found in the bucket, and it was unknown whether there was chametz in the well when he drew to cook, so automatically he cooked in chametzed water; in this he ruled the cooked dish permitted, only that the water in the bucket alone is forbidden, but the cooked dish is permitted, for the non-Jew drawing into the bucket put the chametz at that hour, but that chametz was not in the well at all. And it is astonishing on Beis Yosef who copied and did not notice; and however it may be, it is difficult, for it implies that if the chametz had been before it was cooked it would be forbidden, yet chametz in cold does not forbid as above; and it is possible he holds like the Rashba that Beis Yosef brings, who disagrees with the Rosh and holds cold also forbids; and we hold like the Rosh and Tur that cold does not forbid; and it appears that if the chametz soaked in cold water a full day it is forbidden according to all, for pickled is like cooked; and so wrote my master and father-in-law in the name of Maharshal. The Tur wrote, and this is his language in Sefer Rokeach: a wheat kernel found in a pot: the spoon with which they stirred the pot is forbidden; they put it in another pot: all are forbidden; and Beis Yosef wrote on this: it is plain, and therefore he did not copy it in Shulchan Aruch. And I say this requires deep inward study, for at first glance this passage is not the halachah, for Tur Yoreh De'ah siman 93 wrote in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that something forbidden in any amount does not become nevelah-in-essence, and any amount from it does not afterward forbid in any amount, that is from what it releases; and as the Tur wrote there: even if it was forbidden in its own species in any amount, according to R' Yehuda we do not say two "any amounts"; and if so here, where the spoon forbidden from the pot that has a wheat kernel in it is forbidden because of any amount, why should it afterward forbid another pot in any amount? And so explicitly in Levush siman 92, and this is his language: regarding chametz and yayin nesech that are forbidden in any amount, they do not become nevelah-in-essence; and if it fell again into another pot it is nullified in the majority if unrecognized; and if recognized one discards it and the rest is permitted (end quote). And there we wrote that this "if unrecognized it is nullified" is not precise, and they said this only when recognized: one discards it, and its release does not forbid in any amount since it itself was forbidden in any amount; and if so this case here is certainly very difficult. And it appears to me to resolve properly: here with the spoon we are concerned lest the spoon touched the wheat kernel itself in the pot and was forbidden from it, not from the cooked dish in the pot. And we find such in Yoreh De'ah siman 95 regarding meat bowls rinsed in a dairy cauldron: those who forbid there do so because the bowls touch the pan and receive taste from it, and we do not follow the water in the pan; so too here the spoon receives from the wheat kernel itself; and one should not say regarding this "here it was found, here it was," for the way of the spoon is to touch everything in the pot when stirring, and it was forbidden by taste imparted, not by any amount, for there needed to be sixty in what was absorbed in the spoon against the wheat kernel; and it is possible there is not so much absorption, even though we assess the whole spoon everywhere: that is for stringency, to assess against it because we do not know how much comes out of it, but for leniency no, for perhaps there is not so much absorption that there would be sixty in it against the wheat kernel; therefore it properly forbids. (But if one inserted) another spoon into that pot that was forbidden because the first spoon was inserted into it, that spoon is not forbidden so much as to forbid another pot into which they insert it, since it is forbidden only in any amount because of the cooked dish and will not forbid others in any amount; and likewise if they removed the wheat kernel from the pot it fell into and someone came and inserted a spoon into it, it does not afterward forbid by insertion into another pot in any amount, unless there is sixty against the spoon, and it is permitted, as appears to me clearly; and it is a peg that will not move, with Heaven's help.
