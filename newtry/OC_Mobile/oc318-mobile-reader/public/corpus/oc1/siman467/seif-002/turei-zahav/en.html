Since they are resting. The Tur wrote this in the name of R' Ephraim: even though regarding soaking they are forbidden only if they cracked open, that case is different, for he is occupied with it, therefore they do not come to chametzing until they crack open; but here, since they rest, they come to chametzing. And the Ran in the name of the Ra'ah wrote that here we do not need that they cracked open, for it deals with wheat that are cracked open by nature; and specifically regarding barley do we require cracking open. And the Ra'em distinguished between a little dripping and much, as explained in Beis Yosef; and since he mentioned here the view of R' Ephraim, it implies that we hold there is no distinction between wheat and barley, nor between little dripping and much.
By force of a double doubt. The Rama's view here requires further study, for these words the Agur wrote, and Beis Yosef brought them regarding wheat on which water passed through the roof: that we rely on the permission that Ravyah wrote, and the long Mordechai brought in the name of Ravyah: to eat some of them before Pesach or to sell to a non-Jew little by little, and the rest should be permitted by force of a double doubt: perhaps water did not come down on them, and even if it did, perhaps they did not crack open (meaning did not become chametz); but the lower wheat near the attic floor almost certainly had water come down up to a finger's thickness or half a finger, and those one should cast off, and the rest is permitted to keep, but not to eat from them on Pesach; for even though regarding "bal yeraeh" the doubts are nullified in their majority, regarding eating, which forbids in any amount, we are not lenient (end quote). What he wrote "to eat some of them," etc., appears to have a scribal error in his words; for this permission to eat some of them my master and father-in-law of blessed memory brought in the name of Ravyah in the long Mordechai, that it is permitted even on Pesach so that we can say it is a double doubt: perhaps they already ate from the forbidden ones, and even if they did not eat, one can say they will now eat from the permitted; and like the case of a ring of idolatry that mixed and one of them fell into the Great Sea, that all were permitted for benefit together, for certainly there is permitted [among them]; and even if they did not eat from them he can take from them and cast into the sea, or eat from them before Pesach, and the others become permitted; and there is no "nullifying prohibition l'chatchila" here since it is before Pesach, and you have no matzah that does not have more than this prohibition two by two; and in a double doubt we follow leniency even regarding a Torah prohibition (end of his words). And in the Agur we mentioned he mixed two grounds of permission, namely eating some of them (which helps even if there is certain chametz among them) and he mentioned a double doubt because perhaps it did not become chametz; and it appears to me there is some omission in his words. And in any case, since the main permission here is by force of a double doubt, why is eating on Pesach forbidden? For even regarding a Torah prohibition it is permitted with a double doubt, all the more so regarding a rabbinic any-amount. And further, the Rama himself, who wrote here that a double doubt does not help for eating, wrote in siman 453 se'if 3 that if there is among them grain that sprouted, if there is sixty in what did not sprout it is permitted if they grind them, that is even to eat on Pesach; so we do not say "returns and revives"; and why did he rule here to forbid on the ground of "returns and revives"? One must therefore say that here it deals with a case where there is not sixty against the prohibition, and the main novelty he teaches is that it is not permitted even with a double doubt: the first difficulty remains: why in truth does a double doubt not help here? And further, Ravyah indeed permitted because of a double doubt even for eating, as written in his name; and also in the following se'if this is evident; and this requires further study. And for practical law it implies even according to all that the prohibition to eat is because there is no loss in keeping them; implying that if there is loss, for example he has no other, it is permitted even for eating.
