Seif 4. And he sends it to his wife so that she may drink from it. Note: See Magen Avraham, who writes that this applies even if she did not eat with them, as is implied by the Gemara concerning Yalta. At first glance, I found this puzzling. Why did he need to bring proof from Yalta, which is an Amoraic statement concerning a woman, rather than from the explicit Baraisa in Bava Metzia 86b concerning Sarah? The Baraisa states that the angels knew Sarah was in the tent. Why, then, did they ask about her? To endear her to her husband. Rabbi Yose says: in order to send her the cup of blessing. This apparently proves that even though Sarah did not eat with them, they nevertheless sent her the cup of blessing. The Magen Avraham's proof therefore seems surprising. I do not have the commentaries available to see whether they noted this, for I am writing this in a village while occupied with bathing in hot springs. Upon closer analysis, however, the Magen Avraham's words are correct. Indeed, the passage might initially appear to prove the opposite. I had long wondered what Rabbi Yose's statement, “in order to send her the cup of blessing,” answers. Why did they ask where she was? Since they knew her location, they could have sent her the cup of blessing without asking, “Where is Sarah your wife?” The explanation appears to be as follows. The question “where is he?” may be used in two ways. First, when one simply asks where a particular person is, this question applies only when his location is unknown. If one knows where he is, the expression “where is he?” is inappropriate. Second, when several people are dining together and one person connected to that meal, such as a guest dining with the host and his household, sees that the host's son or wife is absent, he may ask, “Where is so-and-so?” The expression is then figurative, meaning: Why did he not come to the meal? In that sense one may ask “where?” even while knowing the person's location, since the real question is why he did not come to eat. Accordingly, one might have answered the Gemara's question, “Since they knew she was in the tent, why did they ask about her?” by saying that they did not ask where she was literally. Rather, they asked why she had not eaten with them. The Gemara does not answer this way because such a question would itself be difficult. It is obvious that, because of modesty, a woman does not ordinarily eat with men. It therefore could not mean, “Why did she not eat with us?” Necessarily, “Where is Sarah your wife?” must be understood literally. Since they knew where she was, why did they ask? The Gemara must therefore answer that it was to endear her to her husband. Rabbi Yose, however, does not agree. He understands that the question “where?” was indeed figurative: Why did she not eat with us? One cannot answer that she refrained because of modesty. That consideration applies where no mitzvah benefit would be lost. In such a case, it is fitting for her to act stringently and not eat with men. Here, however, by not eating with them she would forfeit the cup of blessing, whereas if she ate with them she would gain the cup of blessing. She should therefore not have been stringent at the cost of losing a blessing. Rabbi Yose consequently says that the purpose was “to send her the cup of blessing.” Meaning, the question was: Why did she not eat with us? Since a cup of blessing was at stake, she should have joined them. On this reading, the passage could actually imply that if she did not eat with them, she was not entitled to the cup of blessing. The Magen Avraham therefore could not cite this source as proof, and instead cited the incident of Yalta, which indicates as a matter of law that one must send the cup even to a woman who did not eat with them. According to the accepted halachah, however, the earlier passage must be explained differently. There is room to inquire: If a woman did not eat with them, which is preferable? Should she go to them to drink from the cup of blessing, thereby gaining the reward for taking steps toward a mitzvah, or is it preferable that she not go to them and that they send her the cup, so that the reward for the steps belongs to her husband rather than to her? It appears that the ministering angels and Sarah disagreed over to whom the reward for the steps belongs. We find in several places that there are disputes between the heavenly academy and the earthly academy. So too, there was a dispute here between Sarah and the ministering angels. The angels held that the reward for the steps belonged to her, and that she should therefore go to them to receive the cup of blessing. They asked, “Where is Sarah your wife?” They knew she was in the tent, but their question was figurative: Why did she not come here to drink from the cup of blessing? Rabbi Yose explains that their question “where?” meant, “Why did she not come to drink from the cup of blessing?” Sarah's own reasoning, however, was that it was preferable for them to send her the cup of blessing rather than for her to go to them. Thus Rabbi Yose says that the matter concerned sending her the cup of blessing. This is the more reasonable course, and she was not required to go to them to drink it. The matter is therefore resolved. Examine it carefully, for this appears correct.
