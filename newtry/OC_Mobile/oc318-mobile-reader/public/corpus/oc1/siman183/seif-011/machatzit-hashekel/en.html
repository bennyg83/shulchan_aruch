Seif katan 11: They fulfilled their obligation, etc. “Nine blasts,” etc., is the Gemara’s wording. It mentions the Torah-level number of blasts, for D’Oraisa one fulfills the obligation with nine sounds, three sets of tekiah, teruah, tekiah. Because of uncertainty regarding the nature of the teruah, one must hear thirty sounds, as stated at the end of Rosh Hashanah and below at the beginning of siman 590. Since one who heard nine blasts over nine hours certainly paused between them and nevertheless fulfilled his obligation, the same applies here.
According to what is written at the beginning of siman 65, etc. This refers to Krias Shema. Tosafos there hold that Krias Shema and shofar follow the same rule. This compelled them to distinguish between an ordinary delay and a delay caused by circumstances beyond one’s control. They were troubled that regarding shofar the Gemara says that even a delay of nine hours is not an interruption, while regarding prayer it says that if one delayed long enough to complete the entire prayer he returns to the beginning. Tosafos hold that prayer, Krias Shema, and shofar follow one rule. They therefore distinguish that a delay caused by circumstances beyond one’s control constitutes an interruption in all three. Accordingly, the statement that one returns to the beginning after delaying long enough to complete the whole refers to a delay caused by circumstances beyond one’s control. Since according to them even regarding shofar such a delay requires returning, and the present law is derived from shofar, the same applies here.
Regarding excrement, if one recited Bircas HaMazon while excrement was before him, it is uncertain whether he must repeat Bircas HaMazon. Regarding prayer in such a case, he certainly must repeat, as stated above at the end of siman 76.
At the end of the passage beginning “To where does he return,” etc. The Gemara asks where he returns. Rav Zevid in the name of Abaye says that he returns to the beginning, while the Sages say that he returns to the place where he stopped, and the halachah follows the Sages. Rashi explains that this concerns one who interrupts his meal for two others, as discussed below in siman 200: three ate together, two interrupt their meal to recite Bircas HaMazon with zimmun, while the third still wishes to eat. The third must stop eating until the end of the zimmun and then resumes his meal. We rule like Rabbi Sheshes that the zimmun extends through Bircas HaZan and the beginning of Nodeh Lecha. The question is where the third begins Bircas HaMazon after finishing his meal. Rav Zevid says from the beginning of HaZan; the Sages say from where he stopped, namely Nodeh Lecha. Tosafos object that according to the Sages, since he ate after hearing Bircas HaZan from the other two, he would lack Bircas HaZan for what he ate afterward. Tosafos therefore explain that the case concerns three who ate and one went to the marketplace, and they call him and recite zimmun with him, as explained below in siman 194, seif 2. He must remain with the other two until after Bircas HaZimun. If he then leaves and later wishes to recite Bircas HaMazon for what he ate before being called, the Sages properly rule that he begins with Nodeh Lecha, since he fulfilled Bircas HaZan by hearing it from the other two. Tosafos conclude that he fulfilled it because he did not speak in the interim. This implies that if he spoke, even the Sages agree that he returns to the beginning, supporting Magen Avraham’s ruling that intentional speech requires returning to the beginning.
However, it appears to me that this is a scribal error, etc. Maharsha likewise has the reading, “for he did not eat in the interim.” This reading is readily understood. Tosafos objected to Rashi that since he ate after hearing Bircas HaZimun, how could that blessing discharge the food eaten afterward? According to Tosafos’ explanation, however, he did not eat after being called and hearing Bircas HaZimun, and therefore the blessing he heard properly discharged him. If the reading were “for he did not speak,” why would speech enter the discussion? Speech had not previously been mentioned, nor had Tosafos challenged Rashi on that basis. According to the reading “for he did not eat,” Tosafos provide no proof for Magen Avraham.
What I wrote at the end of siman 194 in the name of Ramban refers back to the beginning of Magen Avraham’s words, that even a delay caused by circumstances beyond one’s control does not require returning. Ramban explains Rif’s interpretation of “to where does he return” as referring to the leader of zimmun, who begins, “Let us bless Him from Whose food we have eaten,” and the others answer, “Blessed is He from Whose food we have eaten and by Whose goodness we live.” The Gemara then asks where the leader resumes. Rav Zevid in the name of Abaye says that he returns to the beginning, meaning that he again says, “Let us bless Him from Whose food we have eaten.” Rabbeinu Yonah explains that nivarech has two meanings: sometimes it is imperative and sometimes present tense.
When he says nivarech the first time, it is an imperative addressed to the others, calling upon them to bless. When he says it the second time, it is present tense, meaning: we bless the Creator from Whose food we have eaten. The Sages say that he returns to where he stopped, meaning that he begins, “Blessed is He from Whose food we have eaten and by Whose goodness we live.”
Ramban writes that according to this, Rav Zevid’s reason for returning to the beginning is that he holds that if one delayed long enough to complete the entire unit, he returns to the beginning. He further holds that even a delay long enough to complete one blessing suffices, and follows Rav Nachman that Bircas HaZimun extends only until the beginning of Bircas HaZan. Thus, when the leader said, “Let us bless Him from Whose food we have eaten,” he completed one blessing.
When he then waited until they answered, “Blessed is He, etc., and by Whose goodness we live,” he delayed long enough to complete one blessing, for their response is longer than “Let us bless Him from Whose food we have eaten.” Rav Zevid therefore holds that he must return to the beginning and repeat nivarech. Ramban does not explain the reason of the Sages, whose ruling we follow, that he returns to where he stopped. One might therefore say that they hold that in Bircas HaMazon even a delay long enough to complete the entire prayer does not require returning, despite the fact that the delay was caused by circumstances beyond one’s control, since the leader had to wait for the respondents. This would indicate that in Bircas HaMazon even such a delay does not require returning. At first glance, however, there is no proof, because Ramban is explaining Rif, and Rif does not distinguish between a delay caused by circumstances beyond one’s control and an ordinary delay. Rather, regarding prayer one returns even without circumstances beyond one’s control, whereas regarding Krias Shema and shofar one does not return even when circumstances beyond one’s control caused the delay, as explained in siman 65. Thus the issue would remain unresolved according to Tosafos and Rosh, who do distinguish the two cases, whether in Bircas HaMazon a compelled delay requires returning. Nevertheless, Magen Avraham’s inference is sound: even according to Tosafos and Rosh, whose view Rama follows in siman 65, the leader repeats only from where he stopped, namely “Blessed is He from Whose food we have eaten,” as explained at the beginning of siman 192, even though he was compelled to wait long enough to complete all of Bircas HaZimun. Magen Avraham then rejects this proof by referring to the end of siman 200. There is no proof because the halachah follows Rabbi Sheshes that Bircas HaZimun extends through Bircas HaZan. When the leader waits for the response, “Blessed is He from Whose food we have eaten and by Whose goodness we live,” he has not delayed long enough to complete the entire blessing, which extends until Nodeh Lecha.
