“Exposed” (no partitions): The Tur used this wording only for relieving in a place that is actually exposed; where there are partitions—such as an indoor privy—Beit Yosef says one need not be finicky. He cites Avohu that the Tur’s example “indoor privy” means the partitions are not as far apart as in a huge courtyard. The Taz argues Beit Yosef’s reading is weak: wherever partitions exist, even very distant ones, it should be permitted; “indoor privy” teaches that any walled area is like a house—the Tur opened with “only in an exposed place,” meaning no partitions at all, as the Yerushalmi says “provided there is no wall” (Rosh); hence our privies that have partitions require no stringency whether partitions are close or far. Rashba is explicit: only with no wall at all—i.e. an open field—does “no wall” exclude the presence of some wall; our master’s hints forbid east/west in a field with no partitions but not indoors—implying field privies had no partitions. Yet Tosafos (Sukkah 36) infer from “entering a privy” that there were partitions; “privy” suggests a structure—Beit Yosef cites this later (siman 12); the Gemara in Berachot (“I followed Rabbi into a privy…”) implies partitions—yet east/west is still forbidden. If Beit Yosef meant city walls make the whole town permitted, that cannot be: a wall adjacent to where one relieves is required; Avohu’s reading is primary—one must relieve next to a partition. The Yerushalmi/Rashba “field” language supports requiring adjacency to a wall; a courtyard without sitting near a wall is forbidden like exposure; Rashba limits strict “field” to no fence at all; with partitions in a courtyard there is permission at the wall. Rosh (“our privies with partitions—no concern”) shows intent to exclude large field privies of the Talmud where partitions were far from the spot; ours are permitted because partitions adjoin the place of relief. The Tur’s “field vs house” wording should have been “with vs without partitions,” teaching the field case needs adjacency (=indoors-like). The Tur’s “only in an exposed place” means the relieving spot is exposed even if distant walls exist—heter only when like indoors; practically one should relieve adjacent to a wall in a large roofless courtyard; a roofed courtyard has no east/west issue. Beit Yosef: even one wall suffices per Yerushalmi—when one sits adjacent.
“But,” etc. — One reason is the Divine Presence toward the west, so one may not face or turn one’s back there. Another is orientation toward Jerusalem. The Gemara (Berachot 61a) teaches: one who relieves himself in Judea should not do so east–west—meaning neither back nor front toward either—because Jerusalem is in Judea toward the north of Judah, and Judea runs the length of Israel east–west; relieving east–west would face Jerusalem. North–south is permitted provided one does not face Jerusalem directly (south of Judah). In Galilee (north of Israel) one should not turn north–south but may east–west. Rabbi Yossi forbids only where one can see the Temple Mount; the Sages forbid more broadly—these dispute the corners of Judah/Galilee not toward Jerusalem. A later baraita: Rabbi Akiva forbids everywhere outside the Land; we rule like Rabbi Akiva. Beit Yosef asks why the Tur states simply “east–west is forbidden”—that applies only in lands east or west of Israel (e.g. Bavel); in Judah or Galilee the axes invert. Tosafos resolve Rabbi Akiva’s concern is the Shechinah in the west (Yerushalmi), not Jerusalem; hence outside Israel only east–west is forbidden everywhere. The Tur’s wording follows that dispute. Terumah/Israel directions differ from chu"l because most of the world is not toward Jerusalem; Galilee’s south is still “Land of Israel,” so north–south there honors all Israel. Magen Avraham’s difficulty dissolves if the reason in chu"l is Shechinah in the west, not Jerusalem—Tosafos meant Rabbi Akiva forbids east–west even in chu"l, and the Gemara’s later clause explains his ruling there.
