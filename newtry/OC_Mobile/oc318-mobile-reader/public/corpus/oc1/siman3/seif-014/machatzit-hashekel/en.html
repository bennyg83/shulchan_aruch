(s.k. 14) “Married”: it appears to me if his wife is not with him it is forbidden—so wrote Taz. Bei’ur Halacha in Even HaEzer 23 disagrees. Tosafos Niddah 13a.

From Taz Even HaEzer: he asked on Niddah 13b—Rabbi Tarphon says “hand to his mother [should be cut off]” for placing a hand below the navel; the Sages answered, “Suppose a thorn lodged in his belly—should he not remove it, yet his belly would split?” He answered: better his belly split than descend to the pit. Taz challenged Tosafos: what greatness is Rabbi Yochanan’s “below the belt”—below the navel is presumably the same strip of flesh? He wrote Rabbi Tarphon’s “below the navel” is not literal but means touching the organ; he brought proof from Tosafos Magen Avraham cites—that “split” means the organ ruptures.

So too there 13b—this refers to his own words: Tosafos must hold Rabbi Yochanan’s “below the belt” is not literal, else it would not be a special stringency. You could ask Magen Avraham: perhaps Tosafos read “below the belt” literally but hold Rabbi Tarphon’s “below the navel” is also non-literal—then Tosafos’ question on Rabbi Yochanan’s greatness stands; Magen Avraham therefore held Rabbi Tarphon’s “below the navel” is certainly non-literal, and he proved this well. Hence so too 13b.

Tosafos wrote “his organ may rupture”—I did not find this wording in Tosafos.

The Gemara opened with “organ” and ended with “belly,” as above—“hand to his mother” for below the navel. We rule like Rambam: an unmarried man may not insert his hand even below the navel—see there.

Niddah 13a: Rav Yehuda told Shmuel on the synagogue roof he must urinate; Shmuel said hold your organ and urinate outward. The Gemara objects—holding the organ is like bringing a flood; Abaye answered: treat him like a bolas [military scout]—open barrels forbidden, closed permitted in peacetime; in war both permitted because no time to pour libations—so fear prevents sinful thoughts. Two resolutions: night/roof, or married—end of Gemara. Bei’ur Halacha asks: Tur, Rosh, and Rambam did not cite this fear leniency.

This needs study against Even HaEzer 184: “anxiety” stops menstrual flow—yet we do not say “we are not expert in anxiety.” Is it strained to distinguish pachada from bei’ata? Perhaps rabbinic veset rules are lenient unlike this severe prohibition—see end of Niddah 71a and Shabbat 41a.

See Shabbat 41a; the same resolution (“where there is fear it is permitted”) appears there. The author ties Bei’ur Halacha’s difficulty to Ketubot 27b: a city conquered by troops—all priestesses found there are disqualified lest they were raped; the Gemara contrasts the bolas mishnah; Rabbi Mari answers: no time for libation but time for intercourse—Rashi: strong lust. If lust in town is so strong, how compare Rav Yehuda/Shmuel’s case to bolas? One answer: Rabbi Yochanan spoke for Hezekiah’s view—different conquests. Tosafos (R. Chananel, R. Tam): Hezekiah would erase the intercourse/libation distinction. Mishnah “all priestesses forbidden” speaks of that kingdom’s conquest where they sit secure—no panic; another kingdom’s conquest is like the barrels mishnah—even intercourse has no “leisure” per Hezekiah. Ran challenged poskim who rule like Rabbi Mari since Hezekiah argues; Rabbi Yochanan/Shmuel seem to follow Hezekiah’s resolution. Rabbi Yehuda/Shmuel would then align with Hezekiah’s “no leisure even for intercourse,” so Abaye’s answer in Niddah fits Rabbi Yehuda’s approach: treat like bolas—for them there is no distinction between intercourse panic and libation panic, and neither leads to [forbidden] thoughts—this still needs clarification.

Rosh (Ketubot): we rule like Rabbi Mari’s answer in Avodah Zarah where two mishnayos clash—Rif omitted this sugya because it does not distinguish this kingdom from that—priests always forbidden, wine permitted—this resolves Bei’ur Halacha’s question; Rosh was right not to copy the “fear” answer if we rule like Rabbi Mari who said “there is leisure for intercourse,” so panic does not stop the yetzer for arayos—still difficult for Rambam. Main issue: Rashi in Ketubot—Hezekiah did not reject Rabbi Mari; he too distinguishes intercourse from libation to settle mishnayos; sometimes we are not concerned for intercourse panic (conquest where the king will not destroy a neighboring city)—even for Hezekiah “there is leisure for intercourse,” so the difficulty returns: how did Abaye compare to bolas if the yetzer for arayos applies? One must distinguish cases (perhaps hotza’at zera levatala with “bread in the basket” weakens the yetzer). Bei’ur Halacha’s question stands even for Rosh who rules like Rabbi Mari: for this sin, where there is fear there should be no [forbidden] thoughts—unless we are not expert; were it not for Rashi’s approach we might say even Rabbi Mari’s panic causes concern; even per Rashi, since this sin resembles arayos, Bei’ur Halacha requires greater “panic” expertise—unlike niddah veset where we are expert—this needs clarification.
