Seif katan 10: Whatever the host tells you, do, etc. They asked Rav Huna son of Rav Nachman, “Why, when...?” This is difficult: what did they ask him at the end, when he had already answered? Maharsha raises the same question and answers that initially he could respond only on the basis of proper etiquette, but in the end he gave a stronger answer, that one may not refuse a great person, which is a matter of law derived from Avraham. This explains Rav Huna's response, but Magen Avraham's question concerns those who asked him. Since he had already answered them from proper etiquette regarding sitting on the bed, why did they ask him again regarding drinking the cup? One may answer that nevertheless, proper etiquette requires a person to refuse until urged. Accordingly, when he sat on the bed, he must indeed not have sat immediately, but only after they urged him, because the reason that one may not refuse a great person does not apply there. Tosafos' question, that regarding sitting on the bed the reason “one may not refuse” does not apply, is therefore not directed at Rav Huna, asking why he did not give that reason. Rather, it means: why did he not sit immediately on the basis that one may not refuse? It must be that he did not sit immediately, because his stated reason, “Whatever the host tells you, do,” obligates compliance with the host, but does not dispense with the need to refuse first, as Magen Avraham writes.
This follows from the statement in Arachin 16b, at the end of chapter 3.
Even a casual lodging place, etc. Rabbi Yehudah said in the name of Rav that the prohibition against changing one's lodging place is derived from Avraham, as it is written, “to the place where his tent had been at the beginning.” Rabbi Yose son of Rabbi Chanina derives it from, “He went on his journeys.” The Gemara asks what the practical difference is and answers: a casual lodging place. Rashi explains: a lodging place encountered incidentally on the road because night overtook him. According to the one who derives it from “his tent,” only a fixed lodging place is included; according to the one who derives it from “his journeys,” even a casual one is included. Thus, casual lodging depends on a dispute. Magen Avraham may have ruled stringently according to the view that includes even casual lodging. To say that he understands there to be no dispute does not appear consistent with the passage.
