Seif katan 6: Lest he eat. Nevertheless, he washes inside. One should not ask: either way, what suspicion is there? If he does not eat, he truly need not wash, and if he does eat, they know that he is particular and certainly washed. The answer is that the concern is that he may later eat.
The text should read: “to wash his hands,” as in Rabbeinu Yonah.
See siman 212 regarding what Shelah proved. Although we rule there that the principal food exempts the secondary food even where the secondary food is bread, so that he need not recite HaMotzi, the same applies to those who sit drinking and customarily eat a little bread to sweeten the drink. They need not recite a blessing over the bread, because it is secondary to the beverage, certainly if he expressly intended to eat bread only to sweeten the drink. Magen Avraham cites this ruling in the name of Shelah below in siman 212. Shelah further proved from the words of Rabbeinu Yonah cited above that although the beverages, as the principal item, exempt the secondary bread from HaMotzi, he must nevertheless wash his hands for that bread. So too wherever bread is exempted as secondary, netilas yadayim is still required. Here, one who wishes to drink and not eat has withdrawn his hands from eating, which constitutes the end of the meal. If he then wishes to eat, he would ordinarily need to recite a blessing over the bread. But because the meal has ended and he must recite a blessing over the wine he now drinks, since wine during the meal does not exempt wine after the meal, he need not recite a separate blessing over the small amount of bread eaten with the drink, because the bread is secondary to the beverage and the blessing over the wine exempts it. Even so, he must wash for that small amount of bread, because its secondary status does not dispense with washing. See there at length.
Seif katan 7: To drink. It appears to me that “to drink” is not specific. The Gemara and poskim state generally that two people wait for one another, without mentioning drinking. As Magen Avraham writes immediately afterward, there is no distinction between drinking and another matter, such as cutting bread or waiting until they are given from the loaf, as Darchei Moshe writes in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah. The reason is proper etiquette. Beit Yosef mentioned drinking only as an example. Levush, however, understands that it applies specifically to drinking, lest the drinker resent that the other does not wait for him and his windpipe precede his esophagus. Magen Avraham rejects this and writes that Beit Yosef mentioned drinking only as an example and that the reason is proper etiquette. Nevertheless, Eliyah Rabbah cites Tzemach Tzedek: Two people wait for one another, provided that the delay is not lengthy and that they do not interrupt with mundane conversation.
Tosafos in Bechoros states, etc. There the Gemara says in the name of Rabbi Abba son of Rav Chiya bar Abba: One may urinate in the presence of many because of danger, but one may not drink water in the presence of many. Tosafos writes that Rashi explains that a Torah scholar should be modest in eating and drinking. Yet in Pesachim 86, Rav Huna son of Rav Nachman drank and did not turn his face, and the Gemara explains, “We learned this only concerning a bride,” meaning that the Mishnah states that at the eating of the Pesach offering the bride turns her face and eats because she is embarrassed, but a man need not turn his face. This implies that it is normal to drink before many without turning away. Tosafos answers that this refers during a meal, like the case of a bride. In Hilchos Derech Eretz it is stated that among many one turns his face aside and drinks water. It appears that water is specified because not everyone is accustomed to drink water, only one who is thirsty, whereas other beverages are normally drunk together. The intent of Tosafos in citing Hilchos Derech Eretz requires explanation. If they meant merely to infer that the text specifies water in order to exclude other drinks, they could have inferred the same from the Gemara itself. It is difficult to say that the Gemara mentioned water only incidentally because it had spoken of urination. It therefore appears that Tosafos intended to challenge their own distinction.
That it refers to one sitting during a meal, for otherwise why must he turn his face? He could simply go elsewhere. The teaching should have said only, “One may not drink water before many,” as the Gemara states. It must therefore refer to one sitting at the table during a meal, where he cannot do otherwise except by turning his face. The fact that Tosafos in Pesachim inferred that the case was during a meal from its similarity to the bride, rather than from the wording about turning the face, may be because the comparison to the bride is a stronger inference. Alternatively, they asked him why he did not turn his face, meaning that he failed to do even this minimal act. Not only did he not go elsewhere, he did not even turn his face. This proves that even during a meal one must turn away. Tosafos therefore answers that there is a distinction specifically between water and other beverages, and the incident in Pesachim involved another beverage.
Accordingly, one might say that once we distinguish between water and other beverages, there is no longer a need to distinguish between during and outside a meal. Nevertheless, Magen Avraham logically holds that outside a meal even other beverages require turning aside. Follow the reason: other beverages do not require turning away because people customarily drink them together, as Tosafos writes. This applies specifically during a meal. Piskei Tosafos, siman 95, reads: “They drink water that is not during a meal before many, but other beverages they drink.” The word “not” is necessarily a scribal error. It should read: “They do not drink water that is during a meal before many, but other beverages they do drink.” This is clear.
And a guest, even if her husband is with her, is forbidden. The prohibition against a woman drinking wine is because it arouses desire for immorality. Therefore, if her husband is with her, it is permitted, because she has the prospect of permitted relations. Since we rule below, siman 240, seif 13, that a guest is forbidden in marital relations, she is considered to lack that prospect. If they were assigned a private room, where a guest is permitted marital relations, as stated there, she may drink wine. See Magen Avraham there, seif katan 27. “If she is accustomed to drink wine in her husband's presence” means that if she is accustomed to drink two cups before him, they give her one cup when he is absent. If she is accustomed to drink only one cup before him, they give her no wine at all in his absence. So is the law in Even HaEzer. According to Rif, if she is accustomed to drink much wine before her husband, they give her much wine even in his absence. See Beis Shmuel there. Regarding a nursing woman and wine in her husband's absence, see Chelkas Mechokek and Beis Shmuel.
With other beverages, etc. In this respect there is certainly no distinction between a nursing woman and any other woman. Only regarding wine did Beis Shmuel permit greater leniency for a nursing woman, because it increases her milk.
