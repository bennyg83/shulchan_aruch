But it is permitted to say, etc. This explains the Tur’s citation from the Baraisa that in Jerusalem they would reciprocate meals. I do not understand this permission, for it resembles even more closely a loan of one seah for one seah. In the first case there was initially no language of debt, yet the later arrangement turns it into a debt. All the more so here, where at the first meal he eats expressly as a debt. Even if his intention is that the first meal be a gift and that each later receive a gift from the other, the wording still indicates a debt. It appears to me that the Baraisa should be explained as follows. At the end of chapter Eizehu Neshech, it is stated that Hillel prohibited members of a group who were particular with one another from lending loaves of bread to each other, lest the price rise. Although the halachah does not follow that view, this applies specifically to a loaf of bread. Regarding a meal, however, there may be greater concern for interest, since meals are not equal and people commonly are particular about them. In Jerusalem, however, they were accustomed always to serve large meals, even in lodging places. Therefore, they could properly reciprocate meals, even by saying, “Eat with me,” etc., because they were not particular at all. Thus, in Jerusalem it was permitted in every manner.
