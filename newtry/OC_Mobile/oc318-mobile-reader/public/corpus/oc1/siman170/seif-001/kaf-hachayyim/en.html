1) [Seif 1] One may not converse during a meal, etc. This applies even to words of Torah. Taanis 5b; Magen Avraham, paragraph 1; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Maaseh Rokeach on the Rambam, chapter 7 of Hilchos Berachos, law 6; Darkei Moshe in Hagahos HaTur, paragraph 2; Beis Menuchah, paragraph 9; Yafeh LaLev, paragraph 1. One may, however, speak in order to prevent a prohibition. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 1. Even between one dish and another, one may not speak as long as he intends to eat more. Proof may be brought from simanim 174 and 175 regarding the blessing over wine and HaTov VeHaMeitiv: one person cannot discharge another until he says, 'With your permission, my masters,' so that they clear their throats of food, etc. One might nevertheless say that this applied specifically to them, for they ate while reclining upon the left side. The esophagus was then above and the windpipe, through which one speaks, below. When one speaks, the windpipe opens, and the food above it that should enter the esophagus might fall into the windpipe and endanger him. This does not apply nowadays, when we eat while sitting upright, so there is not as much cause for concern. For this reason, I have not seen people careful about it. Perishah, paragraph 1; Eliyah Rabbah there; Birkei Yosef, paragraph 1; Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 1. Birkei Yosef writes there that according to this, on the sanctified night of the Pesach festival, when one reclines, he must be especially careful to uphold the words of the Sages. Nevertheless, in Shiyurei Berachah there he retracts, because nowadays true reclining is absent, as stated above in siman 167, seif 11. Despite this, the authorities wrote that one may not converse during a meal, implying that this applies even to us, who do not practice reclining. He concludes that people are not careful about it, as Perishah writes; see there. Zechor LeAvraham, under samech, and Beis Menuchah, paragraph 10, cite this. Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 1, writes that the reason is that the public has become accustomed to it, and therefore one should not distinguish between Pesach nights and the rest of the year; see there. Pri Megadim in Eshel Avraham there, however, disagrees and writes that even nowadays, when people do not recline upon the left, one may not converse; see there. Kitzur Shulchan Aruch, siman 42, paragraph 5, writes the same. See also our words above in siman 157, paragraph 16, citing the Zohar; see there.
2) There: One may not converse, etc. Any table upon which words of Torah were not spoken is considered as though its diners ate from sacrifices to the dead. Pirkei Avos, chapter 3; Magen Avraham there. See our words above in siman 157, paragraphs 17, 18, and 19, regarding the order of study that should be recited at the table; see there.
2) Whoever prolongs his time at the table has his days and years prolonged. This is because a poor person might come and he will give him food. Berachos 54b and 55a; Zohar, parashas Eikev, 273b; Or Tzaddikim by R' Meir Poppers, of blessed memory, siman 23, paragraph 8. He writes there that if no poor person comes, or if you are traveling, set aside the poor person's portion. When you later give it, give it to someone who has the fragrance of Torah and knows how to recite Birkas HaMazon.
3) There: Even one who sneezes, etc. Regarding the practice of saying upon sneezing, 'I have hoped for Your salvation,' etc., pious men and people of deeds are careful not to say it. When one sneezes he is preoccupied with the sneeze and cannot properly direct his intent toward the Name, and the punishment of one who fails to direct his intent toward the Name is severe, as stated above in siman 5; see there. They are therefore careful not to say it, because refraining from action is preferable. If, however, he says, 'I have hoped for Your salvation, Hashem,' without pronouncing the Name Havayah, it is permitted.
4) There: He washes the one hand with which he rubbed, etc. This means that one must rub away the droplets of urine so that he does not appear to have a severed urinary organ, as stated above in siman 3, paragraph 37. This is unnecessary only when he stands in a high place and urinates, or urinates into loose earth, as stated in the Shulchan Aruch there, seif 13. This is the meaning of the gloss nearby, 'If he did not rub,' etc. Taz, paragraph 1, writes the same. See our words there, paragraph 38, and in siman 4, paragraph 86.
5) There: He washes one hand, etc. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, writes that he must recite Al Netilas Yadayim, as stated at the end of siman 164, contrary to Agudah; see there. We have already written above in siman 164, paragraphs 16 and 17, that since there is a dispute, he should not recite Al Netilas Yadayim again, because in cases of doubt concerning blessings we rule leniently; see there. If he intends only to drink and not to eat, however, all agree that no blessing is required, for a blessing is not required merely because of the concern that he might eat. Magen Avraham and Machatzis HaShekel, paragraph 2; Beis Menuchah, paragraph 11. See below, paragraph 8.
6) There, gloss: If he did not rub, he does not wash, etc. Levush writes that this applies specifically to eating, but for drinking he must wash, lest people suspect that he rubbed and did not wash. Magen Avraham, paragraph 3, cites this and writes that it is unconvincing, for according to this, even one who went outside and did not urinate would have to wash because of suspicion. Rather, the meaning is that someone might see him rub but not see him wash, and suspect him; when he did not rub, this concern does not apply. He writes that Bach rules likewise; see there. Perishah, paragraph 2, similarly rejects the words of Levush; see there. Beis Menuchah there. This is the strict law, but for cleanliness one must wash even if he did not rub, as stated above in siman 7, seif 2, in our words there, and in siman 164, paragraph 13; see there. The practice is likewise to wash even without rubbing, for cleanliness. This is unlike siman 158, seif 6, which concerns ordinary hands, whereas here he urinated. Understand this distinction.
7) There, gloss: He does not wash at all, etc. This applies when he did not enter a privy. If he entered a privy, he must wash even though he did not relieve himself, as stated above in siman 4, paragraph 65; see there.
8) There: This applies when he returns to drink, etc. Rashi explains that he does not intend to eat any more but entered only to drink, for they customarily remained a long time drinking after the meal. Nevertheless, he must wash, lest he place a piece of bread into his mouth. Rabbeinu Yonah similarly writes at the end of the chapter Eilu Devarim that washing is required because their practice while drinking was to eat a little; otherwise, washing the hands would not be required merely for drinking. Beis Yosef; Taz, paragraph 2. Accordingly, even if they drank without a meal, washing would seemingly be required because their practice was to eat a little while drinking. Rema, of blessed memory, however, writes in Darkei Moshe, paragraph 1, that Rashi's explanation in the chapter Amar Lahem HaMemuneh, Yoma 30a, somewhat implies that washing is unnecessary for drinking even when he puts a piece of bread into his mouth, and is required only for drinking after the meal but before Birkas HaMazon; see there. This is what he writes here in the gloss at the end of this seif, 'The requirement of washing,' etc., in accordance with his explanation in Darkei Moshe. Understand this. Beis Yosef implies, according to Rabbeinu Yonah, that for us, who do not customarily eat bread while drinking, washing is unnecessary. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2, writes the same, although he notes that Levush does not imply this; see there. We have already written above in paragraph 6 that this is the strict law, but for cleanliness one must wash even if he did not rub; see there.
9) There: This applies when he returns to drink, etc. One who is known to be extremely sensitive washes outside even if he returns to drink, for everyone knows that his disposition cannot tolerate doing otherwise. So is stated in the Gemara there. Toras HaBayis, folio 59, writes the same, cited by Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2, and Mishbetzos Zahav. It appears that the same applies to us, who customarily wash in every case for cleanliness, as written in the preceding paragraph. We are comparable to one who is sensitive and wash outside, since everyone knows that washing is customary. Indeed, the present universal practice is to wash outside, because everyone knows of this practice and there is no suspicion.
10) There: If he spoke with another and remained away, etc. Rashi explains that since he remained away for one or two hours, he diverted his attention from the meal and was not careful to guard his hands, which are active. Bach writes the same; Ateres Zekeinim, paragraph 1. Birchas Avraham, section 4, siman 127, writes that after one hour it is possible he was careful not to soil or render his hands impure, but over two hours this is generally ineffective, since the ordinary practice is for active hands to touch an unclean place within two hours. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, paragraph 1, and Olas Tamid, paragraph 2, cite this. Olas Tamid writes, however, that this is not implied by the author's words. He means that the author agrees with Rashi, of blessed memory, that we are concerned even after one hour. One should rule stringently.
11) There: He washes both hands, etc. Tosafos in the chapter Arvei Pesachim and Rosh there write regarding the passage in Yoma that since washing is required, the blessing of HaMotzi is likewise required. The Tur's words, however, indicate that only washing is required, not HaMotzi, and the Rambam's words in chapter 7 of Hilchos Berachos imply the same. Knesses HaGedolah there, paragraph 2. Magen Avraham, paragraph 4, likewise writes that HaMotzi is unnecessary, as Rema states in siman 178, seif 2. Beis Menuchah, paragraph 11.
12) There: If he remained away, he washes both hands, etc. The author's words imply that one who spoke with another and remained away may wash outside, since only one who urinated, which is repulsive to the group, must wash before them. The wording of the Baraisa and the Rambam in chapter 7 of Hilchos Berachos, however, implies that in every case he must wash before them, and this is the primary ruling. Pri Chadash, paragraph 1. Magen Avraham, paragraph 5, nevertheless writes that even according to Maran, of blessed memory, one who remained away must wash before them, even when returning to eat; see there. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 5. Levush writes the same; Derech HaChaim, paragraph 2; Beis Menuchah there.
13) There: He washes both hands because he diverted his attention. The work Tzeror HaChaim writes: I heard from my teacher, R' Peretz, that if one holds bread in his hand, he need not wash his hands, because his attention remains upon it and he will not divert his attention. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 2, in the name of his grandfather, R' Avraham Azulai, of blessed memory, in his handwritten glosses; Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 3; Zechor LeAvraham, under samech.
14) There, gloss: For we are concerned that he might eat, etc. Nevertheless, he washes inside, since drinking is his primary intent and we are concerned that people will say he did not pay attention to washing his hands. Magen Avraham, paragraph 6. See above, paragraph 9.
15) There, gloss: Otherwise, washing is unnecessary for drinking. Washing is unnecessary specifically because of drinking, but washing is required for the purpose of reciting the blessing Asher Yatzar or a blessing over the drink, as stated above in siman 4, seif 18, and in our words there, paragraph 90; see there. Here the case necessarily concerns one who rubbed, for if he did not rub, Rema, of blessed memory, already wrote nearby that washing is unnecessary even during the meal; see there. See above, paragraph 8, and understand this.
