One should not converse, etc. This includes even words of Torah. The Perishah writes that it appears one should not converse even between one dish and another as long as he still intends to eat. His proof is from siman 177 regarding the blessings on wine and HaTov VeHaMeitiv, where one person does not discharge another's obligation even if he says 'Savri' and causes his companions to empty their throats from food, because we fear that one may nevertheless continue eating, answer Amen, and endanger himself. Therefore the Sages were concerned in every case. However, one can say this applied specifically in their times when people ate reclining on the left side. Then the esophagus was above and the windpipe below, and when a person spoke the windpipe opened and food situated above it, intended for the esophagus, could fall into the windpipe and endanger him. Nowadays we eat sitting upright, so there is less concern. This may explain why I have not seen people careful about this, though I have not found this distinction stated anywhere. See above at the end of siman 174, where we rule that when one says 'Savri' he can discharge the others. If so, here too it is certainly permitted to speak between dishes.
And this applies to drinking, etc. Torat HaBayit HaKatzar, folio 59, writes that a person known to be exceptionally fastidious should wash outside even before drinking, because everyone knows he cannot tolerate doing otherwise. According to Rabbenu Yonah's statement that they customarily ate while drinking, and according to the Taz in the name of Rashi, nowadays there should be no need to wash at all merely for drinking. The Levush, however, does not imply this.
[Levush] And it appears to me, etc. I say that not only do the Beit Yosef and Rama rule this way in the name of Hagahot Ashiri, but I also saw the same ruling in the Semag, folio 113, Semak, Agudah in the first chapter of Yoma and in Elu Devarim, and Shiltei Gibborim. It is strained to say that all these sources speak only where he is eating alone. It therefore seems that the distinction is this: where he is actually obligated to wash, we require him to wash inside because of suspicion. Where he has no obligation to wash at all, we do not require washing merely to avoid suspicion. I later saw that the Perishah also disagrees with the Levush and writes that the poskim refer to a case where others saw him rub or clean himself. According to that, if they did not see him do so there is no requirement to wash inside, but this is strained and not implied by the plain wording of the poskim. Magen Avraham writes that we are concerned perhaps someone saw him rub himself but did not see him wash and will suspect him. This too is strained. I am also surprised at Olat Tamid, who questions the Rama's unqualified statement that if he did not rub he need not wash, claiming that this is not the Shulchan Aruch's view because the Shulchan Aruch gives the reason for washing before eating that people know he is fastidious, and does not say it is to avoid suspicion that he rubbed without washing. Olat Tamid infers that even without rubbing he needs washing. This is surprising, because the reason that people know he is fastidious is an explicit Gemara in Yoma 30, so the same question would apply to all the poskim cited above. Moreover, perhaps that is precisely the meaning: people know he is fastidious, so necessarily either he washed or he did not rub. Olat Tamid's view also raises the question why the same reasoning would not apply to drinking. Rather, there is no difficulty. Since it is a mitzvah to rub, as explained in the Gemara there, people will ordinarily suspect that he did so. The Shulchan Aruch's own language proves this, since it says, 'he washes the one hand with which he rubbed,' implying that without rubbing no washing is required.
[4] He washes both, etc. Perishah writes that although we rule that leaving the meal does not require washing the hands and reciting HaMotzi anew, as the Tur writes in siman 174 and in Hilchos Sukkah, nevertheless the Tur cites this passage in order to derive another rule from it regarding a definite complete diversion of attention. This is strained. In my humble opinion, the Tur distinguishes and holds that when one left for an extended period, washing without a blessing is nevertheless required. See Bircas Avraham, page 94. This is likewise implied by Agudah, first chapter of Yoma, unlike Olas Tamid.
[5] [Levush] And regarding this too they said, etc. I say that were it not for the words of the great poskim, I would explain the Gemara in Yoma 30a as referring only to the latter case, where he left for an extended period. After urinating, however, there is no suspicion, for we may assume that he urinated in a manner that did not require rubbing. A proof is the Gemara’s wording: “Rather, he enters, sits in his place, and washes both hands,” which implies that it refers to one who left for an extended period, since after urinating one hand would suffice. However, Rif and Rosh do not quote the words “and washes both hands,” and it appears that these words were absent from their text. As a matter of law, although my explanation appears correct, it is not sufficient to rely upon against all the great poskim. I found an even greater novelty in Toras HaBayis HaAroch and Mishmeres HaBayis, page 157, that it is likely that even one who left for an extended period must wash inside, even before eating, because of suspicion, for what fastidiousness is involved when he merely left to speak? However, Toras HaBayis HaKatzar there rules this way regarding one who left for an extended period and does not mention the law of urination at all, as though the Gemara does not refer to urination, in accordance with my explanation. This requires extensive study.
[6] [Levush] And he may choke, etc. Malbushei Yom Tov writes: In my opinion, it is prohibited even without this concern, as Rama writes in Choshen Mishpat, siman 176 and siman 72. This is somewhat difficult, for if so, it should be prohibited even with three people, since Rama’s wording is that when members of a group eat together and one eats a great deal, the others may remove him. The Levush’s reason is also strained, and Magen Avraham likewise writes that “drinking” is not meant specifically. It appears that the Levush derived his view from Beit Yosef, who writes: “It appears to me that the meaning is that when one person drinks, the other waits for him,” etc. The Levush understood that the rule applies specifically when he drinks, because of danger. But he overlooked the words of Rabbeinu Yonah in the chapter Sheloshah SheAchlu: “While one person drinks, cuts bread, or waits to receive a piece from the one who recited HaMotzi, his fellow should wait for him because of proper conduct. Here the teaching concerns only proper conduct. It does not address the requirement to leave him his share when he interrupts for other matters and delays for a long time.” Beit Yosef too requires explanation for writing, “It appears to me,” without mentioning Rabbeinu Yonah’s words. Tzeidah LaDerech, page 59, writes that two people wait for one another, provided that the delay is not prolonged and that he does not interrupt with idle conversation.
