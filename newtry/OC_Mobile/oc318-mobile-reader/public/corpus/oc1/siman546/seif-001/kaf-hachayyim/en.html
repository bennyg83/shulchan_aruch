1) [Se'if 1] Marriages are not held during the festival, etc. The reasons are that one joy may not be mixed with another, that holding a marriage entails exertion during the festival, and that otherwise everyone might wait to marry until the festival, when a meal is already being made, thereby neglecting the mitzvah to be fruitful and multiply. All this is stated in Moed Katan 8b. It is clear there that marriage is forbidden even without a meal. Magen Avraham, subsection 1. Ritva likewise writes in his novellae to the Talmud there, page 18b, that marriage is a joy even without a meal, while the Torah states, 'You shall rejoice in your festival,' and not with your wife. See there. Birkei Yosef, subsection 1, cites this and writes that the Talmud there likewise proves that marriage is forbidden even without a meal. See there. Tosafos, Kesubos 47a, s.v. demasar, according to one resolution, write that marriage without a meal is permitted because it is not considered so great a joy. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2, explains that Tosafos speaks under Torah law, but concedes that marriage alone is forbidden rabbinically, as Maharsha writes there. See there. Birkei Yosef there, Bigdei Yesha, and Chemed Moshe, subsection 1, write likewise. Maamar Mordechai, subsection 1, also proves from the Talmud and halachic authorities that marriage without a meal is forbidden. See there. Chayei Adam, rule 117, subsection 1; Mikraei Kodesh, siman 59, subsection 1; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 1, write likewise.
2) Even if the bride waived and relinquished her joy, it is forbidden. Gan HaMelech, siman 60; Yad Aharon, Hagahos Beis Yosef.
3) There. Marriages are not held during the festival, etc. The Talmud discusses the reason for the prohibition at length and derives from a verse that one joy may not be mixed with another. Hagahos Sefer Mitzvos Katan writes: 'From here, some take care not to hold two chuppahs on one day. They also bring a proof from the Yerushalmi, which derives from a verse that one joy may not be mixed with another, based on Lavan's statement to Yaakov, 'Complete the week of this one, etc.' This proof is incorrect. There the two joys belonged to one person, for Yaakov married two women. Likewise, regarding the festival, the joy of the woman he marries and the joy of the festival both belong to him. Mixing joys is therefore applicable. Regarding two chuppahs held for two women and two men, however, mixing one joy with another is not applicable. My teacher, Rabbi Yechiel, therefore had the custom to hold the chuppah of an orphaned young man or woman together with the chuppah of one of his children, all on one day. Regarding two sisters, however, Sefer Chassidim writes that their chuppahs should not be held on one day. Some editions state that they should not be held even during the same week, and the custom should not be changed.' These are the words cited by Bach and Taz, subsection 1. Tzavaas Rabbi Yehudah HaChassid, subsection 36, however, writes that even two chuppahs for two men and two women should not be held in the same week, because some unfavorable occurrence may befall them. See there. Sefer Chassidim, siman 477, writes likewise. Yad Aharon, Hagahos Beis Yosef, cites this. See Even HaEzer, siman 62, se'if 2.
4) There. It is permitted to betroth, etc. This is permitted lest another person precede him through prayer. Moed Katan 18b. According to this reason, certainly one may write engagement conditions during Chol HaMoed, as is customary among us. The customary meal after writing the conditions, however, requires further study. Perhaps it takes the place of a betrothal meal and is forbidden, as stated shortly. Alternatively, perhaps this meal does not resemble a betrothal meal held after kiddushin, because only then does the principle that one joy may not be mixed with another apply. It appears to me that it is forbidden. Taz, subsection 2. See there. Yad Aharon, Hagahos HaTur, cites him. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 3, however, writes that Rivash, siman 260, implies that nowadays, when kiddushin is not performed before the chuppah, the first meal after the conditions may be eaten. He writes that Taz's ruling is unpersuasive. See there. Mor Uketziah likewise disagrees with Taz. This also appears to be the view of Chayei Adam, rule 117, subsection 1. Maamar Mordechai, subsection 2, however, although questioning the aforementioned ruling of Taz, concludes that Taz's view is more reasonable. Mishbetzos Zahav, subsection 2, likewise discusses both sides but concludes that one should be stringent regarding a meal held when the conditions are written, since Taz is stringent in every case. See there. There is certainly no reason to be stringent regarding the custom of eating sweets after writing the conditions, as Maamar Mordechai writes there. Mishnah Berurah, subsection 2, writes likewise. See also the following subsection.
5) There. At the time of betrothal. This means that the betrothal should not be performed together with a meal. If a meal is held after the betrothal, it is permitted, even on the same day. Magen Avraham, subsection 2; Levushei Serad. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 3, likewise writes that the prohibition applies only 'at the time of betrothal,' meaning betrothal together with a meal. Taz, subsection 2, however, holds that even if the match was arranged and the conditions were written before the festival, the first meal after writing the conditions may not be held in the bride's house during the festival. See there. Mishnah Berurah in Shaar HaTziyun, subsection 5, however, writes that even a full meal is forbidden specifically when held at the time the conditions are written. If it is not held at that time, there is no reason to be stringent, unlike Taz. Regarding betrothal, Mishnah Berurah, subsection 4, writes that when the betrothal is performed on one day and the meal on another, it is certainly permitted. This is allowed even regarding marriage, as stated below, se'if 3. See there. See below, subsection 14.
