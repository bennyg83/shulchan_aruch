Se'if 1. All four species are invalid if robbed or stolen, etc. Note: in Responsa Mahari Mintz he was uncertain about one from whom a good esrog was stolen, whether the thief can discharge himself with an esrog inferior to it or not. See Responsa Chacham Tzvi and Mishneh LaMelech, what they wrote about this. Now at first glance it seemed possible to bring proof from what is written in Kesubos 99, where it asks about one who said to an agent, "Buy me a kor," and he went and bought him a half-kor, whether he is considered to have transgressed his instructions or to have added to his instructions. It challenges there from the Mishnah in Meilah: if he gave him a gold dinar to buy him a cloak, and he bought with three parts a cloak and with three parts a tallis; see there. This matter is astonishing, for one who examines the Mishnah there sees that it first taught a case where he told him to buy an esrog for a dinar, and he went and bought for half a dinar an esrog and for half a dinar a pomegranate, and both committed meilah. Why did it not challenge from that? Why did it leave the first clause and challenge from the latter clause? It is true that Tosafos in Meilah there wrote on the Mishnah that just as the Talmud explains the reason regarding the cloak, the same applies here. But the matter is astonishing: why did the Talmud in Kesubos point this challenge to the cloak and not to the first clause of the esrog? Therefore it appears that from the esrog there would be no proof, for one can say that it is speaking where he needs it only for the mitzvah. Therefore, for the mitzvah, he is not particular between a large esrog and a small one, so it is not considered a deviation from the owner's intent. But with an optional matter, it is properly considered a deviation. Therefore it proves from a cloak, which is optional. If so, it is proven that for a mitzvah one is not particular between large and small; and if so, the same applies where one stole an esrog: one may say that he can discharge himself with a small one. It is true that one can distinguish, for with robbery, since it has monetary value for sale, if he gives one smaller than it he has caused him monetary loss, as the Mishneh LaMelech and Chacham Tzvi wrote to reject the proofs of Mahari Mintz; see there. But in Meilah, since he returns the remaining money, and there is no monetary concern, only the body of the esrog, one can properly say that small and large are the same to him. In any event, there will be a practical difference where the robber wants to pay him money, and the robbed party wants him to give him only an esrog. Now on the festival we maintain that returning money does not help, but he must return the body of the esrog; and likewise with a gift on condition to return, where we maintain that on the festival returning money does not help, only the body of the esrog. Nevertheless, it appears that if he returns only a small esrog, he may return the excess money, and the owner cannot say that he specifically prefers to fulfill his mitzvah with a large esrog. For this is proven in the Talmud, that this is not a particularity, and it is sufficient for him to fulfill the mitzvah with a small esrog. From the side of the mitzvah, there is no particularity between small and large, and from the monetary side he can return the excess money. So it appears correct and clear in my humble opinion. See my work on Yoreh De'ah, Mahadura Tinyana, siman 144, in the laws of a Torah scroll, in a responsum to the province of Wallachia, what I wrote that is relevant to this law; see there and examine it. See Magen Avraham subparagraph 3, what he wrote. So too it appears in the Talmud, which says that it is not despair, etc. And see my work on Choshen Mishpat, Mahadura Tinyana, siman 71, in Choshen Mishpat siman 128, where we wrote to reject the proof: even according to those who hold it is valid for others, that means for one for whom the transgression was not performed; but for one for whom the transgression was performed, it is certainly invalid, as with one who nullifies a prohibition; see there. Now the view of the Yereim, brought in Magen Avraham, is known: even if robbing a gentile is permitted, nevertheless it is not called "for yourselves," and I already elaborated on this above. The Rav Rabbi Chaim Moshe, Av Beis Din of Praszka, asked me from tractate Bechoros 13b, where it asks according to the one who says robbery of a gentile is permitted: why do I need a verse to exclude overcharge? If robbery is permitted, all the more so overcharge. What does it ask? Perhaps it teaches us that with overcharge too it is considered "for yourselves." I answered that there is no difficulty at all, since it says there: "to your fellow" you return overcharge, and it does not say that you return the acquisition itself. It is proven that it is not speaking of an actual voided sale, but of a case where the sale takes effect, only it is a sale with a sixth overcharge and he returns the overcharge money. If so, this is fine. Granted, with a specific item such as an esrog or lulav, it is relevant to say that it is not "for yourselves." But with overcharge money, granted he is obligated to return it, nevertheless as long as he does not return it, it is not extant; how can it be relevant to say about something not extant that it is not "for yourselves"? Especially here, since the money of a Jew, as long as it has not reached the gentile's hand, has always been the Jew's, and automatically it is "for yourselves." How can it be relevant here to say that it is not "for yourselves"? Therefore his difficulty is no difficulty at all; examine it. Now Tosafos in Sukkah asked: why do we need a verse regarding sukkah, "for yourself," to exclude a stolen one? Let it be derived because it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. I already wrote above several ways to answer their difficulty. However, in my opinion I do not understand their difficulty. Granted, at the beginning of chapter Lulav HaGazul, where it is speaking of the second day of Yom Tov, since that is only a rabbinic mitzvah and it is not relevant to say that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment, therefore since in any event it is a transgression, it is again relevant to say that it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. But with sukkah, which is Torah law, if not for the verse we would have said that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment and it is not a transgression; only the verse revealed that a stolen one is invalid. What, then, was their difficulty? In this one may say that their difficulty is that with this it is not relevant to say a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment to steal another's money, for even with preservation of life, which overrides the whole Torah, nevertheless it is forbidden to save oneself with another's money. If so, all the more so a single positive commandment does not override taking another's money. It is an a fortiori argument: if preservation of life, which overrides the whole Torah, is pushed aside before another's money, all the more so a positive commandment, which is pushed aside before preservation of life, which itself is pushed aside before another's money. However, what they asked in Pesachim regarding matzah of tevel, why a verse is needed, let it be derived because it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, there one can properly say that if not for the verse, a positive commandment would override a negative commandment. With lulav too one can say this, with a practical difference regarding robbing a gentile. In my work Sefer HaChayim, we settled their difficulty according to those who hold robbing a gentile is permitted, only it is not called "for yourselves." But now I saw that even according to those who hold it is forbidden, it is still fine. For what is forbidden to save oneself with another's money is regarding a Jew, since with his body it is forbidden to save himself because of "who says your blood is redder," etc.; therefore the same applies to his money, for "damim" has two meanings. But with a gentile, since it is certainly permitted to save oneself with the life of the gentile, for "who says your blood is redder" does not apply here, if so it is also permitted to save oneself with his money. Therefore, granted that otherwise it is forbidden to rob, nevertheless one can say here that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment. Therefore one needs to exclude robbery from a gentile, which is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, since a positive commandment would override the negative commandment and there would be no transgression. Examine this well. Now regarding what is explained in the Shulchan Aruch, that all four species are invalid if robbed or stolen, whether before despair or after despair, etc. Note: if he bought the esrog with money and did not pay him, whether this is robbery. According to the view of the Chelkas Mechokek, this is robbery; see Even HaEzer siman 120 and what I wrote in my novellae there. Now it appears to bring proof from the Midrash in parashas Emor, as follows: "And you shall take for yourselves on the first day"; Rabbi Chiya taught: "and you shall take" means by purchase and not by robbery; "for yourselves" means from your own, to exclude the stolen, end quote. This is astonishing: why are both needed? Therefore it appears that the intent is that what it says "by purchase and not by robbery" excludes that initially it should not come into his hand by way of robbery, but only by way of purchase. Afterward it teaches us that even if it was in his hand by way of purchase, if afterward he does not pay him, this too is a way of robbery. This is not excluded from the language "and you shall take," for at first it came permissibly into his hand by way of purchase. Therefore it excludes from the language "for yourselves," that in any event now that he does not pay, it is robbery and not yours, and he has not fulfilled. However, we do not derive law from Midrash; one can also say as I wrote long ago in my commentary in my novellae to Aggadah there. But it appears to bring proof for this from the Talmud in Sukkah 31b, in the incident of that old woman who was crying out: the Exilarch and all the Rabbis are sitting in a stolen sukkah. Rav Nachman said: this is a screamer; she has only the value of the wood. This is difficult: why did he still not find merit for her? Perhaps she cried out because they had not even paid her money. If it is possible that the Exilarch sat in a stolen sukkah, it is also possible that they did not take account of her cry and did not even pay her money. If so, she cried out properly, for if they did not pay her, it is robbery retroactively in their hands. Why did Rav Nachman not take account of her to ask at least whether they paid or wanted to pay her or not? Rashi sensed this, for he explained that she cried about the wood itself; but this is forced, and the Talmud does not imply so. Rashi follows his own approach, for I proved in his name that he holds such a case is robbery; see what I wrote about this in my novellae to Even HaEzer. Therefore he was forced to explain that her cry meant about the wood. But according to what I wrote, that it is not robbery, there is no need to explain this way; examine it. Rather, this is only because the Rabbis enacted that the body of the wood belongs to the robbed party only insofar as he must pay money, so even if he does not pay, it is still his, like an actual sale. If so, Rav Nachman properly said: since she is crying that the Exilarch and all the Rabbis were sitting in a stolen sukkah, he properly said that she is a screamer and noisy, since she has only the value of the wood. If so, even if they did not pay her, it is still not stolen. Therefore she was crying improperly that they were sitting in a stolen sukkah, and it is proven that she is noisy. If so, if this is true with a rabbinic acquisition, since they transferred ownership to him on the condition that he pay, even if he does not pay it is not robbery in his hand, and this is an a fortiori argument: if where it came into his hand through prohibition, since they transferred ownership to him it is his even if he does not pay, all the more so where it was with his knowledge and came into his hand permissibly, it is his even if he does not pay, as it appears to me. Nevertheless, practical law still requires analysis. Examine this well.
