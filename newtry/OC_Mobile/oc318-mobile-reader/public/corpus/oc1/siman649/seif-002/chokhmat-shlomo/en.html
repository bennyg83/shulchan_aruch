(There, se'if 2 in the gloss) One who is prohibited by vow from benefiting from his fellow's lulav, or from his own lulav, does not fulfill with it on the first day, for it is not "for yourselves." Note: here it is explained that the Rashba holds that items forbidden for benefit cannot be acquired. So too he wrote regarding a woman who vowed not to benefit from someone and he betrothed her, etc. Now one learned person asked me from what we say in Bava Kamma 67b: and if you would think despair acquires, once the time of prohibition arrives, he certainly despairs, etc. What does it ask? It has become prohibited, and an item forbidden for benefit cannot be acquired. I answered him that it appears there is no difficulty at all, for two reasons. One reason is that there is a distinction between the beginning of acquisition and the completion of acquisition. The Rashba holds that items forbidden for benefit cannot be acquired only regarding something that until now was not in his domain at all, and now we want to bring it into his domain; in such a case, when it is forbidden for benefit, it does not become in his domain. But when it is already in his domain, such as with a robber, where previously too it was in his domain regarding liability for accidents, and despair acts only to complete the acquisition, then even when it is forbidden for benefit it also completes the acquisition. Know this, for in any case the view of the Rivash is that one can acquire items forbidden for benefit, and therefore it is preferable to minimize the dispute. One may say that even the Rashba said his rule only about something that was not yet in his domain at all; but regarding what was already in his domain, to complete the acquisition he agrees with the Rivash. A distinction similar to this is proven according to the one who says mere despair acquires. It is difficult: we maintain regarding a lost object that if it came into his hand before despair, he does not acquire it because it came into his hand through prohibition; why then does a robber acquire through despair, even though it came into his hand through prohibition? Perforce the reason is that with a lost object it was not in his domain at all from the beginning until now, and therefore he does not acquire it since it came into his hand through prohibition. But with a robber, since it was already somewhat in his domain regarding liability for accidents, the acquisition is completed even though it came into his hand through prohibition. If so, one can say this distinction in our case as well regarding items forbidden for benefit. Another reason appears, and this is the main one in my eyes: in any case, all chametz is not in a person's domain, yet the verse made it as though it is in his domain. If so, we see that regarding chametz the Torah said that the prohibition of benefit does not remove his acquisition. Only with the chametz of others does he not transgress over it, because the Torah did not make it as if it were in his domain except where, were it not for the prohibition, it would be in his domain, and only because of the prohibition of chametz it would not be in his domain. In such a case the Torah said that the prohibition of chametz does not remove his acquisition, but it is judged as though it were not chametz at all and remains in his domain. But if, even without the prohibition of chametz, it would not be in his domain, then the Torah did not place it in his domain. Therefore here too, since he has already despaired, granted that he despairs only because of its prohibition, nevertheless, ultimately now he has despaired. Since he has despaired, if there were no prohibition of benefit here, the robber would acquire it; only because it is forbidden for benefit would we say he did not acquire it. In this case we again say that the Torah transferred ownership to him against his will, for the verse made it as though it were in his domain. Therefore wherever, were it not for the prohibition of chametz, it would be his, the prohibition of chametz does not remove the acquisition; in this the verse revealed that it made it as though it were in his domain, as though it were not chametz. To this the Talmud answers that there it is different, because this one despairs and this one does not want to acquire. If so, granted that his unwillingness to acquire is because of the prohibition, nevertheless we follow the present situation, and now he does not want its acquisition. In such a case, even without its prohibition he would not acquire it if he does not want to acquire it; and regarding chametz itself too, his disclosure that he does not want it helps, and the Torah did not make it in his domain in such a case. Therefore here too he did not acquire it. This is fine and correct; examine it well. Now regarding the dispute among the authorities whether items forbidden for benefit are called "for yourselves" or not, it appears to me there is clear proof that items forbidden for benefit are called "for yourselves" from what is stated in Sanhedrin, chapter Chelek, 112b: the Rabbis taught, etc.; Rabbi Shimon says: "your animal," but not the animal of a firstborn or tithe. In what case? If we say they are unblemished, they are spoil of Heaven. Rather, they are blemished animals; then they are its spoil. This is difficult to me: what does it ask? With a blemished firstborn that was found to be a tereifah, it requires burial and is forbidden for benefit, and the same applies to blemished tithe. If so, what is the question? Let us say it refers to blemished animals that were tereifah, and therefore they are not spoil of Heaven, since they are blemished, and they are also not its spoil, since they are forbidden for benefit and require burial. Since we maintain that all those that are burned may not be buried, because burning is more severe, one might have thought that it requires burning like all movable property of an idolatrous city. Therefore it teaches that it does not require burning. And although the hide belongs to the kohanim or to the owners, nevertheless it is not considered its spoil, like dough half belonging to the idolatrous city and half to another city, where we say earlier there that half is permitted and half forbidden, because it is possible to divide it while kneaded. So too here: not only where it has already been slaughtered, where it can be divided; even while it is alive, there regarding an animal half belonging to another city, Rashi explained that it cannot be divided because half of it is like slaughtered, etc., and an animal is not rendered fit with one sign. If so, that is fine regarding a permitted animal that stands to be eaten. But here, where in any case it is forbidden for benefit and stands only for burial, in this it is possible to divide it. Therefore the meat would not be its spoil, since it is forbidden for benefit, and it would also not be spoil of Heaven. What, then, is the challenge? Perforce, items forbidden for benefit are called "for yourselves" and are called its spoil, and it properly asks that they are its spoil. This is a clear proof in my humble opinion; examine it well.
