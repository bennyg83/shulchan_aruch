Gardens of the young attendants, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote that here the garden was never donated for idolatry. According to the Rama there in Yoreh De'ah siman 143:3, it must at least be speaking where the garden does not stand in the non-Jew's idolatrous courtyard. Likewise the Rama's rule, that even a tree planted before idolatry is permitted, refers here to where their attendants or non-Jews planted it for the attendants' own benefit, to eat its fruits, not where they planted it for the adjacent idol. If they planted it for idolatry and the fruits belong to its attendants, even according to the Rama in siman 143 it would be forbidden for a Jew to buy from it all year, because he benefits their priests. This resolves what Sha'ar HaMelekh in Hilkhot Lulav asked, and what the Gra also hints here, that the Shulchan Aruch contradicts itself: in Yoreh De'ah siman 142:13, regarding non-fruit trees planted before idolatry, he rules that they are included in the adornments of idolatry, while here he permits taking from them for a mitzvah. Here it refers to where they planted it for themselves. I found similarly in Levush: gardens of idolatrous attendants may be used to take lulav or other species for a mitzvah, because they are guarded there for their own benefit and not for idolatry; even a tree planted before idolatry, as long as they do not worship the tree, was planted for their own benefit. That means that presumptively we say they planted it for themselves, unless we know it was planted as an adornment for idolatry and only the fruits belong to their priests; then it is called an idolatrous adornment, one may not benefit from it, and on the first day one does not fulfill with it because it is not called "yours," and even on the second day it is lechatchilah forbidden.
I found that Nahar Shalom also asked this question from Yoreh De'ah siman 142:13 and answered differently, though similar to what we wrote: there, where it speaks of non-fruit trees, we must assume they were planted as adornment for idolatry, whereas here, where it speaks of fruit trees, it is better to assume they were planted for their fruit, as Levush wrote.
It further appears to me to answer somewhat differently: here we speak of the four species, regarding which it is stated above that the non-Jew himself must cut them. According to Rabbenu Yerucham cited in Darkhei Moshe, that itself counts as nullification, so the prohibition of idolatrous adornment is nullified. Only the concern that something for Heaven should be treated more stringently lechatchilah remains; for that we are lenient and say they planted it for themselves. There, however, the discussion is the basic law whether it is included among idolatrous adornments, with the practical consequence that a Jew may not cut it himself.
For the mitzvah. See Mishnah Berurah, where I cited Sefer Chasidim that nevertheless, if other species are available, he should take the others. Peri Megadim writes that perhaps gardens of idolaters outside are permitted for taking an etrog even if they are designated for them. His words fit only the first answer above, that the Mechaber speaks even where they stand in the idolatrous courtyard but were not donated for idolatry; to that he adds that when they are outside the courtyard, Sefer Chasidim did not speak. But according to the Rama there, our law speaks even where the garden was donated for idolatry, and nevertheless it is permitted because it stands outside the courtyard; even so the Magen Avraham cites Sefer Chasidim that it is better to take others. The practical conclusion is that where the garden was not donated for idolatry and also stands outside the idolatrous courtyard, one need not be particular about this.
