The gardens of the youths, etc. See the Mishnah Berurah in what I wrote, that here we are dealing with a case where the garden was never donated for idolatry. According to the Rema there in siman 143:3 in the gloss, it will at least be speaking where the garden does not stand in the courtyard of the idol. Likewise, the Rema's law, where he wrote that even a tree planted before the idol is permitted, speaks here where their attendants or the non-Jews planted it for the needs of their attendants, to eat their fruits, and it is not speaking where they planted them for the nearby idolatry. For if they planted them for the idolatry and the fruits belonged to its attendants, even according to the Rema there in siman 143 it would be prohibited for a Jew to buy it even during the year, since he benefits their priests. This resolves what the Sha'ar HaMelekh in Hilchot Lulav challenged, and the Gra also alluded to this in our matter, that the Shulchan Aruch contradicts itself: there in Yoreh De'ah 142:13, regarding barren trees planted before the idol, he rules that they are included in adornments of idolatry, while here he permits taking from them for the mitzvah. Here he is speaking where they planted it for their own needs. I found something similar in the Levush, who writes: gardens of attendants of idolatry, one may take from them a lulav or other species for the mitzvah, because they are kept there only for their own benefit and not for the idolatry; and even if the tree is planted before the idol, as long as they do not worship the tree, they planted it for their own benefit. End quote. He means that ordinarily we say they planted it for their own benefit, except where we know that the planting was for the adornment of idolatry and only the fruits would belong to their priests; then they are called adornments of idolatry, it is prohibited to benefit from them, and one does not fulfill the obligation with them on the first day by law, because they are not called 'for yourselves,' and even on the second day it is prohibited ab initio, as above.
I found in Nahar Shalom that he also raised this difficulty from Yoreh De'ah 142:13, and answered in another way, also similar to what we wrote: there, where it speaks of barren trees that have no fruit, we must assume that ordinarily they planted them to be an adornment for idolatry. This is not so here, where it speaks of fruit-bearing trees; it is better to assume that they planted them for their fruit, as the Levush wrote.
It further appears to me to answer in a slightly different way: here, where it speaks of the four species, it is stated above that the non-Jew must cut them himself. If so, according to Rabbeinu Yerucham cited in the Darkei Moshe, this is included in nullification, and the prohibition of adornment of idolatry has been nullified. What remains is only that for something used before Heaven one should be stringent ab initio; for this we are lenient and say they planted it for their own needs. There, by contrast, he speaks only regarding the essential law, whether it is included in adornments of idolatry, and the practical difference is that it is prohibited for a Jew to cut it himself.
For the mitzvah. See the Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote in the name of Sefer Chasidim that nevertheless, if there are others he should take the others. The Peri Megadim wrote that nevertheless one can say that in the gardens of idolaters which are outside, it is permitted to take an esrog even if it is designated for them. End quote. His words apply only to the first answer that we wrote above, that the Mechaber is speaking even when they stand in the courtyard of the idolatry, only that they were not donated for the idolatry. Regarding that, the Peri Megadim wrote that when this is combined with the fact that they stand outside the courtyard, the Sefer Chasidim did not speak of such a case. But according to the Rema there, our law is speaking even where they donated the garden for idolatry, and nevertheless it is permitted because we are dealing with where they stand outside the courtyard; yet the Magen Avraham brought in the name of Sefer Chasidim that it is better to take others. The practical conclusion is that where they did not donate the garden for idolatry and they also stand outside the courtyard of the idolaters, one need not be particular about this.
