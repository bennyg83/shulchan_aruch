(Subparagraph 3) Except, etc. And so wrote in Milchamos, etc. Why did he need to bring proof for his words? At first glance, is this not explicit in the Talmud, where Rav Huna said to the gentile merchants: you should not cut them. This is because ordinary gentiles are robbers of land, and land cannot be stolen, and despair does not help with land. Anything attached to the ground is like the ground, and what is attached is not acquired until the time of cutting, when it becomes detached. Therefore the gentile should cut it, so the gentile will be the robber, and there will be despair in the hand of the gentile and change of domain in the hand of the Jew. Presumably, Rav Huna wanted to make a remedy so that they would fulfill even on the first day. If so, one must say that such a case is called "for yourselves." However, the Magen Avraham needed to bring proof in order to reject the view of the Levush, who wrote regarding this law as follows: there are those who say that stolen and robbed are invalid only for the thief and robber, etc., but regarding others, etc., it is valid, because there is despair and change of domain. However, others too should not recite a blessing over them on the first day, which is a Torah obligation, for this is not "yours." This is specifically here, since the species itself is stolen, only he acquired it through despair and change of domain. This is unlike above, meaning the law written by the Rema that the gentile should cut it, where there is despair and change of domain, for there the species itself is not stolen, only the land in which it grew was stolen in the hand of the gentile, and the esrog was not in the world when the land was stolen. Therefore it suffices when the gentile cuts it, etc., end quote. According to this, there is no proof from the words of Rav Huna. Therefore he needed to bring proof from the words of Milchamos, who wrote this regarding a stolen lulav, not like the Levush, and even where the item itself was stolen; see there.
And so it appears in Tosafos. That is to say, from their words it also implies unlike the reasoning of the Levush. For they brought proof that where he acquired it before the mitzvah, without the assistance of the mitzvah, it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, from the Talmud's difficulty to Rav Huna: let him acquire it through change of name, as above. What proof is there from Rav Huna? There it is different, for it is more lenient than here, since he did not rob the hadas itself but only the land on which the hadas grew; unlike with an offering, where he robbed the animal itself, which is more severe. Rather, necessarily, one should not distinguish in this, unlike the Levush.
And so it appears in Rashi, etc. And nevertheless, for others it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. That is to say, Rashi disagrees with Tosafos and holds that even if he acquired it before the mitzvah, nevertheless it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression.
For he wrote the reason for Rav Huna. If a Jew would cut it, where there is no change of domain, only mere despair, Rav Huna holds that mere despair does not acquire. And even if you say that mere despair does acquire, nevertheless it would be a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Regarding the Talmud's question, that he should acquire it through physical change or change of domain, although according to Rashi even after acquisition it is nevertheless a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, the Maharsha answered that since there are two acquisitions, one being mere despair, and also mere change acquiring, in such a case it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. But according to Rav Huna, after the gentile cuts it, and in such a case one fulfills with it, where despair is in the hand of the gentile and change of domain is in the hand of the Jew, what does this help, since there is only one acquisition, namely mere despair? Mere change of domain certainly does not acquire, and there is still a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Rather, one must say that regarding others there is no concern of mitzvah that comes through a transgression. And although we are now assuming that Rav Huna holds mere despair acquires, and if so we have still not heard that regarding others it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression except after acquisition, unlike for us, where mere despair does not acquire, so when it comes into the hands of others the robber has still not acquired it, and perhaps in such a case even regarding others it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Nevertheless, he holds that according to us, if it came into the hands of others before the robber acquired it, and according to Rav Huna after one acquisition, the cases are equal. For according to Rav Huna, with one acquisition it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, and nevertheless, if it came into the hands of others, it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. So too for us, even if it came into the hands of others before he acquired it, it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression.
But in Milchamos, etc. And so it appears in the Gemara regarding the gentile merchants, where he said, etc. That is to say, according to Rashi's explanation mentioned above, Rav Huna holds mere despair acquires, only he approaches it because of mitzvah that comes through a transgression. If so, whether a gentile cuts it or a Jew cuts it, it is called "for yourselves," for there is despair, and mere despair acquires. The distinction between a gentile cutting and a Jew cutting is regarding mitzvah that comes through a transgression: when a gentile cuts it and afterward it comes into the hand of a Jew, change of domain is added to the despair. If so, what he said, that there is despair in the hand of the gentile, is extra language. He should have said only that in the hand of the Jew there is change of domain, and therefore it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Rather, one must say that as long as it has not been acquired, even if it comes into the hands of others, it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Therefore, so that it should not be a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, he needed to say that there is despair in the hand of the gentile, because through this the gentile acquires it, and afterward, through change of domain, it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. This is unlike what the Magen Avraham wrote initially. And although I wrote above that Rashi necessarily holds that even according to Rav Huna, who holds that after the acquisition of despair it is still a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, nevertheless in the hands of others it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, one must say that Rashi holds that where there is no acquisition, even in the hands of others it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. This is worse than after acquisition according to Rav Huna, unlike what the Magen Avraham initially thought, that they are equal.
