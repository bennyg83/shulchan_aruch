(There, se'if 9) Therefore one who was walking on the way or sitting in his house and heard tekios from the agent of the congregation has fulfilled, etc. Gloss: See the Magen Avraham, who brought what the Ran wrote in the name of the Yerushalmi, and this is his language: "When is this said? Regarding one who is passing by; but regarding one who is standing, there is a chazakah that he intended" (end quote. And the Magen Avraham wondered: for does he not know whether he intended or not?) see there, where he was pressed on this. And in my humble opinion it appears that there is a great practical difference according to what is stated above in the Shulchan Aruch, siman 209, se'if 8 in the gloss: that if others were drinking wine, etc., and he recited the blessing "Who creates the fruit of the vine," thinking that in his cup there was wine, and it became known to him that it was water, when he afterward wishes to drink wine he need not recite the blessing, since his intent was to drink wine afterward, and he also discharged others with his blessing; therefore his blessing was a blessing (see there. And so here too: since we hold that one who heard nine tekios at nine hours of the day has fulfilled) and so there is a difference: if the one walking needed a teruah, for he had already heard a tekiah but had not yet heard also a tekiah, and he was obligated in a tekiah first, and he recited the blessing over the shofar and began to blow a teruah first, and since a teruah before a tekiah [means] he has not fulfilled, automatically there was an interruption between the blessing and the beginning of the tekiah, and he must recite the blessing again. But in this case, since the hearer needed a teruah, and so that one [who recited] discharged [others] with this blessing and this tekiah; therefore, since he discharged others, and his intent was also to blow further, this is not a blessing in vain, and he is permitted to blow afterward in order on the strength of the first blessing. And so this is fine if the hearer intended to fulfill; but if he did not intend, then he did not discharge others, and again his blessing was in vain, and he must recite the blessing anew. And so, since where an obligation or prohibition has been established, one witness is not believed to the contrary, for it is against the chazakah, therefore if there were no chazakah that he intended, but only that he said he intended, he would not be believed to exempt the blower from a second blessing, since having become obligated to recite the blessing, one witness is not believed to exempt him; but if we say there is a chazakah that he intended, then he discharged others with his blessing, and therefore he too is exempt from blessing. And it appears further: since this very law that the Rama wrote in siman 209 is a novelty (that he learns that since they fulfilled, he too fulfills) and reason would suggest the opposite: since he recited a blessing in vain, they too have not fulfilled; and it is more [natural] to subordinate others to him than to subordinate him to others, as the Magen Avraham wrote there. And see siman 213, se'if 2, where the Magen Avraham raised a difficulty that the Shulchan Aruch's words contradict one another, and answered by distinguishing between inadvertent and intentional, and it is forced: what reason is there to distinguish between inadvertent and intentional? And one cannot say that for the intentional it is by way of a fine; first, we do not find that because of a fine they should again recite another blessing in vain; and further, even if they fined him, others did not sin; why should we fine them? And what reason is there to distinguish regarding others between whether the one who blessed was inadvertent or intentional? But it appears that not for nothing did the Rama specify in siman 209 that he wrote that others were drinking wine and wine was before them; why tell us that wine was before them? But it appears: although in general we do not require a proven presumption and revelation of intent that the hearer intends to fulfill (mere intent in the heart that they intend to fulfill suffices) nevertheless this is specifically if the one who blessed fulfilled; then, since his blessing was according to law and he fulfilled, then [for them] mere thought alone does not help unless their thought is recognizable from their deeds; then, since it is evident in the Gemara in several places that thought recognizable from one's deeds is superior to mere thought and is like an act (see in chapter 2 of Beitzah and in the first chapter of Chullin) therefore if there is wine before them, so that their thought is recognizable from their deeds that they wish to fulfill, it is as if they themselves recited the blessing; and so what of it that he recited a blessing in vain; they fulfill, for it is as if they themselves recited, for in this it is like an actual act. But if their thought is not recognizable from their deeds, but only by intent of the heart alone, in that case it is not as if they themselves recited, but only that they heard; and hearing does not suffice, since he recited a blessing in vain. And therefore in siman 213 it refers to where there was no proven presumption from their deeds, but only that they said they intended (therefore it does not help, since he recited a blessing in vain; but if there is a proven presumption from their deeds, it helps) and this is like the law of siman 209 above. And so according to this the Yerushalmi is well resolved: the practical difference is if the blower recited the blessing and sounded a teruah first before the tekiah, so that there was an interruption between the blessing and the beginning of the tekiah and it was in vain, then if the hearer stood, there is a chazakah that he intended, and the hearer fulfilled even though the one who blessed recited in vain; and then on the contrary the one who blessed also fulfilled, as is written in siman 209; and this is clear and very correct in reason. And if we say as I wrote at first (that in this the hearer is not believed against the other, since an obligation was established) one may say further a practical difference according to what is written in siman 585: that if he recited the blessing and could not blow, others should blow without a blessing (provided that the first blowers were [present] at the time of the blessing; implying that we require both) that the hearers be present at the time of the blessing and also that the blowers were then [present]; but if the blowers were not there, even though the hearers were there, it does not help. And so there is a difference: if there was no blower other than this one who stood on the way, then if he stood, since there is a chazakah that he intended, it is permitted afterward to blow without a blessing and he discharges the hearers; but if he walked, since it is a doubt whether he intended, he is not believed to say that he intended so as to exempt the hearers from the blessing (since they became obligated with certainty, one witness is not believed to exempt, for one witness is not believed against a chazakah) so it would appear to say. However, in truth this does not appear correct: for even without his testimony, since at any rate it is a doubt, behold we hold that a doubt regarding blessings is resolved leniently; and we do not find a distinction between whether the doubt is in the root of the obligation or whether he became obligated with certainty and there is a doubt whether he was exempted; rather in every case a doubt regarding blessings is resolved leniently; and so even without his testimony he is exempt out of doubt. Therefore it appears that the main [resolution] is the middle answer we wrote: that in this we require that his thought be recognizable from his deeds; and otherwise it is certainly a blessing in vain without any doubt; and it fits well and is correct, with the help of Hashem; and examine carefully.
