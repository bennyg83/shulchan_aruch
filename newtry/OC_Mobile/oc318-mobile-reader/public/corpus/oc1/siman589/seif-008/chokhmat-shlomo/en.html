(There, se'if 8) One who is occupied with shofar-blowing in order to practice has not fulfilled his obligation, etc. Gloss: Behold the Ran wrote at the end of Rosh Hashanah on the Mishnah that one who hears from one who is so occupied has not fulfilled, for even according to the view that mitzvos do not require intent, here it is worse, since he intends for those children it is "of its kind" and ruins it (see there). And it is very difficult for me from the Gemara there, which objects: "one who hears from one who is so occupied has not fulfilled" [implying] that from one who sounds for himself he has fulfilled; let this be a refutation of R' Zeira, who said to his attendant, "Intend and blow for me" and it answers "since [the earlier clause].." And it is difficult for me: what does it object according to the Ran's view above? For one may say that on the contrary it teaches us an additional novelty: that in truth mitzvos never require intent, and if he heard from a blower not for the sake of the mitzvah at all he has certainly fulfilled, and he need not intend that the other discharge him; only that R' Zeira's case is where his attendant intended to discharge himself; therefore, since it is "of its kind," it ruins it, and therefore he must intend also to discharge R' Zeira. And therefore the Mishnah teaches us an additional novelty: not [only] from one who sounds for himself, which is truly for the sake of the mitzvah (where it is obvious that it is truly "of its kind" and ruins it, and he has not fulfilled) but even if it is like one occupied [without mitzvah intent], one might have thought that since it is only for training and the hearer intends for the sake of the mitzvah proper, this is not called "of its kind" and does not ruin it; therefore it teaches us that this too is "of its kind" and ruins it and requires intent. And this is a great difficulty against the Ran's view, in my humble opinion, and it requires further study; and examine carefully. Also it is difficult for me: what does it object, for one may say as we hold regarding one who seizes for a creditor in a place where he damages others, that he has not acquired, whereas one who lifts a find for his fellow has acquired by virtue of "since he acquired for himself," etc. say likewise here: where the blower has not yet fulfilled, he need not intend to discharge the hearer, by virtue of "since he acquired for himself"; but if the blower has already fulfilled, then we require intent; and R' Zeira's case is where the blower had already fulfilled. Even though it is forced to say that his attendant would fulfill before R' Zeira; nevertheless say that R' Zeira holds that l'chatchila intent is required in every case, and the Mishnah refers after the fact; and this requires further study; and examine carefully. And behold, that which is explained in the Shulchan Aruch ("one who is occupied with shofar-blowing in order to practice has not fulfilled his obligation") seemingly the language of the Shulchan Aruch is not precise, for he took this out of order according to what the Ran wrote in tractate Rosh Hashanah in two places: that even according to the view that one who blew for a song has fulfilled, for mitzvos do not require intent; he concedes that one who is so occupied [for practice] has not fulfilled, for this is worse in that he intends for the sake of a mitzvah of training children, as is written in tractate Zevachim that "of its kind" ruins it, whereas what is not of its kind does not ruin it (see there). And according to this the Shulchan Aruch fits well: first he wrote that blowing in order to practice does not help, and afterward he wrote regarding one who blows for a song that he has not fulfilled; this is the manner of "not only this but even that": not only where he intended to practice, which is "of its kind," has he not fulfilled, but even one who blows for a song, which is not "of its kind," has not fulfilled. But that which he wrote afterward (where the hearer intended and the sounder did not intend to discharge the hearer, that he has not fulfilled) this is "this, and it goes without saying that": for if intending only to train the children, which is "of its kind" and ruins it, all the more so if he intended actually to fulfill his own obligation for the sake of the mitzvah, which is "of its kind" and ruins it, since he did not intend to discharge the hearer; and it is a fortiori that he has not fulfilled: just as the one occupied [for practice] has not fulfilled, all the more so in this case. And likewise it is a fortiori from one who blows for a song: just as one who blows for a song, which is not "of its kind," [still] ruins it, all the more so one who blows for the sake of the mitzvah and does not intend to discharge the hearer has not fulfilled; and this is "this, and it goes without saying that." But in truth it is difficult for me against the Ran from the Gemara of Rosh Hashanah, folio 33, which objects there regarding one who hears from one occupied [for practice] that he has not fulfilled (implying that one who hears from one who sounds for himself has fulfilled) and a beraisa teaches, etc. And according to the Ran's words it is difficult: what does it object? Say that on the contrary it teaches us an additional novelty: that even one who hears from one occupied [for practice], who did not intend for a complete mitzvah but only to train children, and it is only rabbinic; nevertheless it is called "of its kind" and is effective that he has not fulfilled according to the Torah, even though according to the Torah there is no mitzvah at all and it is not "its kind" according to the Torah; nevertheless he has not fulfilled according to the Torah and is obligated to blow again according to the Torah; and there is a practical difference for several matters between whether he is obligated according to the Torah or only by rabbinic law, all the more so where he intended to fulfill for himself and did not intend to discharge the hearer, for in that case it is truly "its kind" and is a Torah mitzvah, which is "of its kind" and ruins it, and all agree that intent is required, and if he did not intend to discharge the hearer it does not help; and this is "this, and it goes without saying that" so what does it object? And behold, thereby the language of the Shulchan Aruch would be well: for from that Gemara he proved that one occupied [for practice] is worse than one who intended for himself; and therefore he properly wrote in the manner of "not only this but even that." But even so, against the Ran it remains difficult: from where for the Gemara to raise a difficulty, for there is reason to say that intending for oneself is worse than being occupied [for practice], according to the Ran's reasoning above; so what does it object? And this requires further study. And behold I examined Rashi's language, who wrote s.v. "One occupied (that is not [sufficient], etc.": "but one who blows for a song, who intends for blowing in general, has fulfilled") end quote. And this is puzzling: for the Gemara wishes to prove only that if he blows for the sake of the mitzvah but does not intend to discharge the hearer, he has fulfilled; and against this it objects from R' Zeira, so why mention here one who blows for a song? And one cannot say that Rashi explains that what the Gemara wrote "but one who hears from one who sounds for himself" means one who blows for a song who sounds for himself (that is, of his own mind, and not because of the day's obligation that rests upon him, but of his own mind) for this is not so: afterward Rashi wrote s.v. "The Tanna of the latter clause also [spoke of] one occupied," and this is his language: "and in truth, one who hears from one who sounds for himself, who did not intend to discharge the hearers, has not fulfilled" (end quote). If so, it is implied that he too explains "one who sounds for himself" as merely that he did not intend to discharge the hearers, but for himself he intended to fulfill, as is evident from R' Zeira's language. So why at first did he explain an intent for a song? And it is further difficult: according to his way, that from here it is evident that for a song too he has fulfilled (he should have raised a difficulty from this against the view that mitzvos require intent; why does it object specifically against R' Zeira) rather it should have said "derive from here that mitzvos do not require intent," as is written in Berachos and in several places "derive from here that mitzvos require intent" here it should have said the opposite: "derive from here that mitzvos do not require intent." And why mention R' Zeira's words? Of necessity it is evident according to the Ran's explanation that one who blows for a song is worse than one occupied [for practice]; therefore it objects only against R' Zeira. And this requires further study; and examine carefully.
