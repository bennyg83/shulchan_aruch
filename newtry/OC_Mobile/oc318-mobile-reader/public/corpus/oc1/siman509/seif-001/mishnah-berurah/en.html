(1) A spit that became bent. Meaning, it bent on Yom Tov. The Mechaber states the law without distinguishing whether it bent slightly or so severely that it cannot be used for roasting without repair. This follows his approach in the Beit Yosef, where he explains that repair is forbidden in every case because it constitutes repairing a utensil. The Rama follows a different approach, as explained in Biur Halacha. See there.
(2) Even though he can, etc. Meaning, he can straighten it without striking it with a hammer.
(3) So that he can roast with it. Meaning, he can use it for roasting only with difficulty. Even so, repairing it is considered excessive exertion.
(4) But if he cannot, etc. This refers, for example, to a spit that became badly bent. The phrase “or it broke” means that the tip of the spit broke, so that it can no longer be used for roasting.
(5) On Yom Tov. If it bent or broke on erev Yom Tov, when it could have been repaired beforehand, it is forbidden by law to repair it on Yom Tov. It is then a preparatory device for food that could have been repaired before Yom Tov, which all authorities forbid. The Magen Avraham cites Maharil that if it broke on the first day of Yom Tov, it may not be repaired on the second day. With respect to the second day, the first day is like erev Yom Tov, since the second day is observed because of the doubt that the first day may have been a weekday. Bigdei Yesha writes that if the spit was not needed on the first day, then according to the Rama it may be repaired on the second day when it is needed. Rabbi Akiva Eiger likewise questions the ruling of Maharil. See there.
(6) It is permitted, etc. See Biur Halacha, where we cited the acharonim who rule that one should be stringent in accordance with Baal HaMaor and Ran. They hold that this is considered a full repair of a utensil, which is forbidden even according to the authority who permits preparatory devices for food. If another spit can be borrowed, all authorities agree that repairing this one is forbidden, as stated by Magen Avraham.
(7) To repair it. Peri Megadim writes that this means sharpening it slightly so that the meat can be placed upon it. A complete repair, however, is forbidden according to all authorities. This resembles the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch below in se'if 2 that sharpening a knife on a stone sharpener is forbidden by law because it is a complete repair of a utensil, even according to those who permit preparatory devices for food. The Rama's ruling applies specifically to a spit, which is a preparatory device for food. Another utensil that became damaged, such as a needle that bent even slightly, may not be straightened according to any view, as stated by the acharonim.
(8) And this should not be taught publicly. The same applies to teaching it to one's students.
