Seif katan 1: And not, etc. As stated in siman 10, seif 3: even if one folded and tied the corners so that it appears as though the garment has no corners, it nevertheless remains obligated, because he intends eventually to untie the knot and the corner was not nullified. The same applies here. The tzitzis are not nullified because he intends eventually to untie the knots. Furthermore, etc. Beit Yosef also challenges them that according to their view, at that time they are wearing a four-cornered garment without tzitzis.
And Levush, etc. As stated at the end of siman 626, it is not “you shall make,” and not something already made, because he initially placed the s'chach validly. He had removed the roof when he placed the s'chach and only afterward closed it. Therefore, when he opens it, the sukkah returns to its original validity. The same applies here: since the tzitzis were originally made validly, when he unties the knots they return to their original validity, and there is no issue of something already made. This itself requires explanation according to Levush, because below he permits initially placing s'chach even before removing the roof; see there. The same is implied by siman 10, seif 6. According to Rambam, if one placed tzitzis upon tzitzis with intent to add, then even if he later cut away the second set, the first set remains invalid because of “you shall make,” and not something already made, even though it was originally made validly. See Beit Yosef there, where this is explicit.
And see Sukkah, etc. The Gemara seeks to explain that the Tannaim disagree whether there is rejection with respect to mitzvos. A hadas whose berries exceed its leaves and are black is invalid; if he reduced them, it is valid. One may not reduce them on Yom Tov because that repairs an object. If he transgressed and reduced them, the first Tanna validates and Rabbi Elazar son of Rabbi Tzadok invalidates. The Gemara initially assumes that all agree a lulav does not require binding. There is therefore no issue of “you shall make,” and not something already made, because when it was bound the hadas was invalid; and after reducing the berries, since binding is unnecessary, the prior invalidity does not create something already made. Alternatively, if they maintain that a lulav does require binding, one must say that although something already made invalidates a sukkah, it does not invalidate a lulav, because we do not derive lulav from sukkah in this respect. Their dispute would then concern whether there is rejection with respect to mitzvos. According to Levush, however, the Gemara should have explained that all agree a lulav requires binding and that something already made invalidates it, but the case is one in which the berries were green when it was bound and were then valid. The binding was therefore originally valid. On Yom Tov the berries became black and afterward he reduced them. There would be no issue of something already made, because the binding was originally valid, and their dispute would concern whether there is rejection. Although that would be a case in which the object was initially fit, then rejected, and later restored, where there is more reason to say there is rejection than where it was rejected from the outset, the Gemara had not yet concluded that distinction between rejection from the outset and an object that was initially fit and later rejected. We must therefore conclude that Levush's reasoning is incorrect.
When he ties two, etc. Shulchan Aruch here discusses tying two tzitzis to one another, while Tosafos discuss tying each set of tzitzis independently.
