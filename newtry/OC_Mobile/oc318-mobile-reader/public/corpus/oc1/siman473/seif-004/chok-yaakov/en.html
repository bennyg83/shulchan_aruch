And karpas. Alternatively, another vegetable that is eaten both raw and cooked and over which Borei Pri HaAdamah is recited, as stated above in siman 260. The custom is to take what is called parsley. It is preferable to take karpas, whose name forms an acronym alluding to the six hundred thousand who performed crushing labor. In German it is called eppich. The later authorities write likewise.
Or salt water. If the first night falls on Shabbos, he should prepare the salt water on Erev Shabbos. If he did not prepare it before Shabbos and has no vinegar, he should prepare only a small amount of salt water, as explained above in siman 321.
And one in remembrance of the Chagigah offering. Even when Erev Pesach falls on Shabbos, the Tur concludes that one should take two cooked foods, unlike the opinion of Rosh. This requires study, for Kitzur Piskei HaRosh states that one should take two cooked foods even when Motzaei Shabbos is Yom Tov, whereas the actual opinion of Rosh there is the reverse.
In remembrance of the Chagigah offering. Rokeach writes that some say two cooked foods are taken in remembrance of Moshe and Aharon.
And charoses. “And they should be closer to him.” Shulchan Aruch attributes this here to Maharil. In truth, I did not find this in Maharil. He writes only: “It is proper to place the eppich vegetable closer to him than the matzos, for if the matzos were closer, he would not need to break the afikoman from the upper matzah, so that he should not pass over the mitzvos.” Charoses, meat, and egg are not mentioned at all. Since no blessing is recited over them and they are brought to the table only as a remembrance, the principle that one may not pass over mitzvos does not apply to them. Moreover, there is an opinion that charoses is not a mitzvah, as stated in the Mishnah in chapter Arvei Pesachim. Maharil was particular only regarding matzah, which is a mitzvah over which we recite “and commanded us,” and whose blessing precedes that of the vegetable. Therefore the vegetables should be placed closer to him than the matzah. Even maror is not mentioned by Maharil as needing to be placed nearer or farther away, although we recite “and commanded us” over it. Perhaps, because people do not favor it and it has no priority regarding blessings, Maharil was not particular about the maror. The proof Maharil brings there from the western lamp may also be distinguished; see there.
And the same applies to roasting. The egg is eaten because it recalls mourning, as explained below in siman 476. Roasted meat is not eaten. This is the custom even in a place where other roasted foods are eaten, because it recalls the Korban Pesach. Taz also concludes that this is the primary ruling. If one forgot to roast the meat or egg before Yom Tov, he may not roast it at night unless he intends to eat it that night or the following day. Mahari Weil, siman 193.
