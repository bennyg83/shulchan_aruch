(33) His obligation. That is to say, the obligation of the mitzvah of maror, to fulfill what is written "upon matzos and bitter herbs they shall eat it"; and now that we have no Pesach, the mitzvah of maror is only rabbinic. Just as one does not fulfill with stolen matzah, as above in siman 454, so one does not fulfill with stolen maror; and therefore one should be careful that a Jew not himself uproot the maror from a gentile's ground even if he gives him permission, for ordinary idolaters are land-robbers, and regarding ground despair does not apply, as below in siman 649, se'if 1 in the gloss; rather the gentile himself should uproot, and then despair applies to it, and afterwards the Jew should buy from him, for by this it is despair and change of domain.
(34) Meaning species of bitter herbs. It refers to all of them; and because we do not know clearly which they are in our language, therefore he wrote in a general way regarding all of them that they are bitter species. And later authorities wrote that tamcha is horseradish ("Chrein") in our language; and regarding chazeres, Chok Yaakov and likewise Chacham Tzvi wrote that it is what we call lettuce ("Salatin").
(35) Maror. It is also a species of bitter vegetable known to them by the name maror, on account of its being very bitter.
(36) But not in the root. Meaning small roots that split here and there at its bottom or at its sides; but the large root on which the vegetable stands is the stalk. And behold, in our countries the other species are not common, and therefore the maror customary in our country to eat is tamcha (and it is horseradish); and one should be careful not to eat it whole, for it is almost a danger and there is no mitzvah in it, for because of its sharpness it greatly harms; and therefore one must grate it on a grater and leave it thus uncovered, and then its sharpness dissipates. And the Gra instituted not to grate it before his coming from the synagogue, so that the taste not dissipate, for then one does not fulfill with it; rather he should grate it after his coming and cover it until the beginning of the Seder [and when Pesach falls on Shabbos he should grate it from while it is still day and place it in a vessel and cover it until the beginning of the Seder], and then scatter it on a plate, and by this its sharpness dissipates, and then press it and estimate that there be a kezayit, and he can eat it well.
(37) Unless they are moist. For dried ones have no taste of maror, and behold they are like mere dust; which is not so regarding the stalk, for since it is thick, even if it is dry it does not lose its taste. And wilted leaves: some say one fulfills with them, and some are stringent even regarding wilted ones.
(38) But not pickled. That is, if he soaked them in water overnight; and some say specifically if he pickled them in vinegar; and therefore l'chatchila one should be careful not to soak the horseradish in water overnight, and b'dieved one may rely to be lenient when he has no others, because with horseradish behold we see that there is still a taste of maror in it. And it appears to me that regarding leaves one should not be lenient, for it is possible that regarding them the taste of maror goes out through overnight pickling in water. And know that one fulfills with leaves even l'chatchila. And behold I have seen several weak people who push themselves to fulfill the mitzvah of maror with stalks, and I do not know why they should not take the leaves for the mitzvah; and perhaps because among them dry and wilted ones are common. However, if they are moist one should not be stringent about this at all.
(39) And not thoroughly cooked. Greatly cooked, and cooked in the manner of cooking; and all of them [are invalid] because through this they have no taste of maror.
(40) And all of them, etc. That is to say, all five species mentioned above; and we do not say that since every vegetable certainly has a bitter taste by itself they do not combine, for the taste of one is nullified in its fellow; it teaches us that since in any case all of them have a taste of bitterness, they combine (Ran).
(41) For a kezayit. It is the measure of half an egg (and see below, siman 486); and if he fulfills the mitzvah with leaves, he should see to press them together, for the space between the leaves does not combine for a kezayit; and one should be careful about this, for if not so the blessing is in vain, since he blesses over eating, and eating implies a kezayit, and also he does not fulfill thereby the mitzvah of maror.
(42) With chazeres. For it is a remembrance of the subjugation of Egypt, which at its beginning was soft and at its end hard; and so too is the nature of chazeres, that at first it is sweet and at its end bitter. And the later authorities wrote that even if it is somewhat more expensive than other maror, it is also proper to seek after it. However, they wrote that in the species of chazeres (namely lettuce) there are very common in the days of Pesach small worms that are not recognizable to those of weak eyesight; therefore one who does not have specially designated God-fearing people who will check it properly, it is better to take tamcha (which they call horseradish) even though it is third according to the order in which they are taught, for Heaven forbid to stumble in a negative commandment because of fulfilling a positive rabbinic commandment; and especially since it is possible to fulfill both of them by means of tamcha.
(43) He should seek the first first. And one who is ill or delicate is permitted to take whichever species is most agreeable to him; and he may also eat the kezayit little by little within the measure of eating a peras, for according to the main law he fulfills with this, as below in siman 475, se'if 6. And if even this is impossible for him because of his health, at the very least he should eat a little or chew in his mouth as a remembrance of the taste of bitterness, but he should not recite a blessing over this.
(44) One of these vegetables. That is to say, the five mentioned above.
(45) He should take wormwood. See in Biur Halacha.
(46) Or another bitter vegetable. Namely those that have the signs mentioned in the Gemara regarding maror: that it should be fit for eating, and that it have sap (meaning: when they cut it, at the place of its cut white sap like milk comes out), and that its face be pale (namely that its leaf is not very green like the leaves of onions and other vegetables, but tends a bit toward white). And nevertheless regarding a blessing one should not recite a blessing over them, because we are not so expert in these signs.
(47) He should make it thick. And he should make it from erev Yom Tov; and if he forgot, it is permitted to make it on Yom Tov.
(48) A little vinegar. Of wine or red wine, in order to soften it, and it shall be a remembrance of blood. If it falls on Shabbos he should put the liquid into it from erev Shabbos; and if he forgot, see above in siman 321, se'if 16, and in the Mishnah Berurah there, s.k. 68.
(49) Such as apples. On account of the verse "Under the apple tree I awakened you"; and likewise the other fruits are all written in Shir HaShirim; and almonds on account of that the Holy One, Blessed be He, watched over the end to bring it about.
(50) That resemble straw. That they are not crushed well and they are long.
