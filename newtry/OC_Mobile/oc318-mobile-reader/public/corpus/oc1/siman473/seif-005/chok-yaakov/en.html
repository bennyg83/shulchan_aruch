Chazeres. Since not everyone is familiar with these vegetables and their names, the terminology varies from country to country, and not every vegetable is found in every place, I have therefore decided to copy here all the explanations of these vegetables that I found in the poskim, so that each person may understand the matter clearly according to the language and country familiar to him. The Gemara in Pesachim explains that chazeres is lettuce. Rashi calls it latuga; Aruch, latuka; Hagahos Maimoniyos, lektika or latich. Levush writes that it is what we call zersich in these countries. My brother-in-law, the author of Sefer Eliyah Zuta, cites a manuscript that challenges Levush and says that it is instead called latich in German and lactuca in Latin. Medical books list seven varieties of it, and it has no connection to the common zersich of these countries. In Berachos, chapter HaRoeh, regarding Bar Hedya's interpretations of dreams, Rashi explains the phrase “your affairs will be doubled like lettuce” as referring to a vegetable that is folded and thick. I heard from one who speaks the Romance language that in the warm countries they have chazeres during Nisan, and it is folded and thick, like what we call head lettuce in these countries, and this is what they eat as maror. See Beit Yosef, who writes that the mitzvah is fulfilled with chazeres even though it is sweet and has no bitterness. Yet in chapter HaRoeh, Bar Hedya said to Rava, “Your affairs will be bitter like lettuce.” One must therefore say that it nevertheless has some bitterness. Aruch writes that mature chazeres is bitter lettuce. Agudah writes: “If latuga is unavailable, one should take horseradish.” Olsheen is called hindivi in the Gemara. Rashi calls it kereshpila; Aruch explains it as a certain wild endive, called sinzoni in the vernacular, and some call it krespinio. Shibbolei HaLeket cites that it is astrola, and some call it in the vernacular damtika endiva. Rav Ovadiah of Bartenura likewise identifies it as endive. It is common among us and is cooked and eaten with sugar and vinegar to reduce its bitterness. Hagahos Maimoniyos identifies it as kerbel, which is also common among us. Maharil, however, writes that since the siddurim prescribe kerbel for the first dipping, it follows that one does not fulfill the mitzvah of maror with it. Tamcha is identified by Rashi as marubya; Aruch calls it kardavya or merayu. The later authorities write that it is what is called horseradish in German, and Tosafos Yom Tov writes likewise; see there where he challenges Rav Ovadiah of Bartenura. The custom has therefore spread in these countries to take horseradish for maror, reciting the blessing over the leaves and taking the stalk for korech. Charchavina is identified in the Gemara as the ivy of the palm tree. Rashi calls it vidla, and Rokeach calls it agrorin. Maror is called merarita in the Gemara. Rashi calls it amerfoyl in the vernacular, and Agudah identifies it as vermouth, meaning wormwood.

Thus writes Riaz in Shiltei HaGibborim, and this is the implication of most poskim. Knesses HaGedolah writes likewise. The Gemara's statement that wilted maror is valid refers to the stalks, as the Gemara concludes. Since we rule that one fulfills the mitzvah with dry stalks, all agree that wilted stalks are certainly valid. Although this is very obvious, I have written it because Magen Avraham writes regarding stalks, “The statement that one fulfills the mitzvah whether they are moist implies that wilted stalks are invalid, and this also appears from Tur and Rambam.” This is an obvious printing error and refers to the leaves, as I have written. See above, siman 336, whether one may remove them if he inserted them into soil before Shabbos so that they would not wilt.
But not pickled. Rashi explains this as pickled in vinegar. The later authorities write that Rashi follows his own view, cited in his name in Yoreh Deah 105, that soaking in water does not have the law of pickling, only soaking in vinegar or brine. We, however, rule that an item soaked in water for twenty-four hours is considered pickled like cooked food. They therefore write that one should protest those who soak the maror for twenty-four hours, because the mitzvah cannot then be fulfilled with it. In my humble opinion, however, the custom of Israel has the force of Torah, and those who soak it so that the leaves remain moist and do not wilt have authorities upon whom to rely. Even the poskim who rule regarding prohibitions that soaking in water for twenty-four hours is considered pickling for purposes of absorption and release would agree here that only vinegar makes the maror halachically pickled. The Gemara on Pesachim 39 states regarding maror that the general principle is that one fulfills the mitzvah with anything that retains the taste of maror. As long as it was not pickled in vinegar, it retains the taste of maror, as can be plainly seen and tasted. There is also proof from the wording of the Mishnah and baraisa there, which teach: “but not pickled, scalded, or cooked.” Tosafot explain that scalding is more intense than cooking, otherwise the sequence would be unnecessary. The poskim agree. Yet the opening phrase, “but not pickled,” would still appear unnecessary if ordinary water-pickling is less intense than cooking, while the sequence “not scalded and not cooked” would proceed from the stronger to the weaker. Rather, the pickling mentioned there must mean pickling in vinegar, as Rashi explains. Vinegar changes and nullifies the bitterness more than scalding or cooking in water. The Mishnah's sequence therefore proceeds throughout from the less obvious case to the more obvious. The Gemara's general principle then includes soaking in water, since the maror retains its bitter taste. Apart from all this, I wrote in my work Minchas Yaakov that several poskim hold that soaking in water for twenty-four hours is not considered pickling. Although we rule stringently regarding prohibitions, maror in our time is D'Rabbanan, and this stringency can itself lead to a leniency, because people may eat the leaves when wilted. No early posek explicitly forbids soaking maror in water. There is therefore clear reason to permit it, and one should not be stringent. A similar point may be made below in siman 521; see there.
They combine to form a kezayis. This is approximately half a hen's egg. The full amount must be present apart from the spaces between the leaves, as stated below in siman 486. One must be careful about this, for otherwise the blessing would be in vain, since the blessing refers to eating and eating implies a kezayis. A sick or delicate person may take whichever species of maror is most palatable to him. If he cannot eat a full amount at all, he should at least eat a small amount or chew it in his mouth as a remembrance of the bitter taste, without a blessing. He should exert himself to the fullest extent possible, even if it causes him pain, to fulfill the mitzvah of the Sages, as I wrote above in siman 472, seif katan 22; see there.

And charoses. It should be made thick. Rokeach, siman 266, writes: “Charoses is prepared on Friday. If one wishes to prepare it on Motzaei Shabbos, which is Yom Tov, he has permission to do so, as stated in Pesachim 117, because charoses is a mitzvah and not merely preparation for a mitzvah.” His ruling that charoses is a mitzvah means that it recalls the mortar and blood, as the Rama writes. This follows Rosh in chapter Arvei Pesachim, and Rambam in Mishneh Torah and Tur in siman 475 rule likewise. Nevertheless, no blessing is recited over it because it is subordinate to the maror, as Tur writes in siman 472. This is unlike the implication of Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah. See Bach, siman 471. Levush is very difficult. In siman 471, seif 2, he rules simply that charoses is not a mitzvah at all, while here and at the beginning of siman 475 he rules the opposite, in accordance with the opinion that it is a mitzvah. This requires study.
