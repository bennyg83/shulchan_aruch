There, seif 3. He says Al HaNissim in Birkas HaMazon in the blessing of HaAretz, and so on. Note: see Magen Avraham, seif katan 9, who wrote that therefore one should first recite Birkas HaMazon in order to remove himself from a dispute. His words contradict what he wrote in siman 693, seif katan 1, where he wrote that even if he mentioned it, we do not make him repeat. If so, granted, regarding a matter that involves a concern of prohibition or an interruption, it is relevant to say that one should remove himself from a dispute. But here, even if he transgressed and mentioned it, we do not make him repeat; if so, all the more so in a case of doubt, no concern of dispute is relevant here. This is unlike what the Taz wrote in siman 693. From the words of the Taz in siman 693, I learned the law that if in Mussaf of Rosh Chodesh or Yom Tov he mentioned Yaaleh VeYavo, we do not make him repeat, even according to the Taz in siman 108. Since Yom Tov is a proper time to mention Yaaleh VeYavo, even though in this particular davening it is not fitting to mention it, it is not considered an interruption according to anyone. So I ruled from reasoning. Now I have found support for my words in the Taz himself here, and this is clear and correct in my view; examine it carefully. It appears further that if there is a doubt whether he mentioned Al HaNissim or not, we make him repeat, for it is words of Kabbalah, which are like Torah words, and a doubt is treated stringently. Although regarding Megillah reading in a doubtful walled city it implies from the early authorities that we do not say by Megillah that a doubt is treated stringently, it appears that one should distinguish based on what I found in Rashi's commentary to tractate Taanis, chapter 1, and in the Tosafos Yom Tov there on the Mishnah, regarding words of Kabbalah. It says, “rend your hearts,” and they explained there that something is called words of Kabbalah only when it was said as a command to Yisrael, but what was said by way of narrative is not called words of Kabbalah. See there. If so, the same applies regarding this rule that words of Kabbalah are like Torah words. Therefore, regarding doing, since it is written, “And Esther wrote,” and so on, “to observe these two days,” and it is also written, “the Jews established and accepted,” and so on, “to observe these two days,” and since regarding doing it is written as a command and acceptance, it is considered words of Kabbalah and is like Torah law, and its doubt is stringent. But regarding remembering, no command and acceptance is written, only the narrative statement “these days are remembered and observed.” That is only in the manner of narration, and this is not words of Kabbalah; its law is only Rabbinic, and a doubt is treated leniently. This is a principle in many places, in my humble opinion; examine it carefully.
