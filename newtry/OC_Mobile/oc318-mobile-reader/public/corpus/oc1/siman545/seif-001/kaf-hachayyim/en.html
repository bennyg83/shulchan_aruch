1) [Se'if 1] It is forbidden to write on Chol HaMoed, etc. The reason is that this melachah is not needed for the festival. Rambam, chapter 7 of Hilchos Yom Tov, law 13. Sefer Mitzvos Gadol writes likewise, and Beis Yosef cites them. See below, subsection 3.
2) There. It is forbidden to write on Chol HaMoed, etc. Rabbeinu Tam permitted writing in the script called mashak, as Orach Chaim and Rabbeinu Yerucham write in his name. Rama and those who agree with him explain this as cursive script, and this is correct, unlike Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1, who interprets it as writing with liquids. This is also evident from the reason given, that it is not complete writing and was not given at Sinai, etc. This is clear. Birkei Yosef, subsection 1. He further demonstrates in Shiyurei Berachah, subsection 1, that mashak means cursive script, unlike Eliyah Rabbah. He writes there in the name of Hagahos Maimoniyos at the end of chapter 7 that regarding a Sefer Torah, there is no concern with our thin writing, meaning cursive script, because it is not complete writing. See there. Chayei Adam, rule 111, subsection 1, likewise writes that the prohibition against writing on Chol HaMoed applies specifically to the script used for a Sefer Torah, tefillin, and mezuzos, which is skilled workmanship. Mikraei Kodesh, siman 53, subsection 1, writes likewise. See below, subsections 11 and 59.
3) There. Even to correct one letter, etc. This is because it is not needed for the festival and is not called a public need, since another scroll is available for the public reading. Taz, subsection 1; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 1. If they have no other Sefer Torah, however, it is permitted, as written below, se'if 2.
4) There. Even to correct one letter, etc. Although the source of this law is the mishnah in Moed Katan 18b, which discusses a Sefer Torah, the words of Shulchan Aruch imply that the same applies to another sacred work, such as books of Scripture, Gemara, and the like. An exception applies when one presently needs the book during Chol HaMoed for study, as written below, se'if 2. See there.
5) There. Even to correct one letter, etc. Although one may not keep an uncorrected sacred book in his house, since he could have corrected it before the festival, it is considered work deliberately scheduled for the festival. Even if the error was discovered only now during the festival and was not previously known, correction is nevertheless forbidden because the melachah is not needed for the festival. Bach; Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, subsection 1; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2; Bigdei Yesha. This implies that even if the error is discovered during the Torah reading in the synagogue, it should not be corrected. Since another Sefer Torah is available, removing this one does not create a festival need; another scroll should be taken out. Eliyah Rabbah there; Bigdei Yesha.
6) There. Even to correct one letter, etc. This refers to a missing letter. Separating letters that touch is permitted even if another Sefer Torah is available. Likewise, if one letter is missing but its outline remains recognizable, one may trace it with a pen, because this is not skilled workmanship. Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, subsection 4; Olas Shabbos, subsection 1; Eliyah Rabbah there; Bigdei Yesha; Mishbetzos Zahav, subsection 1; Mishnah Berurah, subsection 2. Likewise, a bent final kaf that split into two may be repaired with a little ink, because this is not skilled workmanship. Givas Shaul, siman 57. Shaarei Teshuvah cites this. See there. Similarly, if a scribe completed a Sefer Torah before Yom Tov but merely outlined the letters of the words 'before the eyes of all Israel' without filling them, and the community wishes to complete the scroll and dedicate it on Hoshana Rabbah, the letters may be completed during Chol HaMoed. Sdei Chemed, section Chol HaMoed, subsection 12.
7) A Sefer Torah whose panels still require stitching should be stitched by a person who lacks food. Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah there, subsection 5; Olas Shabbos there; Eliyah Rabbah there; Bigdei Yesha; Mishbetzos Zahav there; Mishnah Berurah there. This too refers to a case where another Sefer Torah is available. If no other scroll is available, even a laborer who has food may perform the stitching, as written below, se'if 2. Mishbetzos Zahav there writes likewise.
8) There. Even to correct one letter, etc. If an extra letter is found in a Sefer Torah during Chol HaMoed and another Sefer Torah is available, see Responsa Panim Meiros, volume 1, siman 66. He permits erasing the letter because although erasure is a full melachah, it is not skilled workmanship. One may perform unskilled work for a public need even when the public does not presently need it. He proves this from siman 544 regarding repairing public wells and springs. See there. Shaarei Teshuvah cites this. Responsa Zera Avraham, siman 66, writes likewise. Ikarei HaDat, siman 25, subsection 1, cites it. See there. See Shaarei Teshuvah on se'if 8, who writes that if the extra letter is in the middle of a word and must be scraped away, after which the separated letters must be extended or thickened to bring them closer together, it appears that this requires skill and is forbidden on Chol HaMoed. He nevertheless writes that everything depends upon the circumstances and whether the particular act requires skill. If it does require skill, it is forbidden even if an unskilled person presently wishes to perform it, because the nature of the act requires skilled hands. If it would be repaired in an unskilled manner, that too is forbidden because it does not honor the Torah. See there.
9) If during Chol HaMoed of Sukkos a torn letter is found in the chassan's Sefer Torah, requiring a patch on the outside and subsequent repair of the letter, it appears that this is forbidden. The public does not need this scroll because other sifrei Torah are available. The Sages forbade even correcting one letter, and this repair additionally requires a patch and is the work of an expert craftsman. Another Sefer Torah should be used. Chaim Sha'al, volume 2, siman 43, subsection 1. See there. Ikarei HaDat there cites this. He writes likewise in Yosef Ometz, siman 81. See there for his statement that one should challenge a rabbi only from a work printed during that rabbi's lifetime, which was available to him and which he had read. See there. Certainly, no rabbi should be challenged before the sources are examined in their origins, references, and precise form.
10) There, in the gloss. And for a public need, etc. Judicial decrees and authorizations are public needs, as written above, siman 544, subsection 16. See there.
11) There, in the gloss. For a public need, some forbid it, etc. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 85, writes this and states that ordinary writing forbidden by the Sages is considered skilled workmanship, etc. His words imply that even our cursive script is forbidden for a public need when it is not needed for the festival. See there. Bach, however, disagrees. He writes that it is obvious that writing during the festival was forbidden only in Ashuris script, which is used for a Sefer Torah, tefillin, and mezuzos and constitutes skilled workmanship. Our cursive writing is not skilled workmanship. Therefore, the Sages permitted writing even greetings and one's accounts. Certainly, one may write for a public need even when it is not needed for the festival and without an alteration. Bach nevertheless writes that the custom is to take care to write with an alteration. The alteration should be that the actual letters are not as attractive as those one ordinarily writes on a weekday. If the script itself is as attractive as usual but one merely begins writing in a corner of the paper, this is not an alteration. See there. Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, subsection 3; Olas Shabbos, subsection 1; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 5, cite this. In a place without an established custom, one who is lenient and writes without an alteration has not acted improperly. Darkei Moshe, subsection 4; Eliyah Rabbah there. See below, se'if 7.
