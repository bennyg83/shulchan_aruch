For a public need, some forbid it, etc. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 85, writes this and states that ordinary writing forbidden by the Sages is considered skilled workmanship, etc. His words imply that even our cursive script is forbidden for a public need when it is not needed for the festival. See there. Bach, however, disagrees. He writes that it is obvious that writing during the festival was forbidden only in Ashuris script, which is used for a Sefer Torah, tefillin, and mezuzos and constitutes skilled workmanship. Our cursive writing is not skilled workmanship. Therefore, the Sages permitted writing even greetings and one's accounts. Certainly, one may write for a public need even when it is not needed for the festival and without an alteration. Bach nevertheless writes that the custom is to take care to write with an alteration. The alteration should be that the actual letters are not as attractive as those one ordinarily writes on a weekday. If the script itself is as attractive as usual but one merely begins writing in a corner of the paper, this is not an alteration. See there. Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, subsection 3; Olas Shabbos, subsection 1; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 5, cite this. In a place without an established custom, one who is lenient and writes without an alteration has not acted improperly. Darkei Moshe, subsection 4; Eliyah Rabbah there. See below, se'if 7.
