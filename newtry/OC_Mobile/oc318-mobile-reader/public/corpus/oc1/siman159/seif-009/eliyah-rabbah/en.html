Tilted on its side, etc. Malbushei Yom Tov explains that it was already tilted before the water was placed in it; otherwise it would be the first clause, where it is valid. Eliyah Rabbah questions this, for in the first clause he keeps it on his knees, whereas here it flows by itself after the initial tilt and his force has ceased. The words of the Shulchan Aruch can be explained as a barrel that became tilted on its own, not by a person. The Bach's forced explanation that it keeps wobbling and pouring from the person's action is not implied by the rishonim or acharonim. The Rama in Darkei Moshe writes that if he places his hands to stop the flow and then lets the water flow onto his hands, doing this for each pouring, it is permitted even with a tilted barrel, for that is human force.
And the water flows, etc. See Beis Yosef. Eliyah Rabbah explains Rabbeinu Shimshon: the issue is not literal human force but force of a giver, for even a monkey is called human force in this context when the water comes through a giver. Thus a tilted barrel in the next seif is valid even if it flows all day by itself, whereas an irrigation channel lacks even the force of a giver. Rabbi Yosei, however, requires actual human force. This supports Beis Yosef and Kesef Mishneh against the Bach's emendations.
And one must return it, etc. If there is a spigot, each flow needs renewed human action or force of giving, for the earlier removal of the plug helps only for the first stream. This differs from a barrel tilted in a manner that the action moves the water itself.
That it is different regarding, etc. The later authorities wondered about this, since the Rosh directly compares the law of a spigot to a tilted barrel. Eliyah Rabbah explains that the Rosh means that if he did not return the spigot it is like a barrel already tilted on its side, not like one tilted by a person, for the first removal does not help for a second flow. This also explains why the Rosh and Tur use the language tilted in Rabbi Yosei's words: Rabbi Yosei does not dispute where a person tilts it, for that is actual human force. Thus the Shulchan Aruch rules plainly that tilting is permitted, while by a monkey he cites those who invalidate.
Since its manner, etc. Eliyah Rabbah does not know the source for limiting this to a monkey; perhaps the Mishnah merely speaks in the normal way and the same would apply to other animals. The Levush may have been answering why the Shulchan Aruch validates a monkey yet earlier rules stringently regarding a spigot, because a monkey seems more like a person. Nachalas Tzvi asks and answers this way, but it is forced, for it is all one dispute between the first Tanna and Rabbi Yosei. The Beis Yosef follows his own view that all agree force of giving from a vessel is required, and therefore the spigot must be returned. The Kesef Mishneh seems uncertain whether other animals have the same law as a monkey.
Nevertheless there is, etc. Olas Tamid cites the Levush as saying that he should wash his hands again without a blessing, but the Levush did not write this explicitly; apparently he understood the Levush's ruling in seif 15 to apply here also. Lechem Chamudos writes that if he has no more water, he may rely on this washing.
And one should be stringent, etc. If he already washed, he should wash again without a blessing, whether the water was given by a minor or by a monkey. This is also implied by Yam Shel Shlomo. See Sema at the beginning of siman 235; and what Magen Avraham challenged from Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 2 is answered in Eliyah Rabbah there.
