(s.k. 3) And these words apply, etc. He wrote in Toras HaBayis, etc. That is to say, Beis Yosef in Shulchan Aruch did not distinguish between the vessels or between the size of the hole. In all vessels the law is that if the hole admits liquid, or is larger than that, like one that lets out an olive or a pomegranate, one may not wash through its mouth, but may wash through the hole. The reason is that he holds that since Rava stated without qualification in Chullin, chapter 25, folio 100, that from a vessel punctured so as to admit liquid one may not wash hands, therefore all vessels have one measure, namely the smallest measure that purifies a vessel from its impurity. This is an earthenware vessel designated for liquids, whose measure for purification is admitting liquid. Although for a shard the measure is emitting liquid, that is because it is not a vessel; but for a vessel we do not find a smaller measure than admitting liquid. This is according to Beis Yosef's understanding of the words of the Rashba and the poskim. Therefore Magen Avraham wrote that this is not the view of the Rashba and the other poskim.
And we do not divide the vessel into two. That is to say, one may not say that it is permitted to wash through the hole but not through its mouth, so that until the hole it is valid, but above the hole it is invalid even through its mouth.
These are the words of Magen Avraham, from what the Rashba wrote above, that we do not divide a vessel into two. Therefore one must say that in such a case he permits even through its mouth.
And this is what he intended briefly, etc. For this is the wording of the abbreviated Toras HaBayis brought by Beis Yosef: A vessel that was punctured with a large hole until it admits liquid, etc., is invalid. In what case, etc.? When it does not receive a revi'is from the hole downward. But if it receives a revi'is from the hole downward, it is valid. In what case? With a vessel whose breakage does not remove it from impurity. But a vessel that was punctured, etc., in a way that removes it from impurity is invalid, because it is not a vessel but broken pieces of a vessel. End quote. Since Beis Yosef holds that if it was punctured so as to admit liquid, or even with a larger hole, one may nevertheless wash through the hole, he cannot say that the latter phrase in what case refers to the adjacent case, namely a hole that admits liquid, that if it is in a vessel whose hole removes it from impurity it is invalid even to wash through the hole. Why should that be? Therefore he explained that it refers to the inference from the beginning, which said that if it was punctured until it admits liquid it is invalid unless it receives a revi'is from the hole downward, in which case it is valid through the hole. This implies that if the hole is less than admitting liquid, it is permitted even if it does not receive a revi'is from the hole downward, and even through its mouth. About this he said, in what case is it valid with such a hole? Only where this hole does not remove it from impurity. But if it removes it from impurity, even if it merely emits liquid it is invalid. Therefore Beis Yosef wrote: It seems that one can find a vessel that leaves its impurity through a hole smaller than admitting liquid, such as where it is a shard, meaning that it was originally a broken vessel, and even a hole that emits liquid invalidates it, as we say at the end of Perek HaMatznia. End quote. The difficulty is evident, for the latter in what case refers to the inference from the beginning.
And this is its explanation, in what case, etc. That is to say, the intent of the latter in what case refers to the adjacent matter, as written in Toras HaBayis.
Even through the hole. For according to the Rashba, we do not divide a vessel into two. If so, the Rashba disagrees with Sefer HaTerumah and Shulchan Aruch in two respects: regarding a vessel designated for foods, where a hole the size that admits liquid does not remove it from impurity, if it was punctured that way, one may wash even through its mouth; and when it is designated for liquids, it is forbidden to wash even through the hole.
And this is the opinion of the Rambam, etc. And this is the opinion of the Tur, etc. That is to say, the Tur first wrote as in Shulchan Aruch, that if it was punctured so as to admit liquid, one may not wash from it unless it holds a revi'is from the hole downward, and then one may wash specifically through the hole. He then interrupted with other laws, and afterward wrote: The Rambam wrote that from a vessel that was broken with a break that purifies it from impurity, one may not wash hands, because it is a broken vessel. End quote. The simple meaning of the Rambam is that even if it holds a revi'is from the hole downward, nevertheless one may not wash from it even through the hole, for he stated without qualification that one may not wash, implying not at all. This is the opposite of the beginning of the Tur's words. Therefore Beis Yosef wondered how the Tur could first write the leniency without qualification, and then cite the Rambam without qualification as though he does not disagree with what he wrote first. Beis Yosef therefore concluded that the Tur interpreted the Rambam's words from the fact that he wrote, a vessel that was broken with a break, etc., and did not write, a vessel that was punctured. Necessarily it speaks where it does not hold a revi'is from the hole downward and is like broken, and in such a case the Tur agrees that one may not wash from it at all. Thus far are Beis Yosef's words. But it still requires explanation why the Tur brought the Rambam's words at all, and what new point there is in the Rambam beyond what the Tur wrote first. Therefore Magen Avraham wrote that the Rambam holds like the Rashba, that the measure of the hole follows the matter of the vessel, and therefore he did not mention the measure of admitting liquid. For this reason the Tur brought the Rambam's words to explain his earlier words, where he wrote that it was punctured so as to admit liquid, because he copied the language of the Gemara, for so Rava said, as above. One should not say that in all vessels its measure is admitting liquid; rather, every vessel follows its purpose, and it is valid to wash through the hole only if the measure of the hole is not enough to purify it from its impurity. If so, what the Tur first wrote, the measure of admitting liquid, necessarily speaks of an earthenware vessel designated for foods.
Like olives, etc. Like pomegranates. That is to say, one olive or one pomegranate, meaning of average size, not large and not small.
And that which the Tur wrote, that designation for olives does not help, etc. The Tur brought the words of Semak, who wrote that if it was punctured so as to admit liquid and he designated it for olives, etc., one may wash from it until it is punctured so as to let out an olive. The Tur wrote about this: It is possible that designation for olives helps only with regard to impurity. But regarding washing, whose measure is admitting liquid, since it was punctured to this measure, it is no longer considered a vessel. End quote. But according to Magen Avraham, if it is designated for foods the Tur agrees that the measure must be like letting out an olive. What difference is there whether it was designated initially for foods or whether he designated it after it was punctured? This also appears from the language of the Tur, that there is no distinction, for he wrote: but regarding washing, whose measure is admitting liquid, etc.
This means washing through its mouth. That is to say, the Tur understood the words of Semak, who permits washing through its mouth, since he stated without qualification and wrote that it is permitted to wash from it. Beis Yosef also wrote this according to one explanation; see there. The Tur disagreed with this, for although regarding impurity, if he designated it for olives and it had a hole that admits liquid, the name of vessel still remains upon it, and even if impurity touched the vessel above the hole it is impure, for the name of vessel applies to all of it, nevertheless regarding washing we require that he wash from the hole. The same applies when it does not hold a revi'is: although regarding impurity the name of vessel remains upon it, not so regarding washing. According to this, for the Rashba, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Rambam, and Tur, in an earthenware vessel designated for liquids, if it was punctured so as to admit liquid, and all the more so if the hole is larger, since it purifies it from its impurity one may not wash from it even through the hole, unlike what is written in Shulchan Aruch. But in this we do not rule like the Rashba, that if it was punctured with a hole that does not purify it, one may wash through its mouth. However, I was uncertain: perhaps according to Magen Avraham, since with a vessel we follow the measure that purifies it, then with a metal vessel, where a break does not purify it as long as the vessel still performs something like its original function, as the Rambam wrote at the beginning of chapter 11 of Hilchos Keilim, and this is from the Mishnah in Keilim, perhaps even if the hole in a metal vessel is large, as long as it performs something like its original function, it is permitted to wash through the hole if it holds a revi'is from the hole downward. See the Be'er Heitev and Taz. The Taz brought in the name of the Bach, who explained the Rambam's words, that a vessel broken with a break that purifies it does not mean punctured, but split; and although at the place of the split it does not admit liquid but emits liquid, nevertheless it is forbidden to wash from it, because that split is worse than a puncture and purifies it from its impurity. The Taz disagreed with him, holding that a split does not purify it from impurity and does not remove it from the category of a vessel regarding washing. See Eliyah Rabbah.
And the Rash brought a Tosefta, etc. Although deficient vessels, etc., is a law by itself and has no connection to what was stated above, the plain sense of the Mishnah implies that even if they hold a revi'is one may not wash from them, even through the place of the break, because a break is worse than a hole and purifies it from its impurity. According to this, one must say that what the Tosefta teaches about a leather bottle, etc., that although they are deficient one may wash from them, is as the Taz wrote in s.k. 8. These are his words: A leather bottle and a kefishah are still vessels, except that they have less than they had originally, meaning that they were diminished from above, etc., and the vessel remained as it was originally, only smaller. But a broken vessel, such as the sides of the vessel, even though it can hold a revi'is, nevertheless the name broken vessel is upon it, etc. End quote; see there.
For it is invalid for sanctification. That which a vessel is required for netilas yadayim was based on the law of the waters of purification, as Magen Avraham wrote in s.k. 1.
And Beis Yosef wrote specifically, etc. He removed the above Mishnah from its plain meaning in order to reconcile it with the above Tosefta of a leather bottle and kefishah, that although they are deficient, etc. He holds that a deficient vessel has the law of a broken vessel, unlike what the Taz wrote.
And it is a wonder in my eyes, etc. That is to say, according to Beis Yosef's own approach, since he holds that one may wash from a broken vessel that holds a revi'is without being supported, Beis Yosef asked himself from the Tosefta of the bottoms of wooden vessels, etc., and forced himself in its interpretation. This was unnecessary, for the Tosefta does not speak at all about a broken vessel, as Magen Avraham wrote afterward.
For we learned: the bottom of a bowl, etc. The Rash explained that bottom means a receptacle on the lower side, etc. These are the words of Rav Ovadiah of Bartenura: When the place of their use broke, a small receptacle remained, and he turns it over and uses it. End quote. However, he wrote a different reason from what is written afterward.
The reason is that it was not made to receive. This reason was written by Magen Avraham from his own reasoning, as in s.k. 4, for this is the opinion of the Rosh and the Rashba. We specifically require that he perform an act on it so that it be fit to receive. Therefore we require specifically that he trim and smooth it, unlike what the Bach wrote in s.k. 4, that mere designation helps. If so, since the Mishnah speaks of the bottom of a bowl in such a case, one may likewise say that the Tosefta that teaches the bottoms of wooden vessels, etc., speaks in such a case, where he wants to use the other side, which has a receptacle, but it was not made to receive. Therefore it requires trimming and smoothing. But from the aspect that it is a broken vessel, it should be valid according to the opinion of Beis Yosef, if it has a receptacle of a revi'is without being supported.
And see Tosafot, Shabbos, folio 16a, s.v. hachi garsinan Rashi, etc. And the beginning of chapter 2 of Keilim, where it is explained that earthenware vessels become impure from their backs, namely that small receptacle on the other side, which was made somewhat for receiving but not for complete receiving. See there in Tosafot and Rav Ovadiah of Bartenura. In any event, since it was made somewhat for receiving, it is better than a sack and resembles a stopper, for which repair helps, as Magen Avraham wrote in s.k. 5.
And in the Tur it implies that even a broken vessel that receives a revi'is, etc. This is how it should be read. This is from the fact that he wrote: A broken vessel or a whole vessel, even if it holds a revi'is, if it cannot stand, etc., without being supported, it is not a vessel. End quote. This implies that if it receives a revi'is without being supported, even a broken vessel is called a vessel in the opinion of Beis Yosef. For the Tur wrote, if it cannot stand, etc.; presumably this also refers to a broken vessel.
And so it appears in Tosafot, that is, in Chullin, folio 107, where they wrote proof for their explanation that the reason a stopper is invalid is because it cannot sit without being supported. These are their words: And so it appears in tractate Yadayim, where it counts a barrel stopper together with the sides of the vessel, which lack no carving, but do not sit without being supported. End quote. This implies that if the sides of the vessels receive a revi'is without being supported, one may wash hands from them.
One can explain according to the plain sense of the Mishnah. That is to say, tractate Yadayim teaches that one may not wash, not with the sides, etc., which Magen Avraham brought above.
