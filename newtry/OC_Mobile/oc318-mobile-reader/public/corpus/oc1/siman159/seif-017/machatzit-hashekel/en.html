(s.k. 17) Tilted on the ground, meaning that it rolled by itself; and see se'ifim 10 and 11. That is to say, he wrote there that if he tilted a barrel, etc., and it went, etc., the washing counts for him, necessarily because this is called the force of one who gives, or human force, as Magen Avraham wrote in s.k. 23. If so, what he wrote here, that if it was tilted the washing did not count, necessarily speaks where it rolled by itself, and even at the beginning there was no force of one who gives. According to this, what Magen Avraham wrote in s.k. 16, that he places it, etc., and shakes it with his knees each time, etc., is not a condition meaning that only if he shakes it each time it counts, but if he did not shake it each time it does not count. For is it worse than tilting a barrel in se'if 10? Rather, Magen Avraham stated the reality, that when the barrel is placed between his knees, its way is that he shakes it each time. In truth, the law of a barrel placed between his knees follows all the more from the law of tilting a barrel in se'if 10, and Beis Yosef did not need to teach the law of its being placed between his knees. Rather, he used the language of the Mishnah, which first teaches that when it is placed between his knees, all agree that the washing counts, and then teaches the case of a tilted barrel and a monkey, in which the Rabbis and Rabbi Yosei disagree according to the Rambam and Tur, each according to his approach, as written in s.k. 23.
