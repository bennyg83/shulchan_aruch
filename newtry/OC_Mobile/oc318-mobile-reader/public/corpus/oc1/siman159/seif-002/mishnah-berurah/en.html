(10) But if he pours, etc. The area below the hole is still considered a vessel because it holds a reviis. Pouring through the hole is comparable to pouring through a spigot, as discussed below in seif 5. This is the view of the Shulchan Aruch, which follows the Tur. Many poskim disagree and hold that an earthenware vessel designated for liquids, once pierced with a hole large enough to admit liquid, is entirely considered a broken vessel and may not be used at all, even through the hole. It differs from a spigot, which was made for that purpose initially. Nevertheless, when no other vessel is available, one may rely on the Mechaber and pour through the hole. Regarding an earthenware vessel designated for food, whose measure for impurity is a hole large enough to release olives, and regarding non-earthenware vessels, which do not lose susceptibility to impurity through a hole admitting liquid, Magen Avraham indicates that all agree one may pour through the hole. According to the conclusion of many later authorities below, siman 162, seif 3, pouring through the hole is permitted only if the hole is somewhat large, so the stream flows continuously. Otherwise it should not be done initially, because it resembles washing in installments. All this applies under pressing circumstances; initially one should certainly seek a whole vessel.
(11) Since it holds a reviis. Some later authorities write that this applies only when the vessel was punctured. If it cracked, even if it holds a reviis below the crack and the crack is only large enough to release liquid, it is invalid, because the crack makes it a broken vessel even regarding impurity, since it is liable to split completely. Taz disagrees and holds that a crack is treated like a hole, and it is invalid only when liquid emerges drop by drop, which is the measure of admitting liquid. Chayei Adam writes that when no other vessel is available one may rely on Taz. Even according to Bach, a copper or other metal vessel, which is strong and will not split completely because of a small crack, is no worse than a punctured vessel. Regarding glass, we wrote in Biur Halachah that even a very fine crack, smaller than one that releases liquid, invalidates the vessel if it can no longer hold hot liquid as it holds cold liquid. Taz writes that whether sealing the hole helps may be learned from Raavad: clay does not help for any vessel, nor do rags; pitch helps specifically for earthenware; and material of the same type as the vessel, metal for metal or wood for wood, certainly helps. Shaarei Teshuvah rules likewise. See Biur Halachah in the name of Nishmas Adam.
