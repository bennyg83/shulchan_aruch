Since it holds a revi'is - See Mishnah Berurah regarding what it cites from Bach concerning a cracked vessel. Chayei Adam explains that the reason is that the entire vessel will ultimately split, while Taz strongly questions the source for distinguishing this case. Mordechai, in the chapter Eilu Devarim, explicitly indicates that there is no distinction between a perforation and a crack, writing: “A vessel that was perforated or cracked and holds a revi'is below is valid.” The same appears in Sefer HaTerumah. This is also implied by the Tosefta, Keilim, Bava Kamma, end of chapter 3, which states that a koz and the Babylonian kod that cracked above but retain the required measure below remain susceptible to tumah. However, the Tosefta at the end of Keilim distinguishes between a hole and a crack regarding tumah: a serving dish or bowl perforated above remains tamei above but is tahor below; if it cracked, then if it can hold hot liquid as it holds cold liquid it remains tamei, and if not it is tahor. Rash cites this, and Rambam codifies it at the end of chapter 12. The plain wording implies that regarding a crack there is no distinction between above and below. Rather, everything depends on whether, because of the crack, it can no longer hold hot liquid. This is common with cracked glass vessels, and the entire baraisa appears to refer to glass vessels. If it cannot hold hot liquid, it is regarded as a broken vessel and is invalid even if the crack is exceedingly fine and does not let liquid out. If it can hold hot liquid, it remains valid. Accordingly, metal vessels, which are strong, are not invalidated by a crack on one side, since they can certainly still hold hot liquid, unless the crack causes a drop-by-drop leak, in which case it is certainly equivalent to a hole large enough to admit liquid. It may be possible to be lenient even with earthenware vessels, for they can often hold hot liquid despite a crack, while Rambam and the Tosefta's case of the bowl refer to glass. This may also explain why Mordechai does not distinguish between a crack and a hole, since he refers to earthenware, and the Tosefta's koz and kod likewise refer to earthenware. One must also consider whether measurement by hot liquid applies only to an article ordinarily used with hot liquid, such as a bowl, as Rash distinguishes in Keilim 3:7 regarding sealing a hole with pitch: sealing is ineffective for an article used with hot liquid but effective for one used with cold liquid. For an article always used with cold liquid, perhaps hot liquid is not the standard at all. All this requires study. The practical conclusion is that a glass vessel that, because of the crack, can no longer hold hot liquid is entirely invalid even if the crack is extremely fine and does not let liquid out. If it can hold hot liquid, it still has the status of a vessel. For other vessels, such as metal, and perhaps also earthenware, the law of a crack is like that of a hole. Regarding sealing, see Mishnah Berurah in the name of Taz; Chayei Adam likewise rules this way, unlike Magen Avraham. See Nishmas Adam. It is obvious that sealing each material with its own kind is considered a valid repair. Clay is ineffective for an earthenware vessel because it was not fired together with the vessel in a kiln. See Rash, Keilim 3:2. Nishmas Adam explains that pitch is not exclusive; there are other substances whose sealing is effective regarding tumah, and the same may apply here. There are also materials that effectively seal other types of vessels. See Rosh Hashanah 19 regarding a glass vessel sealed with lead. Pitch is effective only for earthenware, though this too requires study, for Rash in Keilim chapter 3 cites a Tosefta that pitch effectively seals a metal vessel used with cold liquid. According to Biur HaGra's explanation of that Tosefta, however, it has a different meaning. See Rambam, chapter 20 of Hilchos Keilim, who appears to interpret it as referring to earthenware, and see Raavad there.
