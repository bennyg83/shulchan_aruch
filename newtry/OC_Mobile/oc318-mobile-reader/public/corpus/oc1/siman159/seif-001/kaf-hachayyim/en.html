1) [Seif 1] One may not wash the hands, etc. The Sages supported the requirement of a vessel by comparison either to the water of purification or to the sanctification of the hands and feet in the Beis HaMikdash. Rashba in the name of Halachos Gedolos; Beit Yosef; Magen Avraham, paragraph 1. See below, at the beginning of siman 160.
2) There: All vessels are valid, even vessels made of dung, etc. This is a Mishnah in the first chapter of Yadayim. R' Shimshon explains: 'All vessels,' whether made of wood, bone, or glass, and even vessels made of dung, which are not considered vessels regarding impurity. Beit Yosef.
3) There: All vessels are valid. If a sponge was full of water and one squeezed the sponge and washed his hands from it, the washing is invalid. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 1. He writes that Admas Kodesh, volume 2, siman 5, likewise concludes that a sponge is not a vessel, as we learned in chapter 6 of Parah, and this is obvious. Zechor L'Avraham, paragraph 50; Beis Menuchah, paragraph 3; Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 1; Yafeh LaLev, paragraph 1, cite him. See below, siman 160, paragraph 4.
4) There: Even vessels made of dung. The Rambam, in his commentary at the beginning of chapter 10 of Parah, and R' Shimshon, in chapter 2 of Uktzin, prove that this does not mean, as some explain, a vessel containing a large stone. Tosafos on Menachos 69 likewise prove that it means a vessel made from cattle droppings. Rashbatz, volume 1, siman 121, agrees. See there, where he discusses at length the meanings of an earthen vessel and a barrel cover. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 2. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, similarly writes this in the name of the aforementioned Tosafos; Petach HaDevir, paragraph 1.
5) There: And earthen vessels. This means clay that was not fired in a kiln, but was dried only in the sun or the like, and therefore does not contract impurity. Nevertheless, it is considered a vessel for netilas yadayim. Vessels made of dung or earth must also be whole. One should not think that because they do not contract impurity, a break does not disqualify them. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 2; Mishnah Berurah, paragraph 4.
6) There: It must hold a revi'is. A revi'is here means one-quarter of a log. A complete log equals six eggs, so one-quarter of a log equals one and one-half eggs. Perishah, paragraph 3. See above, siman 158, seif 10 in the gloss, and what we wrote on siman 81, paragraph 3. See also below, siman 160, seif 14.
7) There: If it was perforated with a hole that admits liquid, etc. The view of the Shulchan Aruch in these two seifim follows the Tur, that the measure of a hole that admits liquid applies to all vessels. Regarding impurity, this measure was stated only for a vessel designated for liquids, while the measure for a vessel designated for food is a hole large enough to let out an olive. Other measures are also stated there, as the Rambam, of blessed memory, explains in chapter 19 of Hilchos Keilim concerning the measurements for earthenware vessels. Other measurements are likewise stated for metal vessels in chapter 11. Nevertheless, since Rava mentioned only the law of a hole that admits liquid, we infer that this measure applies to all vessels, even vessels that are subject to immersion. Maran, of blessed memory, writes this in the Beit Yosef in the name of the Sefer Mitzvos Gadol and Tosafos at the beginning of this siman. Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 1; Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 3.
8) There: A hole that admits liquid, etc. If one puts water into the vessel and it drips out drop after drop, it is known that the hole admits liquid. Bach; Taz, paragraph 2; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 2. Mateh Yehudah writes there that when the water runs continuously, the hole certainly admits liquid. But if it does not run continuously, one cannot say with certainty that the hole merely lets out liquid. A hole through which water runs continuously is large and certainly admits liquid, without doubt. When it does not run continuously, the matter remains uncertain and always requires the test of placing the vessel upon water. This is why the Tanna did not state the reverse, 'and if not, it is known that it lets out liquid,' as he states in the other distinctions. Examine this carefully. Ben Ish Chai, parashas Taharos, paragraph 6, writes the same.
9) Know that regarding a vessel perforated with a hole that lets out liquid, the Shulchan Aruch appears to hold that it is not disqualified even when one pours through its mouth and even when it does not hold a revi'is below the hole. The requirements that one pour through the hole and that the vessel hold a revi'is below it apply only when the hole admits liquid. Maran, of blessed memory, writes in the Beit Yosef that this appears from the Rashba's words. He explains that any hole smaller than one that admits liquid does not remove the vessel from its status of contracting impurity and is therefore considered as though the vessel has no hole at all; see there. So rules the Bach; Erech HaShulchan, paragraph 1. The Sefer Mitzvos Gadol and Sefer Mitzvos Katan, however, are stringent. If a vessel was perforated with a hole that admits liquid, one may not wash from it at all, even through the hole. If it was perforated with a hole that merely lets out liquid, one may wash only through the hole, and the vessel must hold a revi'is below the hole, as explained in the Beit Yosef and Bach; see there. One should therefore be concerned for their view and permit use of such a vessel only in pressing circumstances. Regarding the blessing as well, it appears that one should not recite the blessing over netilas yadayim, for we rule that a doubt concerning blessings is treated leniently even against Maran, of blessed memory, and even against the majority, as written above in siman 18, paragraph 7. Instead, he should contemplate the blessing in his heart or recite it without Hashem's Name and Kingship. Ben Ish Chai, parashas Taharos, paragraph 4, writes the same. Likewise, whenever we write below that something is permitted only in pressing circumstances, it appears that one should not recite a blessing over that washing because a doubt concerning blessings is treated leniently. Although netilas yadayim is rabbinic and we rule leniently in a doubt concerning a rabbinic law, one must nevertheless be stringent because of the doubtful blessing, which involves the prohibition of taking Hashem's Name in vain, as written above in the cited paragraph. See also what we write below on siman 160, seif 11, and examine this carefully.
10) There: If it was perforated with a hole that admits liquid, etc. A copper ewer has two openings, one through which liquid is put in and another through which it is poured out. The pouring spout is higher and longer than the opening through which the water is put in. It does not have the law of a perforated vessel, and one may use it to wash his hands for a meal. Ketonet Yosef, siman 8. Maharikash likewise writes in his glosses that one may wash from a vessel with two openings in whichever manner he wishes. He says that he wrote this only because he saw people question the vessel called an ibrik in Arabic; see there. Yad Aharon cites him in Hagahot HaTur and writes: this is unlike the Taz, paragraph 7, who forbids washing from an ibrik and brings a proof from a titros, for there is no proof from there, as one who examines the matter will see. Magen Avraham, paragraph 4, writes that if the spout of the ibrik is shaped like a half-circle, like an open channel, it is invalid, but if it is enclosed it is valid, in accordance with Maharikash. Halachos Ketanos, volume 2, siman 158, agrees. This also appears to be the view of Mateh Yehudah at the end of this siman. Machazik Berachah, paragraph 4, writes that the established custom in Turkey, Eretz Yisrael, and Egypt is to wash from the copper vessel called an ibrik; see there. Erech HaShulchan, paragraph 3, likewise writes that the later authorities agree that a copper vessel with a projecting spout is valid and one may pour from whichever opening he wishes. Beit David, end of siman 72, did not see what the Rambam writes at the end of chapter 4 of Hilchos Beis HaBechirah, that the kiyor had twelve spouts so that all the kohanim engaged in the daily offering could sanctify their hands and feet at once; see there. Beis Menuchah, paragraph 12, writes the same. However, Divrei Menachem in Hagahot HaTur, paragraph 3, writes in the name of his grandfather that an ibrik has two openings, one wide and one narrow, and the narrow opening projects above the wide one. It appears that washing from it is ineffective and the projecting portion must be cut off. Otherwise, one washes from a portion that is not a vessel, for that part can never hold water. Once it is filled to the wide opening, it can hold no more water that would enter the projecting portion. Divrei Menachem agrees with him; see there. Derech HaChaim, paragraph 2, similarly writes that if the spout is higher than the walls of the vessel, it is invalid to wash through the spout; see there. Since the matter is disputed, although Machazik Berachah writes that the custom is lenient, one who fears Heaven should satisfy all opinions. If the spout is higher than the rim, he should pour from the wide opening, which is lower, or cut the spout down until it is lower than the rim and then pour specifically from the spout. If the spout is lower than the rim, he should pour specifically from the spout, because the wide opening above the level of the spout cannot hold water, and he would be washing from a portion that is not a vessel, as explained above.
11) All this applies when the spout is completely enclosed on all sides and can hold water. If the end of the spout is shaped like a half-circle, meaning like half of a tube across its width, and cannot hold water, the following applies. If the spout is higher than the rim and that entire half-circle is above the rim, one may pour from the rim of the vessel, namely the wide opening. But if the spout is lower than the rim, one may not wash from that vessel until he cuts off the half-circle portion and then pours specifically from the spout. The reason he may not use the vessel before cutting the spout is that the rim cannot hold water above the lower spout. Since that portion cannot hold water, it is as though he pours from something that is not a vessel, as explained in the preceding paragraph. The end of the spout, being a half-circle, also cannot hold water and is as though he pours from something that is not a vessel. Therefore, there is no remedy until he cuts off the end of the spout, after which he should pour specifically from the spout. Examine this carefully.
12) Likewise, if the vessel's own rims are uneven, high on one side and low on the other, or if projections extend from them and the gap between one projection and another is large enough to admit liquid, one may not pour from the high place, but only from the low place. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 10. It appears that the same applies if a small portion of the rim broke or developed a crack large enough to admit liquid. One may no longer wash from that vessel, because the area from the top of the rim to the bottom of the break or crack cannot hold water and is not considered part of the vessel. Although the Shulchan Aruch writes that if a vessel was perforated with a hole that admits liquid one may wash through the hole, that case is different. There the hole is below the rim, and when one pours through the hole, the water flows only from the hole. Here, however, the opening is in the rim itself and is wide. When one pours through the opening, the water flows both from the opening and from the rim beside it. That portion of the rim is unfit for washing, as explained above, because it cannot hold water. Therefore, one may not wash from that vessel at all until it is repaired, if possible, unless the break is large enough that all the water will reach the hand through the break alone.
13) There: If it was perforated with a hole that admits liquid, etc. A person must therefore pay attention to copper vessels and similar vessels made from separate pieces joined with tin, for they are prone to develop a hole that admits liquid. Some therefore take care to wash only from a vessel made from a single piece, without a spout, whose rim is even and has no high and low areas. This avoids doubts and also avoids the possibility that it developed a hole that admits liquid without their noticing. In particular, when a person goes to eat at another's home and a vessel full of water is brought before him for washing, he must inspect whether the vessel is valid for netilas yadayim. Perhaps the host is unfamiliar with these laws, and his blessing would be in vain, G-d forbid.
14) There: If it was perforated with a hole that admits liquid, etc. The unqualified words of Maran, of blessed memory, imply that there is no distinction between a hole and a crack, and both have the same measure, a hole that admits liquid. The Bach, however, writes that when a vessel breaks or splits, the entire vessel is considered a broken vessel. Even if liquid does not enter through the break and it only lets out liquid, it is entirely invalid for washing, even if it holds a revi'is below the split. Since this break purifies it from its impurity, it is a broken vessel and completely invalid. This is the ruling we accept, unlike what appears from the Shulchan Aruch. Shiyurei Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot Beit Yosef, paragraph 14; Olas Tamid, paragraph 3; Siddur Beis Oved, paragraph 2; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2, cite him. Although the Taz, paragraph 1, writes that the Bach's ruling has no source, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 12, rules like the Taz, Eliyah Rabbah there partially reconciles the Bach's position; see there. Therefore, a cracked vessel should be permitted like a perforated vessel only in pressing circumstances. So rule Chayei Adam, klal 37, paragraph 7, and Ben Ish Chai, parashas Taharos, paragraph 5. One should not recite a blessing over this washing, as written above in paragraph 9. Ben Ish Chai writes the same there. With regard to a glass vessel, even if the crack is extremely fine and does not even let out liquid, one may not wash from it if it can no longer hold hot liquid as it holds cold liquid. Mishnah Berurah, paragraph 11. However, Mishnah Berurah's distinction there between a crack in a copper or metal vessel and one in other vessels does not appear from the unqualified words of the poskim. Their words imply that they discuss every type of vessel. Therefore, one should be stringent even with a metal vessel, permitting it only when no alternative is possible, and should not recite a blessing, as explained above. Examine this carefully.
15) A gistera, which is a vessel made to catch dripping liquids: Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 1, writes that according to the Rambam, of blessed memory, if it was perforated with a hole that lets out liquid, one may not wash from it. According to Maran, of blessed memory, however, who omits this distinction here in the Shulchan Aruch, the law of a gistera is evidently like that of a whole vessel, and a hole smaller than one that admits liquid does not disqualify it. He writes that this is also the view of the Tur; see there. Therefore, one should permit washing from a gistera perforated with a hole that lets out liquid only in pressing circumstances, and one should not recite a blessing, as written in the preceding paragraph. See below, paragraph 26. See Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 11, who writes that even if only its handle, by which it is held, broke, it is called a gistera; see there.
16) There: A hole that admits liquid, etc. Know that Maran, of blessed memory, appears not to distinguish between an earthenware vessel and other vessels, or between a hole on the bottom and one on the side; all have the same measure. Some authorities, however, say that an earthenware vessel is entirely disqualified by a hole that admits liquid, while for other vessels, when the hole is in their bottom, the measure is a hole that lets out liquid. See Divrei Menachem in Hagahot HaTur, paragraph 2; Nishmas Adam, klal 37; and Taz, paragraph 1. Therefore, when possible, one should be stringent according to this latter view and permit it only in pressing circumstances.
17) There: A hole that admits liquid, etc. If one plugged a hole on the bottom with a rag, it is ineffective, but if the hole is on the side it is effective. If he sealed it with pitch, the repair is effective even though the pitch is not the same material as the vessel, as written in chapter 3 of Keilim. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 4, in the name of his grandfather R' Avraham Azulai's handwritten glosses, citing Zichron Moshe; Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 2. The Taz, paragraph 1, however, writes that plugging with a rag is ineffective even when the hole is on the side, and it does not restore the status of a vessel; see there. Pitch is effective specifically for an earthenware vessel, but not for other vessels; see there. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Siddur Beis Oved, paragraph 2; and Chayei Adam, klal 37, paragraph 3, cite him. Chayei Adam writes that this is unlike Magen Avraham, paragraph 6. Although Mateh Yehudah, end of paragraph 1, writes that since a vessel used for washing is made to hold cold water, sealing it with pitch is effective, unlike the Taz, and Erech HaShulchan, paragraph 2, cites him, nevertheless, as a practical ruling, it appears that one should be concerned for the authorities who forbid it. Responsa Sdei HaAretz, volume 3, siman 3, similarly rules that a copper vessel perforated on its bottom or side may not be sealed with wax, pitch, the adhesive called titkal, dough, wine lees, sulfur, or the like, but only with a type of metal or with tin; see there. Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 1, and Ben Ish Chai, parashas Taharos, paragraph 9, write the same, unlike Beis Menuchah, paragraph 8. See the following paragraph and paragraph 19.
18) If one sealed a copper vessel with tin or an earthenware vessel with clay, it returns to its original valid status. R' Admas Kodesh in the first volume, at its beginning; Birkei Yosef, paragraph 5; Shaarei Teshuvah there. Sefer Sdei HaAretz there, however, disagrees. He writes that sealing a copper vessel with tin is effective, but sealing an earthenware vessel with clay is ineffective; see there. Chesed LaAlafim there and Ben Ish Chai there write the same. One should be stringent accordingly, unlike Beis Menuchah, paragraphs 9 and 10. See the following paragraph.
19) If a wooden vessel was perforated and one forcefully inserted a piece of wood into the hole, the sealing is effective. With a glass vessel, however, no seal can restore its status as a vessel, even one made of tin, lead, or the like. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 10. Regarding Shulchan Aruch HaRav's statement there that sealing an earthenware vessel is effective only with pitch, sulfur, etc., see the Rambam, of blessed memory, chapter 20 of Hilchos Keilim, halachah 3, whose text states the reverse. Therefore, one who fears Heaven should satisfy all opinions and should not wash through the mouth of a perforated vessel unless, if it is a metal vessel, an artisan seals the hole with a piece of metal or tin. If it is a wooden vessel, an artisan should likewise seal the hole specifically with a piece of wood, and the seal must be strong enough that no water emerges at all. Other vessels, such as earthenware or glass, should not have their holes sealed for the purpose of washing through the mouth, so that one does not enter into a dispute. Rather, he should pour through the hole if possible, or use another vessel. If neither is possible, he should not recite a blessing over this washing, but should contemplate the blessing in his heart or recite it without Hashem's Name and Kingship. Therefore, one who purchases an earthenware vessel for washing should check whether it is perforated and the sellers sealed it with lime or clay.
20) Those wooden vessels that are not made from a single piece, but whose bottoms are attached with pitch or titkal, may not be used for washing the hands. This applies even according to the Rosh, of blessed memory, for such a vessel is not comparable to a vessel full of holes. Shiyurei Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot Beit Yosef, paragraph 9; Olas Tamid, paragraph 6; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 6; Chesed LaAlafim, paragraph 2; Beis Menuchah, paragraph 17.
