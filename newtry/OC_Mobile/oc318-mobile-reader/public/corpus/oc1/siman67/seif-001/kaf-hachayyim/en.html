1) [Seif 1] "If one is uncertain whether he recited Kriat Shema, he repeats it and recites the blessings," etc. This is because we rule that Kriat Shema is Torah-level, and regarding any Torah-level doubt one recites the blessing. Beit Yosef in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah; Levush; Bach; Perishah; Taz, seif katan 1. Although above, siman 17, seif 2, we stated that a tumtum and androgynous are obligated in tzitzit because of doubt and should wrap themselves without a blessing, even though tzitzit is Torah-level, Derishah explains that the cases are entirely different. There the doubt is whether the person is subject to the obligation at all, and therefore no blessing is recited because perhaps the person is halachically a woman. Here, however, he is certainly obligated by Torah law to recite Kriat Shema. Since he does not know clearly whether he already recited it, he repeats it and recites the blessings. Knesset HaGedolah writes likewise in Hagahot Beit Yosef. Nevertheless, there remains room to ask: this does not resemble an ordinary birkat hamitzvah, where one blesses over the performance of the mitzvah. Here one does not recite a blessing formulated "to recite Kriat Shema," as explained above in siman 66, letter 27. Why, then, should the blessings be repeated because of the doubtful mitzvah? Responsa Rashba, siman 320, gives another explanation. He writes: It is still difficult regarding Rambam, who explicitly holds that all blessings are rabbinic. Whenever one is uncertain whether he performed the mitzvah, even where the mitzvah itself is Torah-level, or where he is uncertain whether he is obligated at all, such as a child born without a visible foreskin or the eighth day of Sukkot, which is doubtful whether it is the seventh, no blessing is recited. Rambam wrote similarly to the sages of Lunel. Rashba suggests that according to Rambam the original enactment of Kriat Shema was that whenever one is obligated to recite it, he should recite it initially together with its blessings, unless a special circumstance alters the normal order, such as the Temple reading, where only 1 blessing was said. Alternatively, because Kriat Shema is also Torah study, as with Rabbi, who would pass his hands over his eyes and accept the yoke of Heaven through the first verse. Ordinarily, however, when one recites Kriat Shema, he should recite it together with its blessings, just as one who reads after its proper time "has not lost," which the Gemara explains to mean that he has not lost the blessings. Rashba further suggests that Rambam derived this from R' Elazar's statement: if one is uncertain whether he recited Kriat Shema, he repeats and recites it. Since the Gemara simply says "he repeats and recites," this implies that he recites all the paragraphs, even though according to some only the first verse is Torah-level. It did not say that he should repeat only the first verse while omitting the rabbinic remainder. The reason is that once one is obligated to recite Kriat Shema, he recites it according to the basic form of the rabbinic enactment. Rashba adds that he believes he heard this reasoning from his pious teacher regarding why one repeats all the paragraphs and does not suffice with the first verse alone, and perhaps Rambam extended the same principle to the blessings. Kesef Mishneh, chapter 2 of Hilchot Kriat Shema, law 13; Knesset HaGedolah; Olat Tamid, letter 2; Magen Avraham, seif katan 1; Eliyah Rabbah, letter 1. Pri Chadash, however, agrees with the explanation of Knesset HaGedolah. See also Yad Aharon and Maamar Mordechai, letter 2; for practical differences between the explanations, see Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, letter 1. If he knows that he recited the first verse but is uncertain whether he recited the remainder, Magen Avraham writes that Lechem Chamudot, siman 40, appears to hold that he need not repeat it. Yet siman 64, seif 3, implies that he must repeat it. Magen Avraham therefore concludes that the enactment from the outset was that he should repeat. Eliyah Rabbah and Shulchan Aruch HaRav cite this. Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes that the same applies where he knows he recited the first paragraph but is uncertain regarding the second; he must repeat the second. Maamar Mordechai writes that even Lechem Chamudot means only that the blessings need not be repeated; the paragraphs themselves should be repeated without blessings. If he is certain that he recited the first 2 paragraphs but is uncertain whether he recited Parashat Tzitzit and Emet VeYatziv, Bach writes that he must repeat Parashat Tzitzit and the blessing Emet VeYatziv. If, however, he is certain that he recited all 3 paragraphs and is uncertain only regarding the preceding blessings and Emet VeYatziv, he need not repeat the blessings. Taz, seif katan 1; Magen Avraham; Eliyah Rabbah, letter 2, who rejects Olat Tamid's view that one should recite Parashat Tzitzit without Emet VeYatziv. Shulchan Aruch HaRav likewise agrees. Shaagat Aryeh, siman 11, however, rules like Olat Tamid that only Parashat Tzitzit should be repeated, without the blessing Emet VeYatziv. Although Erech HaShulchan, letter 2, disagrees with Shaagat Aryeh, the matter remains doubtful. Therefore in practice it appears that he should recite Parashat Tzitzit and Emet VeYatziv, but should not conclude the blessing with the Divine Name. Rather, he should say "Baruch Ga'al Yisrael," because of the rule that in cases of doubt regarding blessings we are lenient. The same applies at night if he is uncertain whether he recited Parashat Tzitzit and Emet VeEmunah: he should recite them, but should not conclude with the Divine Name. See Shulchan Aruch HaRav and Shaagat Aryeh there. If he is uncertain whether he recited Kriat Shema but knows that he recited Emet VeYatziv, Tosafot write that he does not repeat Kriat Shema. Even if he knows that he merely contemplated Emet VeYatziv, Responsa Beit Yaakov, siman 135, and Eliyah Rabbah, letter 2, rule that he does not repeat. Maamar Mordechai, however, discusses this at length, cites the responsum of Beit Yaakov, disagrees, and writes that only if he knows he actually recited Emet VeYatziv need he not repeat. If he knows merely that he thought it, he must recite Kriat Shema with its blessings. Even where he knows that he recited Emet VeYatziv but is uncertain about Kriat Shema, Maamar Mordechai writes that it is proper to repeat Kriat Shema without the blessings. See also Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, letter 1. Regarding the Torah-level obligation of Kriat Shema, there are several opinions. Some hold that only the first verse is Torah-level; this appears to be Maran's view in Beit Yosef at the end of siman 63. Others hold that the entire first paragraph is Torah-level. Others hold that the first 2 paragraphs are Torah-level as Kriat Shema, while Parashat Tzitzit is Torah-level because of the obligation to remember the Exodus from Egypt. Pri Chadash discusses this at length and inclines to hold that the first 2 paragraphs are Torah-level, bringing proofs. Shaagat Aryeh, siman 2, disagrees and writes that only the first paragraph is Torah-level. Knesset HaGedolah in Hagahot HaTur cites an opinion that all the paragraphs of Kriat Shema are Torah-level. See also Magen Avraham, siman 63, seif katan 8. Although the Sages enacted that one must recite all 3 paragraphs even according to the view that they are not all Torah-level, there is a practical difference for one under great pressure who fears that the proper time for Kriat Shema will pass. As written above in siman 67, letter 52, he should recite Kriat Shema without the blessings, and afterward, when he has time, recite it again with its blessings and pray. If he has sufficient time, he should recite all 3 paragraphs in order to fulfill Kriat Shema in its proper time according to all opinions. If he is pressed, he should recite the first 2 paragraphs. If he is extremely pressed, he should recite at least the first paragraph to the extent he is able. See also what was written at the end of siman 46.
