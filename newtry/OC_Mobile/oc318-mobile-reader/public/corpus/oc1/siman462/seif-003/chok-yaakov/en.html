To knead. Hagahos Ashri, Perek Ein Maamidin, writes: Rabbi Ephraim permitted even bread leavened with the lees of non-Jewish wine, although the lees contain the taste of wine, since the dough itself has no taste of wine and its leavening was caused not by the wine but by the lees, for fruit juice does not cause leavening. At first glance one might infer that dough kneaded with wine lees is not treated as kneaded with fruit juice, but that the lees have the status of water and cause leavening. In truth, however, his words are difficult. Why should the lees not be called fruit juice, since they derive from grapes? Although regarding wine they may be considered mere acidity, as explained above in siman 442 in the responsa cited there, they should nevertheless be classified as fruit juice. His proof from the rule that fruit juice does not cause leavening is no proof at all, because the ordinary use of such lees is to leaven dough kneaded with water, and fruit juice mixed with water causes rapid leavening. Indeed, even regarding the underlying law of non-Jewish wine, his position is rejected by all the authorities and is not accepted, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 123. My learned brother-in-law writes the same in Soles Belulah, and this is the primary ruling, unlike those authorities who distinguish between water mixed in during the harvest and water mixed in afterward. There is no distinction, since it no longer causes unusually rapid leavening, and it is permitted to knead with it. Nevertheless, the dough requires guarding just like dough kneaded with water. Although fruit juice alone does not cause leavening even if it remains an entire day without handling, here there is at least a small amount of water within it, and there is therefore concern that the dough may become chametz at the point where the water is present, while chametz on Pesach prohibits even in the slightest amount.
