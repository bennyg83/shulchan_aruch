Kenesses HaGedolah writes as follows: Raanach, part 2, siman 74, concludes that one who fears Heaven should take into account the opinion that fruit juice without water can cause leavening, and should therefore guard it like any other dough. My father and teacher, of blessed memory, was careful not to eat matzah kneaded with fruit juice except on the last day of Yom Tov. The responsa Tumas Yesharim, siman 108, likewise rules stringently in this matter.
Because it is rich matzah. At first glance this implies that only where it was kneaded with fruit juice alone is it considered rich matzah, but if it was kneaded with an admixture of water it is not called rich matzah. Bach writes this below in siman 471, and Sefer Gevuros Hashem likewise writes that in pressing circumstances one may fulfill the obligation with it. Magen Avraham, however, challenges them below in siman 471, seif katan 1, from the Gemara on page 38, and his words are correct and clear beyond question. This is also explicit in the Yerushalmi, Perek Kol Shaah, halachah 4, and in Tosafos, Rosh, Tur, and the consensus of the later authorities. They explain the baraisa that one may not knead with oil and wine as referring to a case where water was mixed with them. Nevertheless, the Gemara on page 36 challenges Rabbi Akiva's statement that one does not fulfill the obligation with matzah kneaded with oil and wine from the baraisa that permits kneading it on Yom Tov, and answers that one source refers to the first Yom Tov and the other to the second. It follows that on the first Yom Tov, meaning the first nights when one must fulfill the mitzvah of matzah, even an admixture of water renders it unfit. Even smearing it is forbidden on the first nights, as Rif writes explicitly in Perek Kol Shaah, and as Kol Bo writes. This is likewise implied by all the authorities regarding salt; see below, siman 455. They are uncertain whether salt causes the matzah to be called rich matzah even though it was kneaded with water, since the taste of the salt is perceptible. See Magen Avraham in siman 471, who writes that one does not fulfill the obligation of poor man's bread whenever it contains even a small amount of fruit juice, unless it is an extremely small amount, as with the thanksgiving offering discussed on page 38. In my humble opinion, whenever the fruit juice is nullified in sixty so that its taste is not perceptible, it is considered poor man's bread, since its taste has been nullified. The thanksgiving loaves contained less than sixty because that was the prescribed manner of their preparation, and the same law applies where there is more. There is no concern here that leavening should be prohibited even in the slightest amount. The authorities' uncertainty regarding salt is because salt is different, since its taste is strongly perceptible and it is added specifically for flavor. Rif's prohibition against smearing is because it is not mixed into the matzah, remains independently present, and is not nullified. For this reason the universal custom is not to spread anything upon the matzos on the first days, even after baking.
Fruit juice with water. The same applies if it was first kneaded with fruit juice and afterward came into contact with water before being baked, or the reverse. This is implied by the authorities, and Ran writes so in responsa, siman 59. See above, siman 453, seif katan 22, that even if the fruit juice dried within the flour, if water subsequently came upon it the dough can become chametz.
