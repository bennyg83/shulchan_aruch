To wash and to cut hair, etc. This is the text in all editions of the Shulchan Aruch. What it says that he need not wait until night must refer to washing, which is permitted at night. In a similar manner the Beit Yosef explained the words of the Tur, at the beginning of page 376, as mentioned above, unlike Rabbi Moshe Rivkas who deletes the word 'and to cut hair.' His objection that it is not found in the Mordechai or Hagahos Ashiri is not difficult, for the Rama follows his own reasoning in Yoreh Deah, siman 359, seif 3, that one may not cut hair close to evening. He also holds that they derived washing from hair cutting, as he wrote in Darkei Moshe. Rabbi Moshe further wrote that 'he need not wait' means that he should wash in cold water. If he means that the Rama permits only cold water on Erev Yom Tov, he overlooked the Darkei Moshe in Yoreh Deah, who wrote there in the name of Terumas HaDeshen, siman 287, that our practice permits even hot water. If he means Yom Tov itself, where the Rama in siman 511 wrote that the practice is to forbid washing in hot water, and therefore he was troubled by the phrase 'he need not wait,' his explanation that he should wash at night in cold water is difficult. Even without Erev Yom Tov, cold water is permitted on the seventh day of mourning even by day, as the Shach writes in siman 381. In truth there is no difficulty from siman 511, for in such a case even hot water is permitted there. One may also say that the Rama teaches that although the prohibition of washing in hot water on Yom Tov is only a stringency and decree, one might have thought it better to permit washing on Yom Tov than to permit a mourner to wash on Erev Yom Tov. He therefore teaches otherwise. I saw that the Shiyarei Kenesses HaGedolah questioned the Rama in Yoreh Deah for deriving from Mahari Mintz, siman 4, that cutting hair is forbidden until close to night, while in Mahari Mintz it is clear that one may shave the entire day. He therefore pressed to reinterpret the Rama's words that 'close to night' refers to washing and laundering, but hair cutting is permitted. This is not correct for one who examines the language of the Rama there. Moreover, hair cutting is certainly stricter than laundering, and the Bach and all the later authorities show that the Rama's view is to forbid hair cutting, as is explicit in the Rama and Levush here. He was also not precise, for the Rama relied on Mahari Mintz only that on Erev Pesach it is better to shave before midday. Regarding other Erev Yom Tov cases he ruled explicitly like Mahari Weil, siman 10, that hair cutting is forbidden until evening, as is clear in Darkei Moshe to Yoreh Deah. In my work on Yoreh Deah I also concluded that the Rama's words are correct: on Erev Pesach one may wash immediately after midday. The Shach, who challenged him, was not precise in this, for he understood that the Rama derived it from Mahari Mintz and therefore questioned him. This is not so, for in this matter the Rama ruled like the Tashbetz brought by the Beit Yosef and Or Zarua, as he writes explicitly in Darkei Moshe. What the Shach wrote, that the Bach omitted the Rama's statement that on Erev Pesach everything is permitted, is also imprecise, for he relied on what he later wrote in the name of the Tashbetz that it is permitted. All this is clear to one who examines it. The conclusion is that if the seventh day falls on Erev Yom Tov, everything is forbidden until close to night, and close to night he may cut his hair and wash in hot water. On Erev Pesach, however, he may cut his hair before midday, and wash and launder immediately after midday.
