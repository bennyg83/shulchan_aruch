Two hundred eighty-two and two-thirds cubits, and so forth. It requires study where the total distance between the two outer towns is exactly two hundred eighty-two and two-thirds cubits, but the middle town is not positioned precisely midway and instead inclines toward one side. Were it viewed as standing in a single line between them, the distance from it to one town would be less than one hundred forty-one and one-third cubits, while the distance to the other would exceed that measure. In such a case, perhaps the three cannot be considered one town, because the open space on one side exceeds one hundred forty-one and one-third cubits. That village would therefore not join, leaving only two towns, and the rule of viewing them as adjacent despite a distance of two thousand cubits does not apply to two towns. Two towns receive only one hundred forty-one and one-third cubits, as explained in seif 7. Alternatively, since the total space is no more than two hundred eighty-two and two-thirds cubits, perhaps even though the middle town inclines somewhat to one side, we view it as though it were placed precisely in the center. It may not be necessary for it to stand exactly opposite the midpoint. The Mishnah’s wording, “If there are one hundred forty-one and one-third cubits between the two outer ones,” implies that it must be precisely centered, so that neither side exceeds that measure. Rambam, however, states generally that there must be two hundred eighty-two and two-thirds cubits between them, which appears to imply that the exact placement is immaterial, provided that the total distance does not exceed the measure. We would then view the middle town as though it stood exactly between the other two. This requires study. I later found that Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 94, addresses this question and appears to require that the middle town be exactly centered, equally distant from both. His statement there that a town shaped like the Greek letter gamma is not squared, and that one merely draws a diagonal line like the chord of a bow, is incorrect. Rashba in his newly printed novellae and Ritva state explicitly that it is squared, as the questioner there maintained. It is surprising that he did not consult Ritva.

Beyond all three of them. See Mishnah Berurah, where we explained that even for the middle town the two thousand cubits are measured outward from the two outer towns. Ritva writes this, and the commentators likewise explain Rambam, from whom Shulchan Aruch copied this ruling. In my humble opinion, Rambam may mean that even for the outer towns the boundary is measured two thousand cubits from the middle town and beyond. All three are considered a single town with three corners. One who lives at one corner may walk to either of the other corners and then two thousand cubits beyond it. This accords precisely with Rambam’s language: “The three of them are considered one city, and the distance is measured in every direction beyond all three.” The commentators, however, did not mention this interpretation.
