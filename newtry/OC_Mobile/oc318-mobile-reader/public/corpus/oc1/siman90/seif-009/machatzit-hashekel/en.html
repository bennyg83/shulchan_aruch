(Note 9) And this is the opposite of, etc. — for the main point is, etc., that in their days the entrance to the Temple was in the western wall and they would pray and turn their faces toward the east; and therefore one who stands outside on the side of the entrance — that is, on the west side — with his back to the synagogue and his face toward the side opposite him, and if so his face is toward the west, he certainly appears like a heretic according to all, for there are two matters of ugliness: one, that his back is to the synagogue; two, that he turns his face to a side other than that toward which the congregation standing in the synagogue turns. And when he stands on the west side and turns his face toward the Temple — that is, toward the east — there are two matters of propriety: that his face is toward the Temple and toward the side toward which the congregation turns, that is, toward the east — it is certainly permitted according to all, and they disagree only about one who stands in the east of the Temple. For according to one view, the main thing that makes him appear like a heretic is that his face turns to a side other than that toward which the congregation turns; but as to his back being to the synagogue, for this they are not concerned. And if so, when he stands on the east side and turns his face toward the east, even though his back is to the synagogue, they are not concerned. But when he turns toward the west, even though his face is toward the synagogue, since he turns to a side other than that toward which the congregation turns, it is like a heretic. In the second view the law is reversed: everything depends on his back not being to the synagogue; but as to his turning to a side other than that toward which the congregation turns, they are not concerned. And Terumat HaDeshen wrote, and this is its language: and the same is the law when he stands in the Temple — he may not pray toward the west; only he is not called wicked since he stands in the Temple and joins the congregation, end quote.
