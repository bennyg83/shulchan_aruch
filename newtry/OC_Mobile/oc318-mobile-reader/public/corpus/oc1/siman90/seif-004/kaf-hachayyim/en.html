19) [Se'if 4] One must open openings, etc. Maran writes in Kesef Mishneh, Hilchos Tefillah 5:6, in the name of a responsum of Rambam, that synagogues and places designated for communal prayer do not require windows. The statement that one should pray only in a house with windows concerns an individual praying in his home, as Daniel did. According to Zohar, Pekudei 251a, however, even a synagogue requires windows: 'All these windows and apertures stand to gather all the prayers that ascend from below to above, to attend to them, and to bring them before the Holy One, blessed is He. Therefore, any synagogue without windows is not a proper place to pray, for the synagogue below corresponds to the synagogue above. The synagogue above has windows, as we said; so too below. Above, Knesses HaGedolah has twelve supernal windows; so too this lower synagogue. Each corresponds to the other, for the worlds stand in corresponding form, and the Holy One, blessed is He, raises His glory through all.' Maran here in Shulchan Aruch also appears to hold this view, since he rules without qualification. Moreover, his subsequent words, 'It is good for a synagogue to have twelve windows,' imply that his initial requirement to open openings applies wherever one prays, whether at home or in a synagogue. It also implies that initially any number of windows may be opened, while afterward he says that a synagogue should preferably have precisely twelve, as stated in Zohar. Levush's words are likewise; see there. See Nehar Shalom 3 and Yafeh LaLev 4 on his words; examine carefully.
20) There: One must open openings or windows, etc. Rashi explains that the windows cause one to concentrate: he looks toward heaven and his heart becomes humbled. Tur writes that according to this explanation they must open toward the direction in which he prays. Beis Yosef cites this and writes that Rambam likewise rules in Hilchos Tefillah 5 that one must open openings or windows toward Jerusalem so that he may pray facing them. The students of Rabbeinu Yonah write that perhaps the reason is that seeing light settles his mind, enabling him to concentrate properly. Beis Yosef cites them; see there. See also below, siman 95:8. According to the Zohar cited in the preceding paragraph, however, the reason is neither peace of mind nor looking outward, but a mystical one. A practical difference is that even a blind person must pray in a synagogue with windows. Zohar, Beshalach 59b, in Raaya Mehemna, further states: 'That synagogue should have windows, as it is written, "Its windows were open," corresponding to above. Of this it says, "He looks through the windows, peers through the apertures." You might say that even a field suffices so the spirit can ascend. It is not so, for we require a house. A house below must be found corresponding to the supernal House, so that the supernal dwelling may descend to the lower dwelling. Furthermore, that prayer and that spirit must ascend and emerge from confinement by a straight path toward Jerusalem,' etc.; see there. This implies that according to Zohar too, the windows must face Jerusalem.
21) There: So that he may pray facing them. Accordingly, here in our holy city Jerusalem, may it be rebuilt and established, where some pray facing east and others facing north, etc., in order to direct themselves toward the Beis HaMikdash, the windows must be made on the side they face in prayer: if they face east, on the east; if north, on the north; and so forth.
22) There: It is good for a synagogue to have twelve windows. Lechem Chamudos, at the end of the chapter Ein Omdin, writes that these certainly need not all face Jerusalem. The reason given also makes clear that they should open on all sides. Eliyah Rabbah 3 cites him. Solel Boneh in Makor Chaim 1 writes that these twelve windows correspond to the twelve permutations of the Divine Name and that it is preferable to have three on each side. Ateres Zekeinim 3 likewise writes that his grandfather, the gaon, had a synagogue in the holy community of Vienna and built twelve windows in it, three on each side. Chesed LaAlafim 2 writes likewise.
23) If one has no house with a window, but only a dark house or a field, the dark house is preferable, for praying in a field is brazen. Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham 4.
24) On the night of Yom Kippur, during Maariv, one should not close the synagogue windows with boards, because they must face Jerusalem. Pri Megadim, Mishbetzos Zahav, end of paragraph 2.
25) Zohar, Vayakhel, implies that one should not stand directly opposite an open window to pray. Rather, the window should be sealed with glass, or he should stand somewhat distant from it. Rabbi Akiva Eiger's glosses. The custom is to build synagogue windows higher than the height of any person. Chasam Sofer, Orach Chaim 27; Pischei Olam, end of paragraph 7.
