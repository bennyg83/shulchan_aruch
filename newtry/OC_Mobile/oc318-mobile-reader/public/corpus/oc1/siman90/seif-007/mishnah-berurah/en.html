Behind the synagogue. Responsa Yad Eliyahu, siman 7, wrote that what was said about one who prays behind a synagogue applies only when the congregation is praying the Shemoneh Esrei and he too is praying the Shemoneh Esrei; see there. Ma'amar Mordechai disagrees.
The congregation. Meaning, it speaks of one praying on the side where the entrance is open, namely the west. Therefore, if he turns his face toward the synagogue, he is praying in the direction in which the congregation prays, and is not called wicked. But if he turns his face away from the synagogue, he is praying opposite the congregation, who pray eastward while he prays westward, and therefore he is called wicked, for it appears like two authorities, Heaven forbid. According to this explanation, if he stands behind the synagogue on the eastern side and turns away from the synagogue while praying eastward, as the congregation also prays, he too is not called wicked.
Opposite. This is the view of Ri cited in Tosafot there. He explains 'behind the synagogue' as behind the eastern wall, where there are no entrances. When one stands behind that wall with his back toward the wall, even though his face is eastward and he is praying in the direction that the whole congregation prays, he is nevertheless called wicked, for he is among those about whom it says, 'their backs were toward the Temple of Hashem.' But if he turns his face toward the synagogue, even though he is found praying westward while the whole congregation faces east, we have no concern, because the whole congregation prays toward the eastern wall where the ark is, and his face is also toward that wall.
Both. He should pray only on the west and turn his face toward the synagogue. Although this is permitted according to all views, it is permitted only in pressing circumstances; otherwise it is a mitzvah to pray in the synagogue, as stated in seif 9.
On other sides. Namely north or south. It speaks where he cannot turn his face eastward, such as where there is excrement before him as far as the eye can see; otherwise it is better that he turn his face eastward. Nevertheless, even all this is permitted only in pressing circumstances, as written in the previous seif katan.
That he turn his face. So that his back not be toward the synagogue. Although he still does not pray in the direction in which the congregation prays, since he is not praying opposite them we have no concern. All this applies when he stands north or south of the synagogue. But when he stands east or west of it, it is forbidden even if he wants to turn himself north or south.
Toward the synagogue. The same applies to courtyards built on the eastern wall, where it is permitted to pray, for it is a room and does not appear as if his back is toward the synagogue. So wrote Kenesses HaGedolah, that he saw many communities whose courtyards were on the east side, and they prayed with their faces eastward and their backs toward the synagogue's eastern wall. Nevertheless, when building courtyards, it is better to make them on another side and not the east, for some question this; see Eliyah Rabbah.
