A small object is not considered, etc. The Beit Yosef, in the name of Abudraham, brings proof: if so, how do people pray in the synagogue before the benches on which they sit? Regarding our benches, which are ten high and four wide, one must say that we consider them fixed objects like a cabinet or chest. However, a shtender requires study; see Magen Avraham and Taz. A bench in a house is called non-fixed and is an interposition, as the Agudah writes. The Rokeach's language is: a bed, chair, and table that are not fixed interrupt. This escaped the Taz; see there. Regarding our beds designated for sleeping, the Beit Yosef wrote that they do not interrupt, while the Bach, Taz, and Nachalas Tzvi disagree and bring proofs, but one who examines them will see that they are easily rejected. It seems to me to distinguish: a bed that usually remains always in one place does not interrupt, as opposed to a bed moved from place to place. The Taz wrote that if it is not easy to pray another way, such as where ten pray in some room and not everyone can stand without an intervening object, one should not enter another room because of this, since an interposition is not prohibited but only affects the preferred form of the mitzvah. Nevertheless, he should close his eyes or pray from the siddur and not look outside.
And a person does not intervene, etc. The Beit Yosef brings proof from the Gemara's statement that one should not pray behind his rebbe, implying that behind another person we have no concern. The Bach writes that this can be rejected: behind one's rebbe there is a prohibition, but behind another person there is no prohibition, though initially one should be careful because of interposition. One must say that this is the view of those who say a person intervenes. But it is surprising, for how will he explain the Gemara 27, which says that Rabbi Yirmiyah would pray behind Rav because he was a disciple-colleague, showing that even initially he prayed behind a person? Therefore it seems to me to explain that it does not mean literally behind him, but rather close to his rebbe and not standing equally with him. This is Rashi's explanation: not opposite his rebbe, beside him, appearing as if they are equal; and not behind his rebbe, for that is like arrogance. Literally behind him is not arrogance. Rather, it means that he stands beside him but somewhat behind, which is still arrogance because he stands at his side. But directly behind another person is always an interposition. Tosafot did not understand Rashi this way, and therefore wrote that others explain it as appearing to bow to his rebbe. In my humble opinion Rashi was correct not to explain that way, for 'behind' does not mean literally behind, as we said.
