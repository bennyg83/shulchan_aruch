Such as an ark or a platform, and so forth. The Gemara teaches: Abba Binyamin said, 'Regarding two matters I was troubled all my life, one of them that my prayer should be before my bed.' What does 'before my bed' mean? If it means literally in front of my bed, Rav said that nothing should intervene between one who prays and the wall. Rather, it means that my prayer should be close to my rising from bed. Rashi explains that he would perform no work from the time he rose from bed until he prayed. Tosafos wrote concerning the rule that nothing should intervene that a fixed object, such as an ark or platform, is not considered an interruption, whereas a bed does not appear fixed. Beit Yosef wrote that this applies specifically to the beds in the era of the Sages of the Gemara, which were used for sitting, but our beds, which are used for sleeping, are fixed. This is difficult, for the Gemara's explanation that his prayer was close to his bed means after he rose from his bed in the morning. Why, then, did the Gemara not interpret the statement literally, as the questioner did, with reference to a sleeping bed? One cannot answer that in the era of the Gemara the same bed was used both for sleeping and sitting, and was therefore not fixed because it had to be moved at mealtime. In Bava Metzia, in the chapter HaMekabel, the law concerning the property left for a debtor states that he is given two beds, one for eating and one for sleeping. Why, then, did the Gemara not explain it literally as referring to the sleeping bed? It therefore appears to me that this distinction is incorrect, and every bed constitutes an intervening barrier. Regarding Tosafos' distinction between an ark or platform and a bed, one must understand the difference. The synagogue platform is also moved at times, as we find in Taanis that during a drought fast they would bring the platform into the city square. Rather, it appears that these objects serve the needs of prayer in the synagogue, and therefore the concept of an intervening barrier does not apply to them. This is why it is customary in all synagogues for each person to have a lectern before him upon which he places his siddur, and it is not considered an intervening barrier at all, even if it is ten tefachim high and four tefachim wide. This is because it serves a prayer-related need. A bed and other utensils that do not serve prayer-related needs, however, are not considered fixed for purposes of prayer and do constitute a barrier. For the same reason, it appears to me that a table in one's home is also not considered an interruption or barrier to prayer, because the table serves a prayer-related need when one places a sefer upon it. Nevertheless, even regarding an object that intervenes between him and the wall, when it is not readily possible to pray otherwise, such as when ten people sometimes pray in a room and not everyone can stand without an object between himself and the wall, one should certainly not delay prayer for this reason and go to another room. The reason for avoiding such a barrier is that it may disturb concentration, as Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Rambam. It is only an enhancement of the mitzvah and is not actually forbidden when crowding makes it necessary. It nevertheless appears that one can remedy this by closing his eyes during prayer, as stated in seif 23, or by praying from a siddur and not looking outward, so that he will not lose concentration. This appears correct.
