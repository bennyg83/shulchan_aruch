Beit Yosef wrote, and the same applies to our bed made for lying — it is called fixed; and the Bach disagrees and holds it is not called fixed, since sometimes they take it and set it in another place, and he proved this from Tosafos there. And Magen Avraham refutes his proof there, and the Peri Chadash agreed with the Bach, not like Magen Avraham there. And see the Taz, who challenged Beit Yosef why he does not answer as plainly as the questioner holds, and it is discussed regarding a bed made for lying there, and so too in Peri Chadash there. And I did not understand: if so, what was he boasting all my days — "I was distressed," meaning I was careful and cautious, etc. — what is this caution? Would it not have been better if he had prayed at the wall? And it is simple.

(and four handbreadths wide). And it requires investigation, for the custom is to pray before the shtender on which siddurim are placed, and it is ten handbreadths high and four wide — Magen Avraham there. And the Taz wrote: all things that are for the need of prayer in the beit hakneset — interposition does not apply; and therefore the custom is that each one has a shtender even though they are ten handbreadths high and four wide. And for this reason it appears that a table at home is also not considered an interruption and interposition for prayer, for the table is a need of prayer on which one places the book. Nevertheless it appears that even regarding something that interposes between him and the wall, if it is not easy to pray in another place — such as sometimes when they pray on Yom Kippur in some room and it is not possible for every person to stand without interposition to the wall — certainly one should not delay prayer for this and go to another room to pray, for this is only a mitzvah from the preferred manner and there is no prohibition when it is necessary because of crowding of the place. Nevertheless he should close his eyes or pray from within the siddur and not look outside, so that he not come to nullification of intent — so far the Taz.
