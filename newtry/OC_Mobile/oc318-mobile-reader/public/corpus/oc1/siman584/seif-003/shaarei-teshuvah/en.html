Midday, see Baer Heitev. Shevut Yaakov, vol. 1, siman 140, wrote regarding one who had a slight ailment and could not go to synagogue. His children and household pressed him to have the shofar blown for him and then eat, but he refused, and because of their pressure he vowed not to eat until after the time when people leave the synagogue. When they completed their prayer and wanted to leave, a sage came and preached words of rebuke for an hour or two, delaying their departure. He wrote that at first glance one may say that in vows we follow common speech, and in common speech his intent was the time when people leave the synagogue, meaning after the time of prayer; Torah study time is separate, and he did not have this in mind. Even if he had been healthy and gone to synagogue, he could have left after the time of prayer, for we rule like R. Yehoshua at the end of chapter 2 of Beitzah, who says "half for God," etc. If so, perhaps the intent of the one who vowed was only that he should not eat earlier because of his ailment, but not that he should delay so long. However, it can be compared to what is written in Rosh Hashanah 30, where R. Zeira asks: if they stirred to stand, etc. Therefore in our case too, where the people wanted to leave but did not leave, there is a similar doubt. Since the question remained unresolved, doubtful vows are treated stringently; and if they wish to release him, they should release him according to the law of vows needed for Yom Tov; see there. I do not understand the proof from Rosh Hashanah. There, as long as they were still sitting, the time of the court had not ended; here, by contrast, their failure to leave was a delay not caused by the synagogue departure time after the completion of prayer, but by another matter that delayed them. Why should the lenient reasoning he first wrote be displaced, that his intent was only the time of departure from prayer, and that time had already ended? Therefore it appears that if he has no one to release him, one may be lenient in this, especially for a person with an ailment who finds it hard to fast until the actual time they leave. See below siman 652 in the Magen Avraham, that even tasting is forbidden to one traveling who expects to reach a place with a lulav; he should wait until midday, but should not fast more than half the day; see there. It appears that nowadays, when the custom on Rosh Hashanah is to delay well beyond midday, if one has no shofar he too must wait, unless the fast is very difficult for him. See there in Shulchan Aruch regarding one who forgot and ate, etc.; one may derive from there to here, though there is some room to distinguish according to the distinctions written by Tosafot in Sukkah and Shabbat. See siman 232, 432, and 692; I have been brief.
(*) In Shulchan Aruch, seif 1: and they lengthen the piyyutim, etc. Anyone who can should take time to study the explanation of the piyyutim before praying. See above siman 100, where the Taz wrote that with piyyutim one must arrange his prayer beforehand; see there.
In the piyyut "Melekh Elyon," the Mateh Moshe wrote that one should say "melekh evyon balah verad shachat," meaning "balah" with a bet, not as printed with a kaf. Eliyah Rabbah cites this; see there.
