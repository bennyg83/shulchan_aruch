And there should not be an intervening object. See Taz, who holds that when the object is less than three tefachim high, even standing upon a utensil is permitted. See siman 90 concerning this, since it is subordinate to the ground. The reason given here, that three tefachim constitutes elevation rather than an intervening object, applies to a raised mound of earth and the like, where the concept of an intervening object is inapplicable and only elevation applies. Here the Mechaber also says that one should bear in mind that his prayer resembles the Temple service. Perishah, section 2, and Levush here, however, imply that even upon utensils lower than three tefachim, when there is no fear of falling, it is nevertheless forbidden, similar to the Temple service, where an intervening object is forbidden. Still, the grasses spread on Yom Kippur do not constitute an intervening object, because they are subordinate to the ground. This differs from a utensil, which is significant and does not become subordinate. See Bach. Peri Chadash writes that according to this, one should be permitted to pray only barefoot, yet “he placed socks on his feet and prayed,” and one may not pray barefoot except on Tisha B'Av. See siman 51, seif 5. He brings a proof from the first chapter of Shabbos, page 2, from Rashi's discussion of kohanim. There, however, socks would in any event be forbidden because they constitute an extra garment, for the verse prescribes trousers “from the hips to the thighs.” Rather, that passage means that because they walked upon a marble floor, they did not place boards or the like upon it to prevent it from being so cold, since an intervening object between them and the floor was forbidden. Our socks, by contrast, are subordinate to the body and do not constitute an intervening object. See Magen Avraham, siman 76, letter 6, who writes that a sandal is subordinate to the body. See there. In the phrase beginning “This applies,” the four words “there it is not applicable, etc.” are a scribal error.
