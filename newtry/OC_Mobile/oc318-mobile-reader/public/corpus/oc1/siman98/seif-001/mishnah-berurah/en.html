(1) The meaning of the words: A person should not contemplate Divine Names and mystical unifications, but should pray according to the plain meaning, understanding the words with heartfelt concentration, unless he has entered the secrets of Hashem and knows how to direct them with inner desire and awe. Otherwise, Heaven forbid, he causes great damage through this. See Magen Avraham in the name of the Zohar and Responsa Maharshal, siman 98, which discusses this at length and records that Rabbi Shlomo said that after learning the secrets of Kabbalah he prayed like a newborn child. See Penei Yehoshua at the beginning of the chapter Ein Omdin, who writes that the intentions explained here in the Shulchan Aruch cannot be contemplated during prayer. Rather, they should be contemplated beforehand, while during prayer one must concentrate on the meaning of the words.
(2) He should remain silent, etc.: Sefer HaGan writes that to dispel an improper thought during prayer, one should say three times, “Pi, Pi.” These are initials for Palti and Yosef, who overcame the inclination of their hearts, as stated in Sanhedrin 21b. He should afterward spit three times, not fully, but gently, with his tongue between his lips while spitting, and the thought will certainly depart. Magen Avraham writes regarding this: “It does not appear proper to me to do this during the Amidah, for it constitutes an interruption, and who knows whether it is a proven remedy?” Eliyah Rabbah cites Kitzur Shelah as follows: A remedy for removing an extraneous thought is that before prayer one should pass his right hand over his forehead three times and say each time, “Create for me a pure heart, God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me.” If an extraneous thought comes to him during prayer, he should pause briefly, pass his right hand over his forehead, and contemplate that verse.
(3) His young children: The Shelah strongly protests against those who bring children to the synagogue. This refers to children who have not yet reached the age of education, because they play and jump in the synagogue, desecrate its sanctity, and disturb the concentration of those praying. Moreover, when they grow old they will not abandon the bad habit in which they were trained in childhood, namely behaving wildly and treating the sanctity of the synagogue with contempt. Once a child has reached the age of education, however, the opposite is true. His father should bring him to the synagogue and teach him the ways of life, to sit with awe and fear. He should not allow him to leave his place and should urge him to answer Amen, Kaddish, and Kedushah. See there, at the end of the laws of prayer and Torah reading. See also Tanna DeVei Eliyahu, part I, chapter 13, regarding the severe punishment of a father who allows his son to answer with idle and foolish speech in the synagogue.
