Maariv. The ma'aravos belonging to the second night are recited even when the first day falls on Shabbos. On other festivals, when the first day falls on Shabbos, the ma'aravos of the first day are recited on the second night and those of the second night are deferred, as Magen Avraham writes that this is the primary ruling of the Gemara at the end of Sukkah, where it states that “Yimotu” is deferred, contrary to the custom of Posen. Nevertheless, on the second day of Pesach one always recites the ma'aravos belonging to that day, because of “the firstborn” and the counting. This is the accepted custom.
We begin counting the Omer. Why do we recite a blessing over counting the Omer on Yom Tov without concern that this will lead people to treat the second day of Yom Tov lightly? For this very reason no blessing is recited over the sukkah on the eighth day, lest one say that it is not the eighth day but the seventh, which is Chol HaMoed, since a blessing is being recited over the sukkah. Here too, one might say that it is Chol HaMoed because a blessing is being recited over the counting, and not the possible first day, for the Torah states, “You shall count from the day after the Shabbos,” meaning the day after Yom Tov. See Magen Avraham, who cites Kesef Mishneh's resolution of this question. See also Temim De'im, siman 241, in the Raavad's glosses; Ran, chapter Lulav VaAravah; and Maharshal, responsum 68, who offer other answers. See also Taz at the end of siman 649. One may further distinguish that there we are concerned that people may treat the day lightly and say it is still Chol HaMoed, the seventh day of Sukkos, because they recite the blessing of dwelling in the sukkah. Here, however, we are not concerned that they will say it is Chol HaMoed and perform melachah, for they have not yet recited Havdalah over the first day of Yom Tov, and its original prohibition remains until Havdalah. We are not so concerned that they will also recite Havdalah contrary to the enactment and custom of the Sages.
Upon each individual. Women and servants are exempt because this is a positive time-bound mitzvah, as is clear from all the poskim. Nevertheless, women have already accepted it upon themselves as an obligation. So writes Magen Avraham.
To count for himself. Agudah writes in Menachos that “you shall count for yourselves” means that each person must count, and the shaliach tzibbur may not count on behalf of everyone. This is also the implication of the Gemara and the early poskim, and Rashba states it explicitly in responsum 126. Although hearing is equivalent to answering, that applies only to the blessing, as Rama writes in seif 3, based on the Rashba cited by Beit Yosef. The counting itself is expressly derived from the verse, “you shall count,” meaning that each person must count, just as “you shall take” concerning lulav is expounded to require each person to take for himself. The Tosefta at the beginning of Lulav HaGazul likewise implies this. Although most poskim hold that counting in our time is D'Rabbanan, the Sages enacted it in the form of the Torah law. This is clear from the poskim and is also written by Levush and Maggid Mishneh. Levush ultimately concludes, based on the Rashba cited by Beit Yosef, that one fulfills the obligation through the shaliach tzibbur's counting. This is incorrect, for Rashba refers only to the blessing, as he states explicitly in responsum 127. Beit Yosef's expression “through the shaliach tzibbur's counting” is not exact, as is evident from the beginning and end of his discussion. Rama's ruling in seif 3, which is based on that Rashba, likewise clearly refers to the blessing.
Standing. After the fact, if he counted while seated, he has fulfilled his obligation.
And recite the blessing. Radbaz, responsum 27, explains why a blessing is recited over this counting but not over a niddah's or zavah's counting: counting seven clean days is not an unavoidable mitzvah. If she does not wish to return to her husband, enter the Mikdash, or eat sacred foods, but prefers to remain impure, she may do so; therefore she does not recite a blessing over her counting. This is difficult, for by that reasoning how can she recite a blessing over immersion, and how can one recite a blessing over shechitah and many similar mitzvos? Once she wishes to immerse for her husband, she must count and immerse, so why should she not recite a blessing? Moreover, according to the opinion that immersion at its proper time is a mitzvah, what can be said? The explanation of the early authorities is therefore primary, as Tosafos in Kesubos 72 and other poskim write: if she sees blood, her counting is retroactively nullified, and therefore she does not recite a blessing. Radbaz answers that although one recites Aneinu at Shacharis on a private fast even though an unforeseen event may occur and interrupt the fast, it nevertheless remains a partial-day fast, whereas a niddah's counting is entirely nullified. In my humble opinion, no answer is needed, for an unforeseen event is uncommon, whereas blood is common in a woman, who is full of blood.
First. A Shehecheyanu is not recited over counting because it contains no joy or pleasure. So write the early authorities. Maharil gives another reason. See Levush, Ran at the end of Sukkah, and Radbaz, siman 296.
And he counts the days and the weeks. If he counted the days but not the weeks, the consensus of the later authorities is that he has fulfilled his obligation after the fact. If he counted the weeks but not the days, Magen Avraham writes that he has fulfilled his obligation, as the Tur writes. His intent is as stated in the name of Avi HaEzri: on the eighth day he may say only, “Today is one week and one day,” and need not say, “Today is eight days,” because he already counted those days in the previous week. The same applies on the ninth day and onward, until the fourteenth day, when he says, “Today is fourteen days, which are two weeks.” On the fifteenth day he says, “Today is two weeks and one day,” and so forth. If, however, on the seventh or fourteenth day he did not mention the days, he may not have fulfilled his obligation. My brother-in-law in Eliyah Zuta writes this in the name of Ri. Noda BiYehudah, first edition, records two opinions regarding one who counted the days through initials, such as “Today is fourteen days” written or pronounced by abbreviation: some say he did not fulfill the obligation, and others say he did. It appears to me that in such a case he should count again without a blessing, since according to the consensus of most poskim counting the days and weeks in our time is only D'Rabbanan. Magen Avraham writes that one must count specifically in a language he understands; otherwise it is not considered counting at all.
Today is the first day of the Omer. The consensus of the later authorities is that one should not say “that today.” In truth, one who does say it has not erred and has authorities upon whom to rely, including Rokeach, Tanya, Maharal, and the text printed in Pesach Me'ubin. The meaning is that the word “that today” gives the reason for reciting the blessing now over counting the Omer, namely that today is such and such a number of days. Regarding the formula “baOmer,” which Rama records, Taz discusses at length why one should not say “laOmer.” Yet most old texts state that one should say “laOmer,” as do Kol Bo, Tanya, Shibbolei HaLeket, Rokeach, Rashba's responsa, Beit Yosef, and Ran. The word “laOmer” means that this is such and such a day belonging to the Omer count, whereas “baOmer” implies that it is now the time of the Omer, though the time of bringing the Omer was only the first day, the sixteenth of Nisan. Shelah likewise writes that one should specifically say “laOmer.” Nevertheless, one should not change an established custom, for those who follow it have authorities upon whom to rely.
Until he reaches fourteen days. Maharshal Kaidanover, responsum 27, writes that initially one should state the smaller number first, following Rabbi Yehudah in Yoma 55. In places that count the larger number first, following Rabbi Meir, one should follow the local custom. Today the smaller number is stated first. Shibbolei HaLeket, siman 73, writes that through ten one says “days,” while from eleven onward one says “day,” this being an elegant form of Hebrew expression.
Until forty-nine days. Tosafos in Megillah 20b, s.v. Kol HaLaylah, write that one says, “May it be Your will that the Beis HaMikdash be rebuilt,” because this is merely a remembrance of the rebuilding of the Beis HaMikdash, whereas shofar and lulav involve an act. Maharil writes that upon reaching the forty-ninth day one should say that they are seven weeks, and that the following day is Erev Shavuos. The custom is to recite the chapter of Tehillim “May God be gracious to us,” as Magen Avraham writes. Recently, people have printed a formula for counting based on the hidden teachings and the intentions of the sefiros. Similarly, during the circuits with the lulav they recite verses arranged according to the intentions of the Arizal, and prayer books have been printed with “service of the Creator” and many similar kabbalistic compilations. This has spread among the masses as though anyone who wishes to assume the name may do so. In truth, honor is unbecoming for a fool, and “the honor of God is to conceal a matter.” I shall copy Maharshal's words in responsum 98. He writes that he saw many who were excessively exacting to put on the tefillin shel yad while seated. Know that these are recent innovations of people who wish to join the kabbalists and hidden midrashim. Their weak sight cannot gaze into the light of the Zohar, and they know neither its source nor its intent; they merely found this in the books of Rabbi Moshe Cordovero. All my teachers and holy ancestors, who served the great sages of the world, did not act this way, but followed the Talmud and the poskim. Open your eyes: if this had substance, it is impossible that no author would have mentioned it, nor would there be no hint in the Bavli, Yerushalmi, Sifra, Pesikta, or She'iltos. One who cannot properly grasp its secret may come to destroy the plantings. Therefore, my beloved, do not follow their way, and do not involve yourself in hidden matters with those who pride themselves on innovations as though they understand the Torah's secrets, when it would be enough if they understood the revealed Torah. How great was Rabbi Shimon of Kinon's strength: after studying works of Kabbalah, he said that he prayed like a newborn child. Therefore, continue according to your custom and let them keep theirs, but know that one who changes has the weaker position and our position is stronger. From Maharshal's words it follows that one should not change established custom even to follow kabbalists, and certainly not because of other changes mentioned in recent books. Even if some change may be permitted for an exceptional individual renowned and established in piety among those who know the hidden wisdom, if he wishes to direct his intent in some manner or alter his practice based on a novelty found in later authorities, this too should be done modestly and not publicized so as to change the custom or cry out against those who follow the old custom, as though Torah innovations originate with them. Hashem knows whether their intent is upright. See siman 68, where Magen Avraham cites the kabbalistic writings: customs rooted in the prayers should not be changed from the custom of one's place, for there are twelve gates in Heaven corresponding to the twelve tribes, and each tribe has its own gate and custom. Only matters stated in the Gemara are uniform for all. The Yerushalmi states: although we sent you the order of prayers, do not change the custom of your ancestors. See also siman 30, siman 207, siman 410, siman 501.
