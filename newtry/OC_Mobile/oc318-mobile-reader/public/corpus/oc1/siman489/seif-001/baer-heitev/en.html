On the night. The Maariv piyyutim associated with the second night are recited even when the first day falls on Shabbos. On other festivals, if the first day falls on Shabbos, the piyyutim of the first day are recited on the second night and those of the second day are omitted. On the second night of Pesach, however, the piyyutim appropriate to that day are always recited, because they concern the firstborn and the counting of the Omer. Such is the custom.
To count. See Magen Avraham and Chok Yaakov. Women are exempt from counting because it is a time bound positive mitzvah. Rambam. Nevertheless, they have already accepted it upon themselves as an obligation. Magen Avraham.
Over. If he wishes, he may hear the blessing from the chazzan and count for himself, because the verse states, “You shall count for yourselves,” meaning that the counting must be performed by each individual. See Magen Avraham and Chok Yaakov. If he transgressed and heard the counting from another person, he fulfilled his obligation if both the listener and the speaker intended to discharge and fulfill the obligation. Pri Chadash. See Yad Aharon in his glosses to Beit Yosef. It is obvious that one may count in any language, provided he understands that language. If he does not understand Hebrew and counts in Hebrew, he does not fulfill the obligation, because he does not know what he is saying and this is not considered counting. Magen Avraham.
Standing. The verse says, “From the standing grain you shall begin to count.” The word may be read as though it means, “while standing.” After the fact, if he counted while seated, he fulfilled the obligation. Rambam.
And to recite a blessing. Regarding why a blessing is recited over this counting but not over the counting of a niddah, see Radbaz, part 1, siman 27, and Chok Yaakov. Shehecheyanu is not recited over the counting because it contains no joy or benefit. The later authorities. See Chok Yaakov.
And the weeks. If one counted only the days and did not count the weeks, or counted only the weeks and not the days, he must repeat the counting with a blessing. Pri Chadash and Rabbi Shmuel HaLevi, Orach Chaim, siman 5. Knesses HaGedolah, Magen Avraham, and Chok Yaakov, however, agree that after the fact he fulfilled his obligation. It therefore appears to me that he should count again without a blessing, because blessings do not prevent fulfillment. Knesses HaGedolah further writes in the name of Rabbi Shmuel HaLevi, and agrees with him, that if one counted using an abbreviation, such as saying, “Today is four days,” or said, “eight days, which are one week and one day,” he fulfilled his obligation. Pri Chadash writes that he did not fulfill his obligation. If he said, “forty minus one days of the Omer,” he fulfilled his obligation. Mahari Molcho; Yad Aharon.
Today. One should not say “that today.” Chok Yaakov writes that one who does say it has not erred. See there.
Of the Omer. Taz writes this formulation as well. The Arizal’s version is “to the Omer,” and Shelah writes likewise.
One. One should not use the feminine form, because the word week is masculine. One should always place the smaller number first, such as “one and twenty days.” Until ten, one says “days,” while from eleven onward one says “day.” This is the elegant form of Hebrew speech. The custom is to recite the chapter of Tehillim “May God be gracious to us” after the counting. See what Chok Yaakov writes concerning this.
Forty nine. See Rosh at the end of chapter Arvei Pesachim. Some ask that the verse states, “You shall count fifty days,” although only forty nine are counted. Rosh explains that Scripture commonly expresses a number that reaches one less than the next multiple of ten by using the rounded multiple and disregarding the missing one. See there. According to this, the Gemara’s question in Kiddushin 38a requires study. A baraisa states, “The children of Israel ate the manna for forty years,” although it was forty years minus thirty days. According to Rosh, what is the question, since this is the ordinary scriptural manner of counting? See Yad Aharon.
