It is forbidden to eat new grain. And see Yoreh De'ah, siman 293, where the Rama wrote a leniency for new grain; and in the Shelah he wrote that this does not apply in the lands of Poland, where most of the produce, and almost all of it, is sown after Pesach, and they also do not bring produce there from other lands. However, this is specifically regarding barley, oats, and spelt; but wheat and rye, the vast majority are sown in Cheshvan, and therefore new grain does not apply to them. And see there in the Shaarei Kohen, who wrote a leniency for beer made from new grain. And it is written in Responsa of the Rama that even one who is careful about new grain need not be concerned about absorption from vessels, for all new grain nowadays is only a rabbinic doubt: for even if it were a definite prohibition it would be nullified in the majority according to the Torah, all the more so new grain, which is only a mere stringency; for Mahara'i wrote that one should not expound in public to forbid it, because we follow the majority of grain that comes from the old; therefore it appears to me that one need not be so concerned about vessel absorption, end quote. Now, what he wrote that it is nullified in the majority according to the Torah means that the taste of the new grain is nullified in the majority according to the Torah, and this is specifically like with like, for example if he first cooked wheat and now cooks wheat again; but if he now cooked meat, it is unlike with like, and according to the Torah sixty [parts] are required; see Yoreh De'ah, siman 98. And what he wrote that we follow the majority of grain, that is regarding something that is doubtful from which produce it was made; but if it is clear to him that it was made from new produce, it is forbidden even regarding vessel absorption, for it is definite and not a doubt. And it appears to me that the world relies on what R' Baruch wrote, that new grain outside the Land is rabbinic (and so wrote in Likkutei Maharil), and they did not decree now outside the Land, since we are not adjacent to the Land of Israel, end quote. And so wrote in Terumat HaDeshen, siman 191, in the name of Or Zarua, that new grain is rabbinic, and the reason is that he holds the law follows the first Tanna; and even though an anonymous Mishnah at the end of Orlah follows R' Eliezer, nevertheless it is an anonymous ruling and afterward a dispute; and furthermore, since in Kiddushin he taught the words of the first Tanna anonymously, that too is anonymous, as stated at the beginning of Beitzah; and if so, the later anonymous ruling is preferable, as Tosafos wrote in Chullin, folio 43. And even though there is no fixed order for the Mishnah, nevertheless we return to the rule that in every place we hold the law follows the first Tanna. (And in Keneses HaGedolah, in the rules of the Gemara, siman 32, he wrote in the name of the Ritva that even in two Orders there is no order for the Mishnah; but in Kesef Mishneh, chapter 9 of Rotzei'ach, he wrote that in two Orders there is order; and see Tosefos Yom Tov, first chapter of Kiddushin, chapter 9 of Sotah, and chapter 7 of Terumos, end quote, Keneses HaGedolah; and in Tosafos Yevamos, folio 42, and at the end of Shabbos it is proven that even in two Orders there is no order.) And what the Rosh wrote that R' Akiva disputes the first Tanna is not a difficulty, for even the first Tanna holds "your dwellings" implies wherever you dwell, and he establishes it as produce that comes from the Land of Israel to outside the Land, as explained in the Yerushalmi; and R' Baruch holds that even according to the first Tanna new grain is forbidden by rabbinic law, and specifically in places adjacent to the Land of Israel, comparable to terumah, as Tosafos wrote. Thus it appears to me according to R' Baruch, and to justify the custom of the world; and it seems that Tosefos Yom Tov wrote likewise. But a conscientious person should be stringent in whatever he can be careful about.
