To eat new grain. The same applies to liquids extracted from it, as stated in Chullin 120: liquids extracted from tevel and new grain have the same status as the substances themselves. The early poskim therefore agree that beer made from new grain is forbidden. Rambam states this explicitly in Hilchos Maachalos Assuros 10:22, as do Rokeach, siman 294, and the other poskim who sought grounds to permit beer in our time. Their very effort shows that they hold that extracted liquids have the same status as the grain. This is unlike Shach in Yoreh Deah 293, citing Lechem Mishneh's commentary to the Mishnah, who strains to say that extracted liquid is permitted. His position has no foundation in the accepted halachah of the Gemara and early poskim, as Shach himself elsewhere and Beis Hillel also write. The principal basis relied upon against the many early authorities, including Rif, Rambam, Rosh, Tur, Kol Bo, Rokeach, Semak, and Semag, who hold that new grain is forbidden according to the Torah even today, must be the reason cited by Rama in Yoreh Deah 293 from the early authorities. Rashba, siman 209, and the aggadah at the beginning of Kiddushin likewise state that we rely upon the majority of grain being old or having taken root before the Omer. Even a majority not presently before us is followed according to Torah law. See Rashba, responsum 120. Even if one country has grain that did not take root before the Omer, the borders between countries are not closed. Shelah writes that in Poland one should be careful regarding barley, oats, and spelt, which are not imported there from other countries and are almost entirely sown after Pesach. Likkutei Maharil states that in certain years of the minor cycle planting is delayed until after Pesach and one must be careful; see his lengthy discussion. Nevertheless, I have not seen people careful even about this, including men of piety and practice, scarcely one in a thousand. They rely on Maharil's statement in his Likkutei that one need not be careful because some poskim hold that new grain outside Eretz Yisrael is only D'Rabbanan. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 291, writes this in the name of Or Zarua, and Magen Avraham writes similarly that the Sages did not impose the prohibition in our present distant lands. A further ground for leniency is Bach's ruling in Yoreh Deah 293 that the prohibition of new grain does not apply to grain belonging to a non-Jew. Although this is contrary to the implication of the Yerushalmi, Tosafos at the beginning of Kiddushin, Rosh's responsum, klal 2, siman 1, and Semak, and although Birchas HaZevach, Shach, and Taz reject Bach's proofs from our Gemara, Taz concludes that some early poskim agree with him. Piskei Tosafos to Menachos also records this view in the name of Rabbi Eliezer of Ivra. Combined with pressing circumstances, as Taz writes there, this explains the custom to be lenient.
