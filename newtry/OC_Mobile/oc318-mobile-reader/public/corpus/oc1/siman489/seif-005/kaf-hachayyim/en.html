The counting begins, etc. Most commentators maintain that counting the omer nowadays is only rabbinic, a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash, where the omer meal-offering was brought on the sixteenth of Nissan and the count began on the day it was offered. This follows Ameimar in Menachos, chapter Rabbi Yishmael, who says it is merely a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash and therefore counts days but not weeks. Although other Amoraim there counted both days and weeks, the early authorities explain that they did not disagree with Ameimar about the essential point or maintain that counting nowadays is required under Torah law. Rather, the Jewish people count days and weeks as a mitzvah practice. Rambam, chapter 7 of Hilchos Temidin, and Sefer HaChinuch, however, maintain that counting the omer is required under Torah law even nowadays. According to them, the scholars of Rav Ashi's academy who counted days and weeks disagree with Ameimar: it is not merely a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash but a Torah obligation, and the law follows them. The language of the Talmud somewhat supports this position. It says, 'Ameimar counted days and did not count weeks; he said it is a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash.' If all agreed that it was a remembrance, it should have said, 'He did not count weeks because it is a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash.' By saying, 'He said it is a remembrance,' the Talmud implies this was Ameimar's own view and not an uncontested rule. The authorities make similar inferences elsewhere. Birkei Yosef, subsection 1. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2, writes that the primary ruling follows those who maintain that counting nowadays is rabbinic. Chayei Adam, rule 131, subsection 1, writes likewise.
