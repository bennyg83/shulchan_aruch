1) [Se'if 1] He may recite it while walking or standing, etc. Nevertheless, sitting is preferable, so that he can recite it with greater awe, concentration, and composure. Hagahos Maimoniyos, Hilchos Krias Shema, chapter 2, writes likewise in the name of Raavyah concerning Rabbi Yehudah's statement, "upon your heart; while standing" in Berachos 13b; certainly sitting is even preferable. Darkei Moshe, paragraph 1, and Ateres Zekenim, paragraph 2, cite this. See also what we write below in paragraphs 6 and 10.
2) There: But not while lying supine, etc., or lying on his back, etc. Rashi in the chapter Hayah Korei explains that his organ may become erect in his sleep and be visible to others. Agudah there implies that the reason is improper thoughts. Regarding lying on his stomach, Nehalos Chaim, folio 11, explains that it is degrading because he appears to lie in the manner of marital relations; furthermore, it may arouse him and cause a seminal emission in vain. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1. If one transgressed and recited while supine, see Pri Megadim, Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 1, who is uncertain but inclines to rule that after the fact he fulfilled his obligation. Yeshuos Yaakov, however, writes that he did not fulfill it even after the fact; see there.
3) There: He may recite while lying on his side. Gloss: Since he was already lying down and standing would entail exertion. This means that it is burdensome to dress again, as Beis Yosef explains in his second interpretation of Rabbeinu Yonah. Lechem Chamudos, chapter Hayah Korei, paragraph 15; Magen Avraham, paragraph 1. This implies that even one lying in his robe must stand. The Gemara's wording, "One lying supine may not recite Krias Shema," likewise implies that even if he is already lying down he may not recite until he stands. The Rav Beis Yosef, however, holds that the restriction applies specifically when one lies on his back and tilts slightly onto his side; when one lies fully on his side, he may recite initially. This is why he omits the view of Rabbeinu Yonah's students. Magen Avraham there; Soles Belulah, paragraph 1; Olas Tamid, paragraph 2. Lechem Chamudos there likewise writes that Shulchan Aruch, which copies Rambam, states the law without qualification according to Beis Yosef's first interpretation, whereas Rema adds the gloss "since he was already lying," etc. Taz, paragraph 1, comments on Rema: This is astonishing, because Rambam, the source of the ruling cited by Shulchan Aruch, permits it initially when one fully tilts onto his side. Beis Yosef writes that Rambam does not require exertion to be involved. Olas Tamid, paragraph 3, also challenges Rema. Pri Chadash, paragraph 1, likewise rules against Rema, who followed Rabbeinu Yonah's students, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 5, concurs.
4) There: If he was corpulent, etc., or ill, he tilts slightly onto his side and recites. Rabbeinu Manoach writes that one who is neither corpulent nor ill may recite only while seated, like a person standing in awe and fear. Kesef Mishneh, Hilchos Krias Shema, chapter 2, halachah 2, cites him; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2. See Yad Aharon, Hagahos on the Tur, who questions Rabbeinu Manoach from the baraisa in which Beis Hillel says one may stand and recite, etc. It appears that Rabbeinu Manoach means this as the preferred fulfillment of the mitzvah, so that one can recite with proper awe, fear, and greater composure, as we wrote above in paragraph 1 and below in paragraph 6. His phrase "may recite only" does not express a strict prohibition, but the requirement of preferred practice so that one's mind will be more settled and he can recite with proper awe and fear. See Kisei Eliyahu and Yafeh LaLev, paragraph 1.
