If he was sleeping, etc. This is not specifically a case of being overcome by sleep; the same applies to another compulsion. So wrote Kesef Mishneh, chapter 2 of the laws of Krias Shema. It is obvious that in any event he must read the entire Krias Shema, even in a drowsy manner, but if he did not read the rest of Krias Shema at all, he did not fulfill the obligation. The Levush wrote that it appears to him that the first verse mentioned throughout this matter also includes ‘Baruch shem kevod malchuso le’olam va’ed.’ It seems to me there is proof from the Tanchuma there, which wrote that from ‘And you shall love’ onward he may walk. So too Tashbetz, siman 241, wrote, which shows that the law of ‘Baruch shem kevod malchuso le’olam va’ed’ is like the law of the first verse, and so it also implies at the beginning of siman 66. However, I found in Rokeach, siman 320: ‘When he reaches Baruch shem and Ve’ahavta, he may walk,’ and this requires study. See above, siman 61, seif katan 17. I also saw fit to mention what the Bach wrote on the Tur’s statement that the essential intent is the first verse: he wrote that the Tur used this wording according to the first view, that one walking must stand until the first verse, and automatically according to the one who rules that one must stand until ‘upon your heart,’ one who dozed must also be fully awake until ‘upon your heart.’ He concluded that this is how we rule, unlike the Shulchan Aruch, who is lenient that intent is needed only for the first verse. So too wrote Knesses HaGedolah. I did not understand their view, first because it is very forced to say that the Tur ruled plainly that intent for the first verse suffices, and then say that this is only according to the first view, when it is not established for him, and it implies that he holds like his father the Rosh that one must stand until ‘upon your heart.’ Moreover, the words of the Rashba’s novellae that I cited above escaped them, for he ruled explicitly that for one sitting or standing it is enough to have intent for the first verse and no more, while one walking must stand until ‘upon your heart.’ This is also explicit in Abudraham, page 31, and in Rabbeinu Yerucham there. The reason seems to me that he holds that walking is worse than lack of intent, just as engaging in work, gesturing, and winking in seif 6 are worse, being forbidden throughout the entire chapter. One must say that these are worse than walking, and so it is explicit in the Rashba’s novellae there. I also found, thank God, explicitly in Kol Bo, siman 9, that the Raavad wrote that although intent of the heart is required only for the first verse, one who walks must stand until ‘upon your heart’ so that he not make it casual. So too it is proven from Knesses HaGedolah, who ruled in the first chapter of Berachos that one must stand until ‘upon your heart,’ and in the second chapter ruled that only the first verse requires intent. It is therefore clear as the sun that the Tur wrote this law of intent for the first verse according to all opinions, and the Shulchan Aruch and Levush ruled well.
