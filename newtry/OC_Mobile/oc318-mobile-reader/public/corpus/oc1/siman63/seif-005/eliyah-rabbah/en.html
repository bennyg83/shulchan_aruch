(5) To stand, etc. Not specifically to stand, but the same law applies to one who sits — so wrote Perishah, and so it appears from Rabbeinu Yona in the chapter "Morning Prayer" on the Mishnah "he was riding." Bach wrote: from Rambam it appears that if one rides or sits in a ship and wagon one need not stand, for for the intent of the first verse alone we do not say a rider is like a walker — until here; and Taz disagrees regarding a rider, for since he is preoccupied with driving the animal he cannot have intention, therefore he should stand on the animal; and so ruled in the responsum of Beit Yaakov, siman 71, and brought proof from siman 94, se'if 4, in Berakhot — Avot, and all this is not necessary. And he relied on proof, as it appears to me, from skilled workers, se'if 7, who read Keriat Shema at the top of a tree, and for prayer one must descend as Rashi explained [Berakhot] folio 16 and Rambam's explanation of the Mishnah — and see siman 90, se'if 3. In any case it is good to be stringent, for it is possible that Bach also only spoke regarding his view that one need not stand except for the first verse; but according to what he concludes — "upon your heart" — even a rider requires standing. And even though one can say that here it does not speak specifically of the first verse, nevertheless it is good to be stringent out of stringency. And it appears in the responsum of Beit Yaakov there that in a wagon, even if he conducts himself [while seated], he need not stand; and on a rider, even if another drives the animal, he should stand — and so is reasonable. What he brought as proof from the Gemara — if he has one who holds his donkey, etc. — is astounding, and there is no need to elaborate.
