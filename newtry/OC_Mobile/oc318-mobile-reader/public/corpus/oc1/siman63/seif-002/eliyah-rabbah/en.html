A transgressor, etc. I found a writing as follows: It implies that only if he is sitting and wants to stand is it forbidden, because it appears to everyone that he is acting out of stringency; but if he is standing, he may sit, for then it does not appear that he is acting out of stringency, but rather because he needs to sit. It seems that this also applies to the evening Krias Shema. But the Bach wrote that at night, if he was upright, it is forbidden for him to recline; these are his words, and it requires study, for it is forced to distinguish between sitting and reclining. The Rashal wrote in his glosses to the Tur that on Yom Kippur everyone agrees that it is permitted to stand, for he is not doing so because of arrogance but because this is its mitzvah. See Yam Shel Shlomo, chapter 4 of Bava Kama, siman 41: one who is stringent in a matter that is permitted is placed in nidui, unless it is known that he does so for the sake of Heaven.
To stand, etc. It is not specifically to stand; the same applies to sitting. So wrote the Perishah, and so it implies from Rabbeinu Yonah in the chapter Tefillas Hashachar on the Mishnah of one who was riding. The Bach wrote that from the Rambam it implies that if one is riding, or sitting in a ship or wagon, he need not stand, because regarding intent for the first verse alone we do not say that riding is like walking; these are his words. The Taz disagrees regarding riding, because since he is occupied with guiding the animal he cannot have intent, and therefore he should stand on the animal. So too ruled Responsa Beis Yaakov, siman 71, and he brought proof from siman 94, seif 4, regarding the blessing of Avos. None of this is compelling. A supporting proof, as it seems to me, is from workers in seif 7, who read Krias Shema at the top of a tree, while for prayer one must descend, as Rashi explains on Berachos 16 and as the Rambam explains in his commentary on the Mishnah; see siman 90, seif 3. Nevertheless it is good to be stringent, for perhaps the Bach spoke only according to the initial assumption that one need stand only for the first verse, but according to the conclusion, until ‘upon your heart,’ riding also requires standing. Although one could say that here ‘first verse’ is not precise, nevertheless it is good to be stringent as a stringency. It implies in Responsa Beis Yaakov there that in a wagon, even if he himself is driving, he need not stand; but when riding, even if another is guiding the animal, he should stand. This is reasonable. The proof he brought from the Gemara, that if he has someone to hold his donkey, etc., is astonishing, and there is no need to elaborate.
