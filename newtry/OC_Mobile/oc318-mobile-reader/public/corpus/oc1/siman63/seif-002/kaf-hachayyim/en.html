5) [Seif 2] "One who wishes to be stringent," etc. Maharshal in Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 7, siman 41, writes that a person who acts stringently in public regarding something permitted, where there is concern for religious ostentation, is subject to excommunication. If it is known that he acts for the sake of Heaven, he is not excommunicated. If he acts stringently in the presence of his Rav while his Rav rules leniently, however, he is subject to excommunication even if his intent is for the sake of Heaven. Even where the permission is not completely obvious, he should not act stringently against his Rav's ruling unless he has evidence that refutes it. Magen Avraham, seif katan 2; Soles Belulah, letter 2; Eliyah Rabbah, letter 4; Chesed LaAlafim, letter 2. See Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, and Machatzit HaShekel, letter 2. Birkei Yosef, letter 1, cites Rabbi Menachem of Beit Meir, brought in Shitah Mekubetzet, Bava Kamma 81b, that where a matter's permissibility is obvious, it is improper to act stringently in the presence of one who is greater in wisdom while that scholar follows the lenient practice. If he does so, the greater scholar may excommunicate him. If the stringent person is known for piety, however, he may conduct himself stringently. See Responsa Maharadakh, end of responsum 26, and Ginnat Veradim, Yoreh De'ah, rule 3, end of siman 6. 6) There, "One who wishes to be stringent by standing when he had been sitting and reciting it standing is called a transgressor." Tur concludes in the name of Rav Amram that he should be rebuked, because its mitzvah is while seated. Beit Yosef explains that this means only that it may be recited even while seated. Perishah, letter 6, however, writes that it specifically should be recited seated. Rama in Darkei Moshe, letter 1, appears to agree. He cites Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 2, that sitting is preferable to standing, apparently disagreeing with Maran and holding that sitting is the preferred form. Pri Chadash, letter 2, likewise explains Tur in the name of Rav Amram to mean that the principal mitzvah is seated, so that a person's mind is especially settled, similar to the conclusion at the end of Perek Sheloshah SheAchlu that in all those cases the halacha is that one sits and recites the blessing. Maamar Mordechai, letter 2, cites him and approves. This is likewise implied by Bereishit Rabbah, chapter 48, on the verse, "He was sitting at the entrance of the tent." R' Berechyah in the name of R' Levi says that "sit" is written. Avraham wished to stand, but Hashem told him, "Sit." So too your descendants will sit: when Israel enters synagogues and study halls and recites Kriat Shema, they sit in My honor, while I stand over them, as it says, "God stands in the congregation of God." The same appears in Bamidbar Rabbah, Parashat Naso, chapter 11, and Midrash Chazita on "My Beloved resembles a gazelle." Yafeh LaLev, letter 2. Shaar HaKavanos, discourse 2 concerning the repetition of the Amidah, and Pri Etz Chaim, Shaar Kriat Shema, end of chapter 29, likewise imply that since Kriat Shema corresponds to Beriah it should be recited seated. Although Shaar HaKavanos, discourse 6 concerning Kriat Shema, might imply that if one wished to say it standing this would not matter, according to the mystical teaching sitting remains preferable. See Ateret Zekenim, letter 2, and Ben Ish Chai, Parashat Vaera, letter 16. 7) There, "And recite it standing," etc. Sefer HaKanah, cited in the newer Tzafnat Paneach, states that in Eretz Yisrael Kriat Shema should be said standing and outside Eretz Yisrael seated. This distinction does not appear to be accepted by the halachic authorities or kabbalists. In Eretz Yisrael and outside it alike, "the righteous sit." Kaf HaChaim by Rabbi Chaim Palagi, siman 14, letter 32. This likewise follows from Shaar HaKavanos and Pri Etz Chaim cited in the preceding letter, because the reason for sitting is that Kriat Shema corresponds to Beriah, a reason that does not distinguish between Eretz Yisrael and outside it. 8) There, "He is called a transgressor." This implies that he nevertheless fulfilled his obligation. Even according to Rav Yosef, who says that one who acted like Beit Shammai "did nothing," the meaning is that he did not fulfill the mitzvah in its proper manner. The Gemara compares this with one whose head and most of his body were in a sukkah while his table remained inside the house, where after the fact he fulfilled the obligation. Ran writes similarly at the end of Arvei Pesachim regarding the Mishnah's statement that one who did not say these 3 things on Pesach did not fulfill his obligation. Ramban writes likewise in Milchamot at the beginning of Berachot, as does Tur at the end of siman 66. Erech HaShulchan, letter 1. See Mateh Yehudah, letter 1; Pithei HaDevir, letter 2; and Yafeh LaLev, letter 2. 9) There, "He is called a transgressor." If he stands merely in order to dispel sleep, it is permitted. Chesed LaAlafim, letter 2; Ben Ish Chai, Parashat Vaera, letter 15.
