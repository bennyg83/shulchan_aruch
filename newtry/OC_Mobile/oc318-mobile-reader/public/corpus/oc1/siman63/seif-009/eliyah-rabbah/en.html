(9) If he was asleep, etc. Not specifically compelled by sleep — the same applies to other compulsion; so wrote Kesef Mishneh, chapter 2 of Laws of Keriat Shema. And it is simple that in any case we require that he read all of Keriat Shema, even through dozing; but if he did not read the rest of Keriat Shema at all he has not fulfilled. Levush wrote: it appears to me that the first verse mentioned in all this matter includes also "Blessed be the name of His glorious kingdom forever and ever" — until here. And it appears to me [there is] proof from Midrash Tanchuma there, who wrote that from "and you shall love" onward he may go; and so wrote Teshuvot HaRashbatz, siman 241 — behold the law of "Blessed be the name" is like the law of the first verse; and so it appears at the beginning of siman 66. However I found in Rokeach, siman 320, thus is his language: and when he reaches "Blessed be the Name" and "and you shall love" he may walk — until here, and it needs examination; and see above siman 61, note 17. I also saw to mention what Bach wrote on what Tur wrote — the main intention is the first verse; thus is his language: he formulated it thus for the first view, that a walker must stand until the first verse, and automatically for one who ruled that one must stand until "upon your heart," even one who dozes must be fully awake until "upon your heart." And he concluded that thus we formulate, unlike Shulchan Arukh who is lenient that one does not need intention except for the first verse — until here; and so wrote Knesset HaGedolah. I did not descend to the end of their minds: one, that it is a great strain to say Tur would rule anonymously that intention in the first verse suffices, and we should say he [formulated] for the first view — what he did not establish, for it implies he holds like Rosh his father, that until "upon your heart" [requires standing]. And further, they omitted the words of the novellae of Rashba that I brought above, who ruled explicitly that whether sitting or standing it suffices to intend for the first verse and no more, and a walker must stand until "upon your heart" — and so explicitly in Avudraham, folio 31, and Rabbeinu Yerucham there. And the reason appears to me that he holds a walker is worse than one without intention, as one who is engaged in his work is worse — signaling and winking in se'if 6, which is forbidden for the whole paragraph. And one must say that these are worse than a walker, and so it is explained in the novellae of Rashba there; and so I found explicitly in Kolbo, siman 9, thus is his language to Raavad: he wrote that although intent of the heart is not required except in the first verse, a walker requires standing until "upon your heart" so that he not make it casual — until here. And so it is proven in Knesset HaGedolah, who ruled in the first chapter of Berakhot that until "upon your heart" [requires] standing, and in the second chapter ruled that only the first verse requires intention. But it is clear as the sun that Tur wrote this law of intention in the first verse for everyone, and Shulchan Arukh and Levush ruled well.
