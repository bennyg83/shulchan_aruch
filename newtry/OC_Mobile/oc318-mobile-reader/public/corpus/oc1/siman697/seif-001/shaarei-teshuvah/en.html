On the fourteenth. See Baer Heitev and Taz, who cites Hagahos Tashbetz that Rabbeinu Yechiel of Paris was accustomed to increase the meal and invite other people, as implied by the Mishnah’s language, “There is no difference between the first Adar and the second Adar,” etc. Rama therefore fittingly concludes, “A person of good heart feasts continually.” See Birkei Yosef, who writes that Rama, of blessed memory, wisely fashioned an ending corresponding to his opening, with two uses of the word “continually.” He opened his glosses with, “I have set Hashem before me continually,” and concluded, “A person of good heart feasts continually.” See there. It appears to me that because Rama wished to conclude with a favorable matter and with a timely expression, he employed the elegant scriptural phrase, “a person of good heart.” It means that it is good to make a feast during both Adars. Perhaps this is also Taz’s intent when he writes that Rama fittingly concludes in this manner. There is also an allusion, because the numerical value of the words “a good feast” equals “the first Adar.” One may further explain this according to what is written above in siman 670, gloss to seif 2, concerning Chanukah. Some say that there is a minor mitzvah in increasing the meals, and the custom is to recite songs and praises during the additional meals, thereby making them meals of a mitzvah. The same may be learned here. When one increases the meal somewhat in order to satisfy the stringent opinions, he should recite songs and praises, and it will then certainly be a meal of a mitzvah. Rama’s conclusion, “A person of good heart feasts continually,” may allude to this. The Gemara in Arachin 11 asks for the Torah source for the essential obligation of song: “Which service is performed with joy and goodness of heart? This is song.” Rashi cites the verse, “Behold, My servants shall sing from goodness of heart.” Tosafos notes that the expression is also found regarding words of Torah. Rashi on Rus cites a Midrash interpreting “his heart was good” as referring to words of Torah. Likewise, Shabbos 63 interprets “it will improve your heart” as referring to Torah study, although this is not written explicitly. Accordingly, “A person of good heart feasts continually” means that when a feast is accompanied by goodness of heart, namely songs, praises, and words of Torah, it is always proper. I wrote elsewhere concerning the verse, “Israel encamped opposite the mountain,” that the evil inclination departed from them. Rashi therefore explains that they encamped as one person with one heart. The verse “with all your heart” is interpreted as referring to both inclinations, but here, because the evil inclination departed, they possessed only one heart. In the verse speaking of “joy and goodness of heart,” where the word “good” is placed beside “heart,” it is more reasonable to interpret the phrase as referring to song. The evil inclination does not depart through song alone, but through Torah, which is its antidote, and while both inclinations remain there are still two hearts. In the phrase “his heart was good,” however, it is fitting to interpret it as Torah study, because while a person is occupied with Torah it protects and rescues him. The Sages similarly said that three people who ate at one table and spoke words of Torah upon it, etc. See what I wrote in the introduction to Responsa Beit Ephraim, Orach Chaim, concerning the verse, “They went to their tents joyful and of good heart.” This is also the meaning of our request, “Enlighten our eyes through Your Torah.” Through Your Torah, as the Sages said, the light within it returns a person to the good. Then, “attach our heart to Your mitzvos” and “unify our heart,” so that the two hearts become one in love. This causes the heart to cleave to the mitzvos when the opposing inclination is no longer beside it. A person should especially strengthen himself in words of Torah wherever there is some celebration, even when it is a celebration of a mitzvah. See the end of Sukkah concerning the Simchas Beis HaShoevah. The same principle is explained above in siman 529. A person eats, drinks, and rejoices on a festival, but he should not be drawn after meat, wine, laughter, and frivolity. Laughter and frivolity are not joy, but wild revelry and foolishness. We were not commanded concerning wild revelry and foolishness, but concerning joy that includes service of the Creator. This is Rambam’s language. The commentators explain the verse, “Of laughter I said, it is madness,” to mean that laughter, whatever its form, is wild revelry. See Taz above. The continuation, “And of joy, what does it accomplish?” means that joy cannot be categorically declared improper, because there is genuine joy of a mitzvah. A person must therefore consider what this joy accomplishes, meaning what good it contains, and determine whether it is joy of a mitzvah. Since eating, drinking, and exuberance can lead a person into laughter and frivolity, he should take the antidote that sweetens the celebration, namely words of Torah. The joy of Hashem will then be his strength, and his heart will become good through words of Torah. This is the meaning of “A person of good heart feasts continually.” May it be Hashem’s will that we have one heart toward our Father in Heaven. May we raise our hearts with our hands toward the One who opens His hand to repentance. May our words find favor before the mighty scholars whose entire desire is to remain diligent at the doors of Torah and mitzvah and to straighten a highway along the path of life. Whatever remains doubtful to them in the laws, or sealed within Apukei Magenim, Shaarei Teshuvah and Yad Ephraim will enlighten the eyes, and he called the name of the place Machanayim.
May Hashem restore our captivity through the resettlement of Zion and Jerusalem. May they become one, the tree of Yehudah together with the tree of Yosef that is in Ephraim’s hand.
