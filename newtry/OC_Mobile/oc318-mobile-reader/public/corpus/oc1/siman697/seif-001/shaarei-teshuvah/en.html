On the fourteenth. See Baer Heitev and see the Taz, who brought in the name of Hagahot Tashbetz that Rabbeinu Yechiel of Paris was accustomed to increase the meal and invite people, as implied by the language of the Mishnah, "there is no difference between Adar," etc., and that the Rama fittingly concludes, "and a good-hearted person feasts always." See Birkei Yosef, who wrote that our teacher the Rama z"l wisely established an ending that echoes the opening: two "always" statements in their order. He opened at the beginning of his glosses with "I have set the Lord before me always," and concluded with "and a good-hearted person feasts always," etc.; see there. It appears to me that because he wanted to end with a good matter, and a timely word, he took the fine scriptural phrase for elegance, "good-hearted," meaning that it is good to make a feast in both Adars. Perhaps this is the intent of the Taz when he wrote that the Rama fittingly concludes, etc., as I have written. There is also a hint, for the words mishteh tov have the gematria of Adar Rishon. One may further say, according to what is written above in siman 670 in the gloss, seif 2, regarding Chanukkah, that some say there is a slight mitzvah in increasing meals, etc., and the custom is to say songs and praises at the meals they increase, and then it is a seudat mitzvah; see there. From there we may learn here as well, that when one increases the meal somewhat to satisfy the stringent views, he should say songs and praises, and then it is certainly a seudat mitzvah. To allude to this, he concludes, "and a good-hearted person feasts always," based on what we say in Arakhin 11: from where is the essential obligation of song derived from the Torah, etc.? Which service is with joy and gladness of heart? Say: this is song. See there in Rashi, who brings from Isaiah 65, "behold, My servants will sing from gladness of heart," and see there in Tosafot, that we also find it regarding words of Torah, where Rashi wrote on Ruth that the Midrash explains "his heart was good" as referring to words of Torah, and likewise in Shabbat 63, "your heart will be good" refers to Torah study; only it is not written explicitly; see there. Thus he said, "and a good-hearted person feasts always," meaning that with goodness of heart, namely songs, praises, and words of Torah, the feast is always proper. According to what I wrote elsewhere on "Israel encamped opposite the mountain," that the evil inclination departed from them, and therefore Rashi explained "encamped" as one person with one heart, because "with all your heart" means with both inclinations, and here, when the evil inclination departed, they had only one heart: in the verse "with joy and gladness of heart," where "good" is adjacent to "heart," it is more reasonable to expound it as referring to song, for the evil inclination does not depart through song alone, but through Torah, which is its antidote, and then there are still two hearts. But there, where it says "his heart was good," it is fitting to expound it as referring to Torah study, for while one is occupied in it, it protects and rescues. So too they said: three who ate at one table and spoke words of Torah upon it, etc. See what I wrote in the introduction to Responsa Beit Ephraim, Orach Chayyim, on "they went to their tents joyful and good-hearted," etc. This is what we say: "Enlighten our eyes in Your Torah"; one may say the intent is through Your Torah, as the Sages said, "the light within it returns one to good," and then "attach our heart to Your commandments," and "unify our heart," etc., that the two hearts be unified into one for love, etc. This causes the heart to cling to commandments once there is no adjacent evil inclination. One must especially strengthen himself in words of Torah in a place where there is some joy, even if it is the joy of a mitzvah. See at the end of Sukkah regarding the Simchat Beit HaShoevah. So too it is explained above in siman 529: a person eats, drinks, and rejoices on the festival, but should not be drawn after meat, wine, laughter, and frivolity, for laughter and frivolity are not joy but revelry and foolishness; we were not commanded regarding revelry and foolishness but regarding joy that contains service of the Creator. This is the language of our teacher the Rambam z"l. The commentators z"l explained "of laughter I said, it is mad" to mean that in whatever form, laughter is revelry; see the Taz above. But "of joy, what does this accomplish?" means that with respect to joy one cannot decide absolutely that it is improper, for in truth there is joy of mitzvah. Therefore one must take to heart and know "what this accomplishes," meaning what good it has, whether it is joy of mitzvah or not. Yet since it is possible that from eating, drinking, and exuberance one will be drawn to laughter and frivolity, one should take an antidote to perfume the joy with words of Torah, and the joy of the Lord will be his strength, and his heart will be good with words of Torah. This is "and a good-hearted person feasts always." May it be His will that we have one heart for our Father in heaven, and that we lift our hearts to our hands, to the One who opens His hand in repentance; may our words be accepted before the mighty ones whose whole desire is to be diligent at the doors of Torah and mitzvah, to straighten a path in the way of life. And what may be uncertain to them in laws, or sealed in Apukei Magenim, in Shaarei Teshuvah and Yad Ephraim, will enlighten eyes, and he called the name of the place Machanayim.
