One who bows at "u'vechol koma," etc. In a baraita it states: one who bows in the thanksgiving of Hallel and in the thanksgiving of Birkat Hamazon — behold this is unseemly. And R' Yehuda HaChasid wrote similarly regarding "Nishmat" — "and to You alone we give thanks"; and the Gemara did not cite this because the utterance of Nishmat Kol Chai is not as clear as Hallel and Birkat Hamazon; but it is the same for all other thanksgivings that one does not bow in them except in the thanksgiving of prayer alone — end quote. It is difficult: why should it not be here as in the middle of the [Eighteen], where there is no prohibition? It seems to me the word "modim" has two meanings: one, in the manner of praise and celebration; the second, in the manner of submission — and we find in the Gemara in their saying "so-and-so admits to so-and-so" — this is the manner of submission, meaning by this he subdues himself, the one disputing with him, in another place. When he says "hakol modim" one cannot say so; then its meaning is in the manner of praise and celebration, meaning everyone praises this thing. From here in Hallel — "Hodu laHashem ki tov" — it is in the manner of praise; and similarly in Birkat Hamazon; and similarly in Nishmat — "and to You alone we give thanks" — meaning, we praise. And that which is written here, "and every upright one shall bow before You" — even though this is certainly language of submission, nevertheless he does not bow, since he does not say it about himself but rather that the whole world says they shall prostrate before Him, may He be blessed; and similarly "all creatures shall prostrate before You" of Rosh Hashana and Yom Kippur. But if he says "and we bow and prostrate" — that is about ourselves; and therefore we find properly in the Jerusalem Talmud that a person leaves him in one corner and finds him in another corner from abundant bowings and prostrations. The poskim wrote that this was after he finished the eighteen blessings. And this is the rule in our hands: whatever is not in the [Eighteen] one may bow and prostrate as one wishes — that is, in a place where he prays about himself, where he bows and prostrates. And in what is written in the Musaf of Yom Kippur, "and the priests and the people, etc., were bowing," etc. — nevertheless the whole congregation bows even though it is not about themselves; for there it is different, since we act as a memorial that we now liken our souls to the place of the priests when the Temple stood, as explained in this. And similarly in what is written in the Musaf of Yom Kippur in Avodah, "they sinned, they transgressed, they rebelled — the children of Israel" — we liken ourselves as though we are those House of Israel and regret our sins; and properly did those who strike their hearts, in the manner they do when saying "Ashamnu." And I saw some who err on this and say one should not strike the heart in that utterance since it does not refer to ourselves — but it is not so; and it is the same as with the bowings of the utterance "and the priests, etc., were bowing," etc., as explained.
