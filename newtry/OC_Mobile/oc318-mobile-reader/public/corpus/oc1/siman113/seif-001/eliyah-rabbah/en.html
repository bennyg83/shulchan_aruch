[1] In Avos, etc. The Tur cites the verse, ‘I will make you into a great nation,’ corresponding to our saying ‘the God of Abraham.’ According to the Re'em, this is because Hashem ordinarily associates His Name not with an individual but with a nation, as in ‘the God of Israel,’ ‘the God of the Hebrews,’ and ‘the God of the nations.’ The Perishah explains that Hashem would make Abraham himself, apart from his descendants, into a great nation, which was possible only by associating His Name with him. The Tur further writes that ‘I will bless you’ corresponds to ‘the God of Isaac.’ This may be understood by prefacing the Gemara in Taanis: With what could one bless a tree? If with children, it already has children; if with wealth, it already has wealth; if with Torah, it already has Torah. Rather, may it be Hashem's will that the offspring of your innards be like you. The same difficulty applies to Abraham, for we find that he already possessed every blessing: wealth, as Rashi explains on ‘and the souls they had acquired’; Torah, as stated in the Midrash; and children, according to the plain meaning of ‘I will make you into a great nation.’ In what sense, then, would ‘I will bless you’ apply? It means that we say ‘the God of Isaac’ just as we say ‘the God of Abraham,’ so that the offspring of his innards would be like him. The Tur further writes that although the opening contains no explicit mention of kingship, ‘the God of Abraham’ is equivalent to kingship. One should not ask why he did not raise this question regarding the other blessings of the Amidah, since a blessing adjoining another blessing does not require mention of kingship, as stated below in siman 118. The Levush discusses this at length below in siman 214. Kol Bo, siman 11, writes that kingship is not mentioned because when Abraham first came, Hashem's kingship was not yet known throughout the world. Rabbi David Abudraham writes that we do not recite ‘Who sanctified us with His commandments and commanded us concerning the mitzvah of prayer,’ because prayer is a Torah mitzvah without a fixed measure, and it is in a person's hands to say less or more. He also writes that these bows are intended to remind a person before Whom he stands and that his end is to become dust. Alternatively, they allude to drawing blessing from above downward. Seder HaYom and other works write that during the four bows one should have in mind the four letters of the Name Havayah, and while straightening the four letters of Adnus. Thus, at the opening of Avos one should have in mind drawing down the yud, bringing the flow to the foundation called ‘the Life of the worlds,’ where union occurs; when straightening, he should have in mind raising the alef of Adnus to join the yud, and similarly with all the letters.
