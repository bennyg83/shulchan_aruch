Seif 2, in the gloss. "And some have written that one should be careful to recite Pitum HaKetoret from the written text and not by heart, etc." NB: This is only according to the simple understanding that what the Sages said, "whoever engages in the parashah of the olah is as if he offered an olah," means that it is as though he himself actually offered it. On that basis they said regarding Pitum HaKetoret that if one omitted one of its ingredients he is liable to death. But according to what I wrote in my work on Orach Chayim, siman 1, where I proved that the recitation is not like an actual offering, but only as though he handed it to a kohen to offer, and I also found this in Tosefet Shabbat, the matter is compelled: for we are certainly tamei met and zarim, and therefore one who hands deficient ketoret to a kohen is certainly not liable to death. It is possible that this is the reason of the Beit Yosef cited by the Magen Avraham, who wrote that one is not liable to death through recitation; perhaps it is for this reason. Examine carefully.
Ibid. In the gloss: "And they recite Mourner's Kaddish after Aleinu, etc." NB: A case came before me concerning one person who was uncertain whether his father died on the 20th of the month or on the 21st, though in any event he died on one of those two days. Another person was uncertain whether his father died on the 19th, the 20th, or the 21st; on the 19th there were other yahrzeits, and this doubtful mourner could not say Kaddish. On the 20th and 21st both doubtful mourners came to say Kaddish. The one whose doubt is only between those two days claimed that he should say the Kaddish on both days, for then on one day he will certainly be saying it properly, whereas the other remains doubtful even across both days; therefore he is preferable. I was asked for the ruling. I saw that in Responsa Meir Netivim he was asked about this and brought proof from Yevamot 96 regarding five whose children became mixed, for we do not say that one should perform yibbum with all of them. He inferred that where there is a possibility that each will fulfill the mitzvah, that is preferable to one person alone fulfilling it certainly. In my humble opinion this is not a proof. First, according to what Tosafot wrote in Pesachim regarding five whose Pesach skins became mixed: they asked there that semichah does not invalidate, and answered that it is better for one to enter a possible karet than for most of them to act improperly. Their words show explicitly that there is a distinction between a case of only two, where there is a doubt between them as to which acted improperly, and a case where the majority acted properly; then they should not now act improperly for the sake of the minority who faces a possible karet. So too here: perhaps there, specifically because there are five and in that sort of case it is impossible to fulfill a definite mitzvah unless one performs yibbum with all of them, if we say to fulfill a definite mitzvah, only one would act, the minority of them all. But if each performs yibbum with one and chalitzah with one, each performs a doubtful mitzvah, and a doubtful mitzvah is also a mitzvah; therefore it is preferable that most perform a doubtful mitzvah than that the minority perform a certain mitzvah. Especially since there the discussion follows the view that the mitzvah of yibbum precedes chalitzah; if we say they should all perform chalitzah, they would certainly act improperly. Thus, since most would not fulfill the mitzvah of yibbum and, on the contrary, most would act improperly by giving chalitzah to their yevamah, the majority should not act improperly for the sake of the individual who would act properly. It is possible that even there, if only two had become mixed, we would say that one should perform yibbum with all, so that one certainly fulfills the mitzvah, rather than both performing it doubtfully. And even if we say that with two the law would also be that each performs yibbum with one and chalitzah with one, nevertheless it is not comparable. There, if we say one should perform chalitzah with all, he certainly acts improperly according to the view that the mitzvah of yibbum precedes; just as this one would certainly perform the mitzvah, so the two others would certainly do the opposite, improperly. Their improper action results only through this one acting properly, so what makes one stronger than the other, that this one should certainly act improperly so that the other should certainly act properly? Therefore both should act doubtfully. But in our case, if the doubtful mourner does not say Kaddish on the 20th and 21st, that is not certainly improper, for perhaps his yahrzeit is not then at all but was on the 19th. Thus he acts only doubtfully improperly, not certainly, while the other acts certainly properly. And even though he did not say Kaddish on the 19th, so that he too certainly acted improperly, that was not caused by this claimant but by another; this claimant's certain proper action did not create the other's certain improper action, only a doubtful improper action through him. Therefore here the certain is preferable to the doubtful. There, however, whatever improper act is done by that one comes only through the other's proper act, so what makes one stronger than the other? Furthermore, there it is an active requirement, telling one person to do an act of chalitzah so that another may perform yibbum. He can say: what do I have to do with your certainty, that I should act improperly so that you act properly? My doubt is preferable to me over your certainty, for a person is close to himself. But here we do not force the second to perform an act; it is merely passive, and we are uncertain whom the congregation praying there should give more Kaddish to, this one or that one. For others, for whom both are equal, we say that it is more fitting to give the Kaddish to the one who will say it certainly than to the one who will say it only doubtfully; for us, the certain is preferable to the doubtful. This appears correct in my humble opinion, unlike the view of the Responsa Meir Netivim mentioned above. Examine carefully; this is clear.
