When an announcement must be made, it should be made before the chazzan begins Ashrei, not between Ashrei and Lamnatzeach, and certainly not while Ashrei is being recited, because this disrupts the congregation’s concentration. Amudei Shesh and Sema, Choshen Mishpat, siman 267. Beit Yosef, siman 550, also implies this. It appears to me that if one arrives at the synagogue while the congregation is reciting the order of Kedushah, he should recite it with them even before his own prayer. Some authorities hold that an individual may not recite it alone. Likewise, he may recite it before the two introductory verses, namely “A redeemer shall come to Zion” and “As for Me, this is My covenant,” and afterward recite those two verses. See Yalkut, parashas Eikev. All the more so, he may postpone Ashrei and Lamnatzeach in order to recite the order of Kedushah with the congregation. Aleinu. The writings state that Aleinu should be recited after each of the three daily prayers. Knesses HaGedolah, siman 234, writes that this is the practice in his region. Sefer Shtei Yados, however, cites Tolaas Yaakov that Aleinu should not be recited at Minchah. At the end of Aleinu one should recite the verse, “Hashem shall be King,” etc. The writings. One should say “and the seat of His glory.” Tur. One should say, “He is our God, there is no other,” because that is the language of the verse. Tashbetz.
We bow. One should bow and prostrate himself so that he not appear to deny the statement he is reciting. Emek HaBerachah. See siman 113. The great author of Levush recorded the laws of Kaddish here. I will add several practical rulings that have become clear to me. Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 80, writes that a person observing a yahrzeit has the right to all Kaddishim of that day, including Kaddish after study chapters, Shir HaShirim, and Rus. I have seen the custom to draw lots for the Kaddish after Shir HaShirim. It appears that this practice spread because people had not seen the responsum of Maharam Mintz, which had not yet been printed. Since the general customs of Kaddish were established according to his rulings, why should this law be different? The Kaddish recited on Friday evening after Mizmor Shir LeYom HaShabbos belongs to the mourners, unless the yahrzeit belongs to the group responsible for Kabbalas Shabbos. Everything follows local custom. When four or five people draw lots and two receive identical highest values, such as both drawing the letter tav, while the others receive lower values, the entire lottery is void and everyone must draw again. The two may not say that the others already lost and that only they should redraw. A proof comes from Yoma, chapter 2, where, when several kohanim were equal, the supervisor told all of them to extend their fingers and everyone participated in the new lottery, although those tied had come closer than the others. I heard this from the great Rabbi Menachem, av beit din of Krotoschin. If one person alone receives tav and the others receive equal lower values, the first person certainly wins and only the others redraw. If two or three people drew lots and another arrives the next day, the earlier division is void and they must draw again, as stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 175, seif 3, regarding brothers who divided an estate and then an additional brother arrived from overseas. If the person had been in the city and failed to come, he forfeited his right. His failure to attend demonstrates that he waived his right to be earlier and accepted being last. This resembles the rule in Choshen Mishpat, siman 176, seif 25, and Bava Basra concerning gifts for a wedding attendant: if he was in the city, he should have come. If one person recited Kaddish and another arrived the next day and said, “I will recite today in exchange for the Kaddish you recited yesterday, and we will draw lots for the third Kaddish,” while the first says, “Since you did not come yesterday, I acquired that Kaddish, and today’s two Kaddishim should be divided between us,” some wish to compare this to Choshen Mishpat, siman 171, where one partner used a shared courtyard for several years and the other cannot demand equal use for the same period, because until division each partner uses his own share. The comparison is entirely incorrect. There, he used his own continuing share. Here, a particular Kaddish does not belong jointly to both. It resembles two people who jointly own movable property and one consumes some of it, after which the other may consume an equivalent amount. One also cannot distinguish that there is a financial loss there but no loss here, since if the first person had not recited Kaddish, the other mourners would have done so. The newcomer may say, “I knew and relied upon your attending the synagogue and reciting Kaddish, and therefore I remained outside.” His absence indicates only that he waived the first position, not everything. The established custom where two people have yahrzeit and there are three Kaddishim is to draw lots. Whoever wins first recites two Kaddishim, unlike Tzemach Tzedek, siman 26, who requires a new lottery for the third Kaddish. See the end of Sukkah. When two people have yahrzeit and one must depart early on a journey the next morning, he cannot compel the other by saying, “Give me the Kaddish now and take tomorrow’s.” If the other demands a lottery, the law follows him. This is not an improper insistence, because he gains from it. Perhaps he will receive the Maariv Kaddish and thus recite both. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 311, and siman 174. Although Shach there limits the rule to a case of loss, his words are not compelling, and this case is also unlike his case. The other may further say, “I wish to advance myself in performing the mitzvah.” When there are three mourners, they draw lots rather than assigning one Kaddish to each, because each wishes to perform the mitzvah earlier. Beit Yosef, siman 201, implies that it is preferable for a son to hire someone to recite Kaddish in his place rather than have someone recite it without payment. See Yoreh Deah, siman 376, and Responsa Rama. If a deceased person has no son, his grandson should also be granted Kaddish. The other mourners recite two Kaddishim and the grandson one. If two people were compelled to abandon Judaism, one was killed and one died naturally, the son of the one who was killed takes precedence. Since he was killed, his death served as atonement. In addition, the one who died naturally had other coerced Jews with him and did not confess before them, indicating that he had not intended to return. Binyamin Ze’ev, siman 203. See Yoreh Deah, siman 340, seif 5. The custom is that when there is a mourner within thirty days, all Kaddishim are given to him, while the Kaddish after a chapter of Tehillim belongs to the other mourners. If four yahrzeits occur on the same day, each receives one Kaddish. If Shir HaShirim is also recited and the custom is to draw lots among all the mourners and yahrzeits, and one yahrzeit wins that lottery, the mourners cannot say, “Since each yahrzeit already has one Kaddish, you cannot take the chapter of Tehillim Kaddish.” The yahrzeit may answer that Heaven granted him this right and that his good fortune caused it. The law follows the yahrzeit. I found written in the name of Maharan that regarding Kaddish we do not say that part of the seventh day counts as the entire day. Therefore, if one person’s relative died during the week and another person’s relative died on Sunday, they are equal on Shabbos. He also ruled that if a person was mourning for his father and afterward his mother died, he receives all Kaddishim because he is within thirty days for his mother. Nevertheless, he still divides the other Kaddishim with the mourners by virtue of his father. One whose mourning was annulled by a festival, or who buried his relative during a festival, has the status of one within shivah. Shach in Yoreh Deah. It appears to me that he must nevertheless give one Kaddish to a yahrzeit, like a minor within shivah. The reason a yahrzeit receives nothing on Shabbos against an adult within shivah is that the yahrzeit can recite Kaddish on Motzaei Shabbos, as implied by the customs. A minor within shivah displaces a mourner within thirty days, as implied by Rama’s gloss there. Some refrain from reciting Shir HaYichud. See Responsa Rama for their reasoning. Rama himself rules that it should be recited and that it was instituted according to Kabbalah. Rivash, siman 115, writes that if a person’s mother died and his father objects to his reciting Kaddish, he may not recite it because honoring his father takes precedence. Rama, Yoreh Deah, siman 376, writes that the father cannot prevent him. In some places, the custom is that on the day a mourner stops reciting Kaddish, all the Kaddishim are given to him.
Evening and morning. The Arizal writes that Pitum HaKetores should not be recited at night. Perhaps the phrase means that it should be recited after Minchah or before it. It requires study why we recite it after Mussaf, since according to all opinions the incense preceded the additional offerings and belonged to the daily offering. Perhaps our intention is to depart from prayer with words of Torah. The writings state that its purpose is to drive away the external forces. Shelah writes that it should be recited at Shacharis both before and after the prayer.
The song. I found written that the phrase “the Mishnah of the song that the Leviim recited,” etc., is an error. Tur’s intent is that each day one should recite the chapter of Tehillim appropriate to that day. Rambam states this explicitly in the order of prayer, and Knesses HaGedolah writes likewise. In my opinion, Tur intends that the Mishnah also be recited, so that one departs from prayer with words of Torah. Tur writes concerning Shabbos, “for the day that will be entirely Shabbos,” followed by “Rabbi Elazar said in the name of Rabbi Chanina, Torah scholars,” etc. This passage clearly does not belong to the daily chapter of Tehillim. Furthermore, why would one need to recite “Rabbi Elazar said,” etc.? After verses, Kaddish may always be recited. After Mishnah, however, the passage of Rabbi Elazar is recited before Kaddish, as stated in siman 54. Darkei Moshe also supports this. He writes that Rashi’s version reads “in the Beis HaMikdash,” while Rokeach’s reads “in the Mikdash.” Maharil corrected his siddur according to Rokeach. This textual difference would be irrelevant unless the Mishnah itself was recited. Nevertheless, here Shulchan Aruch altered the wording of Tur and the Mishnah and wrote, “They recite the song that the Leviim,” etc., indicating that only the song, not the Mishnah, is recited. It concludes, “at Shacharis alone,” meaning that at night no song was recited. Here the discussion concerns weekdays. On Shabbos, however, the custom is to recite the Mishnah as printed in all siddurim. Beit Yosef cites Orchos Chaim that the songs were a mitzvah only in the morning. Rabbi Moshe Alshich asks from the explicit Mishnah in Rosh Hashanah, chapter 4, that the Leviim erred in the song of the afternoon offering, proving that song was recited then. He answers that the song was indispensable to the offering only in the morning. See there. It appears to me, however, as Tosafos write at the end of Rosh Hashanah, that if the libations were brought at night, no song was recited. Although initially they should not be delayed until night, the offering remains valid if this occurred. The morning song, by contrast, could not be deferred at all. Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 3 of Hilchos Tefillah, writes that the song over the offering was recited while much of the day remained, because song cannot be recited after the daily afternoon offering, even though much of the day remains, since the incense and lamps still follow it. Accordingly, once Minchah is complete, it is as though the daily afternoon offering has already been brought, and the song can no longer be recited. In the Beis HaMikdash the song was recited when the wine libation was poured upon the offering. At Shacharis, however, it may be recited afterward because the time for song continues throughout the day. The end of Rosh Hashanah implies that on Yom Tov they did not recite the weekday chapter of Tehillim but other songs. Therefore, some places recite different Tehillim on Yom Tov according to Maseches Soferim. These are not necessarily the same songs recited in the Beis HaMikdash. Nowadays people are not meticulous about this, because the recitation is only commemorative. They also recite the chapter of Tehillim after Mussaf, although Mussaf had its own song, as stated at the end of Rosh Hashanah. Knesses HaGedolah writes that his community recites the daily chapter of Tehillim after Shacharis and the Mussaf chapter of Tehillim after Mussaf. Maamar, siman 25.
He should omit one. Beit Yosef writes that one is liable to death only because he entered the Mikdash unnecessarily, which is not relevant today. Be’er Sheva writes that Beit Yosef erred. Yoma 53 states that even where one is not liable for the entry, such as where he entered inadvertently but burned the incense intentionally, he is liable for the incense. It further states that if he omitted the herb that causes the smoke to rise, he is liable to death. One must therefore be careful regarding the eleven spices and the herb that causes the smoke to rise. Regarding the remaining details there is less concern. Beit Yosef further writes that liability to death applies only to burning the incense, not to reciting the passage, and only when done intentionally. Meticulous people therefore recite it every day. Bach writes in the name of Maharshal that it is unnecessary to recite “Rabbi Elazar said,” etc., because the Kaddish follows Aleinu, as explained above. Nevertheless, the custom is to recite it.
