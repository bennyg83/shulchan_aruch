Aleinu LeShabeach. The writings state that Aleinu should be recited after each of the three daily prayers, and Knesses HaGedolah, siman 234, rules similarly. Sefer Shtei Yados, however, cites Tolaas Yaakov that it should not be recited at Minchah. At the end of Aleinu one should recite the verse, “Hashem shall be King,” etc. One should say “and the seat of His glory.” One should say “He is our God, there is no other,” as stated by Tashbetz. Aleinu should be recited with awe and fear, because all the hosts of heaven listen, and the Holy One, blessed be He, stands with His heavenly retinue. They all answer, “Praiseworthy is the people for whom this is so; praiseworthy is the people whose God is Hashem.” This is from Mateh Moshe. The Arizal said “and therefore we hope to You” with the conjunction “and,” and so writes Kanfei Renanim of Rabbi Avraham Azulai and Yad Aharon.
And we bow. He should bow and prostrate himself deeply. This is from the Arizal and Magen Avraham.
Mourner’s Kaddish. One who is uncertain whether his father or mother died should recite Kaddish when no other mourner is present. This is from Maharil. Grandsons may recite Kaddish. Other mourners, however, recite two Kaddishim while the grandsons recite one. There is no distinction whether they descend through a son or daughter. Responsa Knesses Yechezkel, however, writes that only a son’s son may recite Kaddish, but not a daughter’s son. Certainly a daughter herself has no right to recite Kaddish in the synagogue. If people wish to arrange a private minyan for her, they may do so. See there at the end of part 14. A person observing a yahrzeit has rights to all Kaddishim of that day, including Kaddish after study chapters, Rus, and Shir HaShirim. This is from Maharam Mintz, siman 80, and Magen Avraham rules accordingly. Knesses Yechezkel, however, writes that everything obligatory, such as leading the prayer, its Kaddishim, the Kaddish after Shir HaYichud, and likewise Barachi Nafshi and study chapters, belongs to the yahrzeit. Regarding Koheles, Rus, and Shir HaShirim, lots should be drawn, because these readings are merely customary. They are comparable to Kaddish after Tehillim and the daily chapter of Tehillim, for which lots are drawn. The Kaddish recited on Friday evening after Mizmor Shir LeYom HaShabbos belongs to the mourners, unless the yahrzeit is a member of the group responsible for Kabbalas Shabbos. Everything follows local custom. Knesses Yechezkel writes there that one whose yahrzeit falls on Friday has no claim to that Kaddish. Rather, the mourners and the person whose yahrzeit falls on Shabbos draw lots. See there. When four or five people draw lots, and two have identical letters, such as both drawing tav, while the others drew lower numbers, the entire lottery is void and all must draw again. The two may not say that the others already lost their portions and only they need redraw. If one person alone drew tav and the others drew equal lower numbers, the first certainly won. If two or three people drew lots and another arrived the following day, the original division is void and they must draw again. The one who recited Kaddish at Maariv does not participate in the new lottery. If the newcomer was already in the city and failed to come, he forfeited his right, because his absence indicates that he waived it and accepted being last. If one person recited Kaddish, and another arrived the following day and said, “I will recite today in exchange for the Kaddish you recited yesterday, and we will draw lots for the third Kaddish,” while the first replied, “Since you did not come yesterday, I acquired the Kaddish, and today’s two Kaddishim should be divided between us,” the second person is correct. He recites the first Kaddish, and they draw lots for the second. If there are four yahrzeits and only three came the previous evening, the three divide all the prayer Kaddishim and the fourth recites the Kaddish after the daily chapter of Tehillim. If there are so many yahrzeits that even all available Kaddishim do not suffice for equal division, one who did not attend Maariv loses his right. All the authorities agree that even where one mourner is capable of leading the prayer and another is not, the one who leads does not thereby lose his rights to Kaddish. If both are capable of leading, they draw lots to determine who leads first and who second. If two people have yahrzeit and one must leave early on a journey, he may not compel the other by saying, “Give me the Kaddish now and you may take tomorrow’s.” If the other demands a lottery, the law follows him, because he may say, “I wish to advance myself in performing the mitzvah.” When two people died under compulsion, one being killed and the other dying naturally, the son of the murdered person takes precedence over the son of the one who died naturally. Since the former was killed, his death itself served as atonement, and he certainly repented before dying, unlike one who died naturally. When a mourner is within the thirty days, all Kaddishim are given to him. The Kaddish after a chapter of Tehillim belongs to the other mourners. In places where a resident takes precedence over a visitor, a visitor nevertheless has the right once to lead the prayer and recite Kaddish. Shach writes that if a mourner within thirty days is present, the visitor receives no Kaddish at all. A person studying in a local yeshivah has the status of a resident. A teacher whose wife does not live elsewhere also has the status of a resident. See Knesses Yechezkel concerning this. Regarding Kaddish, we do not apply the rule that part of a day counts as a full day. Therefore, if one mourner’s relative died during the week and another mourner’s relative died on Sunday, they are equal on Shabbos. One who mourns for his father and subsequently loses his mother receives all Kaddishim because he is within thirty days for his mother. Nevertheless, regarding the other Kaddishim he divides equally with the other mourners by virtue of his father. Magen Avraham writes this in the name of Maharan. Knesses Yechezkel challenges this, for according to that reasoning one who mourns both father and mother should recite twice as many Kaddishim as another mourner, which is not so. A single remembrance suffices for both. He resolves it as follows. If the person is within thirty days for one parent, the prayer Kaddishim belong to him, while mourners within twelve months recite Kaddish after Tehillim and similar passages. If several yahrzeits occur on a day and the mourner within thirty days is therefore entirely displaced from the prayer Kaddishim, he may divide the other Kaddishim with the mourners because of his father. Otherwise, he does not recite twice as many Kaddishim, because a single remembrance suffices for both. An adult mourner within shivah who goes to the synagogue on Shabbos takes precedence over a yahrzeit, and certainly over one within thirty days. A minor within shivah, however, has the law of one within thirty days, and the yahrzeit receives one Kaddish. This is from Mishnas Binyamin, siman 73. Shach, siman 376, writes that even against a minor within shivah, a mourner within thirty days receives one Kaddish. Magen Avraham rules that the minor entirely displaces the mourner within thirty days, while the yahrzeit receives one Kaddish. One whose mourning was annulled by a festival, or who buried his relative during the festival, has the law of one within shivah. A minor within shivah has this status because he attends the synagogue throughout all seven days, and the yahrzeit receives one Kaddish. If on Shabbos there are a minor within shivah, an adult within shivah, and a yahrzeit, the adult recites two Kaddishim and the minor one. The adult may say to the minor regarding the third Kaddish, “I am not taking it from you, but from the yahrzeit.” Eliyah Zuta expresses doubt where the adult within shivah arranges a minyan in his own home, whether on Shabbos he might be equal to a minor within shivah, so that the yahrzeit would receive one Kaddish. We do not distinguish in this manner, and the custom follows what was stated above. A mourner within thirty days receives all prayer Kaddishim. The remaining mourners within twelve months recite Kaddish after Tehillim and similar passages. When there is a resident yahrzeit and a resident within thirty days, the yahrzeit receives one Kaddish at Maariv and the mourner within thirty days receives two Kaddishim at Shacharis. When there is a visiting yahrzeit and a resident within thirty days or within twelve months, the yahrzeit receives one Kaddish. When there is a visitor within thirty days and a resident within twelve months, they divide all the prayer Kaddishim. A resident yahrzeit displaces a visiting yahrzeit. When there are a resident yahrzeit, a visiting yahrzeit, and a resident within thirty days, the visitor recites one of the two Shacharis Kaddishim that would otherwise belong to the mourner within thirty days. The resident yahrzeit cannot say to the visiting yahrzeit, “I precede you,” because the visitor may reply, “I am not taking from your portion, but from the mourner within thirty days.” Regarding Kaddish after Tehillim, where a yahrzeit draws lots together with the mourners, if a resident yahrzeit waives the lottery and allows a visiting yahrzeit to recite one Kaddish, the mourners cannot displace the visitor, because a yahrzeit takes precedence over them. If they already drew lots and the mourners won, they may tell the visiting yahrzeit, “Your own fortune caused your loss.” The visiting yahrzeit nevertheless participates in the lottery with them, because relative to them he is equal. For a separate repetition of Shema near the entrance, meaning a minyan formed in the foyer before the door, visitor and resident are equal. A visitor, however, has no share in Kaddish after Tehillim, the daily chapter of Tehillim, other Tehillim, Shir HaShirim, Rus, or Koheles, except when he has a yahrzeit, as stated above. The universal custom is that on the day one stops reciting Kaddish at the end of eleven months, he recites all Kaddishim. Nevertheless, he does not displace a yahrzeit or a mourner within thirty days. He should stop reciting Kaddish one day earlier, and not on the calendar date that will become the yahrzeit. For example, if the death occurred on the third of Shevat, he should stop on the second of Teves. The reason for stopping is so that his father not be considered wicked. If he recited Kaddish on the third of Teves, that would already be one day in the twelfth month, and part of a month counts as a month. It would therefore be as though he recited for twelve months. He should consequently recite through the second of Teves, stop that day, and observe the yahrzeit on the third of Shevat. The yahrzeit is always determined by the date of death, not the date of burial, even when burial was delayed for several days and even during the first year. If a person’s mother died and his father objects to his reciting Kaddish, the father cannot prevent him. A visitor who recited one Kaddish in any synagogue has no further right to Kaddish in any synagogue of that community for thirty days. All these rulings I derived from the words of the early and later authorities. See Knesses Yechezkel at the end of part 14, and Eliyah Rabbah, who cites many laws and responsa concerning these matters.
Evening and morning after the prayer. Taz asks that the Gemara states that nothing precedes the daily morning offering except the incense. Since the prayers were instituted corresponding to the daily offerings, Pitum HaKetores should be recited before Shemoneh Esrei. He concludes, “Based on this, I personally adopted the practice of reciting Pitum HaKetores before Baruch SheAmar.” Magen Avraham asks why we recite it after Mussaf, since according to all opinions the incense preceded the additional offerings. He answers that perhaps our intention is to depart from prayer with words of Torah. Shelah writes that it should be recited at Shacharis both before and after the prayer. In the evening it should be recited before Minchah, but not after Maariv, because offering the incense after lighting the lamps was invalid. See Knesses HaGedolah, Magen Avraham, and Be’er Sheva.
The song that the Leviim. I found written that the formulation “the Mishnah of the song that the Leviim,” etc., is an error. Tur’s intent is to recite on each day the chapter of Tehillim belonging to that day. Rambam writes similarly in the order of prayer, as does Knesses HaGedolah. Magen Avraham writes that in his opinion Tur intends that the Mishnah also be recited, so as to depart from prayer with words of Torah. He concludes that on Shabbos the custom is to recite the Mishnah as printed in all siddurim. Knesses HaGedolah writes: I observed a custom in all places that on Rosh Chodesh, Chol HaMoed, Chanukah, and Purim, instead of the daily chapter of Tehillim that the Leviim recited, they recite another chapter of Tehillim appropriate to the day. On Rosh Chodesh, Barachi Nafshi; on Chol HaMoed Pesach, Hodu LaShem; on Chol HaMoed Sukkos, KeAyil Taarog; on Chanukah, Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis LeDavid; and on Purim, Al Ayelet HaShachar. I am astonished who permitted them to change the daily chapter of Tehillim that the Leviim recited. He concludes that the daily chapter of Tehillim should not be omitted. Rather, both the daily chapter of Tehillim and the chapter of Tehillim appropriate to the occasion should be recited. Rama of Fano, siman 25, rules similarly. As to which is recited first, he concludes that the daily chapter of Tehillim precedes, because when a frequent and an infrequent mitzvah coincide, the frequent one takes precedence. On Chanukah, however, Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis is recited before the daily chapter of Tehillim because of publicizing the miracle. See there and Magen Avraham.
Lest he omit. See the question raised by Be’er Sheva in Kerisos 46 against Beit Yosef in this siman. Taz and Magen Avraham raise the same issue. Responsa Shaar Ephraim, siman 16, attempts to defend Beit Yosef, but his explanation does not reflect Beit Yosef’s wording. Beit Yosef implies that even if one omitted the herb that causes the smoke to rise, he is not liable to death. This is the opposite of Shaar Ephraim’s interpretation and requires study. See Mishneh LaMelech, Mikraei Kodesh, page 125b, Birkas HaZevach, Pri Chadash, and Eliyah Rabbah, who discusses this at great length. Regarding the “mei raglayim” mentioned in Pitum HaKetores, Kol Bo writes that there is a certain spring called Mei Raglayim. The phrase “because of honor” means that the spring bears a degrading name. Be’er Sheva, however, argues at length that actual urine is intended. See there and Bnei Yaakov.
And he bows and departs. Maharil bowed three times while leaving his place toward the aron kodesh. Every time he exited the synagogue doorway he did so like a student departing from his teacher. When leaving the synagogue, he did not turn his back toward the sanctuary, but walked sideways. He acted similarly when descending from the platform. See Magen Avraham.
