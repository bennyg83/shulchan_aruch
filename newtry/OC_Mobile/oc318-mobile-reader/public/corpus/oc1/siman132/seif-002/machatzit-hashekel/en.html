Seif katan 2: They bow, etc. Rama writes that one should pause between “who cannot save” and the word “but we,” so that it not appear that the words “but we” refer back to the preceding passage, as though we too, Heaven forbid, join the nations of the lands.
“All the Kaddishes” means those called prayer Kaddishes, namely the Kaddish after Aleinu. “To cast lots” means between one observing a yahrzeit and mourners.
It belongs to the mourners unless there is a yahrzeit, etc. Meaning, one whose yahrzeit occurs on Shabbos. If his yahrzeit was on Friday, he has no claim to the Kaddish after “A Tehillim, a song for the Shabbos day,” because that chapter of Tehillim belongs to Shabbos. Even according to our practice, under which reciting the chapter of Tehillim does not constitute acceptance of Shabbos until Barchu, as Magen Avraham writes at the end of siman 261, the chapter of Tehillim itself nevertheless belongs specifically to Shabbos. I later saw that Baer Heitev writes the same in the name of a manuscript responsum.
A proof may be brought from the beginning of the second chapter of Yoma. Although all Temple services were allocated among the kohanim by lottery, the Sages initially instituted no lottery for the morning terumas hadeshen until they saw that the matter led to danger. The Gemara explains that since terumas hadeshen was performed before dawn, while it was still night, one might have thought the kohanim did not consider it an important service, since all services are performed by day, and therefore they would not quarrel over who should perform it. No lottery was needed; whoever wished could do so. Another reason is that because it was performed before dawn and sleep exerted its force, many kohanim would not come, so there was no need to fear contention. If many nevertheless wished to perform it, they would run up the thirty-two-amah ramp, and whoever first reached within four amos of the altar merited the service. If two were equal within those four amos, the supervisor told them, “Extend your fingers.” Rashi and Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura explain that everyone extended a finger. This was the lottery: each kohen extended one finger, the supervisor announced a number, and the one on whom the count ended won. He would announce one hundred or two hundred, begin counting from one, and repeatedly circle through their fingers, since it is forbidden to count Jews directly, until he reached the announced number. Thus, although only the two who reached within four amos of the altar had qualified, they nevertheless had to participate in the lottery together with all the kohanim. Eliyah Rabbah rejects this proof, arguing that there it was impossible for only those two to conduct the lottery, as Tosafos Yom Tov writes. Tosafos Yom Tov states that a finger lottery cannot be conducted between two people, for if the agreed number is even or odd, once one knows with whom the count begins, one also knows on whom it will end, so it is not a genuine lottery. This does not refute the proof. Tosafos Yom Tov wrote this only regarding Rashi and Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura, who explain that the instruction “Extend your fingers” was addressed to all the kohanim. Why did they not explain that it was directed only to the two who were equal within the four amos? Tosafos Yom Tov answers that this cannot be, because no genuine lottery could be made between only two. If the person announcing the number also began the count, he could ensure victory for whichever one he wished: an even number would award it to the second, and an odd number to the first. If many kohanim participate, however, it is not easy to know on whom the count will end. Yet even according to Rambam and Ritva, who hold that the one announcing the number did not begin the count, a lottery between two would technically be possible because the announcer did not know with whom the count would begin. Nevertheless, the expression “Extend your fingers” implies a true lottery like the other Temple lotteries, and that is inapplicable to only two people. Moreover, there would be no need to say “Extend your fingers.” Although one may not count Jews and they therefore extended fingers to be counted, with only two there is no need to count them at all. One person could state that the count begins with Reuven and ends with Shimon; then another person, who does not know with whom it begins, could announce one hundred or two hundred, and it would automatically be known without any counting that Shimon won. If he announced one hundred and one, Reuven would win. Necessarily, as Rashi and Rabbi Ovadiah explain, all the kohanim were told to extend their fingers. Magen Avraham’s ruling is nevertheless compelling. The Mishnah itself is difficult: if the two kohanim who were equal within four amos had already qualified, why should everyone take part in the lottery? They should have drawn lots only between those two. According to Rambam and Ritva this could have been done through counting, and even according to those who hold that the announcer began counting, so that it could not be done between only two, they could have instituted that he not begin the count or used a different kind of lottery. Why should those two lose after having already qualified and be required to draw lots with all the kohanim? Necessarily, when such a tie occurs, the earlier qualification is entirely void, and even those two have not acquired the right. The same applies here. However, Responsa Me’il Tzedakah, siman 10, disagrees with Magen Avraham for another reason. There, even the two who qualified through running had not acquired an absolute right, for it was insufficient that one should merit a Temple service merely because he was fleet-footed. Initially, they thought a lottery unnecessary for the reasons stated above. When it happened that many wished to perform the service, they allowed the fastest runner to win, even though this was not a complete acquisition, because no alternative was available while they did not wish to institute a lottery. When two were equal and a lottery between them was therefore unavoidable, why should the others lose, since those two had never acquired a complete right through running? Here, however, the two claimants received a complete right from Heaven, while the others lost. The lottery should therefore be only between those two. See there, where he derives additional laws from this distinction.
But if one of them has, etc. For then the first has certainly acquired the right. When two are equal within the four amos, neither has a complete acquisition, since each must at least draw lots against the other who is equal to him. The entire lottery is therefore void. Here, however, one person alone acquired a complete right, so with respect to him the lottery remains valid.
Two or three, etc., and the next day another came, etc. This follows Choshen Mishpat, siman 175. The case is a day with three Kaddishes, Maariv, Shacharis, and Minchah. Two people drew lots the previous day. Reuven won the right to recite the Kaddish at Maariv, Shimon at Shacharis, and they had not yet drawn lots for Minchah. Reuven recited Maariv. Before Shimon recited the Kaddish at Shacharis, Levi arrived. Shimon cannot tell him, “You recite it at Minchah, for I already won Shacharis yesterday.” Rather, the earlier arrangement is void, and Shimon and Levi must draw lots for the Kaddishes of Shacharis and Minchah. Choshen Mishpat there states similarly: Two brothers inherited three fields. By lottery each took one entire field, and they divided the third field between them, each taking half. A third brother then arrived. They cannot tell him, “Take the field that I took half of and our brother took the other half.” Rather, they must draw lots anew for all three fields, because the division is void. It is further explained there that even if they redraw lots and the third brother receives the field that the first two had divided, each of the original two brothers, who had each won one field in the first lottery, may nevertheless tell the other, “The first lottery and division were already annulled, and we too must redraw lots between ourselves.” The same applies here. If two people drew lots and one won Maariv and the other Shacharis, while no lot was yet drawn for Minchah, and a third arrived before the first recited Maariv, they cannot tell him that he should recite Minchah. They must draw lots with him. Even if the result is that the third recites Minchah, the earlier lottery between the first two is still void. The one who had won Maariv did not acquire it and must draw a new lot with the one who had won Shacharis, just as in the case of the fields.
And if he was in the city, etc. As stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 176, seif 25: Two partners have a claim against an outsider. One partner sued that outsider and lost in court. The second partner may sue him again and present a different argument. But if the second partner was in the city when the first sued and did not appear before beis din, he is presumed to have waived his own claim and relied upon his partner’s arguments and suit, and may not sue again.
The same appears in Bava Basra, chapter 9, 145b. Their custom was that when a person married, his friends and groomsmen would come to rejoice with him, and each would bring a gift for the celebration. Later, when one of those groomsmen married, the original groom was required to attend his celebration and bring him a wedding gift. This obligation could be collected through beis din if he neither attended nor brought the gift. If he was in the city when his groomsman married and did not come to rejoice with him, he must pay the full value of the gift. He cannot say, “I did not know that you were marrying. Had I known, I would have come with my gift and rejoiced with you,” because since he was in the city, we presume he certainly heard and knew, and waived participation in the celebration. He must therefore pay the entire gift. Such a gift is called shushvinus.
As stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 171, end of seif 8.
One of the partners, etc. Meaning, in a courtyard that cannot be divided, because it is too small for each partner to receive an area fit for use, they cast lots so that one uses it alone for one or two years, and afterward the other uses it alone for one or two years. They draw lots to determine who uses it first. It is concerning this that he writes: If one of the partners used alone a courtyard that cannot be divided, etc.
For there we say that he used his own portion, and it still exists. Meaning, even had his partner been present, this one would have been entitled to use the entire courtyard in a manner that neither harmed nor prevented the other partner’s use, for before division each partner has a right throughout the entire courtyard. Since I was not present, your use did not harm me, and now the entire courtyard remains before you for division as it did previously. What I used was my own, and now we will divide by lottery as stated above. Here, however, the case resembles partners in movable property.
This is not so, for nevertheless he can say: I knew, etc. Meaning, no proof can be brought from the courtyard case, as explained above. On the contrary, this resembles partnership in movable property. You sought to reject that comparison through reasoning, arguing that this case also does not resemble movable property. He therefore writes that the absent partner can say, “I knew,” etc.
The established custom, etc. This is unlike Tzemach Tzedek, etc. See the end of Sukkah. Tzemach Tzedek brings proof from Bava Basra 7b that in a lottery we say a person acquires only the smaller right that can be attributed to the lot. The same applies here: we say he acquired only the right to be first, but not also the right to recite two Kaddishes. One might ask what benefit there is in reciting first such that we can say the lottery awarded only this. Tzemach Tzedek therefore brings proof from Megillah 21b that precedence itself is a benefit. Reason also supports this, for one should not allow a mitzvah that comes to his hand to become stale.
Magen Avraham alludes to the end of Sukkah to show, as Tzemach Tzedek writes, that reciting first is itself considered a benefit. There were twelve loaves of lechem hapanim. On Shabbos they were divided between the outgoing mishmar and the incoming mishmar, since the kohanim were divided into twenty-four watches, each serving in the Beis HaMikdash for one week. On Shabbos a new mishmar entered service and the mishmar that had served that week departed. The first Tanna holds that six loaves were given to the outgoing mishmar and six to the incoming mishmar. Rabbi Yehudah disagrees and holds that the outgoing mishmar received only five while the incoming mishmar received seven. The incoming mishmar received one extra loaf as compensation for closing the gates. The outgoing mishmar opened the Temple gates in the morning, while the incoming mishmar would have to close them in the evening because the outgoing mishmar’s period had already ended. The Gemara asks: Why does the outgoing mishmar not say to the incoming mishmar, “Give me the six loaves that are due to me”? There is no advantage in your receiving one extra loaf, since on the following Shabbos you too will have to give one loaf to the next incoming mishmar. The Gemara answers that the outgoing mishmar nevertheless has an advantage, following the saying, “A small gourd now is better than a large gourd later.” Rashi explains: If a person offers his fellow a small gourd immediately, or the option of leaving it to grow into a larger gourd later, people prefer to take it immediately while still small. A small gourd now is better than a large one later, for no one knows what the day will bring. All the more so regarding a mitzvah, it is preferable to perform it earlier while one has the opportunity, rather than wait, for one does not know what may happen afterward. Magen Avraham therefore agrees in law with Tzemach Tzedek, although he already wrote that the custom is otherwise. Know that below Magen Avraham brings proof from the custom itself that being first is considered a benefit.
For we do not compel a person to forego his right on grounds of the attribute of Sodom. Meaning, this person benefits while the other loses nothing.
As stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 311: One rents a particular ship from another and says, “I will load this ship with wine,” without designating a particular wine. He loads wine and the ship sinks together with the wine. He need not pay the rental fee, because he can say, “Give me the ship I rented and I will load it with other wine, for I did not designate this wine, but I did designate this particular ship.” Although the owner wishes to provide another ship, the renter may refuse. Even though this seems like the attribute of Sodom, since one ship is equivalent to another, we do not compel him to yield because he gains through his insistence, for thereby he is exempt from the rental fee. The same applies here.
The same is implied in siman 174. Sema also cites this proof in siman 311. There, two people own two fields jointly and wish to divide them. One of them owns another field adjacent to one of the jointly owned fields and says, “Give me without lottery the field adjoining the one I already own, so that my two fields will be contiguous.” Although the two jointly owned fields are equal, the other may object and insist that they draw lots. Even though this appears to be the attribute of Sodom, since the fields are equal, we do not compel him because he may profit through his insistence. The lottery may award him the field adjacent to his fellow’s field, and in order to induce him to exchange it so that the other’s two fields will be together, the other may pay him additional money. Shach in siman 311 writes that this applies specifically where the person suffered a loss, meaning there, where his wine sank, and therefore the law was lenient in not compelling him to forego his right when he benefits through insisting. But in an ordinary case it would not apply. In truth, Shach himself retracts this conclusion there. See there.
For when there are three, they cast lots, etc. This proves that being first is itself a benefit. Above he brought another proof from the end of Sukkah.
It is preferable for his son. Meaning, when a son is mourning for his father or mother and wishes to travel far away to a place where he will be unable to recite Kaddish, he therefore wants to appoint another person to recite Kaddish in his place. Rather than have someone recite it without payment. The reason may be like the law in Bava Kamma concerning one who injures another and must pay for his medical care. If the injurer says, “I will provide a physician who will heal you without charge,” the injured person may say, “I do not want that; pay a physician.” People say, “A physician who heals for nothing is worth nothing,” meaning that because he works without pay he will not exert himself as much in the treatment. The same applies here: when one pays another to recite Kaddish, he will be more careful in its recitation. Furthermore, mussar works write that a mitzvah performed without cost is not considered as precious.
To grant the same right to a grandson, and likewise to a daughter’s son.
Two who died under compulsion, etc. Since he was killed, meaning by non-Jews and unlawfully, this itself provides some atonement. Therefore, this applies even if he did not repent. So writes Shach in Yoreh Deah, siman 340, seif katan 10.
All the Kaddishes. Meaning, those called prayer Kaddishes.
One Kaddish for each. Meaning, one must give each person observing a yahrzeit one Kaddish from the Tehillim.
The practice is to cast lots, and see what I wrote above that the custom does not follow Responsa Maharam Mintz. According to Maharam Mintz, the yahrzeit claimant receives his right without a lottery. Therefore, according to him, no yahrzeit claimant would receive a Kaddish associated with a chapter of Tehillim, since each yahrzeit claimant already has a Kaddish and it is as though there are four prayer Kaddishes, in which case the yahrzeit has no claim to the chapter of Tehillim Kaddish. Magen Avraham’s ruling was written only according to the custom that a yahrzeit claimant acquires no right to the Kaddish after Shir HaShirim except by lottery.
He also ruled, etc. From the law of the thirty days, etc., on account of his father. Eliyah Rabbah disagrees, arguing that according to this, one who is within twelve months for both his father and mother should recite Kaddish twice, once for his father and once for his mother, which is incorrect. It appears that Maharan also agrees that he recites only one Kaddish and it counts for both. This is evident from his formulation: one who is within twelve months for his father and within thirty days for his mother. He did not state one who is within twelve months for both father and mother. Accordingly, even in this case, where he is within twelve months for his father and within thirty days for his mother, he has only one Kaddish, like any ordinary mourner within thirty days.
One whose mourning was annulled by a festival. Meaning, he observed part of the shivah before the festival, and the festival then cancels the remaining shivah. He need not resume the laws of shivah after the festival.
Likewise one who buried his dead during the festival. During the festival he observes only private mourning practices and attends shul. After the festival he observes shivah, as explained below in siman 548 and in Yoreh Deah, siman 399.
Similar to a minor. Meaning, a minor within shivah who attends shul throughout all seven days receives all the Kaddishes. Nevertheless, if there is a yahrzeit claimant, the minor must give him one Kaddish. An adult mourner, however, attends shul only on Shabbos. If there is a yahrzeit claimant on Shabbos, the adult mourner within shivah need not give him any Kaddish. One whose mourning was annulled by a festival, or who buried his dead during the festival, attends shul throughout the seven days and therefore has the law of the minor.
This is the reason that on Shabbos the yahrzeit claimant receives nothing against an adult mourner within shivah, as explained above.
That he may recite it on Motzaei Shabbos. Although the day of the yahrzeit has already passed, he nevertheless benefits from reciting Kaddish because it is in the prayer immediately adjacent to the yahrzeit and no other arrangement was possible. This applies to an adult mourner, who will not attend shul on Motzaei Shabbos. A minor, however, will also attend shul then, so the yahrzeit claimant would receive nothing unless the minor gives him a Kaddish on Shabbos. Even if the minor’s seventh day falls on Shabbos, so that the yahrzeit claimant could recite Kaddish on Motzaei Shabbos, no distinction was made. Alternatively, it may happen that on Motzaei Shabbos another minor within shivah is also present, and the yahrzeit claimant would then receive no Kaddish at all.
It appears to me that a minor within shivah takes precedence over a mourner within thirty days and need not give him any Kaddish. A yahrzeit claimant is superior because his time is fixed. This is also the view of Responsa Maharam of Lublin cited by Shach, siman 376. Shach there disagrees and holds that a mourner within thirty days takes precedence over a yahrzeit claimant. Since the minor must give one Kaddish to a yahrzeit claimant, he must certainly give one to a mourner within thirty days.
On the day he ceases. Meaning, at the end of eleven months.
As stated in Yoreh Deah. Thus, if the death occurred on Rosh Chodesh Shevat, the end of eleven months is the end of Kislev. He ceases reciting Kaddish on the last day of Kislev, not on Rosh Chodesh Teves, which is already the beginning of the twelfth month, for the twelve months conclude at the end of Teves. So writes Baer Heitev, and this is correct. See citations 4, 24, 40, 50, 80, 97, 100.
