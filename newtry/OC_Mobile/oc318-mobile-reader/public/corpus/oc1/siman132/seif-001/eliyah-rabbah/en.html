[1] Kedushah, etc. Levush writes that Ya'ancha BeYom Tzarah was instituted before Seder Kedushah because Kedushah itself was instituted as a result of distress. Rabbi David Abudraham writes that it was placed at the end of the prayer because Midrash Tehillim states that after a person prays, he is told, "May your prayer be answered." David therefore placed it after eighteen psalms, since Ashrei HaIsh and Lamah Ragshu constitute one psalm. Kol Bo, siman 13, writes that it is recited quietly except for the first verse, because one may not recite passages from Scripture by heart, while the first verse is exceptionally familiar. Rabbi David Abudraham explains that "You are holy, enthroned" means that Hashem waits for and arranges the prayers of Israel before those of the angels. Alternatively, the Holy One, blessed is He, waits until the last congregation in Israel has prayed and then accepts all their prayers together. Rabbi David Abudraham writes that the common practice of saying in Kaddish, "le'alam u'le'almei almaya," is mistaken; rather, one should say, "be'alam u'le'almaya," meaning that in the world of the angels He is separate and distinct from them. Lest one argue that because He is so exalted He certainly does not direct His attention toward human affairs, the verse states, "Hashem, Master of legions, the whole earth is filled," etc. Why did they institute in this Kedushah the verse "Hashem shall reign forever," which Moshe said, while in the standing Kedushah we say "Hashem shall reign forever and ever," which David said? Since this order is translated into Aramaic, and there is no authorized Targum of Kesuvim. Rosh writes that he received a tradition that when Yonasan ben Uziel translated the verse "Hashem shall reign forever," a Heavenly voice emerged and said, "Who is revealing My secrets to human beings?" because this verse contains the end of days. Kol Bo writes that we say "Hashem shall reign" in order to use the Targum of Onkelos, which is superior to that of Aquila. Levush writes that Seder Kedushah is not recited at Shacharis on Shabbos and Yom Tov because it is phrased as a request. His words are difficult, for it is recited at Minchah in the same form used every day. The primary reason appears to me to be that of Mateh Moshe: "I found it written that it was instituted at the end of the prayer for the sake of the unlearned who arrive late, so that they would not lose the opportunity to hear Kedushah. They therefore established for them a miniature form of prayer containing Ashrei, followed by Kedushah, mention of Zion, and Torah study. Accordingly, this Seder Kedushah contains eighteen mentions of Hashem, corresponding to the eighteen blessings. It is not recited at Shacharis on Shabbos and Yom Tov because everyone comes to the synagogue before Kedushah. The reason it is recited at Minchah is that they would deliver a discourse before Minchah and customarily conclude the discourse with verses of redemption and Kedushah; the unlearned also came to hear the discourse." I saw the same in Abudraham. It is therefore recited in Aramaic so the unlearned can understand it. According to this, latecomers should concentrate on this Kedushah even while occupied with another part of the service. Magen Avraham writes that if one enters the synagogue while the congregation is reciting Seder Kedushah, he should recite it with them even before his own prayer. Likewise, he may recite it before the two verses, "U'Va LeTzion," etc., and "And as for Me, this is My covenant," etc., and then recite those two verses afterward. Certainly, he may recite Ashrei and Lamnatzeiach afterward. See the beginning of siman 292. When saying, "Blessed is our God Who created us," etc., one should do so with concentration and heartfelt joy. One should pause between "those who seek You, Hashem" and "Hashem desires." Abudraham writes in the laws of Tisha B'Av that in a mourner's house they do not say, "And as for Me, this is My covenant," because a mourner is forbidden to study Torah.
