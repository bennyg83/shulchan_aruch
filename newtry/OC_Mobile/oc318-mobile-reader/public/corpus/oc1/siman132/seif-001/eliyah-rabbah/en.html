The Kedushah, etc. Levush writes that Ya’ancha BeYom Tzarah was instituted before the order of Kedushah because the Kedushah itself was instituted through distress. Rabbi David Avudraham writes that it was instituted at the end of prayer, as Midrash Tehillim states that when a person prays, he is told, “May your prayer be answered.” David therefore placed it after eighteen psalms, because Ashrei HaIsh and Lamah Ragshu count as one. Kol Bo, siman 13, writes that it is recited silently except for the first verse, because one may not recite written passages from memory, while the first verse is especially familiar. Rabbi David Avudraham explains “You are holy, enthroned” as meaning that Hashem waits and prepares Himself for the prayers of Israel before those of the angels. Alternatively, the Holy One, blessed be He, waits until the final group of Israel has prayed and then accepts all their prayers together. Rabbi David Avudraham writes that the common people who say in Kaddish “le’alam ulele’almei almaya” are mistaken. They should say “kaddish be’alma ule’almaya,” meaning that in the world of the angels He is separated and distinct from them. Lest one say that because He is so exalted, all the more so He does not observe human affairs, Scripture says, “Hashem of Hosts, the entire earth is filled,” etc. One may ask why this Kedushah includes the verse “Hashem shall reign,” said by Moshe, while the standing Kedushah includes “Hashem shall reign forever,” said by David. The answer is that this order is translated into Aramaic, and Kesuvim are not translated. Rosh writes that he had a received tradition that when Yonasan ben Uziel translated the verse “Hashem shall reign forever,” a heavenly voice emerged and said, “Who is revealing My secrets to human beings?” because the verse contains the time of the redemption. Kol Bo writes that we say “Hashem shall reign” in order to translate it according to Onkelos, whose translation is greater than that of Aquila. Levush writes that the reason this Kedushah is not customarily recited at Shacharis on Shabbos and Yom Tov is that it is formulated as a request. His words are strained, because it is recited at Minchah in the same language as on weekdays. The primary reason appears to me to be what Mateh Moshe writes: I found written that it was instituted at the end of prayer for unlearned people who came late and would otherwise miss hearing Kedushah. They therefore instituted for them a kind of abbreviated prayer, consisting of Ashrei, then Kedushah, mention of Zion, and Torah study. There are consequently eighteen mentions of the Divine Name in this order of Kedushah, corresponding to the eighteen blessings. It is not recited at Shacharis on Shabbos and Yom Tov because everyone comes to the synagogue before Kedushah. It was retained at Minchah because people would expound publicly before Minchah and it was customary to conclude the discourse with verses of redemption and Kedushah. The unlearned would also come to hear the discourse. I likewise saw this in Avudraham. This is also why it is in Aramaic, so that the unlearned would understand it. Accordingly, those who arrive late should concentrate upon this Kedushah even if they are occupied with another section. Magen Avraham writes that if one enters the synagogue while the congregation is reciting the order of Kedushah, he should recite it with them even before his own prayer. He may likewise recite it before the two introductory verses, “A redeemer shall come to Zion,” and “As for Me, this is My covenant,” and afterward recite those two verses. All the more so, he may recite Ashrei and Lamnatzeach afterward. See the beginning of siman 292. When saying, “Blessed is our God Who created us,” etc., one should say it with concentration and joy of heart. One should pause between “those who seek You, Hashem,” and “Hashem desires.” Avudraham writes in the laws of Tisha B’Av that in a mourner’s house one does not recite “As for Me, this is My covenant,” because a mourner is forbidden to study Torah.
