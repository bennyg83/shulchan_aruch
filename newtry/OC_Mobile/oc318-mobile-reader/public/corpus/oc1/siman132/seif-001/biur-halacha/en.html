Kuntras Ma'amar Kaddishin. In it the laws of Kaddish are explained briefly, collected from Levush, Magen Avraham, Kenesset Yechezkel, Derekh HaChayim, Magen Gibborim, and other authorities. There are five divisions in saying Kaddish: a mourner during the seven days, during the thirty days, during the twelve months, the day of cessation, meaning the day on which he stops saying Kaddish, and a yahrzeit. Each has its own rank. The reason and order of these divisions appear to have been established by Chazal according to the measure of judgment that rests upon the deceased, and Kaddish protects. Therefore the first seven days require more mercy and merit; the thirty days require more than the twelve months. Yahrzeit has two reasons: either because on that day the mourner's fortune is weakened, as Levush writes in Yoreh De'ah siman 402:12, or in order to bring nachat ruach to his father and mother, protect them, and atone for their souls. On the day of cessation, the custom is that the mourners give him all the Kaddishim, because since they customarily stop a month earlier, and he waives that month for them, they granted him this precedence. For that reason he does not take precedence over a yahrzeit or a thirty-day mourner, for they gain nothing through him and already have precedence over him.
The seven and thirty days are counted only from burial, even if much time passed between death and burial; they are not counted from death or from when the mourner heard, as the Rama writes in Yoreh De'ah siman 376:4, because the primary judgments of Gehinnom begin with the closing of the grave. A yahrzeit is counted from the day of death, as written in Yoreh De'ah siman 402:12 in the gloss regarding fasts, and there is no difference whether one was present at the death or only at the burial, as Levush writes there, even in the first year. The day of cessation appears from Levush to be counted from burial; if many days passed between death and burial, it is counted only from burial. We do not say "part of the day is like the whole" for the seven and thirty in this matter, for even at Minchah he still has the merit of the seven and thirty, as the later authorities wrote.
One stops saying Kaddish one month before the yahrzeit, and one day before the yahrzeit date in the following month. Thus if his yahrzeit falls on 7 Shevat, he says Kaddish only through the sixth day of Tevet, inclusive. In a leap year he stops two months earlier, because the months have caused the judgment to be removed from him, and he also stops one day earlier as above. A major mourner within seven days who goes to the synagogue on Shabbat, even if he made a minyan at home during the week, has all the Kaddishim and displaces the thirty-day mourner and the yahrzeit mourner, all the more the twelve-month mourner, from all prayer Kaddishim. But for Shir shel Yom or Tehillim Kaddish, the yahrzeit takes one Kaddish without lottery, and the remaining Tehillim Kaddishim are divided by lottery between the yahrzeit and the other mourners. A minor mourner within seven days who goes all seven days to synagogue, and the same applies to a major mourner who sits his shivah in the synagogue, has all regular Kaddishim even against a thirty-day mourner; but a yahrzeit gets one Kaddish. If there are many yahrzeits against all the Kaddishim, the minor seven-day mourner is entirely displaced. If shivah was canceled by a festival, nevertheless for seven days from burial he has the law of the seven regarding Kaddish, and likewise a festival does not cancel the thirty-day status for Kaddish. If his father died on a festival, he has the status of a minor seven-day mourner during the festival for Kaddish; after the festival, although he practices mourning, he no longer has the seven-day status for Kaddish except that seven days are counted from burial, and afterward he has only the law of a thirty-day mourner. One who has greater right regarding Kaddish, such as a seven-day mourner, a thirty-day mourner, or a yahrzeit, also has the corresponding right to lead the prayers at the amud.
Regarding Kaddish during the seven days, a guest and resident are equal for all Kaddishim, and a seven-day guest displaces resident thirty-day and yahrzeit mourners. A major seven-day mourner and a minor seven-day mourner are equal; nevertheless, if there is also a yahrzeit and there are three Kaddishim, the yahrzeit is entirely displaced: the major seven-day mourner says two Kaddishim and the minor seven-day mourner says one, for the major may say to the minor, "I am not taking this Kaddish from you, but from the yahrzeit, over whom I take precedence." A thirty-day mourner has all obligatory Kaddishim against a twelve-month mourner, but Kaddish after Tehillim, Shir shel Yom, Mizmor Shir Chanukat, and the Kaddish of Perisat Shema made at the doorway belong to the twelve-month mourners. When a thirty-day mourner and a yahrzeit are present, the yahrzeit says one Kaddish at Ma'ariv, and the thirty-day mourner says two Kaddishim at Shacharit and Minchah. If the yahrzeit did not come to synagogue at Ma'ariv, he says the first Kaddish at Shacharit. If there are many yahrzeits, the thirty-day mourner is entirely displaced. A thirty-day mourner displaces the day-of-cessation mourner, leaving him only one Kaddish. If one is a thirty-day mourner for his father and a twelve-month mourner for his mother, he has only the Kaddishim of a thirty-day mourner, for one remembrance counts for both; but if there are enough yahrzeits that the thirty-day mourner is entirely displaced, he at least has Kaddish among the Tehillim Kaddishim like other twelve-month mourners, as Kenesset Yechezkel rules, not like the Magen Avraham in the name of Maharan, and this is primary.
A yahrzeit displaces a twelve-month mourner from all prayers and obligatory Kaddishim of that day, but cannot displace mourners from Kaddish after Tehillim, Shir shel Yom, or Mizmor Shir Chanukat. Regarding the Kaddish of Mizmor Shir le-Yom HaShabbat, if his yahrzeit falls on Friday he certainly has no share in it; if it falls on Shabbat, Eliyah Rabbah implies that it belongs to the yahrzeit, but the current custom has spread that the yahrzeit receives only the Kaddish after Aleinu, as Peri Megadim writes. Kenesset Yechezkel implies that the mourners and yahrzeit should cast lots, and similarly for Kaddish after the Five Megillot or the Kaddish de-Rabbanan after Bameh Madlikin.
If two mourners are equal, such as both within seven, both within thirty, both within twelve months, or both yahrzeits, they cast lots. Whoever wins the lot to say Ma'ariv leaves one Kaddish at Shacharit to the other without lottery, and for the third Kaddish they cast lots. If there are many mourners, they likewise cast lots; one who says Ma'ariv by lottery is not included again until the other mourners each say one Kaddish, and when the cycle returns they all cast lots anew. This applies where they cannot lead the prayers. If both can lead the prayers and both are acceptable to the congregation, they cast lots in such a way that one leads until Ashrei and Uva LeTziyon and the other leads from Ashrei and Uva LeTziyon. If one cannot lead and the other can, the one who led does not thereby lose his right in the Kaddishim, since the other cannot lead. If one is acceptable to the congregation and the other is not, it is as though the latter cannot lead. If two have yahrzeit and one wishes to depart early the next morning and says, "Give me the Ma'ariv Kaddish now and you take tomorrow's," while the other says, "No, let us cast lots," the law follows the second, who can say, "I wish to precede myself in the mitzvah." If several yahrzeits cast lots and two have letters equal in numerical value, the entire lottery is void and all the yahrzeits cast lots again. But if one had a larger letter that won him the lottery and afterward two were equal, the first has won, and the others cast lots again, including one whose letter was lower than those two.
One who had a yahrzeit but could not say Kaddish, such as because he was traveling or did not receive a Kaddish, may say Kaddish at Ma'ariv after the yahrzeit day.
One who forgot the day of his father's or mother's death should choose one day in the year on which to say Kaddish, provided he does not encroach on others' rights to Kaddish. The same applies if he is uncertain whether his father or mother died; he may say Kaddish out of doubt if he does not take from others. One who regularly prays in a particular synagogue or study hall and comes to say Kaddish in another synagogue may be displaced by the mourners there, even if he is within the seven days, for he is worse than a guest: a guest has no place to pray, but he does have a place. If brothers are in synagogue saying Kaddish for their father or mother, and there is another mourner who is the only son of his father, they all divide by lottery together; we do not consider the brothers, who are sons of one person, as one person, because all are obligated to say Kaddish for honoring father and mother.
The Magen Avraham wrote in the name of a responsum of the Rama that if one has no son, it is proper to give Kaddish to his grandson; nevertheless, the other mourners say two Kaddishim and he says one, and in this matter one follows the custom. A mourner leads the prayers at the amud even on days when Tachanun is not said, but on days when Lamnatzeach and E-l Erech Apayim are not said, the mourner should not lead. On Erev Yom Kippur, however, the custom is that a mourner leads. The Magen Avraham wrote that it is better for the son to hire someone to say Kaddish in his place than for someone to say it for free; see the responsum of Chatam Sofer, who writes not to rent a privilege in place of the mourners.
Now we will explain some laws of a resident and a guest. If both are thirty-day mourners, or both twelve-month mourners, or both yahrzeits, the resident completely displaces the guest from leading the prayers and saying Kaddish at the amud. But regarding Perisat Shema and the Kaddish at the doorway, they are equal and divide by lottery. Nevertheless, the first time, a twelve-month guest may lead the prayers and say orphan's Kaddish in the place of a resident twelve-month mourner; and once every thirty days a guest has permission to lead and say orphan's Kaddish. If there is a resident yahrzeit and a thirty-day guest, the guest says the third Kaddish and the resident says the first two. If there is a guest yahrzeit and a resident thirty-day or twelve-month mourner, the guest yahrzeit has only one Kaddish, namely the first. A thirty-day guest and a resident twelve-month mourner are equal and divide all the prayers and Kaddishim. If there is a resident thirty-day mourner, a resident yahrzeit, and a guest yahrzeit, the guest yahrzeit also receives one Kaddish; the resident yahrzeit cannot say, "I take precedence over you," because the guest yahrzeit can answer, "I am not taking from you, but from the thirty-day mourner." Thus the resident yahrzeit says the first Kaddish, the guest yahrzeit the second, and the thirty-day mourner the third.
Now we will explain who is called a guest and who is called a resident. If the deceased has sons in another city and they come there, they are not called residents because their father lived there and was buried there. Only those who live within the community are residents. All who dwell in the community are called residents, whether they pay taxes, serve the community, or are the poor of the city with fixed residence. If one establishes his residence within the community, then from the time he is obligated to bear the burden with the residents he is considered a resident. If a householder has a teacher, yeshiva students, or a servant hired for a term, and he is unmarried with no wife and children, he too is called a resident. But if he has a wife and children elsewhere, he has the status of a guest. If a householder raises an orphan in his home, even for pay, and the orphan has no father or mother elsewhere, he is called a resident; but if he has a father or mother elsewhere, he is not called a resident, even if the householder raises him as charity, and certainly if he is there for pay.
