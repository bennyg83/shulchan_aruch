Paragraph 3: It is considered, etc. Shabbos prayer also begins with that word. Since his thought was directed toward the weekday prayer, we follow his intention.
Raavad comments on Berachos 12a's case of holding wine, beginning with intent for Shehakol, and ending Borei Pri HaGefen (valid because even Shehakol would suffice) and the reverse case of beer, beginning with intent for HaGefen but ending Shehakol. Rashi says the spoken opening was correct while its intended ending was wrong; the Gemara asks whether opening intention or correct conclusion controls. Raavad asks why incorrect intention should invalidate when the words were correct and mitzvos do not require intention. Rosh answers that no intention is preferable to deliberately redirecting intention to another blessing. Mordechai therefore says that on Yom Tov one who accidentally concludes for Shabbos corrects within toch kedei dibbur, as below in siman 487. Though the Gemara's question was unresolved and might be lenient, Tosafos rules stringently; see Magen Avraham 209:3. Alternatively, the beer case is lenient only because its correct conclusion may control. Here everything depends on the opening: once he says Atah Chonen he certainly completes it. Consequently, even saying only Atah, which could begin Atah Kidashta or Atah Echad, while intending a weekday blessing is as though he spoke that intention and began the weekday blessing, requiring completion.
But according to what was written above, siman 209, etc.; therefore Beis Yosef omitted, etc. Tosefes Shabbos objects that Shulchan Aruch in 209 follows Rambam: holding beer or water, intending Shehakol, but mistakenly concluding HaGefen is valid because intention was correct despite improper speech; the reverse likewise. The alternative Rashi view also does not follow Raavad. Why then omit Terumas HaDeshen here? Magen Avraham means his own conclusion at the beginning of siman 209: most poskim do not follow intention. Therefore, after saying Atah correctly, intention alone does not force a weekday ending. Beis Yosef omitted Terumas HaDeshen because this law depends on the siman 209 dispute. In 209 Shulchan Aruch first gives Rambam's view, then Rashi's as 'some say,' and finally Rif's distinct case. Magen Avraham explains that despite presenting Rambam primarily, Beis Yosef did not resolve the law absolutely but adopted each leniency because of doubtful blessings. Correct intention with wrong speech may rely on Rambam; correct speech with wrong intention may rely on Rashi; Rif addresses a different case of correcting speech within toch kedei dibbur. Likewise here, after only Atah in an Atah Kidashta or Atah Echad prayer, doubt whether intention controls means he should not complete a weekday blessing. Siman 479:6 similarly follows Rashi leniently: one who began intending to count four but remembered and correctly concluded five does not repeat.
Unlike Levush, etc. Levush is still more difficult, for in siman 209 he validates even one who intended and concluded HaGefen but corrected to Shehakol within toch kedei dibbur.
