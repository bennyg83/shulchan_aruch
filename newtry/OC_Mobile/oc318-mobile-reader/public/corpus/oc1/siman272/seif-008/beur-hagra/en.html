Seif 8: One may recite Kiddush, etc.; and some say that one may not recite Kiddush. This is the ruling of the Gaon, who disqualifies cooked wine. His proof is from Menachos 86b, where the Mishnah states, “and not cooked wine,” and if one brought it, it is invalid. The same should therefore apply to Kiddush. Rosh rejects this proof in Bava Basra. The case of a vine trained over a fig tree, which disqualifies the wine for libations, is discussed in Bechoros 17a, yet the same disqualification does not necessarily apply here. Moreover, the Gemara in Bava Basra asks whether the verse comes to exclude diluted wine, and says that diluted wine is superior. If wine invalid for libations were necessarily invalid for Kiddush, this question would be impossible. There is therefore no proof from libations. One may further ask why the Gemara in Bava Basra did not list cooked wine as excluded. This may be answered, because we rule that cooked foods that changed for the worse receive the blessing Shehakol. The Gemara therefore did not list cooked wine, since even Shehakol may be recited over it.
See Tosafos, Pesachim 109b, in the passage beginning “four cups,” who cite the Jerusalem Talmud in Terumos. It follows from there that one fulfills the obligation of the four cups with cooked wine and may recite Kiddush over it. According to this, the ruling of Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh Deah, Hilchos Terumos, seif 63, requires explanation. It states that one should not separate cooked wine for uncooked wine, although cooked wine is superior. Rambam himself rules like the first Tanna against Rabbi Yehudah. One must say that Rambam explains the statement “the positions are reversed” to mean that Rabbi Yehudah reverses his own position and rules there like the first Tanna, who excludes it.
This creates another difficulty. Why does Rambam rule concerning blessings that Borei Peri HaGefen is recited over cooked wine, and in Hilchos Pesach rules unequivocally that one may recite Kiddush over it? The answer is found in the Jerusalem Talmud, second chapter of Terumos. The Mishnah states that wherever a kohen is present, one separates from the finest produce, and where no kohen is present one separates from produce that will keep. Rabbi Yehudah says that one always separates from the finest. The Jerusalem Talmud asks that Rabbi Yehudah elsewhere permits cooked wine because cooking improves it. Rabbi Yochanan answers that Rabbi Yehudah reverses his position. Rabbi Elazar says that he does not reverse it: there the case concerns a kohen, while here it concerns the owner. Rabbi Elazar and Rabbi Yochanan dispute the reason. One says that cooking reduces the number of people who will drink it, and the other says that it reduces its volume. The Jerusalem Talmud does not identify which authority gave which reason. From Rabbi Yochanan’s statement that Rabbi Yehudah reverses his position, it follows that Rabbi Yehudah’s concern is that cooking reduces the number of drinkers.
Accordingly, all opinions may agree that cooking improves the wine. Rabbi Yehudah nevertheless disqualifies it for terumah because fewer people drink it. Rambam explains that although cooked wine is improved, not everyone drinks it, and this deficiency is significant for terumah. The first Tanna holds that the improvement is more important. Regarding Kiddush, however, the reduced number of people who drink it is irrelevant, since the wine itself is improved. The correct reading of the Jerusalem Talmud is as I wrote in my commentary there, namely that Rabbi Elazar says the reason is that it reduces the number of drinkers. See there.
See Rosh, Bava Basra 97b, who discusses this at length. Further proof appears in the Jerusalem Talmud, Shabbos, chapter 8, and Shekalim, chapter 3: May one fulfill the obligation with conditum? From the teaching of Bar Kappara that conditum has the law of wine, it follows that one fulfills the obligation with conditum. May one fulfill it with cooked wine? Rabbi Yonah says that one fulfills the obligation with cooked wine. See there.
See Yoreh Deah, siman 217, seif 16, concerning conditum. Maharil explains it as Beit Yosef cites in his name. So too in Yalkut, parashas Yisro, end of remez 272: Rabbi Elazar said that words of Torah are compared to conditum. Just as conditum contains wine, honey, and spices, so words of Torah, etc. In the Babylonian Talmud it is called anomalon, Shabbos 140a and Avodah Zarah 30a.
