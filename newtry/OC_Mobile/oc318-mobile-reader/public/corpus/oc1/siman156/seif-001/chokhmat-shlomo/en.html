Note: See Magen Avraham, seif katan 2, who writes that anyone who states a teaching without naming its author violates a prohibition, etc. See Nazir 56b, where it says that whenever a teaching is transmitted through three people, we mention the first and last but do not mention the middle one. This implies that in such a case one does not violate the prohibition. Accordingly, if a later authority cites the words of an earlier work that itself cited ancient authorities, one need mention only the later authority and the original authority, but not the intermediaries. See there. Come and see how carefully Chazal treated this matter, making it a formal statement and a proof for practical law. In our many sins, today people do the opposite and wrap themselves in a tallis that is not theirs. May the good Hashem atone for them. Examine this carefully. Magen Avraham further writes there, in seif katan 2, that one should not utter an indecent expression, and therefore should not say beitzim in the Talmud but bei'im. At first glance, the opposite appears from the first chapter of Sanhedrin. It relates that Rabbi once went to a certain place and saw that they were kneading their dough in impurity. He asked why, and they replied that a student had come and ruled that mei betzaim does not render food susceptible to impurity. He had expounded mei beitzim, “egg liquid,” but they understood mei betzaim, “marsh water.” If he had said mei bei'im with an ayin, they would not have confused it with marsh water. Necessarily he said beitzim, which appears contrary to Magen Avraham. Yet careful examination proves the opposite. The Gemara concludes that at that time they decreed that a student should not rule unless he had received authorization from his teacher. At first this is difficult to understand. What does authorization have to do with the mistake? Rashi explains that they would grant authorization only to one whose speech was clear so that people would not err because of his pronunciation. This is exceedingly strained. Why should a person with imperfect pronunciation be excluded from the heritage of Hashem? Rather, this proves the view cited by Magen Avraham in the name of Maharshal. Pesachim states that a person should speak only in refined language. Of two students, one said that a teaching was like a weary goat and the other compared it to a weary pig. They investigated the latter and found a trace of disqualification in him. Since he spoke in unrefined language, they discerned that something improper was present. Likewise here: “No wrong will befall the righteous.” Had the student spoken in refined language, people would not have been led to err. Since he spoke in an unrefined manner, this revealed a trace of disqualification, and therefore his words led others into error. They consequently ruled that a student should not issue rulings unless he had authorization from his teacher, for presumably authorization would be granted only to one known to use refined speech, from which it would follow that he is righteous and that no error would result from his words. This supports Magen Avraham, citing Maharshal in Yam Shel Shlomo, chapter 7 of Bava Kamma. Examine this carefully. Regarding Magen Avraham's statement that one who reports a teaching without naming its author violates a prohibition, Gittin 13a asks whether Rabbi Shimon was himself the nasi or whether he quoted it in the name of the nasi. At first glance, what practical difference does this make? It appears that the difference is whether one who cites a teaching in its author's name must specify the actual name, or whether a descriptive designation is sufficient, such as saying “the nasi said.” Although he does not state the name and there might be another nasi, a reference to “the nasi” ordinarily means the nasi of that time and is sufficient. Another practical difference may concern testimony that a woman's husband died. The Knesses HaGedolah in Even HaEzer, siman 17, and several later authorities, as cited in Beis Shmuel seif katan 55, write that if a non-Jew or witness says “So-and-so died,” the presumption is that he means the person of that name in that town. In my opinion, this was the Gemara's doubt. If Rabbi Shimon meant “in the name of the nasi,” then even without identifying which nasi, it is considered naming the author, because it plainly refers to the nasi of his generation. The same would apply to “So-and-so died,” which ordinarily refers to the person in that town. But if Rabbi Shimon himself was the nasi, perhaps saying merely “the nasi” would not count as naming the author because his name was not specified. The same uncertainty would apply where one says only “So-and-so died”: perhaps he does not necessarily mean the person of that name in that town. This also explains why the Gemara concludes teiku, for teiku is appropriate only where there is a practical halachic consequence. With Hashem's help, this is correct. Examine carefully.
