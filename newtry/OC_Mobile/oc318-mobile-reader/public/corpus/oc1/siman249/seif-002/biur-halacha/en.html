It is forbidden to establish, and so forth. Meaning, to hold a formal meal, even on a single occasion in his lifetime. Peri Megadim writes that an engagement feast proves this. See also the Maggid Mishneh in the name of some commentators, as cited by the Beit Yosef. They therefore cannot be discussing merely an unusual meal, for otherwise the Jerusalem Talmud, which forbids an engagement feast, would contradict them. See Bach.
To which he is unaccustomed on weekdays. Even if he is extremely wealthy and holds a lavish meal every day like a Shabbos meal, he should nevertheless refrain from doing so on Friday so that he will eat with appetite at night. This is evident from Rashi to Gittin 36b, s.v. on Friday; see there. See below, siman 288:7 in the gloss, that such a person must vary Shabbos daytime meal by advancing or delaying it, so that Shabbos day is distinguishable from the other weekdays.
Even if it is an engagement feast. See Biur HaGra, who writes that if he has already become engaged, the feast is permitted, as stated in the third chapter of Pesachim. He does not mean that the engagement took place the previous day, for the Jerusalem Talmud certainly forbids the feast in that case, and there is no proof for it from Pesachim. Rather, once he has become engaged, the feast is consequently permitted on that same day as well, in accordance with Magen Avraham. His wording implies that, initially, becoming engaged on Friday is forbidden. This is very difficult, for the Jerusalem Talmud explicitly permits it. It appears to me that the Gra wrote “if he has already become engaged” in order to resolve Magen Avraham’s objection. The Jerusalem Talmud states that an engagement feast may not be held on Friday, but that becoming engaged is permitted; Shmuel adds that even on the Ninth of Av one may become engaged lest another precede him. Magen Avraham objected that this contradicts the Mishnah in Pesachim, which permits an engagement feast even on Pesach eve, and certainly on Friday. His answer, that the Jerusalem Talmud refers only to one who became engaged the previous day, whereas if he became engaged on Friday he may also hold the feast, is very strained in the wording of the Jerusalem Talmud. The Gra therefore intended to answer the objection as follows. According to the Jerusalem Talmud’s initial view, when a person asks on Friday whether he may become engaged, we do not ordinarily permit it because of the honor of Shabbos, namely because he will then need to hold a feast that day, unless he expressly agrees not to hold the feast that day. This is the meaning of its statement that becoming engaged is permitted. If, however, he asks whether he may hold a feast that day after he has already become engaged, we rule leniently because it is a mitzvah feast. The Mishnah in Pesachim discusses this latter case. All this is only the Jerusalem Talmud’s initial position. Once Shmuel concludes that even on the Ninth of Av engagement is permitted lest another precede him, it follows that on Friday too it is initially permitted in every case, and afterward the feast is also permitted. Thus, as a matter of law, since we rule like Shmuel below in siman 551:2, all agree that one may become engaged on Friday and may consequently hold the feast. The Gra’s intent was only to explain the Jerusalem Talmud’s initial view, according to which even engagement itself should be avoided when one intends to hold a feast.
Because of the honor of Shabbos, so that he enters, and so forth. Peri Megadim inclines to say that the reason is not that he should have an appetite. Rather, the principal reason is that he demeans the honor of Shabbos by making Friday equal in this respect to Shabbos day.

It is permitted. See Magen Avraham, who cites the Levush: if both meals can be maintained, that is preferable; if not, Shabbos meal is deferred. This means that he will be entirely unable to eat at night because his earlier eating was excessive, yet he need not be concerned because he is presently engaged in a mitzvah feast and can fulfill the three Shabbos meals the next day, as stated in siman 274. Peri Megadim explains the Levush this way. As a matter of law, however, this requires substantial study. Shabbos night meal is an actual obligation, whereas a circumcision feast or redemption feast is merely a mitzvah and not an absolute obligation. If he estimates that eating now will cause him to lose the night meal entirely, he should refrain. There is no proof from the Shulchan Aruch, which permits only a meal that will diminish his appetite at night, not one that will eliminate the mitzvah entirely. Even if the prohibition against establishing a meal is because it demeans Shabbos, a mitzvah feast is not demeaning; and if the reason is lack of Shabbos preparation, perhaps the Sages did not apply that stringency to a mitzvah feast and relied upon him to prepare at least a minimal dish for Shabbos. But if his eating will be so excessive that eating at night is not legally considered eating at all, it is reasonable that he must refrain from eating so much. I later found that Ateres Zekeinim likewise requires care not to eat so much that the night meal will be lost. Although he is overly stringent even about eating to satiety, for which there is no source, one must certainly avoid eating and drinking in a manner that may entirely prevent the night meal. Magen Avraham cites Bach that it is a mitzvah to begin before the ninth hour. This is explicit below in siman 695 in the gloss concerning the Purim feast, and even more strongly there; it is surprising that he cited Bach. Biur HaGra there likewise treats this as obvious. We can learn from our case that, after the fact, the Purim feast may be held even after Minchah. Darkei Moshe cites Or Zarua that it is a mitzvah to eat Shabbos meal with appetite. Since bread is the principal part of the meal, one should not eat pastries before Shabbos meal, for he will then be unable to eat the principal Shabbos meal with appetite. Darkei Moshe disagrees: since we rule that one may continue eating even from Minchah until dark, appetite is evidently not a concern. Magen Avraham agrees with Or Zarua. Although we rule like Rabbi Yose that one may eat after Minchah, rather than like Rabbi Yehudah who forbids eating and drinking then so that one enters Shabbos hungry, this only means that we do not impose a daytime restriction because of appetite. Once the time of Shabbos meal arrives, Rabbi Yose too agrees that it is a mitzvah to eat with appetite. Tosafot to Pesachim 99b appears contrary to this reasoning, but several authorities evidently disagree with Tosafot. Since bread is the meal’s principal component, one should therefore not first eat pastries or similar foods, so that he will desire the bread, just as Rabbi Yehudah forbids eating after Minchah. This is clearly Magen Avraham’s intent. I later found that Machatzis HaShekel explains it similarly, namely that Magen Avraham permits eating while it is still day; see Peri Megadim. In places where hands are washed before Kiddush, it is plainly forbidden to eat before the blessing over bread, lest there be a long interruption between washing and eating bread. Magen Avraham further objects that this causes an unnecessary blessing and improperly gives precedence to the blessing over pastries before the blessing over bread. In our communities, where hands are not washed before Kiddush, people follow Darkei Moshe and eat pastries after the morning Kiddush before the meal. Shulchan Aruch HaRav defends this custom and writes that one should not protest those who are lenient. Nevertheless, some meticulous people, where possible, follow Magen Avraham: immediately after Kiddush they wash, recite the blessing over bread, and eat everything within the meal. Shulchan Atzei Shittim writes likewise. In any event, all agree that it is proper not to fill one’s stomach with other foods before the bread meal.
From establishing a meal, and so forth. Meaning, from eating bread to satiety in his ordinary weekday manner. Eating a small amount to quiet his hunger is not considered establishing a meal for this purpose.
From the ninth hour onward. See Mishnah Berurah. The rule that the hours are seasonal applies where the day is not extremely short. When the day is very short and, after his ordinary meal, he will certainly lack appetite at night, this is no better than the case of a delicate person discussed below in siman 470:3 and siman 471:1 in the gloss, where we are stringent. Although matzah is more stringent because there is an obligation to eat it, there is at least a mitzvah here as well. Even though a delicate person need not fast on Friday except as an act of piety, that refers to a complete fast; in our case, which requires only advancing the meal, it is certainly a mitzvah to do so.
