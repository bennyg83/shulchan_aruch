(seif kaf-gimmel) As the law for one unwitting. Rabbi Yisrael Isserlin extended to distinguish, for he questioned how he said that a drunk person is called unwitting—for we taught at the beginning of Berachot: an incident where the sons of Rabban Gamliel came from the banquet house after midnight and said to their father, we did not recite Shema; and he said to them, if you did not rise [for dawn] you are obligated to recite; and it is impossible that a drunk person is called unwitting and under compulsion—even if the pillar of dawn rose they recite until sunrise, as above siman 58 seif 5. And he answered that the sons of Rabban Gamliel were reclining at an optional meal, which is not called compulsion since they were not obligated to feast there; and here it deals with a obligatory meal, which is called compulsion. He further answered that specifically for Keriat Shema he is not called under compulsion, for they should have recited before sunrise even though they were drunk—and this is per the view that permits a drunk person to recite Keriat Shema; and even per the view that forbids, in any case shatui is plausible to him that he may recite, and they should have recited before they became completely drunk (and this is not like Rama in the gloss, for it implies he equates Keriat Shema to prayer in every matter)—unlike prayer, where properly he is called under compulsion, for even shatui is ab initio forbidden to pray. He further wrote to distinguish also in prayer: sometimes he is not under compulsion—even though shatui is forbidden to pray, nevertheless if he began to drink after the time for prayer already arrived he is not called under compulsion, end matter. And from all these distinctions he did not mention any distinction in Shulchan Aruch; therefore it must be said he retracted.

And his view that his sons etc.—meaning, to resolve Rabbi Yisrael Isserlin's difficulty from the sons of Rabban Gamliel: it must be said the sons of Rabban Gamliel were drunk and did not linger until after midnight from drunkenness, but remained at the wedding hall to rejoice in their joy; therefore they were intentional—and likewise in the book Derech Moshe.

But a drunk person etc.—who did not know etc.; with this he rejects the second distinction, that for Keriat Shema he is not under compulsion since they should have recited while still in their drunken state; and also the first distinction between optional joy and obligatory joy he did not hold to distinguish.

And even if they began etc.—with this he rejects the third distinction; therefore he left it unspecified here in Shulchan Aruch. And that which he did not write that if he did not think he would have respite he is not called under compulsion—that is a simple matter.
