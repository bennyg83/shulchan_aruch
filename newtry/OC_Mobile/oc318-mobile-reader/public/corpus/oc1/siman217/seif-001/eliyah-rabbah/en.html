[1] Into his shop, etc. This is called an apothecary in the vernacular. Orchos Chayim. Olas Tamid writes that this is difficult, for above, siman 216, seif 2, one who took a fruit to eat and did not intend to smell it does not recite a blessing even though it emits a fragrance. Here too, the person entering does not intend to smell. One may answer that the case concerns one who enters intending to smell, but if he enters for another purpose, then even though he smells the fragrance he is not obligated to recite a blessing because he had no intention to smell. The practical ruling requires study. I am astonished that he did not see the Bach's words in this siman, where Bach raises this question and answers that this case is different. Since the perfumer intends that those entering should smell the fragrance, it is considered prepared for fragrance for people generally, meaning that it was made for fragrance. This implies that even one who enters for his own business must recite a blessing. Independently, there is no difficulty from one who took a fruit to eat, according to Levush's explanation there that the blessing over eating, which is primary, exempts the secondary fragrance. I questioned Levush above, however. Taz was later printed and rules plainly that one who did not intend to smell does not recite a blessing. He writes likewise in this siman and does not mention the Bach's words cited above.
[2] He recites Borei, etc. One should not challenge this from siman 216, seif 10, where one recites the appropriate blessing over each species. There, three species are brought before him and he takes each species in his hand in order to recite its blessing. Here, however, he takes no spice in his hand, but smells several kinds of spices together. Perishah writes likewise there, and in this siman he writes: This case is different, because each species is not brought before him separately; rather, the fragrance reaches him from several kinds of spices. See above, siman 216, seif katan 16.
[3] He intended to return, etc. Above, siman 8, one who removed his tallis intending to put it on again must nevertheless recite a new blessing. Perishah answers that perhaps that case is different, because the act of removing the tallis constitutes a significant termination. One must also distinguish this case from siman 178 concerning diversion of attention; see there. Magen Avraham writes that this applies specifically when he intended to return immediately. I found in Bircas Avraham, page 78: It may be explained as meaning without delay and without any intervening diversion of attention. I likewise found explicitly in Tashbetz, page 29: It appears that the case is one in which he did not intend to return immediately, but if he intended to return immediately he did not divert his attention. Bircas Avraham further writes there that if he went to a different perfume shop, even if he did not divert his attention he must recite another blessing. See above, siman 178.
