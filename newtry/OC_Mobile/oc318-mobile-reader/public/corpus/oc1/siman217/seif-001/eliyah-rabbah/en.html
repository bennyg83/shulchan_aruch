[1] Into his shop, etc. This is called an apothecary in the vernacular. Orchos Chayim. Olas Tamid writes that this is difficult, for above, siman 216, seif 2, one who took a fruit to eat and did not intend to smell it does not recite a blessing even though it emits a fragrance. Here too, the person entering does not intend to smell. One may answer that the case concerns one who enters intending to smell, but if he enters for another purpose, then even though he smells the fragrance he is not obligated to recite a blessing because he had no intention to smell. The practical ruling requires study. I am astonished that he did not see the Bach's words in this siman, where Bach raises this question and answers that this case is different. Since the perfumer intends that those entering should smell the fragrance, it is considered prepared for fragrance for people generally, meaning that it was made for fragrance. This implies that even one who enters for his own business must recite a blessing. Independently, there is no difficulty from one who took a fruit to eat, according to Levush's explanation there that the blessing over eating, which is primary, exempts the secondary fragrance. I questioned Levush above, however. Taz was later printed and rules plainly that one who did not intend to smell does not recite a blessing. He writes likewise in this siman and does not mention the Bach's words cited above.
