And likewise one who smells utensils, etc. Tur disagrees and writes: I do not understand his words. He evidently considers the fragrance to have been made for smelling, since he exempts it only because it has no tangible source. In truth, it was made for fragrance, so that the utensils would become perfumed. Why, then, should one not recite a blessing over it? Beit Yosef asks why Tur did not raise the same question in siman 216, regarding the ruling codified in seif 6 that if the spices were strained out of oil, some say that no blessing is recited because the fragrance has no tangible source. Tur should have challenged that ruling as he does here. One cannot answer that Tur's statement above, “My father the Rosh wrote without qualification concerning all of them that one recites Borei Atzei Besamim,” shows that he indeed disagrees for the same reason mentioned here. The Rosh does not discuss strained oil at all; he refers only to the cases mentioned in the Gemara. It appears to me that there the spices generated a fragrance in the oil, but once they were strained out the oil remained by itself. Oil itself is not intended for fragrance but for eating or anointing. We already ruled in seif 2 that if one took something to eat rather than to smell, no fragrance blessing is recited even though it has a pleasant odor. The blessing over this oil is therefore determined by its intended use, namely eating. Only if the spices remain present does one recite a fragrance blessing over the spices, not over the odor contained in the oil. Here, by contrast, one intentionally gives the utensils a fragrance, and their benefit consists in smelling pleasant. They have no other blessing. According to Tur, one should therefore recite a blessing over their fragrance. I later found that Maharshal answered similarly. One might ask why the Shulchan Aruch wrote above that it is proper to avoid intentionally smelling the oil because of the uncertainty, yet did not write the same here, since the rationale is identical. What difference is there between fragrance absorbed into oil and fragrance absorbed into a utensil? There is no difficulty. The Shulchan Aruch certainly rules that no blessing is recited over a fragrance without a tangible source, meaning a substance into which the fragrance was absorbed, following Rambam, because a doubt concerning blessings is resolved leniently. Above, however, it is possible to avoid intentionally smelling the oil, and it is therefore proper to do so. Here, by contrast, perfuming utensils is common practice, and one cannot require people to abandon an ordinary need. They may therefore continue the practice and rely on Rambam's view not to recite a blessing. This appears correct to me. I have seen some householders who have no spices for Havdalah, but the wooden vessel in which spices are ground retains their odor, and they recite a blessing over it. According to every opinion, this is a blessing in vain. Even according to Tur, a blessing applies only to utensils deliberately fumigated in order to impart fragrance to them. Here, however, he intended only to use the vessel for its work and did not intend to create a fragrance in it; the odor arose incidentally. It is therefore considered not made for fragrance, and no blessing applies. This is clear in my opinion.
