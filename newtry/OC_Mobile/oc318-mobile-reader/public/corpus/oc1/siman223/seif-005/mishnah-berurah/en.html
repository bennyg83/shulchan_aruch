(19) He and his household. If he purchases utensils for himself and other utensils for his household, he recites Shehecheyanu over his own and HaTov VeHaMeitiv over those of his household. It is good for him that he merited to see his household dressed in honorable clothing, and He does good to others, namely his household, who benefit from this good. This applies specifically when he purchased them for his wife and children. If he purchased them for his servant or maidservant, it is not considered good for him, for it is as though he sold the items to them as part of the wages due them, unless he too uses those utensils.
(20) As a gift. This applies specifically to utensils or clothing that bring the recipient joy, but not to money, because receiving money causes him greater embarrassment, and he cannot bless over it.
(21) To him and to the giver. If the recipient is poor, it benefits the giver because Hashem enabled him to perform charity. If the recipient is wealthy, the giver is pleased that he accepts from him, so it is also beneficial to the giver. This is the Rosh’s explanation of the Yerushalmi. Some later authorities disagree with this ruling and hold that according to the conclusion of the Babylonian Gemara, HaTov VeHaMeitiv was instituted only where others also receive physical benefit. According to this, one who receives utensils as a gift should recite Shehecheyanu, not HaTov VeHaMeitiv. Others rule like the Shulchan Aruch and require HaTov VeHaMeitiv rather than Shehecheyanu. In practice, it appears preferable to recite Shehecheyanu and not HaTov VeHaMeitiv, as clarified in Biur Halachah. Nevertheless, where the custom is to provide clothing for orphaned boys, each orphan should recite Malbish Arumim and HaTov VeHaMeitiv, since several boys benefit. They should not recite Shehecheyanu, because according to most poskim, where HaTov VeHaMeitiv is recited, Shehecheyanu is not.
