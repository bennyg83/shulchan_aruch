On condition that he bake matzah in it. Rashba derives this from the opening of the chapter HaSocher Es HaPoel: if one hired a worker for other labor, then even if he later tells him, “Carry barrels of wine for me,” the wages remain permitted. At first glance, the proof is unclear to one who studies the sugya. Beit Yosef explains that Rashba interpreted the Mishnah differently, but his words are strained and highly difficult, as Nachalas Tzvi also discusses at length. Rashba’s proof must be as follows. There, although the worker carried barrels of wine used for idolatrous libation, since he was not hired for that task his wages are permitted. So too here, even had the non-Jew not baked chametz in the oven he would still owe the rental payment, because the oven was not rented for chametz. Even had he baked nothing at all, he would owe the rent. The rent is therefore permitted. This also appears to be Magen Avraham’s understanding.
And if he bakes chametz in it. Taz objects: in siman 246:3 it is ruled that a stipulation that a non-Jew rest the Jew’s animal on Shabbos is ineffective because the non-Jew is not trusted; all the more so here, where he will certainly bake chametz. I do not understand his objection at all. The cases are unrelated. There, a person is commanded that his animal rest, so the non-Jew is not trusted and renting it to him is forbidden. Here, is a person commanded that a non-Jew not bake chametz in his oven? The only prohibition is benefiting from wages paid for chametz. If he did not rent the oven for baking chametz and is therefore not paid for chametz, then even though the non-Jew will certainly bake chametz, the Jew does not benefit from chametz wages and the rent is permitted.
A house in which to live. The consensus of early and later authorities is that an unspecified rental for residence is permitted. The ruling excludes only a case where the house is expressly rented for storing chametz during Pesach or on Erev Pesach. See Magen Avraham at the end of seif katan 11. This resolves the passage in Pesachim 6a that if he designated a house for the non-Jew, he need not eliminate the chametz. The sugya implies that the house was rented to the non-Jew, yet the rental was permitted. It must concern an unspecified rental, or one made some time before Pesach. There is therefore no contradiction with the Yerushalmi that forbids renting for chametz, contrary to Rashba, responsum 177, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 440, who considered the Yerushalmi and Bavli disputed. There is no need to divide authorities who agree; there is no dispute, in accordance with Agur, whose view Rama records without qualification. Shaar Ephraim, responsum 6, asks whether a Jew who sits and guards the chametz violates the prohibition of desiring the continued existence of a forbidden item, and concludes that it appears permitted. His son explains in a gloss that merely sitting there without committing an act does not constitute desiring its existence, distinguishing this from one hired to work with wine used for idolatrous libation. This is difficult, for Yoreh Deah 132:6 expressly rules that even guarding it without payment is forbidden because he desires the prohibition’s continued existence. The same is plainly true of chametz. He also overlooked Rashba’s responsum cited above, which, despite being lenient regarding rental, is stringent against sitting and guarding. Perhaps Shaar Ephraim meant only that after the fact the wages are permitted, unlike wages for wine used for idolatrous libation, which remain forbidden after the fact, as Rashba writes in the responsum cited by Beit Yosef in siman 440.
