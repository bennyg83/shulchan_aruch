5) [Se'if 2] One should tell the non-Jew before Pesach, etc. Taz, subsection 1, distinguishes as follows. If one can settle the account each week, the stipulation made before Pesach is effective. If the entire interest was assessed at once and one cannot settle it until the agreed time, a stipulation before Pesach is ineffective; the stipulation must have been made at the beginning of the transaction, as stated above in siman 245 regarding a partnership with a non-Jew. See there. Nehar Shalom, subsection 1, however, writes that Taz's ruling is not compelling. Even if one cannot settle the account until the agreed time, one may stipulate before Pesach, and this case is unlike siman 245. See there. Chemed Moshe, subsection 2, and Mishbetzos Zahav, subsection 1, write likewise. Mishbetzos Zahav writes there that when the governing law prevents either party from retracting, one should initially be stringent like Taz, while the law after the fact requires further consideration. Ba'er Hetev, however, writes that even in that case one should not be stringent after the fact. See there. This also appears from Chok Yaakov, subsection 3, who writes that one should not be stringent in these laws, because according to most authorities a Jew does not acquire movable property without physically taking possession. See there.
6) There. And once one stipulated with the non-Jew, etc. This implies that without such a stipulation before Pesach, it is prohibited, as Beis Yosef writes in the name of Avi HaEzri. See there. Levush writes likewise. Bach, however, writes that initially one should take the opinion of Raavyah into account, but after the fact it is permitted even if no stipulation was made at all. See there. Magen Avraham, subsection 2; Chok Yosef, subsection 2; Mekor Chaim, subsection 2; Chayei Adam, rule 124, subsection 14, cite this ruling. Peri Chadash writes that nothing need be said before Pesach; one may ordinarily accept after Pesach the loaves of chametz that should have been given during Pesach. This is implied by Hagahos Maimoniyos and Mordechai cited by Beis Yosef. See there. Chok Yaakov, subsection 3, writes that the primary ruling follows the implication of the Shulchan Aruch and Levush: initially, it is proper to make this stipulation. If one does so, then even if the non-Jew later gives chametz that remained over Pesach, it is permitted. If no stipulation was made, but the non-Jew later gives flour, money, chametz made after Pesach, or something else in exchange, it is permitted, because proceeds received in exchange for chametz are permitted, as stated above in siman 443. The same law applies when one purchased the allotment of certain non-Jewish officials, meaning that the government gives those officials a fixed amount of bread each week and at the beginning of the year they sold a Jew all the bread for the entire year. One should not be stringent in these laws, because according to most authorities a Jew does not acquire movable property without physically taking possession. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 194, and Shach there, subsection 1. These are his words. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 3, however, rules like Bach: initially a stipulation is required. After the fact, if no stipulation was made and the non-Jew brings loaves that became chametz during Pesach in payment for the week of Pesach, the Jew may accept and eat them. In subsection 4, he writes that the same law applies to one who purchases the allotment of those non-Jewish officials. Initially one must stipulate with them. After the fact, if no stipulation was made and after Pesach they give the Jew bread that became chametz during Pesach in payment for the week of Pesach, the Jew may accept and eat it. See there.
7) Shulchan Aruch HaRav's ruling that the bread of those non-Jewish officials may be eaten is because it has the status of bread baked by a professional baker, since it is made for distribution and as a gift. This is stated in Zivchei Tzedek on Yoreh De'ah, siman 112, subsection 24, in the name of several authorities. See there. His ruling that bread received as interest may be eaten likewise applies when it is bread baked by a professional baker. If it is a private householder's bread, it is prohibited for consumption, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, siman 112, se'if 2. Even bread baked by a professional baker is permitted only in a place where the custom is to treat such bread leniently, as stated there in Yoreh De'ah.
