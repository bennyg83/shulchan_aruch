It is forbidden to rent a utensil to a non-Jew during Pesach so that the non-Jew may cook chametz in it, because the Jew thereby profits from something from which benefit is prohibited. It is permitted, however, to rent the utensil before Pesach for a period that includes the days of Pesach, such as for three or four weeks, allowing the renter to use it as he wishes. This is permitted even when it is known that the non-Jew will cook chametz in it during Pesach, because the entire rental payment may be attributed to the days before and after Pesach, so the Jew is not considered to profit from prohibited benefit. One who is stringent even in this matter is praiseworthy. This is from Maharam Yaffe. Regarding those who hold an arenda, a leased commercial concession, and receive measures of malt from each brewing performed in the kettles, the law depends upon the terms of the lease. If the kettles belong to the nobleman who grants the lease, but the Jew is responsible to repair them when they break, and when they become worn the Jew must purchase new kettles with his own money and return them intact when the lease ends, the kettles are regarded as being in the Jew’s domain. It is then certainly forbidden for him to receive the actual measures of malt, because the Jew acquires them as payment for use of the kettles, and they are actual chametz that remained over Pesach. Even receiving money is forbidden, because it is equivalent to benefiting from the proceeds of chametz itself, since the established practice is to give the chametz itself as payment for use of the kettles, as explained in the cited responsum. Even if the Jew receives nothing, there would apparently be a prohibition because he desires the chametz’s continued existence. However, when he has no power to prevent them from brewing, since they pay him according to the terms of the arenda, he is regarded as compelled. Accordingly, if he does have the power to prevent the brewing, he may not initially allow them to brew, because he desires the chametz’s continued existence. It has already been explained that everything may be permitted through a sale. Before Pesach, the Jew should sell to a non-Jew all the measures that will become due during the week of Pesach and receive payment immediately. Even if that non-Jew is his acquaintance and later returns the measures to him after Pesach, it is permitted. If, however, the arenda is arranged so that the Jew bears no responsibility whatsoever for repairing the kettles, because the brewers use their own kettles, and the Jew merely receives measures from each brewing, the case resembles one who purchases a clerical tax right. He should stipulate with a non-Jew before Pesach that the non-Jew will give him money instead. After the fact, he may accept the measures even if he made no stipulation. If he does not know who will brew and therefore cannot stipulate with that person, it is regarded as an after-the-fact situation. This is from my teacher, Bach.
