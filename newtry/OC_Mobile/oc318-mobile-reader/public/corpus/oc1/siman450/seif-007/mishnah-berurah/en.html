(24) So that he may cook chametz in it. This is forbidden because the Jew desires the chametz to continue to exist. If the chametz were to spill while the vessel stands on the fire, the vessel might crack. He is therefore considered to benefit from the chametz.
(25) But he may rent him a donkey. He does not care if the chametz is lost. The acharonim ask why both renting the vessel and renting the donkey should not be forbidden because one profits from an item from which benefit is forbidden, which is forbidden l'chatchilah as explained above. They conclude that this ruling follows the view that profiting from items from which benefit is forbidden is prohibited only with idolatry and similar prohibitions. According to the view we follow, which is stringent and forbids such profit l'chatchilah in all cases, it is likewise forbidden to rent a donkey specifically for transporting chametz. Even according to this stringent view, this applies only when the donkey was rented expressly for transporting chametz. If it was rented generally for a period of time and the renter then transported chametz on it, it is permitted, since the rent would have been owed even if nothing had been carried on it, as explained above regarding an oven.
(26) And some permit heating, etc. We do not say that this too is comparable to desiring the absorbed chametz in the walls of the vessel to continue to exist for the vessel's sake. Although while using the vessel he wants it to remain intact, and thus seems to want the absorbed chametz to remain because one cannot exist without the other, this reasoning is not applied to absorbed chametz that is not physically present.
(27) And to wash with them. Or to wash one's hair with them, because he benefits only from the vessel and not from the chametz absorbed in it, even though the absorbed taste is released into the hot water. The acharonim write that this applies specifically to a vessel designated for washing or laundering and not normally used for food. A vessel sometimes used for eating or drinking must be put away in a secluded place and should not be used even for washing or laundering, lest one come to use it for food. Occasional use, however, need not be treated stringently. This will be explained in siman 451.
(28) And this is the custom. Regarding selling chametz vessels to a non-Jew during Pesach, some permit it for the reason stated above, since he benefits only from the vessel itself and not from the absorbed chametz. This does not apply when the non-Jew pays more because of the useful absorbed substance, for then the Jew benefits from chametz. Others forbid the sale. The acharonim discuss a Jew who leases from a nobleman a brewery known as a malt house. Anyone wishing to brew beer uses that building and pays rent to the Jew. If non-Jews are allowed to brew there during Pesach, the Jew desires the chametz to continue to exist because of the brewery vessels, since if the malt were to spill while the kettle stands on the fire, the kettle could crack. By accepting rent he also profits from an item from which benefit is forbidden. Moreover, the established practice was to give the Jew a measure of malt as payment rather than money. Consequently, as soon as the non-Jew brews, the Jew acquires his measure of malt. Even if it is delivered only after Pesach, it is chametz belonging to a Jew that remained over Pesach and is forbidden. The acharonim advise that he sell his rights in the malt house to a non-Jew, or rent them out for several weeks so that the days of Pesach are absorbed within a broader rental period. If he did not lease the building itself but only the right to receive the measures paid by the brewers, it is sufficient to instruct them not to separate his measures during Pesach. They should merely record in their ledger what they ground during Pesach, and he may collect payment after Pesach. The same applies to one who leases a mill.
