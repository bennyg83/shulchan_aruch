(1) Before Pesach he must repay him. This applies even if he borrowed the loaf on erev Pesach shortly before the time for destroying chametz. Had he not borrowed it, the lender would have had to destroy it, and had he failed to do so it would have become forbidden for benefit. Nevertheless, the borrower must return another loaf after Pesach, because he borrowed it while it was permitted and still constituted full monetary property. The later authorities write that even if the Jewish borrower transgressed by retaining the chametz until after Pesach, when it became prohibited, he must still pay. We do not say that the lender cannot receive its value because it would be proceeds of chametz, as discussed above in siman 443. This is not considered proceeds of chametz, because once the borrower received the loaf it became his property, and the lender retained only an ordinary monetary claim. If a Jew lent chametz to a non Jew before Pesach and the non Jew repaid him with equivalent chametz after Pesach, it is permitted, since he is not returning the original chametz itself. Shiltei HaGibborim in the name of Riaz. His words imply that if the non Jew returns the same chametz, it is prohibited. Peri Chadash disagrees, arguing that it is not chametz owned by a Jew through Pesach, since until now it belonged to the non Jew. Once it was lent to him it entered his possession, and is therefore chametz of a non Jew that remained through Pesach, which is permitted even for eating. In practice, one should be stringent like the first opinion, because returning the identical chametz makes it appear like chametz owned by a Jew through Pesach.
(2) If he does not repay him. There is no concern for prohibited interest from lending one measure for the same measure with ordinary loaves, because the difference is generally slight and people are not particular about it. The later authorities.
