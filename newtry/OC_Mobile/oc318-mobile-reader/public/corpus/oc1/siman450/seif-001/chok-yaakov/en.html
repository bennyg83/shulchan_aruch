He must repay him after Pesach. Tur writes: “There is no prohibition here of chametz that remained over Pesach.” He derives this from the beginning of the chapter HaSocher Es HaPoel, where the household of Rabbi Yannai borrowed produce from poor people during Shemittah and repaid them in the eighth year. Tosafot explain that because the original produce was no longer present when repayment was made, the repayment was not considered an exchange for Shemittah produce. According to this reasoning, repayment during the Shemittah year itself would likewise be permitted once the original produce no longer exists. All the more so here, since chametz does not transfer its prohibition to its proceeds, and the loan was made while the chametz was permitted, the money should be receivable even during Pesach. Why, then, did the authorities write that repayment must occur after Pesach? The Gemara’s mention of repayment in the eighth year proves nothing, for it merely recounts the event as it occurred. At the beginning of Rosh’s discussion it appears that the principal reason for the leniency is that the Shemittah year had ended, but at the conclusion he agrees with Tosafot that once the original produce no longer exists the repayment is not considered its exchange, so the question remains. Perhaps the authorities specifically wrote “after Pesach” to teach that even repayment with loaves is then permitted. Kenesses HaGedolah writes that some places customarily avoid borrowing loaves before Pesach for repayment after Pesach because of concern for interest, since lending a se’ah for a se’ah is prohibited. See Yoreh Deah 162:1 in the gloss, where it is explained that loaves may be lent for loaves.
