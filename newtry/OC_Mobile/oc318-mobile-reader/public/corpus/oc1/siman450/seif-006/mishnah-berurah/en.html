(13) To his servant. Even though the servant's food is his responsibility, since he did not tell him to purchase chametz.
(14) Even though he knows that he will purchase, etc. If the servant is hired to him for the year, he should not eat in the Jew's house because of suspicion, as stated by the Agudah.
(15) Chametz. He may not say to him, “Take this dinar and buy chametz from the shopkeeper and eat,” even if he is an unrelated non-Jew whose food is not his responsibility. Since the Jew himself may not purchase chametz on Pesach, he may not tell a non-Jew to purchase it. Although the non-Jew buys it for himself and not for the Jew, since the Jew gives him a dinar and tells him to buy chametz with it, and the non-Jew buys it at his instruction, he appears to be the Jew's agent. It is permitted, however, to say, “Buy chametz with your own dinar and eat.”
(16) Go and eat, etc. This applies specifically when he said, “Go and eat chametz,” or at least when he knows with certainty that he will eat chametz. Since the shopkeeper gives the food to the servant on the understanding that the Jew will pay him, the shopkeeper acts as the Jew's agent, and it is as though the Jew fed him chametz directly. Since the servant's food is his responsibility, the Jew thereby benefits from the chametz with which he feeds him.
(17) And some permit, etc. Since the Jew has not yet paid the shopkeeper, what the shopkeeper gives the servant is not considered the Jew's chametz. Rather, the Jew merely incurs a debt to the non-Jew because he guaranteed payment. This is the primary ruling. Nevertheless, l'chatchilah it is proper to be concerned for the first view with respect to one's servant or maidservant whose food is his responsibility. He should not tell the servant to buy on credit at his expense, but should instead give him cash and allow him to purchase whatever he wishes.
(18) In this case as well. According to this view, it is also permitted to tell the shopkeeper, “Give chametz to my workers and I will pay,” for the reason stated above. Regarding a child who urgently needs to eat or drink chametz, one should bring him to a non-Jew's house and ask the non-Jew to feed him chametz. One must be careful not to take it into his own hand, because he would thereby acquire it. If the non-Jew refuses to give it free of charge, one may promise to pay him afterward, since this is permitted according to the basic law. One may not, however, pay before or at the time the food is given, because he would then acquire the chametz, which is forbidden according to all opinions. If the child cannot be taken outside and the non-Jew must bring the chametz into the Jew's house and feed him there, the Jew should tell the non-Jew to gather the remainder and take it home. When the child needs to eat again, the non-Jew should bring it back and feed him. If it is impossible to find a non-Jew each time, one may be lenient and allow the non-Jew to leave enough chametz in the house for several feedings, provided the Jew explicitly states that he does not wish to acquire it, since otherwise his domain acquires it for him, as explained in siman 448. He should instruct a minor to feed the child. The adult should not feed him himself, both because one should not touch chametz l'chatchilah, as explained in siman 446, and because of the concern of acquisition. If possible, the chametz should be hidden where the Jew's sold chametz is stored, and the child should be brought there to eat. Otherwise, a partition ten tefachim high should be erected before it, or at least a vessel should be inverted over it. All this applies only when the child is not endangered. When there is danger, one need not be exacting in these matters, so that the feeding will not be delayed, as explained in siman 328.
(19) Unless he prepaid a dinar. If he prepaid the shopkeeper and instructed him to give food to his servant when he came, it is considered as though the Jew fed him the chametz directly, because the shopkeeper is the employer's agent. This is forbidden even if the Jew does not know with certainty that the servant will purchase chametz. Yoreh De'ah, siman 132, se'if 4, states that even when he prepays a dinar, it is forbidden only if he says, “Keep this dinar until you give food to my worker.” If he permits the shopkeeper to spend the dinar immediately, it is permitted, because when the worker later receives the food no dinar remains through which the employer could acquire it. Several acharonim disagree and rule that it is forbidden in every case. Prepayment is not literal. The same applies if the Jew gives the dinar at the time the shopkeeper gives the chametz to the servant. The exclusion applies only when he pays afterward.
(20) Or he personally transferred it. Meaning, the employer himself took the chametz from the non-Jewish shopkeeper for his servant and gave it to him. Even if he paid nothing, it is forbidden, because he acquired the chametz by taking possession of it from the non-Jew.
(21) And it is forbidden to purchase, etc. There is concern that he may eat from it. Moreover, he desires the chametz to continue to exist. In addition, a non-Jew cannot ordinarily serve as a halachic agent, with the result that the Jew himself acquires it and violates bal yera'eh. Even if he does not draw the chametz out of the seller's domain but merely pays during Pesach, it is forbidden, because some authorities hold that a Jew acquires movable property from a non-Jew through payment alone.
(22) Even with his own money, etc. Nevertheless, if the Jew did not intend to acquire it for himself and accepted no responsibility for it, there is room to permit eating it and benefiting from it after Pesach. This is the view of the Peri Megadim. See Shaarei Teshuvah.
(23) Belonging to a non-Jew. It is likewise forbidden during Chol HaMoed Pesach to tell a non-Jew to purchase chametz on the Jew's behalf, even if the chametz will not be brought into the Jew's house, because some poskim hold stringently that a non-Jew can act as an agent. B'dieved, if he purchased it but did not bring it into the Jew's domain, it need not be forbidden after Pesach, as stated in the novellae of Rabbi Akiva Eiger. The Peri Megadim writes that when a non-Jew purchases grain from another non-Jew and the grain contains a small amount that has become chametz, a Jew may serve as broker between them, since the payment is not being made for the chametz. If a non-Jew's animal is loaded with chametz, a Jew may unload it to prevent suffering to the animal. Although the non-Jew will be grateful to him, and ordinarily this could be considered desiring the chametz to continue to exist in order to receive gratitude, it is permitted because the Jew does not intend to receive that gratitude.
