But he should not tell him, “Go and eat.” This applies when it is known that the non-Jew will eat chametz, as Magen Avraham writes. This is also implied by the authorities in Yoreh Deah, siman 133, and is explicit in Agudah in the chapter HaSocher Es HaPoel. It is somewhat contrary to the Gemara’s wording, which implies that even a mere possibility is forbidden.
And some permit. Since Shulchan Aruch presents the first opinion without qualification, its ruling is evidently to be stringent, following its usual method. Bach and Kenesses HaGedolah rule likewise. Although in Yoreh Deah, siman 133, regarding wine used for idolatrous libation, Shulchan Aruch rules leniently, and Tur likewise permits there when no dinar was advanced, or at least when no specific dinar was designated, here the unqualified ruling is stringent. The special severity of chametz must be different. Hagahos Perishah writes this, and it is also evident from Bach, who follows Rashba leniently there but is stringent here. I have elaborated to reject Magen Avraham’s assertion that the latter view is primary and that the prohibition applies only when a dinar was advanced and that same dinar remains in the shopkeeper’s possession. The later authorities agree that chametz is treated more stringently. Accordingly, one who has a child or ill person in his home who must eat or drink chametz should instruct a non-Jew to give it to him, without saying, “I will repay you,” or should carry the child outside the house. In any event, the Jew should not touch the chametz. This applies where such an arrangement is possible and a non-Jew is available. If not, and the child or patient has a great need, he should do whatever is possible, for everything is permitted where life is endangered. See siman 616 and Nachalas Shivah, responsum 14. It is nevertheless proper to erect a partition ten handbreadths high to prevent one from eating the chametz. See Magen Avraham at the end of siman 343 and what I wrote in this siman, seif katan 9.
