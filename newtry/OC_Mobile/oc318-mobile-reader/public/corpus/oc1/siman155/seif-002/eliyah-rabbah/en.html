[2] And it is good, etc. We do not practice this at all, because the later authorities wrote in the name of Mordechai that it was said only regarding grain grown far from inhabited areas, which did not hear the sound of a rooster or ships, and those accustomed to it must eat it constantly every day. The Taz writes that one who fulfills the statement according to its plain meaning loses nothing, while Magen Avraham writes that the Gemara does not imply this. Were I not afraid to say so, I would suggest that he erred because Shabbos 66 speaks of a simpleton who did not hear a sound, etc., as a parable, and he understood it as referring to morning bread. This is astonishing, for it also states there that it did not see the sun, moon, or rain. How would this apply to grain? It is also impossible for one who examines the Gemara to explain that passage as referring to morning bread. According to his approach, it would be more plausible to connect it to Berachos 56, where it says, “The bread fell into the pit and could not be found.”
