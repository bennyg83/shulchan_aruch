Nevertheless, he should recite, etc. See Mishnah Berurah regarding our statement that Maamar Mordechai disagrees with Peri Chadash. Although Shaarei Teshuvah above in siman 6, citing Shaliach Tzibbur, writes that Maamar Mordechai merely questions Peri Chadash, one who examines Maamar Mordechai will see that this is not so. Shaliach Tzibbur reached that conclusion because Maamar Mordechai writes at the end that on one occasion he was unable to recite the blessings before prayer, and he was nevertheless careful to recite at least Elokai Neshamah and Bircas HaTorah in order also to avoid the concern of Peri Chadash. In truth, however, he does not retract his earlier words at all. Certainly it is preferable initially to act this way, but it is not indispensable after the fact. Shaarei Teshuvah above also did not disagree entirely in practice with Peri Chadash, apparently because he relied on Shaliach Tzibbur's statement that even Maamar Mordechai, who disagreed, did not have the confidence to dispute Peri Chadash in practice. I say regarding this matter as well that one who wishes after the fact to rely on Peri Chadash should not be rebuked, since Chayei Adam and Derech HaChayim copied his ruling. Yet one who wishes to recite the blessing and rely on all the authorities we cited has likewise lost nothing. One who wishes to fulfill his obligation according to all opinions should, in my view, sleep a little during the day. When he awakens, he should then recite Elokai Neshamah, for Beit Yosef in siman 231 writes that Agur, in the name of Rabbi Yehudah of Speyer, states that one who awakens from daytime sleep must recite Elokai Neshamah. Shibolei HaLeket writes likewise in the names of Rabbeinu Simchah and Rabbi Avigdor Kohen, and Sefer HaTanya also rules so. Beit Yosef concludes there, however, that this is not the accepted custom. Here, therefore, one may act this way according to all opinions. Toras Avigdor also writes so. Know further that Peri Megadim offered the advice mentioned in Mishnah Berurah regarding Bircas HaTorah. If one forgot it and began the blessings of Krias Shema, but has not yet recited Bircas HaTorah, he should explicitly intend during Ahavah Rabbah not to fulfill the mitzvah of Bircas HaTorah through it. He will then, according to all opinions, remain obligated in Bircas HaTorah. In my humble opinion, this requires very great study. Maamar Mordechai explains that the Gemara says one has already fulfilled his obligation through Ahavah Rabbah because Ahavah Rabbah itself is Bircas HaTorah. His words are indeed supported by the Yerushalmi in the first chapter of Berachos, which calls Ahavah Rabbah Bircas HaTorah, since Krias Shema is also Torah study. How, then, can one divide his intention between the two? Regarding Krias Shema, he intends that this blessing serve as its blessing, for although blessings do not prevent fulfillment, it was instituted initially for this purpose. Yet regarding other Torah study, he intends to recite this blessing on condition that he not fulfill Bircas HaTorah through it. See above what I wrote in Biur Halachah to siman 47, in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah, regarding this matter, which at first glance supports Peri Megadim. The practical ruling requires study. In my humble opinion, if one forgot Bircas HaTorah and began the blessings of Krias Shema, it is preferable that he explicitly intend during Ahavah Rabbah to fulfill Bircas HaTorah through it, and he should endeavor to study immediately after the prayer. Ahavah Rabbah will then count for him in place of Bircas HaTorah according to all opinions, as written in siman 47. See there in Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 16. By following this advice, he also entirely avoids the concern of reciting an unnecessary blessing.
All the blessings. See Mishnah Berurah regarding what we wrote in the name of the poskim concerning the time for the blessings. Derech HaChayim, Hilchos Onen, rule 5, appears to understand that according to Magen Avraham in siman 70, seif katan 1, the morning blessings for every person may be recited only until four hours into the day, like the time for the blessings of Krias Shema. The exceptions are Bircas HaTorah, if it was not discharged through Ahavah Rabbah, and the three blessings "Who did not make me a non-Jew," "a slave," or "a woman," whose time extends throughout the day. This is incorrect. Magen Avraham speaks only of one who was an onen during the principal time of obligation in the morning and was then exempt, and who therefore remains exempt after four hours. One who was obligated at the beginning of the time of obligation is certainly still obligated even after four hours. This is explicit in his wording, and it also appears from Levushei Serad, Chochmas Adam, and Shulchan Shlomo. Machatzis HaShekel states it explicitly. It is possible that regarding an ordinary person Magen Avraham agrees with the Gra, Nehar Shalom, and Maamar Mordechai that after the fact the time extends throughout the day. At least until midday, however, his wording explicitly proves that one has not lost the blessings. It is also possible that Derech HaChayim there refers only to one who was exempt at the beginning of the period of obligation. Although Nehar Shalom and Maamar Mordechai disagree with Magen Avraham even in that case, Shaarei Teshuvah and Dagul Merevavah uphold his ruling as halachah. If so, however, Derech HaChayim was exceedingly terse. The conclusion from all this is that until midday there is certainly no concern that one will lose the blessings on this account. Even after midday one may rely after the fact on the lenient position, since I have not found any posek who assigns a fixed time to the blessings according to our present practice of reciting them over the customary experiences of the world. After writing all this, I found Responsa Rashba, cited in Beit Yosef to siman 4, beginning "It appears from the words of the Rosh." His language seems to indicate that the morning blessings were principally instituted for the morning, and that for this reason washing the hands was likewise instituted specifically in the morning. See there. Therefore, ideally one should certainly be stringent and recite them in the morning at the time of prayer. After the fact, one may rely on the great authorities cited above at least until midday, since the intrinsic obligation of prayer itself extends until midday, as stated below in siman 89, seif 1. See there. One who recites them later after the fact should not be rebuked, as explained above.
