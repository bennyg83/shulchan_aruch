The first passage. Rabbeinu Yonah writes, based upon the Midrash, that one should recite the entire Shema, which contains 248 words, to protect the 248 limbs. See what I wrote in siman 235. And he recites the blessing HaMapil, etc. This implies that HaMapil should be recited after Shema, so that the blessing is adjacent to sleep. This appears to be the primary practice. The order in Tur and Shulchan Aruch, however, implies that after Shema one recites HaMapil and afterward recites Yoshev BeSeiser and the other protective passages. Their reason must be that Shema is not recited merely for protection. Rather, it is derived from the verse “Speak in your heart,” as stated in the Gemara, which teaches that one should recite words of Torah or a supplication close to sleep. Shema must therefore precede HaMapil so there will be no interruption between the blessing and sleep. Yoshev BeSeiser and the other passages, however, are recited for protection and are not considered an interruption. Rambam, chapter 7, and Hagahos Maimoniyos hold that Shema is also recited last. Avudraham writes likewise in the name of Rav Hai Gaon, who holds that Shema itself is also for protection. Tosafos at the beginning of Berachos proves this from the Gemara, and the Jerusalem Talmud states similarly. The Jerusalem Talmud, first chapter, records a dispute. Rabbi Zeira would recite Shema and repeat it until he fell asleep, indicating that Shema is said last. Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi would recite psalms afterward. Rambam and Hagahos Maimoniyos rule like Rabbi Zeira, whereas Tur and Beit Yosef rule like Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi. It is therefore astonishing that Rama writes that one should recite Shema and repeat it, contrary to Beit Yosef’s view. For practical halachah, it appears that the ruling follows Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi, as Rif writes in the chapter Bnei HaIr. In truth, the psalms are not obligatory at all. Only Shema and HaMapil are obligatory. The custom to recite the psalms arose because Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi recited them. Even he recited only the two psalms. This matters for one who is ill or compelled. The fact that women are not accustomed to recite this may be because they hold that since it applies only at night, they are exempt as from a time bound positive mitzvah, just as they are exempt from Shema. Close to his bed. Seder HaYom writes that one should not recite Shema immediately upon entering bed, but only when he sees that sleep is coming upon him. Knesses HaGedolah writes that it should be recited immediately, lest sleep overtake him afterward and he fail to recite it. There is no concern for an interruption unless he performs another activity in the interim. He should sleep immediately. If he must engage in marital relations, he should wash his hands afterward and then recite Shema. At the very least, he should recite HaMapil and Shema after relations. Shelah and Knesses HaGedolah. While lying down. This applies specifically to the obligatory Shema. If he already recited Shema after nightfall and now recites it in bed for protection, he may recite it while lying down, for Rabbi Zeira recited and repeated it until he fell asleep. He repeats and recites it. It appears to me that the same is true if he reflects upon words of Torah, because the Gemara says that a Torah scholar need not recite Shema, since he is accustomed constantly to review his learning. Yoshev BeSeiser. Although it is forbidden to use words of Torah as a remedy, using them for protection from possible harm is different. See Yoreh Deah, siman 179, seif 10. One who sleeps alone in a house is seized by Lilith. Shabbos, chapter 23. “House” here means a room. Maharil. The following require protection from destructive forces: an ill person, a woman after childbirth, a bridegroom, a bride, a mourner, and a Torah scholar at night. Berachos 54. Some are accustomed to recite the deathbed confession before sleeping. It appears to me that this is improper. Berachos 60 teaches that one should not say, “If I die, may my death be an atonement,” because one should not open his mouth to the Satan. Berachos 19 likewise says that one should not say, “I sinned greatly before You and You have not collected from me even one thousandth,” because this sounds as though he asks to be punished and thus opens his mouth to the Satan. One cannot distinguish that a bathhouse is a place of danger, because night and sleep are also dangerous, as stated at the beginning of siman 231. Rather, one should examine the deeds he performed throughout the day. If he finds that he committed a sin, he should confess it and resolve not to repeat it. He should be especially careful to examine common sins, such as flattery, falsehood, mockery, and lashon hara.
