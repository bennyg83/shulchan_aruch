Seif 4. One who is uncertain whether he recited Bircas HaMazon, etc. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 7, who writes that he must also recite the fourth blessing, so that people should not come to treat it lightly. A clear proof may be brought from Pesachim 39b, where Rami bar Chama asks whether one may fulfill the mitzvah of maror with maaser sheni. Perhaps one cannot fulfill the Torah-level mitzvah of matzah with it, but may fulfill the Rabbinic mitzvah of maror; or perhaps there is no distinction. Rava says, “It is reasonable that matzah and maror are alike.” Rashi explains that they are juxtaposed, but this is difficult, because juxtaposition applies only to Torah law, not to a Rabbinic enactment. According to the principle above, however, the meaning is clear. If matzah and maror were entirely separate matters, one could say that although maaser sheni is invalid for Torah-level matzah, it is valid for Rabbinic maror. But since matzah and maror are performed together, if one were invalid for matzah but valid for maror, people might belittle the Rabbinic mitzvah. Rava therefore says that since matzah and maror are paired, maror too is not fulfilled with maaser sheni. This is a proper explanation, with Hashem’s help. After writing this, I saw that Pnei Yehoshua also noticed the difficulty in Rashi’s words and explained that since matzah is juxtaposed to maror and maror to matzah, maror at the Torah level would likewise be invalid with maaser sheni. Therefore, even now that maror is Rabbinic, the Sages enacted it in the pattern of Torah law. This is difficult, for what did Rava add? Rami bar Chama himself had already framed the doubt as whether the distinction follows from maror being Rabbinic, which necessarily assumes that if it were Torah-level it would certainly be invalid. If Rava’s point were merely that every Rabbinic enactment follows the pattern of Torah law, he should have stated that principle explicitly. It appears to me that Rashi’s intention may instead be explained in light of Gittin 65a, where the Gemara says that the principle that the Sages enacted Rabbinic law in the pattern of Torah law applies only where the Rabbinic law has a Torah root, but not where it has no Torah root. Later the Gemara proves that maaser nowadays is considered to have no Torah root, even though in the Temple era it was Torah-level. Accordingly, Rami bar Chama’s doubt may be whether maror nowadays, since it has no present Torah obligation, was not enacted in the pattern of Torah law, or whether even such a Rabbinic mitzvah follows Torah law. Rava answers that matzah and maror are parallel and interconnected, so maror is considered to have a Torah root. Where there is a Torah root, the Sages certainly enacted it in the pattern of Torah law. This is very correct. Regarding the ruling in Shulchan Aruch, siman 184, seif 4, that one who is uncertain whether he recited Bircas HaMazon must repeat it because it is Torah-level, see Magen Avraham, who writes that he should repeat even the fourth blessing so that people not belittle it. At first glance this is difficult from siman 67, where one uncertain whether he recited Krias Shema repeats it together with its blessings. Taz explains that one also recites blessings in a case of doubt concerning a Torah obligation, while Magen Avraham, citing Rashba and Kesef Mishneh, explains that the Sages enacted the blessings in that manner. Why did Magen Avraham not explain there as he does here, that omitting the Rabbinic blessings in a case of doubt would cause people to treat them lightly? It must be that this concern applies specifically to the fourth blessing of Bircas HaMazon, because the Sages already treated it more leniently regarding laborers, and therefore also enacted that amen should not be answered aloud after it, lest people belittle it. Since it already had a lenient status, they were concerned that if people saw that it was not repeated in a case of doubt, they would belittle it further. Bach writes that today, when laborers recite it like everyone else, amen may be answered aloud because there is no longer such a concern. According to this, today one uncertain whether he recited Bircas HaMazon should not repeat the fourth blessing, since the concern no longer applies. Rabbi Shimon Chayon and Magen Avraham may disagree with Bach and hold that once Chazal were concerned for belittlement under Talmudic conditions, the enactment remains even today. In any event, since Bach holds that there is no present concern, the matter is doubtful, and one should refrain from reciting the fourth blessing in a case of doubt. This appears correct. In my work Sefer HaChaim I offered another explanation. At present, however, what I have written here appears more correct. There I raised a difficulty from Tosafos regarding one who repeats Emes Veyatziv but not Krias Shema, and my earlier answer resolves that issue. Here, however, the language of the poskim and Rashba cannot be reconciled through that answer. Necessarily, the concern of belittling the Rabbinic enactment applies only in this specific case and not generally. This is clear.
