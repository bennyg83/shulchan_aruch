Seif 1. One who ate in another place must recite Bircas HaMazon before leaving his place, etc.; if he left intentionally, he must return to his place, etc. It appears to me, with Hashem’s help, that a novel ruling may be derived here. The Yerushalmi, Pesachim chapter 4, comments on the Mishnah concerning one who is going to slaughter his Pesach offering, and states: Rabbi Yudan said, as a mnemonic, this parallels the dispute in Berachos. One opinion gives the measure as a kezayis, while Rabbi Yehudah gives the measure as a kebeitzah. It was taught that for less than this amount, he is not burdened to return. What is the distinction between the two cases? This one can be nullified. Korban HaEdah, in Shirei Korban, wonders what is being taught by the statement that for less than this amount he is not burdened to return, since the Mishnah explicitly asks, “Until what amount must he return?” which implies that for less than that amount he need not return. He leaves this unresolved. In truth, his question is difficult to understand. The Yerushalmi may mean that for less than this amount he need not return even for his own sake, which is not explicit in the Mishnah. Alternatively, it may mean that for less than a kezayis he need not return even after the prohibition has taken effect, when it can no longer be nullified. See Magen Avraham in the name of Tosafos. This is straightforward. Nevertheless, in my humble opinion, the passage does not in fact refer to chametz. If it did, why does the Yerushalmi interrupt the discussion with the mnemonic from Berachos, and why is that mnemonic needed at all? At first glance, one might have said that this proves that the Yerushalmi follows the view stated in siman 206 that even a whole entity smaller than a kezayis requires an after-blessing, and also the view in siman 184 that all seven species require their blessing to be recited in the place where they were eaten. Accordingly, if one left intentionally, he must return. The Yerushalmi would then be teaching that the law of Berachos is learned from the law of chametz: just as for chametz one returns only for a kebeitzah or a kezayis, so too for Bircas HaMazon one returns only for a kezayis. For less than a kezayis, even if it was a whole entity, he does not return but blesses where he is. Yet according to those who hold that a whole entity smaller than a kezayis requires no after-blessing, the passage may also be explained. Regarding chametz, we rule like the Sages that the measure is a kebeitzah, whereas regarding Bircas HaMazon we rule like Rabbi Meir that the measure is a kezayis. It is true that in the chametz Mishnah Rabbi formulated the Sages’ view in the plural, indicating that he agreed with it, and therefore the halachah follows them. But why, then, do we rule like Rabbi Meir regarding Bircas HaMazon and like Rabbi Yehudah regarding chametz? Necessarily, this supports the view of the poskim that one may not recite Bircas HaMazon unless he ate the required measure, because we rule like Rabbi Meir that eating is a kezayis and satisfaction refers to drinking. Since Bircas HaMazon is Torah-level, we follow Rabbi Meir stringently. Returning to the place of eating, however, and likewise returning because of chametz, are only Rabbinic obligations, so a kebeitzah suffices. According to this, where one left without reciting Bircas HaMazon in his place, the obligation to return is likewise only Rabbinic, and the measure should therefore be a kebeitzah. This is the Yerushalmi’s meaning when it says that the dispute in Bircas HaMazon serves as a mnemonic. The opinions follow their respective positions there. The difference between the cases is that chametz can be nullified, and therefore for less than the required amount one is not burdened to return. The same applies to Bircas HaMazon: since the obligation to return is Rabbinic, for less than a kebeitzah he does not return. I later remembered that what I wrote, that Rabbi formulated Rabbi Yehudah’s view as the Sages’ view, was mistaken. The Mishnah explicitly states Rabbi Yehudah’s position and then the Sages’ position. Nevertheless, the question remains why we rule like Rabbi Meir regarding Bircas HaMazon but like the Sages here, if they follow parallel approaches. Necessarily, Bircas HaMazon itself is Torah-level, while the requirement to return is Rabbinic. The Yerushalmi’s conclusion, however, requires explanation. It asks: Until now we have discussed consecrated meat; from where do we know the law of chametz? Rabbi Yosi bar Bun answers from the Mishnah, “and likewise one who left Jerusalem,” which shows that what falls under one law falls under the other. Korban HaEdah’s explanation is difficult. It appears instead that the Yerushalmi means as I wrote initially: even after the prohibition has taken effect and chametz can no longer be nullified, one is not burdened to return for less than the stated measure. The Yerushalmi then asks what distinction remains between chametz and consecrated meat, since after the prohibition begins neither has a remedy. It answers that chametz is still lighter because at an earlier time it could have been nullified, whereas consecrated meat never had such a remedy. One might challenge that perhaps the Mishnah’s measure applies only while chametz can still be nullified, whereas after that time everyone would require return even for less. The Yerushalmi therefore cites the parallel case of one who left Jerusalem, teaching that the laws are truly equated. Since consecrated meat can no longer be corrected except by returning, the same must be true of chametz once it can no longer be nullified; nevertheless, the measure remains a kezayis or kebeitzah according to each opinion. In any event, although this second explanation is more compelling, the first ruling also appears correct: for less than a kebeitzah one need not return to his original place in order to recite Bircas HaMazon. This may be learned from chametz, since both obligations to return are Rabbinic, and it is reasonable to be lenient in a Rabbinic matter. Examine this carefully.
