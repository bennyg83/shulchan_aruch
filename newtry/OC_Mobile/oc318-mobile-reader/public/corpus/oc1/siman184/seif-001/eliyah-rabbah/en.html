[Levush] And some say he did not fulfill his obligation, etc. The Rama writes that this is specifically according to the Rambam. According to the Rosh, who holds that even one who left inadvertently should initially return to his original place, one who left intentionally does not fulfill his obligation even after the fact until he returns. This implies the practical halachah, and the Bach seems to agree. The Tur and Beit Yosef indeed write this. Yet I am puzzled how they know it. Perhaps after the fact intentional and inadvertent departure have the same law; why multiply disputes unnecessarily? The Taz attempts to prove otherwise from the incident of Rabbah bar bar Chanah, who said he had forgotten a golden dove. He argues that if one fulfilled his obligation after the fact, that case was already after the fact because his companions would not wait for him, just as in the case of Rav Sheshet regarding Tefillat HaDerech. I do not understand this argument. The incident itself concerns one who forgot, and according to everyone an inadvertent departure fulfills the obligation after the fact even if at the present moment he knowingly delays returning, as implied by Rashi at the end of Elu Devarim and all the poskim. Clearly that was not considered the relevant after the fact case. Nor is it comparable to Terumat HaDeshen's discussion of Rav Sheshet, because according to Rav Sheshet one need not stand even initially, as explicit in Berachot 30. Moreover, through this strategy Rabbah bar bar Chanah was able to make the others wait, so the case remained like an initial situation. Nor was his statement truly a lie, because the Gemara itself brings a proof from one who forgot a purse: if one returns for his own honor, all the more so for the honor of Heaven. It therefore appears that he hinted this idea to them. The Taz also discusses at length the first chapter of Berachot concerning one who inclined to read like Beit Shammai and endangered himself, but he overlooked Rabbenu Yonah at the end of Elu Devarim. The Gemara at the end of Elu Devarim also supports my reading: Rav Zevid says the dispute concerns one who left inadvertently, but if he left intentionally everyone agrees he should return and bless. The phrase indicates an initial obligation, parallel to the mishnah's wording regarding an inadvertent case. The Bach argues that the Tur's citation of Rav Zevid would have no practical relevance once the Rosh rules like Beit Shammai, so it must teach a stricter after the fact rule. In my humble opinion this is no proof. It is not universally agreed that halachah follows Beit Shammai here; the Rosh only reports that Rav Amram ruled so. Even according to Rav Amram, one who acts like Beit Hillel may not have committed a prohibition. Baal Halachot Gedolot, folio 10, writes that one who forgot and acts like Beit Shammai deserves blessing because halachah follows Beit Shammai, implying that the other practice is not prohibited. One can also say that the Rosh cites Rav Zevid precisely to teach that in an intentional case the requirement is only to return and bless initially, but after the fact there is no need to repeat. This is demonstrated by the Semag, Semak, Rabbenu Yerucham, and Tzeidah LaDerech, who rule that after the fact an intentional departure has the same law as an inadvertent one even though they rule like Beit Shammai. The Rosh's own abbreviated rulings imply the same. He cites the Rosh's disagreement with the Rambam regarding the inadvertent case, but for the intentional case he rules without qualification like the Rambam. This appears to me to be the practical halachah. One who is stringent and repeats Birkat HaMazon in such a case is acting astonishingly and recites a blessing in vain. Magen Avraham writes that if one is so far away that the food will become digested before he can return, he should recite Birkat HaMazon where he is.
[Levush] End of seif 1. I have already written that the Rama and Bach appear to agree with this, and it seems correct to me because Baal Halachot Gedolot, Semag, Semak, Rabbenu Yerucham, and Tzeidah LaDerech rule so. I saw in Raavan, siman 188, that if one ate he should return to the place where he ate and bless, but if he ate while traveling and did not remember until he had already continued on the road, he should bless where he remembers. I am surprised at the source of this distinction. The Gemara's incident involving the golden dove occurred on the road. Also, Rav Zevid should have distinguished between an inadvertent case in town and on the road. Furthermore, Baal Halachot Gedolot, Semag, and Semak all speak of one who forgot and left for the road or marketplace, showing no distinction. In my humble opinion Raavan was uncertain whether halachah follows Beit Shammai or Beit Hillel and how to understand the Rambam, so he made his own compromise. In town, where returning is not so burdensome, he rules like Beit Shammai. On the road, where returning is burdensome and delay disturbs one's peace of mind, as explained at the end of the previous siman, he rules like Beit Hillel. The same would apply if he ate in town and then left on a journey: he may bless where he remembers. Raavan's examples are merely the ordinary cases. Magen Avraham writes that if one ate in one room while intending to recite Birkat HaMazon elsewhere in the same house, that intention is effective where the first place is not clean. See above siman 178 and Responsa Rabbi Yom Tov Tzahalon, siman 87.
