The Taz objected that the Mechaber himself, in siman 178, seifim 1 and 2, concedes in such a case that one blessing is sufficient. So too, the dispute between the Mechaber and the Rama in the gloss there, seif 2, concerns whether he must first recite Birkat HaMazon before eating again; but if he transgressed and ate a second time, all agree that one Birkat HaMazon is sufficient. See Bach. This is why the Taz asks how the Mechaber established the ruling here in the Shulchan Aruch. What the Taz writes, that Mussaf has no compensation at Minchah, is merely habitual phrasing. According to R' Yehuda, who says that Mussaf may be prayed until seven hours, it is correct. According to us, however, who rule like the Sages that Mussaf may be prayed all day, if the entire day passed it has no compensation at night, as stated in siman 108, seif 6, and at the beginning of siman 286, seif 1; see there. The Taz distinguishes between this case and eating, because eating has no fixed limit. Therefore, according to the Mechaber in siman 178, seifim 1 and 2, if one diverted his attention from eating he must first recite Birkat HaMazon; and if he then transgressed and ate more, he would have to recite Birkat HaMazon twice. According to our practice, however, as the Rama writes there in seif 2, one never needs to recite Birkat HaMazon twice. Here, once he diverts his attention, the obligation of the blessing takes effect immediately. The Rama therefore remained silent here and conceded to the Mechaber. As for what the Taz cites from Yoreh De'ah, siman 19: that case concerns one who had many birds before him, recited the blessing, slaughtered one, and then spoke. He must recite a separate blessing over the second slaughter, because after the first slaughter has ended he must complete that act by covering its blood before slaughtering again. This is unlike eating. If one ate and said, 'Let us recite Birkat HaMazon,' he may not eat until he again recites HaMotzi, yet even so he need not recite Birkat HaMazon twice, as stated in the gloss to siman 178. Birkat HaMazon is not the completion of the act, but praise and acknowledgment, and one acknowledgment suffices. This differs from the entire foregoing discussion. The Taz, of blessed memory, holds that the essential obligation of Birkat HaMazon depends upon the person: if he wishes, he may eat more under the same HaMotzi and afterward recite Birkat HaMazon once. Nevertheless, I do not understand how he proves this from there. On the contrary, the expression 'praise and acknowledgment' implies that the principal distinction is that, for blessings of praise, one acknowledgment suffices; Asher Yatzar is likewise a blessing of praise. See Levush, who gives a different reason for the distinction: here the second blessing comes of itself through the natural functioning of the human body, whereas Birkat HaMazon and blessings over enjoyment arise through his own choice, and therefore he need not recite them twice. As a matter of practical halacha, we presently rule leniently in cases of doubtful blessings, in accordance with Ateret Zekeinim, Shlah, Magen Avraham, Bach, and my revered teacher the pious one, of blessed memory. Eliyah Rabbah, ot 2, agreed that the principal view follows the Shulchan Aruch and Taz; nevertheless, a doubt concerning a rabbinic obligation is resolved leniently, while the severity of 'You shall not take the Name' also applies. If he wishes, he may recite it without God's Name and kingship. The first blessing is the present obligation and the second is compensation, like prayer, as stated below in siman 108, seif 1. According to the Mechaber, however, who requires him to bless twice with God's Name and kingship, there is no concept of compensation here, for the entire day is its time, and indeed indefinitely.
Know. If one urinated, diverted his attention, and did not recite the blessing, and afterward wishes to urinate again, it appears that he must first bless for what has already occurred. One reason is that there is a double doubt: perhaps the halacha follows the Mechaber's ruling here, and perhaps the halacha in siman 178 follows the Mechaber, that even during a meal, if one diverted his attention, he must first recite Birkat HaMazon; all the more so here. See Lechem Mishneh, chapter 4 of Hilchot Berachot, halacha 6, who rules stringently on the basis of a double doubt and requires a blessing. However, we have been uncertain whether a double doubt is effective with respect to blessings according to the Rambam, who permits a matter subject to a Torah-level doubt, or because of the severity of 'You shall not take the Name,' and so forth. With God's help, this will be explained in the introduction to the laws of blessings. Nor do the Rama and the Mechaber together constitute a doubt. Nevertheless, here one can say that it is certainly so: according to all opinions, he must first recite Asher Yatzar, as I have written. See Levush and what I write immediately below concerning this.
Question. Since we rule in accordance with the Shulchan Aruch that throughout the day, even if one relieved himself, cleaned and rubbed his hands, and immediately prays, he does not recite Al Netilat Yadayim if this occurred incidentally, there remains another doubt. If, before prayer, he wishes to taste food dipped in liquid or the like, may he recite Al Netilat Yadayim? Answer. I shall answer briefly. One who examines the Tur, Orach Chayyim 473, where it says that he should wash and rub his hands, and so forth, will see that this means he should recite Al Netilat Yadayim. Beit Yosef, Bach, and Perishah questioned this; see Taz, siman 473, ot 7, and Derishah there. If we say that a double doubt obligates a blessing, as Lechem Mishneh writes in chapter 4 of Hilchot Berachot, halacha 6, then here too there is a double doubt: perhaps the halacha is that one recites a blessing for food dipped in liquid, and perhaps the halacha follows the first opinion in siman 4. If so, regarding prayer according to our practice, Darkei Moshe writes here that because of the doubt concerning blessings we rule like the Mechaber, whereas here there is a double doubt. Yet the fact that the Mechaber, in siman 473, seif 6, did not give this advice (to wash and rub his hands) shows that even a double doubt does not suffice. This is what we wished to clarify. With God's help, it will be explained further in the laws of blessings. This is not the place to elaborate.
What the Mechaber writes. In seif 2, 'if he urinated and did not rub,' I had thought for this reason that he washes only one hand, the one with which he normally rubs. This would fit the passage in Yoma cited by Beit Yosef: for a meal, he washes one hand and rubs with it. Bach, of blessed memory, resolves the passage concerning Kiddush by saying that according to the conclusion there is no dispute, and that he rubs only with his left hand. This is not correct. Cleaning oneself is forbidden with the right hand, but rubbing is permitted with it, as is proven in siman 43 regarding tefillin in a privy; see there. If so, the expression 'there is no dispute' can apply to both hands. The Gemara in Yoma states the strict law, but as a matter of piety both hands should be washed for this reason. This is certainly so when one leaves a permanent privy, where there is an independent doubt because of an impure spirit, as I wrote above. See Magen Avraham concerning this.
Eliyah Rabbah. In the name of Maharshal's responsa, he writes that Asher Yatzar precedes Borei Nefashot, because the more frequent obligation takes precedence; see there.
