(7) A stolen sukkah is valid - The wording is not entirely precise, for we derive from the verse "you shall make for yourself the festival of Sukkot" that a stolen sukkah is excluded. Rather, it means that a stolen sukkah of this sort, where one overpowered another and removed him, etc., is valid.
(8) For land is not stolen - Anything attached to the ground has the status of ground, and it remains in the possession of its original owners; for him it is like a borrowed sukkah.
(9) Without his knowledge - Perhaps the owner of the sukkah objects to this, because he does not want his fellow to see his affairs and his eating without his knowledge, and therefore it is not proper to recite a blessing over it. But it is permitted to enter and sit in another person's sukkah when the owner is not there in his sukkah, for he certainly does not object, since a person is pleased that a mitzvah be performed with his property. Nevertheless, if the owner of the sukkah may come while he is there, even though he was not there at the time of entry, he should not enter without his knowledge, for perhaps the owner will be embarrassed to enter and eat or do some other matter in his presence. Bikkurei Yaakov wrote that if one received permission from his fellow's wife when he is not home, it appears effective, for since his wife permitted it he presumably will not object.
(10) That belongs to the public - The Magen Avraham wrote that it requires study that some have the custom to make a sukkah in a public domain. Even if you say that all Israel waive their rights, non-Jews nevertheless have a share in it. He concludes that for this reason it is forbidden to make a sukkah in the public domain, since non-Jews certainly do not waive their rights. Although after the fact it is valid, one should not recite a blessing over it, for it would be a berachah levatalah. Eliyah Rabbah wrote that it is not a berachah levatalah; see there, where he discusses this at length. Therefore, if one has no other sukkah, he may bless over it. Maamar Mordechai likewise inclines to be lenient. In Responsa Shoel Umeishiv, siman 128, he writes that the street before the house belongs to them and is not called a public domain, especially where they have a document from the city officer granting permission to make eruvin and sukkot; there is no concern in this, as Pitchei Teshuvah writes. See Biur Halacha.
(11) They should not cut - From a non-Jew's forest, even with the non-Jew's consent. The reason for this law and its details will be explained below in siman 649 seif 1 in the gloss. In a place where the local people have permission to take wood from the forest by the law of the kingdom, there is no prohibition if one takes from there for sechach.
(12) Rather, they should buy them from non-Jews, etc. - Meaning that the non-Jew himself should cut them at first. If the non-Jew stole the wood from the forest, see Biur Halacha. The later authorities wrote that the same applies if a Jew cut it and gives it to his fellow: it is also permitted, for there was despair in the hand of the first cutter and a change of domain in the hand of the second.
(13) For every theft, etc. - Meaning, perhaps the non-Jew stole the land. Nevertheless, if it is impossible, one may cut it himself, provided that he takes permission from the owner of the land, even if he is a non-Jew.
(14) One does not make, etc. - And one should not recite the blessing "to sit in the sukkah." Nevertheless, regarding the preceding law in seif katan 13, where in a case of impossibility it is permitted to cut it himself, perhaps one may bless in our countries, for most of the lands that non-Jews have were given to them by the law of the kingdom, and dina demalchuta dina. See below in siman 664 in the Taz; I copied his words here in Biur Halacha.
(15) The enactment resulted - So it should read. Meaning, although by Torah law it is invalid even if he wants to pay for the wood, because he must dismantle the structure and return the stolen item to its owners, nevertheless the Sages already enacted, so that people would be willing to repent, that he pays only the value of the wood and that suffices. They have the authority to do this, for hefker beit din hefker. Consequently, if he pays the value of the wood, it afterwards becomes truly his, and it is permitted to sit in it and also to bless over it.
(16) The value of the wood - If he does not want to give the value of the wood, he has not fulfilled.
(17) But if he stole a sukkah, etc. - Then the sukkah itself is stolen, since it is not attached to the ground, and he is not obligated to return money but the structure as it is built. There is no enactment for penitents here, for he did not toil over it to build it and did not spend expenses on it; therefore it is stolen.
(18) He has not fulfilled - For it is written, "you shall make for yourself the festival of Sukkot for seven days," excluding a stolen sukkah, which is not "yours." From the words of the Magen Avraham it implies that others who sat in it also did not fulfill their obligation, for it is not "yours"; in my humble opinion this requires study. See Biur Halacha.
(19) He has not fulfilled with it - This is not comparable to what was said above in seif 2, for there the land remains in the possession of its owner and is not stolen, and anything attached to the ground has the status of ground. Here, however, the sukkah does not stand on Reuven's land, so he stole only the wood and not the land, and it is stolen.
