(There, seif 3) A stolen sukkah is valid, etc. NB: The Magen Avraham brought in this siman the view of Sefer Yereim that theft from a non-Jew, although permitted according to that view, is nevertheless not called "yours"; see there. I have a great astonishment at his words from the Gemara in Sotah 43b. There, regarding "he dedicated it and did not inaugurate it," excluding a robber, the Gemara asks: let us say this is not like Rabbi Yose HaGelili, who says that "the man who is fearful and fainthearted" means one afraid of sins in his hand. It answers: even if you say it follows Rabbi Yose HaGelili, it is a case where he repented and paid its value. If so, the Gemara asks, it should be like one who bought it and should return from battle, since originally it came to his hand through theft. Why was the Gemara pressed to answer this way? It should have answered that the case is theft from a non-Jew. If so there is no sin, yet it is still not his and is not called "he inaugurated it"; therefore he does not return. Rabbi Yose would hold like the view that theft from a non-Jew is permitted, while the view that theft from a non-Jew is forbidden would follow the Rabbis, who do not hold that the fearful one is afraid of sins in his hand. Thus the sugya would fit all opinions. It is therefore forced that if theft from a non-Jew is permitted, it is truly his, it is his house, and is like an actual purchase; otherwise why would he not return? This is proof against the Yereim and requires great study. I also find difficulty from Sukkah 31, where we say that one fulfills the mitzvah in a sukkah built on stolen land because land is not stolen and it is like a borrowed sukkah. Since we rule that a mitzvah performed through a transgression is invalid, then either stolen land involves the prohibition of theft, in which case it should be a mitzvah haba'ah ba'aveirah, or, if land is not stolen and there is no prohibition, why does Sotah ask that the robber should be excluded as fearful of sins in his hand? Perhaps that case is stolen land, where there is no prohibition of "you shall not steal." See the Magen Avraham's discussion whether a house can be stolen or is like land. This is difficult from here and requires great study. I also have a strong difficulty on the Yereim from Bava Kamma 113, where Rabbi Akiva derives that theft from a non-Jew is forbidden from "after he is sold, redemption shall be for him," that one may not take him by force, and from "he shall reckon with his purchaser," that one must be exact with his purchaser. From where is it clear that his theft is forbidden? Perhaps it is really permitted, and the verse only teaches that since the slave's body is acquired by the non-Jew, his work would not be called "yours"; if so, if one betrothed a woman with it she would not be betrothed, and if one bought an etrog with it he would not fulfill the mitzvah. Therefore the Torah revealed that one may not overcharge him, because otherwise it would not be called "yours." In my view this is a clear proof against the Yereim. I also find great difficulty from the beginning of chapter 2 of Bekhorot, 13b, where the Gemara says that according to the view that theft from a non-Jew is permitted, why do I need a verse to exclude a non-Jew from ona'ah? According to the Yereim, what is the question? Perhaps the verse teaches that with ona'ah it is also called "yours": with theft, although permitted, it is still called theft and therefore is not "yours," but ona'ah is excluded because it is not called ona'ah at all, and therefore is also "yours." This requires great study. The Gemara at the beginning of Lulav HaGazul asks why a verse is needed to exclude a stolen lulav; Tosafot ask that it should be invalid as a mitzvah haba'ah ba'aveirah. There they can answer that the verse was needed to exclude a borrowed lulav, but regarding sukkah that answer is unavailable according to the Rabbis, who say later in HaYashen that one fulfills the obligation in another's sukkah, derived from "all the citizenry in Israel shall dwell in sukkot," teaching that all Israel are fit to sit in one sukkah. I wondered why Tosafot lengthened by saying "and they derive it..." What difference does it make, for the question, where the Rabbis learned it from? It appears to me, with the help of Heaven, that one could have answered Tosafot's question as follows. Since we rule that a person is pleased that a mitzvah be performed with his property, one might think a stolen sukkah should also be valid. But that is not correct, because with regard to the robber, who harmed the owner, the owner is not pleased that he perform a mitzvah with his property. Still, for others who did not harm him, one might say that he is pleased, so there is no transgression; therefore the Torah invalidated stolen sukkah, even for others. Yet if he is pleased, it would be like a borrowed sukkah, and a borrowed sukkah is valid. Rather, one must say that because "yours" is written, it implies that it must be yours. In a borrowed sukkah, since he lends it knowingly, it is his for its use, whether one holds that ownership of produce is like ownership of the body or not, because the mitzvah of sukkah is only the act of dwelling and the use-right is sufficient. But without the owner's knowledge, there is no conveyance, and for conveyance we need the owner's consent. Therefore the verse "yours" is needed to exclude stolen sukkah even for others. What excludes a borrowed lulav from "yours" is different, because lulav involves taking and not enjoyment of produce; but sukkah involves dwelling and benefit, so borrowing can fall within "yours." Thus Tosafot first note that borrowed sukkah is derived from another verse. Were borrowed sukkah included in "yours" by virtue of use-right, no other verse would be needed; since it is learned from another verse, it proves that borrowed is not included in "yours," and Tosafot's question stands. Nor can one answer that the verse is for others who use the stolen sukkah because a person is pleased that a mitzvah be done with his property; if so, even a sukkah of a king would not be excluded, for it would also be borrowed. Therefore Tosafot needed to state the source. This occurred to me only as a note; may our words be accepted before the Creator of the world, who dwells in the heavens. Tosafot at the beginning of Lulav HaGazul ask why a verse of "yours" is needed to exclude stolen; let it be invalid as a mitzvah haba'ah ba'aveirah. It appears that a practical difference would be where a non-Jew compelled him to sit against his will in another's sukkah. If the invalidity were only because of mitzvah haba'ah ba'aveirah, there is no transgression because he was coerced, and if in his heart he intended to fulfill the mitzvah, he would fulfill it. But if the Torah excluded stolen sukkah, then even without a transgression it is not his and not borrowed, and his intent does not help, since there is no act of sitting in a valid sukkah. Another difference is a king, regarding whom Bava Kamma says that a king may break through a fence to make a road for himself and no one protests. At least there is no prohibition of theft, but it still does not become his; therefore there is no transgression, yet he does not fulfill the mitzvah because it is borrowed or not "yours." Another answer to Tosafot's question is where Levi stole from Reuven and Shimon, from each one less than a perutah, and used it as s'chach. Although it is forbidden to steal even the smallest amount, that is because half a shiur is forbidden by Torah law, as the Sema writes in Choshen Mishpat simanim 358 and 361. Still, one can say that mitzvah haba'ah ba'aveirah applies only to a complete shiur and not to half a shiur, for the Shach writes in Yoreh De'ah siman 238 that half a shiur is more lenient even than a rabbinic prohibition with respect to an oath taking effect upon it, and Tosafot also state that an oath takes effect on half a shiur. If so, the verse is needed to teach that even if the stolen amount is below the threshold for mitzvah haba'ah ba'aveirah, it is still not "yours." Further, since together he has a perutah's worth of theft, he is ultimately using stolen s'chach worth a perutah and does not fulfill his obligation. Another difference is where he stole s'chach, placed it on the sukkah, and afterward bought it from the robber. Then, if stolen material is invalid in itself, it is a case of ta'aseh velo min ha'asui, because it was made while invalid. See the Peri Megadim, who was uncertain about this. In my humble opinion this appears correct. But if the invalidity were only because of mitzvah haba'ah ba'aveirah, one must say that the name of mitzvah first takes effect, for if there is no mitzvah at all there is no invalidity except through the fact that it is a mitzvah performed through a transgression. It would therefore not be possible to invalidate it afterward as ta'aseh velo min ha'asui. This is clear and correct; examine carefully.
(There, in the same seif, in the gloss) Nevertheless, lechatchilah a person should not sit in his fellow's sukkah without his knowledge, all the more if he intends to steal it; and likewise one should not initially make a sukkah on another's land without his knowledge, etc. NB: We learn in Sukkah: a stolen sukkah, and one who makes s'chach in the public domain; Rabbi Eliezer invalidates and the Sages validate. The Gemara explains that they disagree whether land can be stolen. At first glance I find this difficult: from where does Rabbi Eliezer know that a sukkah in the public domain is invalid? Were it not for the verse "yours" excluding stolen, even an actually stolen sukkah would be valid; only because the verse revealed "yours" do we exclude stolen. If so, from where do we know that the public domain is also invalid? The Tosafot Yom Tov writes in Orlah 1:2, regarding one who plants in the public domain, a non-Jew who planted, and a robber, that he is liable in orlah. He asks why the Mishnah needs both cases: if planting in the public domain is liable, where despair does not apply, all the more stolen land where the owners despaired. He answers that because land is not stolen, one might have thought stolen land is more exempt than public domain, since the public domain ordinarily belongs to everyone for walking, standing, resting, and the like, and therefore one who plants there is like one planting in his own domain, unlike stolen land where land is not stolen. He also cites Rav Ovadiah Bartenura, who holds that with stolen land one is liable only if the owners despaired, but without despair he is exempt from orlah. According to this, for Rav Ovadiah Bartenura it is certainly evident that the public domain is stronger than individual theft and is not considered so stolen, for in the public domain one is liable even without despair, whereas individual stolen land requires despair. Even according to the Tosafot Yom Tov, who holds that one is liable without despair, it is also evident that the public domain is stronger, as he himself writes; otherwise his question would stand. If so, since sukkah has only one exclusion, "yours," perhaps it excludes only individual theft, while the public domain remains permitted, because each person has a share in it and it is like one planting in his own. From where is Rabbi Eliezer's invalidation derived? One might say it follows from Tosafot's question: why is "yours" needed to exclude stolen; it is mitzvah haba'ah ba'aveirah? Therefore, in truth, for individual theft no verse is needed, and "yours" must exclude the public domain, where there is no transgression because he too has a share in it, but it is not "yours" because it is not like borrowed property given knowingly. But that works only for those who accept mitzvah haba'ah ba'aveirah. What of the view that does not accept mitzvah haba'ah ba'aveirah at all, or the poskim who hold it is only rabbinic? Then perhaps "yours" excludes only individual theft and not the public domain. It appears that the two issues depend on each other. According to Rabbi Eliezer, who holds that land can be stolen, since he steals the body of the land, the public domain is certainly like stealing from an individual, for the land itself is not his and he is now stealing it; indeed public domain is worse, for who can claim and who can waive? Therefore the verse "yours" excludes both, for each has an advantage and a disadvantage and they are balanced. But the Mishnah in Orlah follows the Rabbis, according to our ruling that land is not stolen. If the body of the land remains in the owner's domain and he is not stealing the land itself, but only using it temporarily, then in the public domain, where he too has a share for use, the public domain is indeed stronger than individual property; therefore Orlah lists them as "not only this, but also this." As to what the Rama wrote, that Jews should not cut the s'chach themselves, etc., but should buy it from a non-Jew: the language of the Hagahot Ashri in Sukkah chapter 3 is as follows. The Raavyah, in the name of his father Rabbenu Yoel, wrote that people must be careful not to cut willow for the lulav from fields of Cutheans, even with permission, because ordinary Cutheans are field-robbers, etc.; rather one should hire the Cuthean to cut it, so that there will be owner-despair in his hands and a change of domain into ours. This implies that one may hire the non-Jew to cut. In truth the matter requires study. From where is it clear that this helps? The Gemara speaks of the non-Jew himself, the owner of the field, cutting. Since it is originally his and he intends to steal it, the despair properly occurs in his own hands, and then the change of domain occurs in ours. Also, since he himself is the seller, he acts on his own account and not as the Jew's agent. But if the Jew hires another non-Jew to cut, that non-Jew does not intend to steal for himself and is not acting on his own account, but only for the Jew. If so, the despair does not take effect in the non-Jew's hand, but only when it comes to the Jew who intends to acquire it; it is still as though the Jew stole it. This is all the more difficult according to the Machaneh Ephraim in Hilkhot Sheluchin, who holds that in a case of hire there is agency for a non-Jew, as cited in Sha'ar HaMelekh, Hilkhot Terumot; then the non-Jew is the Jew's agent and it is as if the Jew himself stole it. One cannot say that the Hagahot Ashri means that the very same Cuthean who sells it is hired to cut it, for then why mention hiring at all? If he means another non-Jew, he states the ordinary case, since a non-Jew would not do it for free, and perhaps he teaches precisely that even by hire it is permitted for the non-Jew to cut. But if he means the same non-Jew, why mention hire? Even without hire he would do so, since he needs to sell; if he does not cut, the Jew will not buy. He should have used the Gemara's language, "let them cut it." It must mean another non-Jew, and if so it is difficult why this truly helps. The Rama in fact changed the wording of the Hagahot Ashri and wrote only that one should buy from a non-Jew, etc.; the words of the Hagahot Ashri require study. Regarding whether it is permitted to make a sukkah in the public domain, I wrote to permit it in Sefer HaChayim. Now I have found clear proof for this from the Yerushalmi and the Rashba in his novellae to Bava Kamma, at the end of HaKones. There, Rabbi Yosi says that regarding a Chanukkah lamp one is exempt because he placed it with permission, meaning with the permission of a mitzvah, as stated in chapter HaManiach regarding one who removes his straw. However, not everyone who puts something out with the permission of a mitzvah is exempt; if one made a mitzvah-sukkah at the entrance of his house outside and others were damaged by it, he is liable, as stated in the Yerushalmi, such as those who make a sukkah at the entrance of their shops on the festival. On Chanukkah, however, he is exempt because there is a mitzvah to place it at the entrance of his house for publicizing the miracle. It is clear that his intent is that specifically on Chanukkah, where the mitzvah is to place it outside, he is exempt; but a sukkah, since the mitzvah is not specifically to be outside, he is liable for damages. Yet the sukkah must still be valid and a mitzvah, for otherwise it is obvious he is liable. Necessarily the case is where he made the sukkah in the public domain; if it were in his own domain, he would be exempt from all damages. Thus it is clear that a sukkah in the public domain is valid and constitutes a mitzvah. This is clear and correct. I later remembered that this proof is already explained in Mekor Chayim from the Tosefta; in my Sefer HaChayim I rejected it, see there.
