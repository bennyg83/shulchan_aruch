And likewise on land that belongs to the public - See Mishnah Berurah, where we wrote in the name of the Magen Avraham that he is concerned for a blessing in vain. Several later authorities disagreed with him; see Eliyah Rabbah, copied by the Peri Megadim, that there is no proof from siman 649, and see Mor U'Ketziah, Bigdei Yesha, and Ma'amar Mordechai. The view of all of them comes out that it is certainly not a blessing in vain. This is the language of the Beit Meir: Gloss: "and likewise on land, etc." In the Darchei Moshe he wrote about this: I have not seen people careful about this. But the Magen Avraham wrote that although after the fact, etc., nevertheless it seems to me that one should not bless over it, as written at the beginning of siman 649, and if so it is a blessing in vain. See siman 454, what I wrote about the body of the law in siman 649. Here it is clear that he should bless, and this is not related to that siman. There, he acquired the stolen item, etc.; all this is when one seizes his fellow's property and intends to steal. This is not so in our case, when one makes a sukkah in the public domain. It is nothing other than actually borrowed, for he intends only to borrow the place until after the festival and return it intact without any loss. It is not even borrowing without knowledge, for the public, and even gentiles, see and do not protest; it is actually borrowing with knowledge. It is obvious that he should bless. See there further. End quote. The Bigdei Yesha wrote exactly like this. But what shall we do with what is stated in the Darchei Moshe in the name of the Yerushalmi, copied by the Gra in his commentary: Gamliel Zugga made himself a sukkah in the marketplace; Reish Lakish passed by and said to him: Who permitted this to you? The Or Zarua explained that the rabbis validated one who roofs in the public domain only after the fact, but not at the outset. It is possible to say that this is specifically in the marketplace, where passersby are common and he blocks their way, but not one who roofs at the side of the public domain near the entrance of his house. Something like this I copied inside from Responsa Sho'el U'Meshiv. Afterward I found in Bikkurei Yaakov that he is entirely lenient in this, for another reason: the Rema wrote in Choshen Mishpat siman 162, seif 1, that if the king gave permission to place doors in their alleyway, the law of the kingdom is law, for the markets and streets are theirs and they may do with them what they wish. Now, since everything is under the authority of the king, both the streets within the city and those outside the city, and since the government has authority to protest and does not protest, it presumably forgoes this, allowing a sukkah to be made in the public domain, and there is no prohibition of theft at all. See there, for his view is that one need not take explicit permission for this from the city official, for it is presumably granted, since they do not protest; therefore one may properly bless; see there. In conclusion, those who are accustomed to be lenient in this should not be protested, for those who permit are many.
It further seems to me that if we come to be stringent not to make one even near the entrance of his house, great damage will result. I have seen with my own eyes that people make the sukkah behind the houses, and in small towns, where it is not so common for each person to have a special privy, that place is designated for children to relieve themselves there, and sometimes even adults. It is common that the place is not clean, and even if they want to clean it now, I do not know if that helps by law, for it is included in an old privy. In any event, by law one must inspect the walls of the house there, and I already wrote at length above in siman 83 in Biur Halachah, s.v. "and he should see"; see there. It is better to make a sukkah in the actual public domain than to make a sukkah in such places. See Berachot 24b.
They should not cut, etc. - See Mishnah Berurah in what we wrote, "and in a place, etc." This is from the Taz. He further wrote in siman 664 that the same applies in a place where the forest trees belong to the city official and he gives permission to cut there to anyone who wishes, for free or for money; there is also no prohibition if the Jew himself cuts. Likewise, if a gentile has fields and forests by the law of the kingdom, it is also permitted when he gives the Jew permission to cut, for from now there is no concern for the rule that ordinary gentiles are robbers of Jewish land, since now it is his by the law of the kingdom. But without the gentile's permission, certainly one may not cut, since now he steals from a gentile, and theft from a gentile is forbidden. Although the Eliyah Rabbah wrote about the Taz's ruling that it requires examination, nevertheless in my humble opinion one may rely on this, for even without this, the Riaz in Shiltei HaGibborim and R. Yitzchak permit in places where there never was a Jewish majority. Therefore at least one may rely on the combination of the two permissions together [Bikkurei Yaakov].
Rather, they should buy them from gentiles - If the gentile stole from the forest, then if the forest belongs to one estate owner, at first glance the entire permission is because there is owner-despair in the gentile's hand and change of domain in the Jew's hand. This applies only to the awankari case brought there in the Gemara (page 31), where the despair was already present, except that despair did not take effect on the land and now takes effect on the fruits that were cut. This is unlike here, where the estate owner did not know that the wood had been stolen from him, so it is despair without knowledge, even if he will despair in the end. If so, only change of domain in the Jew's hand remains. In truth, however, this is not so, for we say that this estate owner presumably stole it long ago from another person, and that person's despair takes effect now at the time the wood is cut. But all this works if the forest belongs to an estate owner. If the forest belongs to the government and the gentile stole the wood from it, one must examine how despair applies. Perhaps we say that the government knew beforehand that thieves would certainly be found, and the despair took effect.
And if he stole wood, etc. - If one borrowed laths or the like from his fellow to stand the roofing upon them, and he lent them to his fellow, one may say it is theft and he does not fulfill his obligation. Even according to the decisors who hold that we are not particular about supports made from invalid roofing material, perhaps theft is more severe [Peri Megadim]. His intent is if the second borrower does not pay him for the laths and his use remains in the category of theft; this is as the later authorities ruled, that if he does not pay him the value of the wood, everything remains in the category of theft and he does not fulfill his obligation with the sukkah. But if he pays him, it is no worse than if he himself stole it, where he pays only the value of the wood and fulfills his obligation with the sukkah. Know that what we prohibit as a stolen sukkah is not specifically when the roofing was stolen; the same applies regarding the walls [Peri Megadim, and so wrote Bikkurei Yaakov]. Because of our many sins, many people are not careful about this: they take boards without the owner's knowledge to set up the walls, intending to return them immediately after Sukkot. But in truth they do not fulfill their obligation with this, for in any event they are currently using them through theft, and it is forbidden to recite a blessing over such a sukkah.
And made a sukkah from them, even though he did not attach them - Meaning that he roofed it with the wood and did not attach the wood to the building at all. One might have thought that the enactment for repentant thieves does not apply here at all; therefore it teaches us otherwise.
He has fulfilled through the enactment of the sages - If he answers him that he should wait until after Sukkot and he will return the stolen item itself, it is reasonable that he has not fulfilled, for he is now using them through theft. It is understandable if he says that he agrees to pay him money for his wood, for then we say the rabbis removed the prohibition of theft from him; but not in such a case. See Sha'ar HaTziyun, where we wrote in the name of the Machazik Berachah; one can distinguish.
That is made on top of a wagon, etc. - The same applies to a sukkah built on the ground but not attached to the ground at all, and all the more so one on wheels that can be moved from place to place, for theft applies to it [Peri Megadim].
He has not fulfilled - See Mishnah Berurah, where we wrote that even others who sat in it also did not fulfill. This is from the words of Machatzit HaShekel, who infers this from the Magen Avraham's statement, "see the beginning of siman 649." In my humble opinion this is not clear. There, regarding lulav, where the Torah wrote "for yourselves," we require that it be truly yours, and even a borrowed one is invalid. Therefore if one stole a lulav and gave it to his fellow to fulfill the mitzvah with it, although that fellow did not steal it, nevertheless he does not fulfill, for in any event it is not his and is no better than borrowed, with which one does not fulfill on the first day. This is unlike sukkah, where the Torah prohibited only stolen and not borrowed, as stated in the Gemara and ruled in Shulchan Aruch, seif 2. It is possible that this person too fulfills, for he does not intend to steal it, and it is also possible that the owner intends to permit this other person to sit in his sukkah, since a person is pleased that a mitzvah be done with his property; and here the reason stated in Mishnah Berurah small paragraph 9 does not apply. This requires examination. What the Magen Avraham hinted, to see the beginning of siman 649, means that it is forbidden both before despair and after despair, as there. What he further wrote, that regarding sukkah it is like lulav on the first day of Yom Tov, teaches us that in our case he does not fulfill all seven days by Torah law, just like lulav on the first day of Yom Tov, for it is written "you shall make for yourself seven days," to exclude a stolen one.
