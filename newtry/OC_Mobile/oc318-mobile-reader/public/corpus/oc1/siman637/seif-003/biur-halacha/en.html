And likewise on land belonging to the public. See Mishnah Berurah, where we wrote in the name of Magen Avraham that he is concerned for a berakhah levatalah. Several later authorities challenged him, including Eliyah Rabbah, Peri Megadim, Mor U'Ketziah, Bigdei Yesha, and Ma'amar Mordekhai, and from all of them it emerges that it is certainly not a berakhah levatalah. Beit Meir writes that Darkhei Moshe says he did not see people careful about this; Magen Avraham nevertheless wrote that although bediavad it is valid, one should not bless, as at the beginning of siman 649. Beit Meir rejects the comparison: there the stolen item was acquired, and the case is one who forcibly takes another's property and intends theft. Here, one who makes a sukkah in the public domain intends only to borrow the place until after the festival and return it intact without loss. It is not even borrowing without knowledge, since the public, and even non-Jews, see and do not object; it is like borrowing with consent, and he certainly blesses. Bigdei Yesha writes similarly. Still, what shall we do with the Yerushalmi cited in Darkhei Moshe and copied by the Gra: Gamliel Zuga made a sukkah in the marketplace, and Reish Lakish passed by and said, "Who permitted this to you?" Or Zarua explains that the Sages validated s'chach in the public domain only bediavad, not lechatchilah. Perhaps that applies specifically in the marketplace, where passersby are common and he blocks their path, but not one who makes a sukkah at the side of the public domain near his doorway. I copied something similar inside from Responsa Sho'el U'Meshiv. I later found Bikkurei Yaakov lenient entirely for another reason: Rama in Choshen Mishpat siman 162:1 writes that if the king gave permission to set doors in their alley, dina de-malkhuta dina, because the markets and streets are his and he may do with them as he wishes. Since everything is under the king's authority, both streets in and out of town, and the government could object but does not, it presumably waives the matter and allows making a sukkah in the public domain; there is no theft at all. In his view one need not explicitly obtain permission from the city official, for it is presumed granted since they do not protest. Therefore one may properly bless. In conclusion, those who are lenient should not be protested, for many permit.
It further appears to me that if we are stringent not to make a sukkah even near the doorway of one's house, great harm will result. I have seen people make sukkot behind the houses, and in small towns it is not common that each house has a separate privy; the area is often designated for children, and sometimes adults, to relieve themselves, and it is common that the place is not clean. Even if they want to clean it now, it is uncertain whether that helps by law, since it is included as an old privy. At minimum, the walls of the house there must be checked, as I discussed at length above in siman 83 in Biur Halacha, s.v. "ve-yir'eh." It is better to make a sukkah in the actual public domain than in such places; see Berakhot 24b.
They should not cut, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote "and in a place..." from the Taz. He further writes in siman 664 that if the forest trees belong to the city official, who permits anyone to cut there, whether for free or payment, there is no prohibition if a Jew cuts them himself. Likewise, if a non-Jew owns fields and forests under the law of the government, it is permitted when he gives a Jew permission to cut; there is no longer concern that ordinary non-Jews are land-robbers from Jews, since now it is his by government law. But without the non-Jew's permission one certainly may not cut, since he is now stealing from a non-Jew, and theft from a non-Jew is forbidden. Although Eliyah Rabbah writes that the Taz's ruling requires study, in my humble opinion one may rely on it, because in any case the Riaz in Shiltei HaGibborim and Rabbenu Yerucham permit in places that were never majority-Jewish; therefore the two grounds for permission may be joined, as Bikkurei Yaakov writes.
Rather, they should buy them from non-Jews. If the non-Jew stole from the forest, and the forest belongs to one lord, at first glance the entire permission rests on despair in the non-Jew's hand and change of domain in the Jew's hand. This applies only to the avankarei case in the Gemara, where the owner's despair occurred earlier, though it did not take effect on the land and takes effect now on the cut produce. But here the lord did not know the wood was stolen from him, and it is despair without knowledge, even if he will later despair. Only change of domain in the Jew's hand remains. In truth this is not so, for we say that this lord presumably stole it earlier from someone else, and that person's despair now takes effect when the trees are cut. This works if the forest belongs to a lord. But if the forest belongs to the government and the non-Jew stole the wood from it, one must examine how despair applies. Perhaps we say the government knew beforehand that thieves would certainly be found, and despair already took effect.
And if one stole wood, etc. If one borrowed laths or the like from his fellow to support the s'chach, and then lent them to another, one can say this is theft and he does not fulfill his obligation; even according to poskim who hold we are not particular about a support made from invalid s'chach, theft may be more severe. This is Peri Megadim's intent if the second borrower will not pay for the laths and his use remains theft, as the later authorities rule that if he does not pay the value of the wood, all remains in a state of theft and he does not fulfill with the sukkah. But if he pays, it is no worse than if he himself stole them, where he pays only the value of the wood and fulfills with the sukkah. Know that the invalidity of a stolen sukkah is not only where the s'chach was stolen; the same applies to the walls, as Peri Megadim and Bikkurei Yaakov write. In our many sins, many people are not careful about this and take boards without the owners' knowledge to make walls, intending to return them immediately after Sukkot. In truth they do not fulfill, for at least now they are using them through theft, and it is forbidden to bless over such a sukkah.
And he made from them a sukkah, even though he did not attach them. This means he covered it with the wood but did not attach the wood to the building at all. One might have thought the enactment for repentant robbers does not apply; therefore it teaches otherwise.
He has fulfilled by rabbinic enactment. If he answers the owner that he should wait until after Sukkot and then he will return the stolen object itself, it is reasonable that he has not fulfilled, for he is now using it in theft. If he says he agrees to pay the value of the wood, we say the Rabbis removed the theft-prohibition from him; but not in this case. See Sha'ar HaTziyun, where we wrote in the name of Machaneh Ephraim; one can distinguish.
Made on top of a wagon, etc. The same applies to a sukkah built on the ground but not attached to the ground at all, and all the more on wheels, so it can be moved from place to place; theft applies to it, as Peri Megadim writes.
He has not fulfilled. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote that even others who sit in it do not fulfill. This is from Machatzit HaShekel, who infers it from Magen Avraham's note to see the beginning of siman 649. In my humble opinion this is not clear. There, with lulav, the Torah writes "yours," requiring it truly to be his and invalidating even a borrowed lulav; therefore if one stole a lulav and gave it to another to fulfill with, even though the other did not steal it, he does not fulfill, for it is at least not his and no better than borrowed on the first day. But with sukkah, the Torah forbids only stolen, not borrowed, as stated in the Gemara and ruled in Shulchan Aruch seif 2. Perhaps this other person does fulfill, since he does not intend to steal it; perhaps the owner's intent is to permit this other person to sit in his sukkah, since a person is pleased that a mitzvah be done with his property, and the reason given in Mishnah Berurah s.k. 9 does not apply. This requires study. Magen Avraham's reference to siman 649 means that it is forbidden before despair and after despair as there; and his statement that sukkah is like lulav on the first day teaches that in our case he does not fulfill all seven days by Torah law, like lulav on the first day, because "you shall make for yourself seven days" excludes a stolen sukkah.
