1) [Se'if 1] If human excrement is behind him, he must distance himself four amos, etc. Tur writes that a foul odor with a source, such as the odor of human excrement, requires a distance of four amos when behind him. Rashba explains that "a foul odor with a source" means exposed excrement. Beis Yosef cites him and writes that this is obvious. Prishah, paragraph 1, explains that when it is covered, even an odor that has a source requires for Krias Shema only that one distance himself until the odor ceases. Once it is covered, it is called an odor without a source. His proof is flatulence from the body, which likewise originates in excrement but is termed without a source because the excrement is covered within the body. Beis Yosef similarly writes at the end of this siman that Orchos Chaim, citing Rashba, defines a foul odor without a source as covered excrement. Rokeach, siman 323, however, writes: For a foul odor with a source, such as covered excrement emitting an odor, one moves four amos from the place where the odor ceases and prays. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 10, cites him and writes that this is also implied by Kesef Mishneh, Hilchos Krias Shema, chapter 3, halachah 12, citing Rabbeinu Manoach; by Rambam himself there; and by Rashi, Tur, and the other poskim, who define an odor without a source specifically as flatulence from the body. Shulchan Aruch also appears to agree, since it does not state that covered excrement is termed without a source and mentions at the end of the siman only flatulence. Eliyah Rabbah writes likewise in siman 76, paragraph 1. Chayei Adam, rule 3, paragraph 22, concurs. Therefore, when possible, one should be stringent and not recite while facing covered excrement that emits an odor unless he is four amos beyond the place where the odor ceases. If that is impossible, one may rely upon Rashba and Prishah, who require only that he move beyond the odor. See also below, paragraph 19.
2) There: He must distance himself four amos, etc. Just as one distances himself from excrement, so must he distance himself from a foul odor even when it does not come from excrement. Any odor that ordinarily causes people distress is called foul. The prohibition applies even to one who has an illness and cannot smell, and to a blind person who cannot see, since other people can see and smell it. Night has the same law as day. Something whose natural odor is unpleasant is permitted; only something that became putrid through decay is treated as excrement. Chayei Adam there, paragraph 12; Kitzur Shulchan Aruch, siman 5, paragraph 6; Ben Ish Chai, Parashas Bo, paragraphs 17 and 27. We write likewise below in siman 86, paragraph 2. Shaar Ruach HaKodesh, folio 8, s.v. "Know," writes that when a person smells a carcass or foul odor, it greatly damages his soul and causes it to distance itself from him; the proof is the Motzaei Shabbos spices, which restore the soul. Obviously, only something that became putrid through decay harms the soul. Something naturally possessing an unpleasant odor, such as petroleum and the like, does not harm it, just as recitation while facing it is not forbidden, as stated above and below in siman 86, paragraph 2.
3) There: He must distance himself four amos from the place where the odor ceases, because it is written, "Your camp shall be holy." The measure is four amos because a person's four amos acquire for him wherever he is, as a halachic principle concerning Shabbos, divorce documents, found objects, and other matters. A person's four amos are therefore his domain and the place in which he encamps. "Your camp" means every place of your encampment must be holy; namely, the entire four amos beyond the place where the odor ceases. If the foul odor were within four amos, his entire encampment would not be holy. Levush; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2. See the following paragraph.
4) There: Four amos from the place where the odor ceases. If one prayed within four amos of a foul odor, although strictly he must distance himself four amos from the place where it ceases, after the fact he has fulfilled his obligation because this requirement is rabbinic. For Krias Shema, he must repeat the recitation without its blessings. Nishmas Adam, rule 3, paragraph 6. See Chayei Adam there, paragraph 33, and our discussion above in siman 76, paragraph 38.
5) There: Four amos from the place where the odor ceases. Ateres Zekenim, paragraph 1, writes that some say one need move only to the place where the odor ceases. This is Rambam's view, which Maran did not accept, as explained in Beis Yosef. The later authorities likewise rule like Maran. G-d forbid that one should initially be lenient against them and rule like Rambam. Rambam's view may be followed only after the fact, if one already prayed, in which case he need not repeat the prayer, as stated in the preceding paragraph.
6) There: Even if he has an illness and cannot smell. Tur writes, "Even if he is a toteran who cannot smell." Aruch defines toteran as an illness of the nose. Prishah, paragraph 3, cites this.
7) There: Even at night, etc. This is because "your camp shall be holy" is required. Olas Tamid, paragraph 2; Magen Avraham, paragraph 1. See what we wrote above, paragraph 3. Taz, paragraph 1, writes in the name of the Yerushalmi that even other words of Torah are forbidden, not only Krias Shema. Taz further writes that even in a place already inspected, if excrement is commonly present there; such as before houses where excrement is ordinarily found, and certainly in a courtyard; recitation is forbidden. We cited this above in siman 76, paragraph 33; see there.
8) There, gloss: Or if he is blind. Although he does not see it, if he stands in a place from which he would see it were he not blind, it is forbidden. Beis Yosef, citing Rabbeinu Yonah and Rashba. The later authorities write likewise. Maran did not state this separately in Shulchan Aruch because it is learned from the law of night, as Maran writes in Beis Yosef in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah and Rashba.
9) There: If it is at his side, its law is like something behind him. Maran writes this in Beis Yosef in the name of Rambam. Kesef Mishneh, Hilchos Krias Shema, chapter 3, halachah 8, cites Rabbeinu Manoach that at first glance it should be treated like something before him, as is stated concerning a bent lulav. Perhaps Rambam discusses a case in which one cannot move the excrement behind him; the Gemara says that when possible, he must move it four amos behind him. Thus, when possible, the side is like the front, and when impossible, it is like the rear. Yad Aharon, Hagahos Beis Yosef, writes likewise. This is also implied by Maran below in siman 81, se'if 2, and Pri Megadim, Eshel Avraham, paragraph 2. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 5, however, writes that the unqualified language of all the poskim implies that the side is like the rear; see his distinction between this law and siman 81. Pri Chadash, paragraph 1, writes that initially one should take account of the stringent view, but if one recited Krias Shema with excrement at his side, he obviously need not repeat it; the same applies to prayer. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, distinguishes between side areas before him, which are treated like the front; see there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, paragraph 1, and Ben Ish Chai, Parashas Bo, paragraph 14, cite this. Since the matter is disputed, initially one should be stringent and treat the sides like the front. After the fact, or when no alternative exists, they are treated like the rear, as stated below in siman 81, se'if 2. Kitzur Shulchan Aruch, siman 5, paragraph 8, writes likewise. An area at the side that he can see without turning his head is treated by all opinions like the front. Chayei Adam, rule 3, paragraph 14; Ben Ish Chai there.
