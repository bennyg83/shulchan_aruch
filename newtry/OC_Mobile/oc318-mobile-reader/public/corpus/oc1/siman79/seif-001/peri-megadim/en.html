Even at night. See Taz. Even doubtful excrement has the same law as definite excrement in a place where such doubt is reasonable, and in front of him the required distance is as far as he can see. I saw fit to introduce here an introduction that is needed for these simanim through siman 87.
Know that the law of excrement is divided into ten categories: first, excrement behind him, including at his sides; second, in front of him; third, in a place ten handbreadths high or ten handbreadths low; fourth, excrement in another room with the doorway open; fifth, excrement behind glass; sixth, excrement in water; seventh, a foul odor that has a source and one that has no source; eighth, moving excrement; ninth, this also includes the law of urine, a chamber pot, and a refuse vessel; tenth, a garbage heap and the excrement of animals and wild beasts.
The first: when excrement is behind him within four amos, it is forbidden by Torah law to recite Krias Shema or speak words of Torah, as it is stated, “Your camp shall be holy,” which refers to excrement, and everything within four amos is considered a person’s camp. So wrote the Chidushei HaRashba and the other poskim. If he recited even in a place where the presence of excrement was only reasonably doubtful, he must repeat the recitation, as stated in siman 76, seif 8. We have already discussed many times whether the reason is that whenever the Torah says not to do something, the act is ineffective, following Rava in Temurah 4. One cannot answer that this applies only to an act, because the Gemara challenges from one who gives terumah before first fruits, which involves speech and intent. Nevertheless, one who studies Sema in Choshen Mishpat siman 208 and Yoreh Deah siman 11 concerning one who slaughters on Yom Kippur or Yom Tov will see that this principle applies only where the matter cannot otherwise take effect. Here, by contrast, the place causes the prohibition, and it appears that the requirement to repeat is Rabbinic, as Rava states in Berachos 22, “The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination.” See also siman 185. This will be explained, with Hashem’s help, below regarding urine.
The second: excrement in front of him requires distancing as far as he can see. You already know the dispute between the Rosh and Rashba. According to the apparent view of the Rosh, the verse “no nakedness shall be seen” refers only to ervah, in accordance with the plain meaning of the passage in Shabbos 150a, while concerning excrement there is only the requirement that “your camp shall be holy.” Why, then, is it forbidden in front of him beyond four amos? Several approaches are possible. It may be Rabbinically forbidden whenever he sees it, or it may be forbidden by Torah law because everything visible before him is included in his camp, and every visible area is regarded as part of his encampment even beyond four amos. In my novellae I discussed this at length. I have now seen that Peri Chadash writes that since the verse says “and no nakedness shall be seen,” with the conjunctive vav, it also refers to excrement, meaning whenever it is outside his camp. Nevertheless, this does not apply in another domain, because the verse “your camp” indicates that such a case is permitted. Therefore, excrement in another room is permitted even though he sees it. According to Rashba, however, “no nakedness shall be seen” also refers to excrement, and even in another room it is forbidden by Torah law whenever he sees it. According to this, if one says that the distance of eyesight is only Rabbinic according to the Rosh, the passages in Berachos 22b concerning one who found excrement in his place, and the baraisa requiring him to move it behind him but not repeat the Amidah, are readily understood. A doubtful Rabbinic prohibition is treated leniently, like urine in siman 76, seif 8. Yet even according to Rashba, who holds that the distance of eyesight is a Torah requirement, he rules in siman 81, cited in Magen Avraham seif katan 4, that one does not repeat the recitation, because “your camp shall be holy” was fulfilled and only “it shall not be seen” was violated. Since he did not know and did not see it, there was no Torah violation. According to the Rosh, if everything visible in front of him is considered his camp, this can also be explained. In any event, the Mechaber’s ruling here in seif 2 appears to follow Rashba, as he also rules in siman 81. A practical difference is that according to the Rosh, perhaps even contemplation is Rabbinically forbidden within eyesight, or else the entire visible area in front of him is considered his camp. According to Rashba, however, contemplation beyond four amos but within eyesight is permitted, because contemplation is permitted even before ervah, as stated in Shabbos 150a, and the same applies to excrement where only “it shall not be seen” applies and not “your camp.”
It would appear at first that the Yerushalmi concerning Rabbi Ila and his colleagues, cited by Chidushei HaRashba, Taz, and Peri Chadash, resolves this. Certainly there was no excrement within four amos, for otherwise the prohibition would derive from “your camp.” It was only within eyesight. How, then, could Rabbi Ila issue a halachic ruling there? This would show that contemplation is permitted within eyesight, and rendering a ruling is considered contemplation, as stated in siman 85, seif 2, and Mishbetzos Zahav seif katan 4. But this proof is mistaken, because Rabbi Ila gave a reason for the prohibition, saying that had it been daytime they would have seen what was before them. He should simply have said that it was forbidden. Furthermore, there it would have been permitted to intervene to separate them from a prohibition, as in siman 85. We must therefore say that Rabbi Ila merely said in general that it was forbidden, and only after leaving the place explained the reason. Nevertheless, the law is correct according to Rashba, that contemplation within eyesight is permitted, as I wrote. The fact that the Sages sat there also shows this, for a Torah scholar may not walk through filthy alleyways, which implies that contemplation beyond four amos is permitted. You see that Rabbi Ila and his colleagues knew with certainty that excrement was present within eyesight and that it was forbidden even at night. Had it been only doubtful, the Mechaber rules in siman 81, following the first opinion stated without qualification, that one does not return to the beginning. What Taz wrote, that even doubtful excrement is forbidden, may refer to within four amos and be an independent point. The difficulty concerning moving excrement according to Rashba will be explained below, with Hashem’s help.
The third: when the excrement is in a place ten handbreadths high or ten handbreadths low, it is in another domain. According to the Rosh, even if he sees it, it is permitted, because the verse “it shall not be seen” does not refer to excrement. According to Rashba, it is forbidden, as explained here in seif 2. Whether the elevated place must be ten handbreadths high and four by four handbreadths wide is discussed by Taz, seif katan 3. See what I will write, with Hashem’s help, concerning excrement behind glass. Regarding Magen Avraham’s statement in seif katan 8 that closing one’s eyes permits it according to all opinions, in my poverty I do not presently know his source. If it is derived from moving excrement in Berachos 25a, where Chidushei HaRashba says it is permitted because it is not within one’s camp, the prohibition of “it shall not be seen” according to Rashba still remains. This would prove that closing one’s eyes helps, but it requires study in light of the Yerushalmi and seif 1 here, where the distance of eyesight is also not part of one’s camp but rather derives only from “it shall not be seen,” yet it is forbidden at night. The case of moving excrement can perhaps be understood as being behind him. It is true that Rambam explains it as passing before him in Hilchos Krias Shema 3:13, floating upon the water opposite him, following Rashi on 25a. I have seen that the master’s words are founded on truth and reason, as he wrote in seif katan 1 concerning nighttime, that the requirement is “your camp shall be holy.” He thereby illuminated the matter. According to the Mechaber, siman 75, seif 7, closing one’s eyes helps regarding ervah by Torah law. Concerning excrement, there are opinions that “it shall not be seen” refers only to ervah. According to the Rosh, if the distance of eyesight were merely Rabbinic, the Rabbinic law would be more stringent than the Torah law. The Mechaber must therefore hold that visible excrement before him within eyesight is not a holy camp, and closing one’s eyes does not help. Regarding ervah, by contrast, only “it shall not be seen” applies, so closing one’s eyes helps. Accordingly, in another room, where only “it shall not be seen” applies, closing one’s eyes certainly helps, just as with ervah. According to Bach and the others, however, it is indeed forbidden in another room in such a case, as with ervah. The Mechaber learned this from Rashi and Rambam regarding moving excrement. How could Abaye permit excrement passing before him when he sees it? It must be that he closes his eyes, and moving excrement is not regarded as part of his camp. The ruling is therefore sound. Understand this, and see what I wrote in Magen Avraham seif katan 1 in the name of Perishah and Levush.
The fourth: excrement in another room with the doorway open. One who studies the Rosh on Berachos 25a regarding a foul odor that has a source will see that he writes that an elevated place permits even when one sees the excrement, because “your camp shall be holy” is fulfilled. According to this, excrement before an open doorway is permitted, because an open doorway is considered as though closed, like excrement behind glass. Thus there is a distinction. Even if we assume that a place ten handbreadths high, though another domain, is forbidden when visible according to Rashba, an open doorway may nevertheless be treated as closed and like excrement behind glass. Although he sees it, it is permitted because it is covered. Scripture reveals that covering helps even according to Rashba, as we will explain, with Hashem’s help, regarding excrement behind glass. The same applies before an open doorway. We do not find that Rashba disagrees with this, only concerning a high or low place, where the concept of being as though closed does not apply. It may be that where there is a door, Rashba agrees that it is considered closed. The Mechaber in seif 2 follows his own view in his longer work, where he raised a difficulty from the case of a small courtyard adjoining a large one. See Peri Chadash, who answers that it concerns excrement behind him. Alternatively, it may be explained by closing one’s eyes, as Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 8. According to the Mechaber, this must be the explanation.
The fifth: excrement behind glass is permitted, because the Torah made the law dependent upon covering. Rif’s text reads that “your camp shall be holy” is fulfilled. See my novellae. Even according to Rashba, who holds that “it shall not be seen” refers to excrement, Scripture reveals that covering helps. As I wrote above, covering is not considered another domain, but rather like something buried in the ground, and so Rambam writes in Hilchos Krias Shema 3:9. Know that the glass mentioned in Berachos 25b is explained by Rashi as a glass partition separating between him and the excrement. Rambam likewise explains in halachah 10: “If there was a glass partition between him and the excrement, it is permitted.” The same applies to ervah behind glass. Accordingly, the excrement need not be covered on all sides. A glass partition separating between them is itself the covering. Even according to Bach, cited by Taz seif katan 3, who requires an elevated place to be ten handbreadths high and four by four handbreadths wide, such dimensions are not required for a partition. Whenever there is a separation between him and the excrement, it is permitted, and this is the covering. Therefore, if excrement is outside and he sees it through a glass window, it is permitted according to all opinions, because a glass partition separates between them. Even according to Rashba in seif 2, who forbids it in another domain when visible, it is permitted when there is a glass partition.
The sixth: excrement in water is not considered covered, even though water is considered a covering regarding exposed ervah, as stated in siman 74, seif 8, in Magen Avraham. Regarding excrement it is not a covering, as stated in siman 76, Magen Avraham seif katan 9, because the Torah says “you shall cover it,” and this is not called covering. This differs from exposed ervah. See what I wrote above on this matter.
The seventh: a foul odor that has a source is like the excrement itself and is forbidden by Torah law. Following Rav Chisda, one must distance four amos from the point where the odor ends. It is obvious that if he recited he must repeat, even were we to say that for a Rabbinic prohibition one does not repeat. See Peri Chadash at the end of seif 2. When the excrement is in another room, the Mechaber cites without qualification the first opinion that a partition does not help for a foul odor, and he rules likewise in seif 3, as Peri Chadash writes. Nevertheless, if the foul odor does not reach him, even though there is a foul odor in the room containing the excrement, he need not distance four amos from the place of the odor. This also appears from Beit Yosef and Perishah, based on what Rabbeinu Yonah wrote in the name of the French rabbis and that Rambam forbids it. According to Rambam, even in another domain he follows Rav Huna, who requires only that the foul odor not reach him. Since Rabbeinu Yonah wrote this, it follows that for us as well a partition at least helps in another domain, so that it is enough that the foul odor not reach him, as stated. We must say that this is considered a foul odor without a source. Excrement covered by a vessel is considered a foul odor without a source, as discussed by Magen Avraham here in seif katan 9, and the same applies when it is in another room. Accordingly, if the foul odor reaches him, the prohibition is only Rabbinic, like a foul odor without a source, which is permitted by Torah law. In seif 9, words of Torah are permitted when the odor has no source. Were it a Torah prohibition, words of Torah would certainly also be forbidden, as Taz writes in seif katan 1. Perishah writes that the Torah’s command “you shall cover it” implies that when there is no foul odor it is permitted by Torah law. This requires study, because it appears that the prohibition is Rabbinic, as I wrote. If so, the verse teaches that it is permitted by Torah law in every such case. The words of Levush in seifim 2 and 3 appear contradictory. In seif 2 he requires four amos from where the odor ends, while in seif 3 he writes that it is permitted if the odor does not reach him. This will be explained, with Hashem’s help.
The eighth: moving excrement that does not remain in one place. In siman 76, seif 6, the Shulchan Aruch rules that in front of him the required distance is as far as he can see, and behind him four amos. Rambam and Bach rule that even in front of him four amos suffice. If we were to say that the distance of eyesight in front of him is Rabbinic because beyond four amos it is not his camp, we could say that in a case of moving excrement the Sages did not decree and four amos suffice. The Mechaber, however, follows his own view in siman 75, seif 7, and here in seif 1, and necessarily holds that the distance of eyesight is a Torah requirement of “your camp shall be holy,” as Magen Avraham writes here in seif katan 1. He therefore properly rules in siman 76, seif 3, that in front of him the distance is as far as he can see. Bach follows his own view in siman 75, seif 7. Understand this, and see what I wrote above.
The ninth: urine is forbidden by Torah law only opposite the stream. Once it is merely dripping, and certainly once it is on the ground, the prohibition is Rabbinic. Nevertheless, one must distance from urine as far as he can see when it is in front of him, and four amos when it is behind him. All this applies when it does not emit a foul odor. If it emits a foul odor, it is forbidden by Torah law. A garbage heap, a carcass, and anything that emits a foul odor have the same law as excrement and are forbidden by Torah law. This is what I concluded in my novellae in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah. The same applies to a chamber pot for urine and a refuse vessel for excrement, which are likewise forbidden by Torah law. See below, siman 87, concerning this. See Rambam, Hilchos Krias Shema 3:8.
The tenth: a garbage heap that emits a foul odor, and anything that emits a foul odor, is forbidden by Torah law. The excrement of domestic and wild animals whose food consists of grain is forbidden. See Peri Chadash. With Hashem’s help, this will be explained at the end of the siman, and there is no need to elaborate further here.
