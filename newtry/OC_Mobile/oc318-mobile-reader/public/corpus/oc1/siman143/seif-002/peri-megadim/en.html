Defective and plene spellings. See Taz. Silent letters whose absence does not alter the pronunciation do not require another scroll. If an actual letter is missing in a way that affects the word, another scroll should be taken out, to accommodate the differing opinions. Taz cites Responsa Maharam Lublin 84, which rules that if no other scroll is available and they already began reading and found an error, they complete the required number of olim in that scroll, reciting blessings before and after. This implies that even initially one may continue once the reading began. Our practice follows Rama: if the reading has not begun, the defective scroll is not taken out even for Shacharis, unless an entire chumash is valid. If the reading began and no other scroll is available, they complete the required number of olim. The same may apply at Minchah, even though its reading was instituted because of idlers, for it remains an enactment just like Monday and Thursday. Although Taz implies that the particular crack he discusses was not visible and that an omitted word should not be treated so leniently, Maharam Lublin and Magen Avraham clearly rule this way. Only initially should such a scroll not be taken out.
Know. Whenever the poskim dispute whether a particular defect invalidates the scroll, another should not be taken out. There is a double doubt: perhaps the halachah follows Rambam’s responsum that one may read from an invalid scroll, upon which we rely after the fact, and perhaps the halachah follows the authority who holds this particular defect does not invalidate it. Although elsewhere I wrote that with doubtful blessings the severity of taking Hashem’s name in vain may prevent reliance on a double doubt, siman 489 regarding uncertainty whether one counted the Omer indicates that a double doubt may suffice even to recite a blessing. This will be clarified elsewhere.
If. If two letters are attached, even on Shabbos another scroll need not be taken out, because Rashba may be relied upon. Bach and Taz in siman 32 imply this even where the letters are joined in their middle. If wax dripped upon the text and covers it so thickly that the word cannot be read, see siman 340, Taz, and Eliyah Rabbah. One may perhaps rely upon the principle that anything fit for mixing is treated as mixed, as in conversion. Yet because oral reading is forbidden, one may read the word from a chumash rather than take out another scroll. If wax covers only one place, one may certainly rely on the lenient view. See Responsa Meil Tzedakah 25 and what I wrote in siman 141 concerning words written but not read.
It is written. Responsa Meil Tzedakah 29 rules that in Parashas Noach the correct text is “Vayehi kol yemei Noach,” while some read “Vayihyu.” The principal reading is Vayehi without an added vav, yet on Shabbos another scroll should not be taken out over the dispute. Whenever a doubt exists, another scroll is not taken out. I learned from his words that the rule that missing or added letters do not require another scroll applies only to silent internal letters whose absence does not alter pronunciation. Writing Vayihyu instead of Vayehi invalidates the text, even though the added vav is silent, because the written form alters the reading. See Magen Avraham concerning rachavah and rachavu. Regarding Esh Das, which is written as one word but read as two, if written on two lines it may be invalid, like words written but not read, where the variance invalidates the scroll.
Rambam writes. In Hilchos Sefer Torah 10:1 Rambam lists twenty matters that invalidate a Sefer Torah. A closed section written as open, or the reverse, invalidates it and requires another scroll. If the blank space was less than nine letters, see Shach, Yoreh Deah 275, who cites an opinion that three letters suffice after the fact. Where three letters were left, another scroll should not be taken out because the dispute creates a double doubt, as explained above. The same applies if a Divine Name extends beyond the margin or part of it was suspended, since some hold these valid. In every disputed case another scroll should not be taken out. Rambam in Hilchos Tefillin invalidates a partially suspended Divine Name, but in Hilchos Sefer Torah chapter 10 does not include it among the twenty defects. Perhaps only those twenty preclude public reading, while a partially suspended Name remains doubtful. In any event, one may read from it because of the double doubt.
If. If the top of a lamed enters another letter, or if the descending strokes of nun, tzadi, or pei enter letters on the line below, see siman 32, seif 28, Taz, and Magen Avraham. Since some validate such writing, another scroll certainly should not be taken out. If no other scroll is available, one may perhaps even read from it initially with blessings, because perhaps the halachah follows Rambam that one may read from an invalid scroll where no alternative exists, and perhaps the scroll is valid. Do not object from Bach in siman 32, who states that a scroll with joined letters should not initially be taken out. He may be discussing a case where a valid scroll exists, or a case in which we have already rejected Rashba, unlike a genuine unresolved dispute. If a letter lacked surrounding parchment from the outset, another scroll likewise need not be taken out, relying on Rashba.
In Yoreh Deah. Siman 274 requires care with the crowns of the letters shaatnez getz. Vocalization marks or cantillation signs invalidate a scroll. Magen Avraham above, siman 36, cites Bach as invalidating a scroll lacking these crowns. Rambam, however, validates it explicitly in Hilchos Sefer Torah 7:10. Some likewise validate vocalization and cantillation because Rambam did not list them among the twenty invalidating defects in chapter 10. Accordingly, for these defects not only need another scroll not be taken out; if no other scroll exists, one may perhaps read from it initially. This requires study.
Know. Yoreh Deah 281 lists persons disqualified from writing a Sefer Torah, and some disqualify one written by a mamzer. A scroll found in the possession of a non-Jew is doubtful. I initially thought these too formed a double doubt, but the cases differ. A scroll with a missing letter or textual error may resemble a chumash, from which one could read were it not for the congregation’s honor. A textual error does not create that concern, as explained by Peri Chadash from Rambam’s responsum. But where the entire scroll was written in a disqualifying manner, it lacks the status of a Sefer Torah altogether. Even after the fact, those who read from it did not fulfill their obligation and must begin again from a valid scroll if available. All agree to this. Although Rambam groups improperly processed parchment, a scroll written by an apostate, and a scroll with a missing letter among the same twenty defects and says one may not read from any of them, those categories need not be identical in all respects.
We already wrote. If an error occurred in Parashas Parah or Zachor after part of the passage was read, a valid scroll should be taken out and the reading repeated from the beginning, following Rosh in Yoreh Deah 279, since some hold these readings are Torah-level obligations. The discussion in Yoreh Deah that permits reliance on an invalid scroll is based on public reading being an enactment of the Prophets. Here the obligation may be Torah-level, so one repeats from the beginning without a blessing, since the blessing is not indispensable. Mordechai also implies that even for the final aliyah and maftir, where one may stop, another scroll is taken out. Eliyah Rabbah noticed this as well. See Levush in Yoreh Deah 279 and here, who explains the leniency by public reading being an enactment of the Prophets.
The remaining matters. They will, with Hashem’s help, be explained in Magen Avraham, letters 7, 8, and 9. There is no need to elaborate here.
One further point. Eliyah Rabbah writes that if an entire word is duplicated, another scroll is taken out, because anything added is considered as though something were missing. This requires study. Perhaps the principle of anything fit for mixing may apply, as with joined letters. The phrase “anything added is as though missing” is a received rule in the laws of treifah and need not apply here. This requires study.
The ordinary writer says. I have recorded everything I heard from my teachers and some matters that Heaven enabled me to understand. The laws of Hashem concerning the Sefer Torah are vast and broader than the sea, while the corruption of the times is great. Every God-fearing person should be exceedingly careful, especially scribes of sacred texts. See how Tzemach Tzedek, after issuing a ruling in siman 119, still feared that he had not reached the correct halachah, although he was the leading authority of his generation. What can people like us answer today? Therefore, every person should fear for his soul before issuing a ruling or the like.
