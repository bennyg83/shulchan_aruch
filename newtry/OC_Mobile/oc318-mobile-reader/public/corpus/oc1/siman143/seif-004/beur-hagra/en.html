Seif 4: If an error is found, etc. Rambam wrote in a responsum that one may read from a Sefer Torah in which an error is found. Ran likewise wrote this in chapter 2 of Megillah in the name of Tosafos, as indicated by the statement there that if the scribe omitted letters or verses, etc. There is also proof from Gittin 60a, which speaks specifically of a Sefer Torah missing a sheet. Ramban answered that this applies only at the end, whereas in the middle even a minor error invalidates it. Rashba objected that the opposite should be true: at its beginning or end it is more readily invalidated because it appears deficient, unlike in the middle where the deficiency is not evident. Proof may be brought from Megillah, where even an omission approaching half the text does not invalidate it, whereas at its beginning the Gemara states, even according to the view that begins from Ish Yehudi, etc., as explained below in siman 690, seif 3. Nevertheless, all the other poskim disagree and hold that even a minor error invalidates it for public reading. They prove this from the Mishnah that distinguishes between scrolls of Scripture and tefillin or mezuzos. Regarding tefillin and mezuzos, Menachos teaches that even one missing letter invalidates them, and clarifies that this includes even the crown of a yud. There too, if the leg of the hei in haam or the leg of a vav was severed, it was invalid. All the more so is an actual textual error invalid. Moreover, if a Sefer Torah remained valid, the Gemara would not have asked regarding tefillin, “Is this not obvious?” See what I wrote below in siman 142. Maseches Soferim, chapter 2, states that if the Divine Name is divided, one may not read from it; chapter 3 similarly states that if a letter was scraped away or hair was poured upon it, one may not read from it. Only a Megillah is treated leniently. Furthermore, a Baraisa teaches that a Sefer Torah containing three errors on each column, etc., has a particular law, whereas in Megillah scattered errors throughout the text do not invalidate it. The authorities cited above understand that even the crown of a yud invalidates a Sefer Torah with respect to fulfilling the mitzvah to write one, just as with tefillin and mezuzos, whose mitzvah is not their reading. Public reading, however, is only Rabbinic, and perhaps a defect does not invalidate it, just as with Megillah. See Rashba in Gittin and Ran in chapter 2 of Megillah. They interpret the passage in Maseches Soferim as referring to a case where most of the Sefer Torah is written that way. The passage in Gittin also supports this. Rashba, however, explains that the reference to a missing sheet is not precise, but contrasts it with the absence of four complete chumashim and uses the more extreme formulation because the Talmud often juxtaposes related cases. Rosh answered that the missing sheet had been written but was not sewn into the scroll, as codified in Tur and Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah, end of siman 278. Rabbi Yosef Beirav therefore ruled, as recorded in the Shulchan Aruch, that after the fact one may rely on Rambam’s responsum and Tosafos, but initially another scroll should be taken out and the reading continued from that point, following the other poskim. See Tosafos, Megillah 9a, s.v. bishlama. Their words are strained, since the Mishnah says that it does not render the hands impure, and the Gemara asks regarding Torah. Rav Ashi answers that the Baraisa refers to the other books, implying that initially the Gemara understood it to refer even to Torah. One must therefore explain that the issue was Yegar Sahadusa. The question of Tosafos may instead be answered differently: public reading is more lenient, and even a defective scroll may fulfill the reading, as Rambam writes, unlike the law of rendering the hands impure. A proof is that Megillah certainly remains valid if those words are omitted. This supports the lenient authorities regarding reading from a defective Sefer Torah. Another proof that reading is lenient is that a foreign-language speaker may read from a translation written in that language, whereas a translated scroll does not render the hands impure. Yet the Yerushalmi in chapter 2 of Megillah, which criticizes one who read “Jews” incorrectly, appears at first glance to show that such an error invalidates the reading elsewhere. It may be answered that Torah reading is no stricter than Megillah except in the respect stated at the beginning of chapter 3 of Megillah. Still, the passage in Maseches Soferim is decisive. See below, siman 142, seif 1, where I reject Ran’s position. The primary halachah is that one should not distinguish between reading from a Sefer Torah and its underlying validity. Shabbos 103b states that if the Divine Names were written in gold, etc. The commentators write that the Gemara mentions Divine Names only because that is the ordinary case, even though they are but a tiny part of the scroll. It also says there, if one wrote the Song, etc., even though it is less than a complete sheet. This is all the more so according to those who disagree with Orchos Chaim and hold that in a Megillah even a single topic may invalidate it. See Beit Yosef, Yoreh Deah 279, where Rabbi Yosef Beirav rules in accordance with Tosafos and Rambam cited above and therefore holds that what was already read from the defective scroll combines with the remainder read from a valid scroll. According to the other poskim, who invalidate it, one must return to the beginning with a valid scroll. Mordechai rules that the erroneous word should be read by heart and that the aliyah should be completed from the defective scroll. There is no prohibition of reciting written Torah orally when no alternative exists, as with Megillah, and as in Yoma chapter 7, where the Kohen Gadol reads orally because there is no alternative, due either to the discredit of the first scroll or to an unnecessary blessing. Here too, although there is no concern for discredit because the scroll is indeed defective, the concern for an unnecessary blessing remains. The two reasons are complementary, not contradictory. Mordechai further writes that this applies only where one cannot stop, such as before three verses have been read, within two verses of the end of a section, or for the fourth aliyah on Rosh Chodesh and Chol HaMoed where one must finish because one may not add aliyos. Where one may stop, the reader stops and another scroll is taken out, as in Yoma, where the Gemara asks why another scroll should not be brought. There is no concern for the first scroll’s discredit, and with two different readers there is no unnecessary blessing. This is the meaning of the gloss, “And if, etc.” The first part of Mordechai was not cited because Rama follows the Shulchan Aruch’s approach. Nevertheless, all the later authorities agreed with Mordechai, and his position cannot be refuted.
But is it not, etc. As stated in Kiddushin 30a: “Let them bring a Sefer Torah,” etc.
And in a pressing circumstance, etc. This is the dispute described above.
And if, etc. Rashba writes this in Gittin there, interpreting “it is deficient in its matter” to mean that the deficiency is in the particular chumash from which they are reading. This differs from the present case, where the other chumashim do not invalidate the one being read except because of the honor of the congregation, lest they appear impoverished. Rashi does not explain it this way; see Rashi there.
