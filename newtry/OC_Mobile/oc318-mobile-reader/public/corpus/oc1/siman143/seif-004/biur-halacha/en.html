If an error is found in a Sefer Torah, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, where we set out the three approaches: the view of the Shulchan Aruch, which is that of Mahari Beirav; the view of Mordechai; and Rama's compromise between them. See Magen Avraham, who inclines to rule like Bach, who adopted Mordechai's view. His reasoning is that when another Sefer Torah is taken out, the reader will have to recite a new blessing over it, and when he completes the reading there there may be a concern of an unnecessary blessing. Perhaps the halachah is that one fulfills the Torah-reading obligation even from an invalid Sefer Torah, in which case the first blessing was valid and the second blessing over the same verses is unnecessary. Yet this view is also very difficult. Mordechai himself holds that one fulfills the obligation from an invalid Sefer Torah, as is evident from his words, for the only issue he raises is that written passages may not be recited orally, not that the scroll is invalid. He therefore permits completing the reading there. We, however, are stringent not to read from an invalid Sefer Torah initially even where no valid one is available, because most poskim hold that the obligation is not fulfilled from it. How, then, can we permit reciting the concluding blessing initially, which according to them is certainly a blessing in vain, merely to avoid causing a possibly unnecessary blessing afterward? I found that this question was already raised by the gaon Rabbi Zalman Margolios in his Pischei Shearim. The Gra's view in his commentary also accords with Mordechai insofar as, when another Sefer Torah is taken out, a new blessing must be recited over it. His proof is from the passage in the chapter Ba Lo, cited by Mordechai, and he writes that this proof cannot be refuted. However, regarding Mordechai's principal ruling that one should complete the reading in the invalid scroll and then recite the concluding blessing, the Gra certainly does not agree. He holds that the primary halachah follows the poskim who maintain that one does not fulfill the obligation even after the fact from an invalid Sefer Torah and must return to the beginning in a valid scroll. Accordingly, when he blesses over the valid scroll, there is no unnecessary blessing, because the first blessing is treated as though it never existed, as Rosh wrote in a responsum. Although in earlier generations the custom in several places was to return to the beginning, as Tashbetz records and as the Gra rules, now that the works of the Shulchan Aruch have spread, the later authorities imply that the custom everywhere has become like the ruling of Mahari Beirav, that one need not return to the beginning of the sidrah. After the fact, regarding what has already been read, we rely on the minority of poskim who are lenient. If, however, he is still in the middle of the reading when the error is discovered, there are differing opinions and differing local customs. In some places the custom follows only the Shulchan Aruch and Rama's compromise, as Shach writes in Yoreh Deah, siman 279. In other places the custom follows Mordechai, as cited by Magen Avraham. In truth, where a custom has become established, it is very difficult to change it, for each side has authorities upon whom to rely, and we may apply to them the verse, “Your people are all righteous,” as Shaarei Ephraim writes. Where no fixed custom exists, Magen Avraham appears to rule like Mordechai, and Chayei Adam and Derech HaChayim copied this. Chayei Adam's words are very difficult. In klal 31 he writes that if an error is found in a Sefer Torah, the later authorities, citing Magen Avraham, siman 143, and the Gra's commentary, ruled that where there is no fixed custom, if an error is found in the middle of the reading, even before three verses have been read, the erroneous word is read orally, three verses are completed, and the concluding blessing is recited, and he proceeds to copy all of Magen Avraham's words. His citation of the Gra implies that he adopted this approach because both Magen Avraham and the Gra hold it. In fact, the Gra does not hold that the concluding blessing is recited, for according to him that would be a blessing in vain. When he writes that Mordechai's proof cannot be refuted, he means only that a new initial blessing must be recited when another Sefer Torah is taken out, as I wrote above. Many other later authorities, however, all adopt the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch and Rama as practical halachah: Levush in Yoreh Deah 279, Shach there, Taz, who fundamentally agrees with the Shulchan Aruch, Eliyah Rabbah in the name of several poskim, Nehar Shalom, Rabbi Yaakov Emden, and Magen Giborim. It is therefore very proper initially to follow the Shulchan Aruch and Rama where no contrary fixed custom exists. See Nehar Shalom, who discusses this at length. See also Shaarei Ephraim, who discusses it at length in Pischei Shearim and likewise disagrees with Magen Avraham. He further writes that where the custom is that, if a definite error is found in the middle of the reading, they do not continue and do not recite the concluding blessing over the invalid scroll even if three verses were already read, but rather roll it closed without a concluding blessing and take out another scroll and recite the initial blessing over it, they have authorities upon whom to rely. In truth, this is the view of most Rishonim and of the Gra, except that according to them one should return to the beginning of the sidrah. Nevertheless, it appears that one need not begin from the beginning of the sidrah. Rather, he should begin from the beginning of the reading assigned to the aliyah during which the error was found. Since he must in any event recite a new blessing, it is preferable that he also read those verses from the valid scroll with the blessing. Even so, it is not sufficient merely to reread the verses already read from the invalid scroll. He must add to them and read at least three further verses. End of his words.

In truth, Ran, who cites this ruling in the name of “our teacher,” namely Rashba, as Eliyah Rabbah explains, did not decide it as practical halachah, but only as a theoretical ruling, as he states explicitly. One who examines Rashba's responsa, siman 611, will see that he wrote this only according to one answer. See there. See also Beit Meir, who likewise questions this ruling, and Shaarei Ephraim, who writes that the custom is not to be lenient. See his reasoning in Pischei Shearim. Nevertheless, it appears that one should not protest against a person who follows the lenient view.
