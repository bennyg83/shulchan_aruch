Seif katan 4: If an error is found, etc. One word, etc. Although the Sages said that written Torah may not be recited orally, the prohibition applies specifically to a complete matter or an independent topic. One or two words that have no meaning without being joined to what precedes or follows are not considered a separate written matter and may be recited orally. Maadanei Melech writes likewise.
Since Rambam permits, etc. Rambam wrote in a responsum cited by Abudraham: The blessing is not upon reading from the physical scroll in the manner of the blessing over taking a lulav or sitting in a sukkah, where if the object is invalid no mitzvah was performed. The mitzvah of Torah reading is the reading itself, whether from a valid or invalid scroll. A proof is the statement that one may not read publicly from chumashim because of the congregation’s honor. Is there any greater invalidity than a chumash? Even a Sefer Torah missing one letter is invalid, all the more so a chumash. The Gemara should have said that it is invalid and the blessing would be in vain. All the communities of the West relied on this precision and read from parchment scrolls that had not been processed properly, reciting blessings before and after in the presence of leading geonim. Nevertheless, every congregation should possess a fully valid Sefer Torah and initially read from it publicly. Rashba disagreed and wrote that Rambam himself later retracted in Mishneh Torah, where he lists twenty conditions that invalidate a Sefer Torah and states that one may not read from it publicly, including the omission or addition of even one letter. Rabbi Yosef Beirav wrote that it is difficult to say Rambam retracted, for then all the great geonim whom Rambam says followed this practice would have been mistaken. Rather, Mishneh Torah addresses fulfillment of the mitzvah incumbent upon every Jew to write a Sefer Torah. With a scroll containing even one of those defects, one has not fulfilled the mitzvah. Sefer Bedek HaBayis explains that since one has not fulfilled that mitzvah, it is not proper to read from it publicly. Kesef Mishneh notes that Rambam wrote, “One may not read from it publicly,” rather than, “If they read from it, they did not fulfill their obligation.” Thus Mishneh Torah addresses a case where a valid scroll is available, and initially one may not use the defective scroll. The responsum concerns a case where no valid scroll is available. Therefore, even where a valid scroll exists, if they already read from the defective one they have fulfilled their obligation, as though no alternative existed. After the fact we therefore rely on Rambam.
His words require study, etc. Yoma chapter 7 explicitly states that on Yom Kippur the Kohen Gadol reads from the Sefer Torah the section Acharei Mos and then rolls it to Ach BeAsor in Emor. Since both concern one subject and the sections are close enough that the scroll can be rolled while the translator renders the final verse of Acharei, there is no affront to the congregation through waiting. He then reads by heart the passage UveAsor in Pinchas, because it is too distant to roll there within the translator’s pause. The Gemara asks why another scroll should not be brought, already rolled to UveAsor. Rav Huna son of Yehudah answers that this would cast discredit upon the first scroll, and Reish Lakish answers that it would require an unnecessary blessing. The two answers are complementary. This proves that even after reading from a valid scroll, if another is brought one must bless again. According to Rabbi Yosef Beirav, although we rely on Rambam that the reading from the invalid scroll counted, it should be no better than reading from a valid scroll, and taking out another would require a new blessing.
And so is implied in siman 140. See Magen Avraham there, seif katan 4, concerning the incident involving Rabbi Gershon, who ruled that even if the scroll upon which the blessing was recited was not rolled to the correct place and another scroll was opened, there is room to permit it when both had been taken out together for the two readings, since both were before him and his attention covered both. This implies that only because both had been taken out together was a new blessing unnecessary; if another were now brought, a blessing would be required. See also the end of siman 215 that all agree one may not cause an unnecessary blessing. Why then bring another scroll and bless again? One should instead follow Bach and Mordechai, read the erroneous word orally, and take out another scroll between aliyos.
And in Maseches Soferim, chapter 11, etc. It implies, etc. Why is Maharil’s reason needed, that one or two words may be read orally? If an entire subject or independent passage were missing in the middle of the reading, reading it orally should be forbidden. Yet the Kohen Gadol read an entire parashah orally on Yom Kippur, and the reasons given there apply equally when an error is found during a reading. Maseches Soferim chapter 11 therefore teaches that one may not derive the ordinary law of oral Torah reading from the exceptional case of the Kohen Gadol on Yom Kippur.
Therefore they should read, etc. This returns to what was stated above, that one should follow Bach.
If he began only one word, etc., because in that case the change of practice is not noticeable. So it appears.
Rama wrote in Yoreh Deah, etc. If the Sefer Torah became invalid, etc., for example, if it was torn. One should not say that Rama mentioned a tear rather than an error because when maftir rereads what the seventh aliyah already read, the error would necessarily have been noticed earlier. Experience shows that a reader may overlook an error the first time and notice it upon the second reading. Rather, Rashbatz follows Rashba that when an error is found during the reading, all previous readers are retroactively regarded as having read from an invalid scroll and must repeat from the beginning. Rama therefore speaks of a tear that occurred after seven had read, so the earlier readers read from a valid scroll and fulfilled their obligation.
Rather, because of the honor of the Torah, etc. Megillah 23a explains that the purpose is that the honor of Torah and the honor of the Prophets should not appear equal. Since both involve reading and blessings before and after, maftir first reads from the Torah, thereby demonstrating that Torah reading is primary. Therefore, once the obligatory reading has been completed, reading maftir from the invalid scroll may suffice. When two scrolls are taken out, however, maftir’s Torah reading is an independent obligation, instituted by the Savoraim or Geonim, as Tosafos and Rosh write at the end of Megillah. One should therefore be stringent if an error is found. It follows simply, etc. Rama’s careful wording, “in the second Sefer Torah,” indicates that the ordinary maftir case differs because of Rashbatz’s uncertainty. Yet Rashbatz follows Rashba, who holds that even after the fact reading from an invalid scroll does not count. We, however, rely after the fact on Rambam, and remove another scroll only because of the practice following Rabbi Yosef Beirav. For ordinary maftir, where even Rashbatz is uncertain whether the invalid reading counts, one may certainly rely on Rambam and those who agree with him not to take out another scroll, without concern for changing established practice.
Even if the error was found in the seventh aliyah, etc. Rashbatz was not uncertain in this case. Nevertheless, since we rely after the fact on Rambam, the seventh aliyah fulfills his obligation through what he read from the invalid scroll, and the error should be read orally according to Mordechai.
See siman 282, seif 5, that maftir may count toward the seven. Magen Avraham there concludes that in such a case Kaddish is recited after the Haftarah.
So as not to enter, etc. If the error is found during an earlier aliyah, another scroll must in any event be taken out for the remaining olim, and the practice follows Rabbi Yosef Beirav to take it out during the aliyah, without concern for the dispute over the blessing. If the error is found during the seventh aliyah and only maftir remains, it is preferable for the seventh reader himself to serve as maftir and not take out another scroll, thereby avoiding the dispute concerning a new blessing. Although this explains only why another scroll should not be taken out during the seventh aliyah, one might still take out another for maftir after the seventh reader’s concluding blessing, as Taz rules. Magen Avraham must therefore hold that this would burden the congregation, in accordance with Rav Huna’s answer. The congregation should not be burdened merely for maftir, which is an addition, when the seventh reader can serve as maftir. Had it been possible to take out another scroll during the seventh aliyah, burdening the congregation would not prevent correcting the seventh aliyah with a valid scroll.
And Knesses HaGedolah, etc. He inferred, etc., from the statement that for maftir it suffices to read three verses from the invalid scroll. Knesses HaGedolah understands the reason to be that the day’s obligation was already fulfilled, so the order need not be completed again.
Even if he omitted one verse. Below, siman 282, Magen Avraham seif katan 18 writes that the same applies even if he omitted one word.
Unlike maftir, etc. Rashbatz’s ruling that maftir need read only three verses from the invalid scroll is because maftir repeats material already read, so the entire order was completed with the seventh aliyah. Even so, I do not know what would be lost by reading with maftir until the end of the order. Knesses HaGedolah is not available to me. Perhaps because Rashbatz follows Rashba that reading from an invalid scroll does not count, and since maftir needs only three verses, he did not wish to burden the congregation with additional reading from an invalid scroll that has no halachic effect according to Rashba.
And in Taz, etc. During the recitation of Kaddish, etc. Taz holds that the concern is not an unnecessary blessing but burdening the congregation, following Rav Huna. Therefore, if an error is found during an earlier aliyah, another scroll should be taken out immediately, since it must in any event be taken out for the remaining olim. If the error is found during the seventh aliyah, another scroll should not be taken out during that aliyah because of the congregation’s burden. For maftir, however, another scroll may be taken out while Kaddish is being recited, since no additional burden results. If the error is found during maftir itself, Taz agrees with Magen Avraham.
