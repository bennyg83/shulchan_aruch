And if. See Taz. It appears here that he understands there to be three opinions. The first is that of Rabbi Yosef Beirav, teacher of Beit Yosef: whenever an error is found after the reader has completed the reading and recited the concluding blessing, the reading from the invalid Sefer Torah counts after the fact. One then completes the required number of seven on Shabbos, five on Yom Tov, six on Yom Kippur, four on Rosh Chodesh and Chol HaMoed, and three on weekdays, using a valid scroll. There is no need to begin the seven anew. This relies after the fact on Rambam’s responsum that reading from an invalid scroll counts. The statement that one missing letter means the mitzvah of writing a Sefer Torah was not fulfilled concerns the mitzvah of writing, not public reading, which is an enactment of the Prophets. According to this, regarding Parashas Parah and Zachor, which some hold are Torah-level obligations, it requires study whether reading from an invalid scroll counts even after the entire passage was read and the error was discovered in that column. See siman 146, seif 2, and Magen Avraham, siman 688. All this applies when the concluding blessing was already recited over the invalid scroll. If the error was found during the reading, even after much was read, one should not initially recite the concluding blessing over the invalid scroll. Rather, another should be taken out, three verses should be read from it, and the concluding blessing recited there. Certainly, if fewer than three verses were read, the reading continues from the second scroll, with a concluding blessing but no new opening blessing. Mordechai holds that even if fewer than three verses were read, one recites the erroneous word orally where no alternative exists, completes three verses, blesses, and then takes out a valid scroll. Rama compromises: if fewer than three verses were read, one continues in the valid scroll, ideally reading three verses or completing the section, and recites only the concluding blessing. If three verses were already read, he follows Mordechai and recites the concluding blessing over the invalid scroll. Where one cannot stop because only two verses remain before the end of a section, he reads the error orally. It would therefore appear that Rama should have introduced this as an alternative opinion. Levush here indeed writes, “Some say that if he read three verses he blesses over the invalid scroll.” In Yoreh Deah 279, however, Levush does not write “some say,” implying that his primary view is that whenever the error is found during the aliyah, even after much was read, the reader completes the aliyah in the valid scroll and blesses there, unlike his teacher here. Yet careful study of Beit Yosef here and in Yoreh Deah suggests that Rabbi Yosef Beirav may agree that if three verses were read before the error, one recites the concluding blessing over the invalid scroll, as implied by Shach and Taz there. If so, there are only two opinions, and Rama completely follows Rabbi Yosef Beirav. It is also possible that Rama follows Rabbi Yosef Beirav entirely and never permits reading the error orally, unlike Mordechai. If so, where three verses have been read and one cannot stop because only two verses remain before the section, another scroll should be taken out and the reading completed there. Yet this cannot be correct, for the Kohen Gadol on Yom Kippur reads orally, and the final reader may likewise do so because of burdening the congregation. Regarding maftir, another scroll may be taken out because Kaddish intervenes. The practical law will be clarified in Magen Avraham. According to Taz, if the error is found in the final aliyah, even one remaining verse may be completed in the invalid scroll.
