[1] Free men, etc. This excludes full slaves. Regarding one who is half slave and half free, there is room for doubt. Olas Tamid writes that since the halachah is that we compel his master to free him, he is obligated, as is also implied at the beginning of Chagigah. This is difficult, because below, siman 199, regarding zimun, Olas Tamid writes: “Since the text says slaves and does not say free men, it implies that one who is half slave may be included in a zimun.” Here, however, where the text specifically says free men, this excludes one who is half slave. Nor is there any proof from Chagigah. There, the reason is that the mitzvah applies to one who has only one master. Since we compel his master to free him, it is as though he has only one master, and he is obligated in appearing at the Temple, as Tosafos write there, 4a, s.v. lo. This is especially so according to what I wrote in my novellae to Rashba, Gittin 66a, that the Torah did not exempt a slave because he has another master, but because his master prevents him on account of the loss of his labor. Since we compel the master to free him and the master no longer has rights to his labor, he is obligated in appearing. See there. Regarding other mitzvos, however, his half-slave status remains until his master gives him a bill of emancipation. The same law applies to shofar in siman 589 and to Megillah in siman 689: he cannot discharge even his own obligation.
[2] They complete, etc. Shibolei HaLeket, page 3, states: “If the reading of the haftarah or the Torah began with ten, they complete it.” This implies that if Torah reading began, they complete it but do not begin the haftarah, unlike the implication of Magen Avraham. At the beginning of siman 128 I brought proofs for this and explained these laws there. All this applies when the blessings before and after are recited. Without blessings, Kesef Mishneh, chapter 8 of Hilchos Tefillah, writes that one may read with fewer than ten.
