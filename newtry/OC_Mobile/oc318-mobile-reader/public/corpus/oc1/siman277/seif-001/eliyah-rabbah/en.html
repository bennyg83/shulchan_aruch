Opposite it, etc. This is the Tur's wording. Rama writes "in his ordinary manner" instead of "opposite it." Nachalat Tzvi wonders that even if one opens gently the wind still enters, while Magen Avraham says Rama contains a scribal error. This appears to be the Levush's view. In my humble opinion Rama can be defended from Shabbos 120, which says one opens and closes the door in the ordinary manner, clearly teaching that even ordinary opening is permitted. Rav's objection there likewise appears directed specifically against doing it in the ordinary manner. Rambam and Mordechai also clearly understand the Gemara this way, though they do not explain the wind. Agudah and Rabbenu Yerucham, netiv 12 part 2, explicitly support Rama. The main difficulty is the question of Tosafot and Mordechai on Rashi, who says the concern is that wind may extinguish: behind a door there is no wind, and the Mishnah should have said "opposite the door" rather than "behind it." Bach answers that "behind" means immediately adjacent to the door, but then the same explanation should be used regarding a bonfire. In my humble opinion, the matter is resolved differently. There is ordinarily no outside wind there; Rashi means the gust created by opening the door itself, since the door acts like a fan. I later found this in Rivash siman 394. Thus specifically ordinary opening creates the gust, while gentle opening does not. According to this, even where no street wind is feared one should not open a door or window in the ordinary manner near the lamp. Where outside wind itself is a concern, even gentle opening is prohibited.
[Levush] It is permitted to close, etc. Agudah there implies that closing is prohibited. It appears to me this can be explained like Terumat HaDeshen siman 59: air blowing through an extremely small opening acts like a bellows and strengthens the flame. This also resolves Tosafot's question there. The Gemara does not say "if it burns, let it burn" because extinguishing is much closer to an inevitable result. Maharshal apparently overlooked this. This also differs from Magen Avraham at the end of siman 259. See siman 315 seif katan 3. Nevertheless, the other poskim clearly permit closing.
[Levush] And the same law, etc., that it is prohibited, etc. This prohibition itself is obvious. Rama seems to have written the parallel statement regarding the rule that one may close the window, and Mordechai and Tashbetz state this explicitly.
[Levush] And to lock, etc. The case is where the door is arranged so that locking it knocks and shakes the wall on which the lamp is fixed, making extinguishing likely. This resolves the questions of the later authorities.
