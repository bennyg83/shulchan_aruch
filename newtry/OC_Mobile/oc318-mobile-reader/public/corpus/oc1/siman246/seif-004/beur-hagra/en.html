Seif 4: Some permit. This follows Yevamot 66b: 'Can you maintain that? Granted, etc.; this case is not comparable, but rather,' etc. It follows that in such a case the property is considered his.
And some say that since. This follows Rav Yehudah there, whose view is accepted as halachah, for he says that even in such a case it is not considered his. A supporting baraita is cited there for Rav Yehudah: 'A woman who brings property into the marriage,' etc. Thus, anything one cannot sell is not considered his. The dispute between these two opinions depends upon the Rif's uncertainty there. This also resolves the difficulty in the Rambam, who rules there like Rav Yehudah, yet also rules in accordance with the baraita supporting Rabbi Ami concerning slaves of melog property, etc. The matter is as follows. The Rif was uncertain regarding the baraita about a woman who brings property into the marriage, writing, 'It is not clear to us,' etc. The Gemara there states: Rava said to Rav Nachman, 'But there is a supporting baraita for Rabbi Ami,' etc. At first glance this is difficult: is there not also a supporting baraita for Rav Yehudah? The passage may be resolved in two ways. First, the case is not comparable to Rav Yehudah's, because although he cannot sell the property, it is nevertheless his, as stated there: 'This case is not comparable, but rather,' etc. Second, that baraita represents an individual opinion, because Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel follows his own view in Gittin 41a, and the halachah there does not follow him. The Gemara answers that although a baraita supports Rabbi Ami and the law of the baraita concerning slaves of melog property is correct, nevertheless in the present case Rav Yehudah's reasoning is more persuasive. According to both explanations, the Rambam properly rules in accordance with the baraita concerning slaves of melog property. The practical difference between the explanations concerns whether one should rule in accordance with the baraita about property brought into marriage. According to the first explanation, the Gemara may hold that this baraita is not an individual opinion, as the Baal HaMaor explains at length at the beginning of the chapter Almanah, s.v. 'They sold,' that the Sages do not disagree with him on this point; see there. This is the Rif's statement, 'It is not clear to us,' etc. Here too, the dispute depends on these two explanations. According to the first, the Gemara does not accept the supporting baraita for Rav Yehudah, because even though he cannot sell it, it is nevertheless his. According to the second, that baraita is rejected only because it is an individual opinion, but its underlying rule is correct: anything one cannot sell is not considered his. The Geonim explain the baraita about a woman bringing property into marriage as referring to movable property and as connected to the dispute where she says, 'My utensil,' etc.; likewise the baraita about one who rents a cow, and the discussion there, etc. This resolves the Rambam's ruling, codified in Even HaEzer 90:14, that a husband may not, etc., but if he sold, etc. Since it is unclear whether that baraita should be followed in practice, as the Rif writes, he rules that initially the husband should not sell, but if he sold, what he did is effective.
