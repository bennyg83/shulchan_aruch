Labor on Shabbos. This is forbidden by Torah law, for we rule that rental does not transfer ownership. This applies specifically to labor; however, one need not object if a non-Jew rides a Jew's animal, since a living being carries itself, as stated in siman 305, seif 22; see there. If a Jew rented an animal from a non-Jew, he is commanded that it rest, since rental is treated as acquisition as a stringency. If he then rented it back to a non-Jew, it is permitted in any event. Magen Avraham.

He should declare it ownerless. By Torah law, declaring property ownerless privately is effective. Nevertheless, initially one may not rent it with the intention of declaring it ownerless on Shabbos. Likewise, a sale is effective as a leniency only when he sells it for all the Shabbos days, not for Shabbos alone. See Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 35. The Bach permits initially declaring it ownerless before three people. The Taz writes that even declaring it ownerless before three does not provide a clearly established initial permission; rather, one should use the permitted methods mentioned in seif 5.

The Taz writes: I heard in the name of a certain rabbi who relied on this to permit lending at interest against collateral by structuring it as a sale, even though it is not an actual sale, such as where he is obligated to return it. Nevertheless, he permitted it because the sale was made only to remove the prohibition of interest, just as we say here regarding Shabbos. I say that Heaven forbid one should do this between Jews. Whenever there is no genuine sale, the transaction retains the status of a loan and is forbidden. Here, by contrast, there is a valid declaration of ownerlessness, albeit for a limited time, namely Shabbos, and during Shabbos itself it is entirely ownerless. This is unlike the collateral described above, where the object itself must truly be sold so that the purchaser may do with it whatever he wishes, which is not the case there. See there.
