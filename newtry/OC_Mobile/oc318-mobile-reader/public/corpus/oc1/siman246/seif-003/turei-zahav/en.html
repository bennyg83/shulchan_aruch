It is prohibited to rent, etc. This means that it is prohibited even if the Israelite intends to rely upon declaring the animal ownerless afterward, even before three people. This is evident from the Beit Yosef's conclusion, that there is no clearly established initial permission through this device; rather, permission exists only through the methods stated in seif 5. The Rokeach, siman 300, writes: “If an Israelite's animal is in the possession of a non-Jew, and the non-Jew performs labor with it on Shabbat, since he is not receiving rental payment it is permitted; and if the Israelite sees him, he must protest.” This requires explanation: to what does “it is permitted” refer, when no rental arrangement is involved? Clearly, he means that the Israelite may place his animal in the non-Jew's possession and need not fear that the non-Jew will perform labor with it, since the non-Jew is not being paid. We must still explain that this does not concern lending or renting, for those are prohibited even from Sunday, since the Israelite is commanded regarding his animal. Rather, it concerns giving the animal to a non-Jewish shepherd. In that case, one need not fear that the shepherd will use it for labor. The reason appears to be that the non-Jew is afraid to do so, because under their law he would become liable. My father-in-law likewise explains it and brings proof from what the Beit Yosef cites in siman 305 from Shibbolei HaLeket in the name of Rav Hai Gaon, who states the same rule. Rama's statement that the non-Jew is not trusted regarding this follows Semag, p. 19: “We have already explained in Issur VeHeter that one may not rely at all upon a non-Jew's word, neither to prohibit nor to permit.” That applies when the animal was lent or rented to him, since it came lawfully into his possession and he would not be treated as a thief, so he is not afraid. But when it was entrusted to him for grazing, he is certainly afraid to make unauthorized use of it. We find that this reasoning, that the non-Jew is afraid where he would be caught as a thief, is relied upon regarding wine of non-Jews in the chapter HaSocher of Avodah Zarah. This appears correct to me. A stipulation that the animal rest, however, does not help; see siman 490, seif 5, and what I wrote there.
Nevertheless, no person can acquire it. The Beit Yosef writes: “Therefore, he must specify that he declares it ownerless only for Shabbat. Since on Shabbat it is under the non-Jew's control, having been rented or lent to him, no other person can acquire it. When Shabbat departs, it is as though the original owner reacquires it, because he declared it ownerless only for Shabbat. Although he declares it ownerless only for Shabbat and not permanently, it is valid ownerlessness, as stated at the end of the chapter Ein Bein HaMudar.” I have copied this because I heard in the name of a certain rabbi who relied upon it to permit lending money at interest against collateral by means of a purported sale. Although it is not a genuine sale, such as where the lender remains obligated to return the collateral, he nevertheless permitted it on the ground that the sale was made only to remove the prohibition of interest, just as we do here regarding Shabbat. Heaven forbid that one should act this way with an Israelite. Whenever there is no genuine sale, the transaction retains the status of a loan and interest is prohibited. Here, by contrast, there is a valid declaration of ownerlessness, merely limited in time to Shabbat. During Shabbat itself the animal is completely ownerless, and afterward it returns to its original owner. This is unlike the collateral arrangement, where the object itself must truly be sold so that the buyer may do with it whatever he wishes, which is not the case there. That arrangement resembles the incident of Beit Choron in Nedarim, in the chapter Ein Bein HaMudar, where the Sages said that any gift which the recipient cannot consecrate is not a gift. In this purported sale, the buyer cannot consecrate the object; therefore, it is not a sale. It is also comparable to the ruling in Yoreh Deah, siman 173, concerning one prohibited by a vow from benefiting from another, where the property is given to third parties so that the prohibited person may eat with them; see there. Likewise, seif 4 here states that since the non-Jew cannot sell the animal if he wishes, it remains called an Israelite's animal. Regarding the sale of chametz to a non-Jew for Pesach, one might similarly err and say that another Israelite cannot purchase it from the non-Jew because the first owner intended only to remove the prohibition. According to what I have written, that is incorrect. In siman 442, however, such a priority is recognized for a different reason: the first owner has the prior right to repurchase it before others because of an established commercial relationship.
