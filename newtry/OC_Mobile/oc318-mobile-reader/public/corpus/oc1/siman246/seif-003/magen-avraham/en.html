It is forbidden to rent out. See the end of siman 305, from seif katan 20 onward. The words of the Olat Shabbat in the name of Rema MiPano require study.
For a person is commanded, etc. This is forbidden by Torah law, for we rule that rental does not effect acquisition except regarding overreaching. [Tosafot, Avodah Zarah 15.] Although Choshen Mishpat 260:4 states that the rented courtyard is considered the renter’s courtyard for purposes of acquisition, this must be understood in accordance with Rivash: only regarding the benefit of the rental is it called his courtyard, and acquisition is likewise such a benefit; the substance remains the owner’s. See there, siman 313, and Shach; also Bava Metzia 67, though this is not the place to elaborate. If a Jew rents an animal from a non-Jew, he is commanded concerning its rest, because rental effects acquisition stringently. [Hagahot Ashri.] This requires study, for the Gemara states that a Jew who rents a cow from a kohen may feed it vetch of terumah because he did not acquire it, implying that even leniently we say rental does not effect acquisition. Tosafot in Avodah Zarah 15 explain that its maintenance rests upon the kohen. Nevertheless, it appears to me that if he rented it from a non-Jew and then rented it back to a non-Jew, it is permitted whichever way one reasons.
He should declare it ownerless, etc. By Torah law, declaring property ownerless privately is effective. Initially, however, one may not rent it on the understanding that he will declare it ownerless on Shabbat. Likewise, a sale permits only when he sells it for all the days of the week, not for Shabbat alone. [Olat Shabbat; Tzemach Tzedek.] Bach permits initially declaring it ownerless before three.
Acquired by the non-Jew. This requires study, for Hagahot Mordechai reads, “My animal is acquired by you,” implying that he must say to the non-Jew, “My animal is acquired by you,” so that the non-Jew acquires it. Saying this in his absence is ineffective, for even regarding a Jew it does not work unless another person acquires it on his behalf. It therefore appears to me that he must say it specifically to the non-Jew in his presence, so that he intends to acquire it.
In order to remove, etc. This implies that even on Shabbat no person can acquire it, and Levush writes likewise. Nevertheless, he does not transgress concerning it, since he declared it ownerless, as explained above, unlike the Olat Shabbat. No proof may be brought from Beit Yosef, who was explaining the words of Rabbenu Peretz.
A person is not commanded, etc. Yam Shel Shlomo, Beitzah chapter 5, disputes this and cites Rif and Rosh, who write in the name of the Yerushalmi that one may not ride an animal because of the command that one’s animal rest, and this teaching concerns Yom Tov. See Beit Yosef, siman 305. No proof can be brought from Shibbolei HaLeket, for there the non-Jew rides it and carries loads upon it; on Yom Tov even a person may perform carrying by Torah law, though it is Rabbinically forbidden, as stated in siman 495:3. Rivash 24 likewise writes in the name of Rashba that loading a burden upon an animal in a courtyard on Shabbat does not violate the command that one’s animal rest; only when one drives it does he violate driving an animal. Ran holds that even driving is not present there. See Bach, who did not distinguish this. The Yerushalmi’s statement that riding is forbidden because of the command that one’s animal rest, although a living being carries itself, appears to be based on concern that it may leave the Shabbat boundary, as stated in the Gemara, and it holds that the boundary prohibition is from the Torah. The rider himself commits no prohibition because he sits upon the animal, as Rosh writes in the name of Rashbam in the chapter Mi Shehotziuhu. See what I wrote in siman 305. The Yerushalmi actually states: “One who was riding upon an animal was told: Descend, etc.; it is different because he is commanded concerning his animal’s rest.” It appears to me that although he already mounted, he is told to descend because remaining upon it causes the animal distress, as stated in siman 305:18, unlike one who climbed a tree, as at the beginning of siman 336. Tosafot write similarly on Shabbat 122. Beit Yosef in siman 495 writes that some forbid and some permit the command that one’s animal rest and driving an animal on Yom Tov, and Beit Yosef’s own view there is to forbid. One should therefore be stringent. Using an animal is certainly Rabbinically forbidden, as on Shabbat. See Makkot chapter 3 and the beginning of Mi Shehechshikh.
