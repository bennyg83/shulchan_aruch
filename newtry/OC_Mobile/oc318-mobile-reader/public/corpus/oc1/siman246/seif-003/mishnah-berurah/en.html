It is prohibited to rent, etc. Even on Sunday, and even by inclusion in a broader rental period.
So that he will perform, etc. The correct reading is: 'lest he perform labor with it,' as the Gra notes and as is evident from the Shulchan Aruch itself.
Regarding the resting of his animal. For it is written, 'So that your ox and your donkey may rest.' Although he rented the animal to a non-Jew, rental does not transfer ownership, and it therefore remains the Israelite's animal, regarding which he is commanded that it rest. Some say that, stringently, rental is treated as transferring ownership; therefore, if an Israelite rents an animal from a non-Jew, he is commanded regarding its rest. It is proper to be stringent because the prohibition is biblical. If he rented it from a non-Jew and then rented it back to a non-Jew, it is permitted either way. If he bought an animal from a non-Jew, or sold one to a non-Jew, using only meshichah or only payment, the law is doubtful, and one rules stringently regarding the resting of the animal.
The non-Jew is not believed. Nor is he afraid to perform labor with it, because it is in his possession through a loan or rental and he will not be treated as a thief on that account. This differs from the first case, where he stipulated that it be returned before Shabbat, for then the loan or rental period has already ended before Shabbat. See below, siman 305:23.
He should declare it ownerless, etc. This applies only after the fact, where he already stipulated with the non-Jew and the non-Jew retained it on Shabbat. Initially, one may not rent it on the understanding that when Shabbat arrives he will declare it ownerless. Even if he declares it ownerless before three people, not everyone knows of the declaration, and suspicion may arise that an Israelite's animal is working on Shabbat. Even a sale is permitted only when he sells the animal to the non-Jew as a complete sale for the entire week, not for Shabbat alone, because of suspicion. The Bach permits initially renting it on condition that he declare it ownerless before three people, because the matter becomes public through them and suspicion will not arise. In pressing circumstances one may rely on his view, as will be explained at the end of the siman.
Privately. He is then no longer commanded regarding its rest, because it is no longer his after he declared it ownerless. Although rabbinic law requires ownerless declaration before three people, biblically even a private declaration suffices. Here, since he already rented it, stipulated that it be returned, and the non-Jew retained it, this suffices to avoid a biblical prohibition. The Beit Yosef cites authorities who require three people even in this after-the-fact case. One should therefore do so where possible, and at minimum should declare it ownerless before one person, since many leading authorities hold that even biblically property is not ownerless until the declaration is made before at least one person. One member of his household suffices.
My animal is acquired by the non-Jew. The Magen Avraham holds that this works only if he says in the non-Jew's presence, 'My animal is acquired by you,' for then the non-Jew intends to acquire it, and because it is in his domain his courtyard acquires it for him. If the Israelite says this outside the non-Jew's presence, the non-Jew does not acquire it. Even transferring it to him through another Israelite does not help, as explained in Choshen Mishpat 243:14. Peri Megadim and the other later authorities agree. Plainly, even when he says, 'My animal is acquired by you,' a fixed price is also required; otherwise there is no sale at all.
So that he be saved, etc. Meaning that initially one should not rely on renting it with the intention of declaring it ownerless when Shabbat arrives, or transferring it then to the non-Jew, because neither the declaration nor the transfer is public and there is a concern of appearance. See the end of the siman.
For certainly his intention is not, etc. Meaning that he certainly did not intend it to remain ownerless forever, but only for Shabbat, to remove the Shabbat prohibition; after Shabbat it returns to his possession. During Shabbat itself, however, it is unquestionably fully ownerless, and even a declaration of ownerlessness for one day is effective. There is no need to fear that another will acquire it during Shabbat, because the non-Jew will certainly not allow this while it remains in his possession for his work.
A person is not commanded, etc. The obligation that one's animal rest is derived from 'So that your ox and your donkey may rest as you do,' and that verse concerns Shabbat. Nevertheless, one may rent them for Yom Tov only by inclusion, so that the payment not constitute Yom Tov wages. Maharshal disagrees and holds that Yom Tov and Shabbat are identical in this respect, as in other labors. The Magen Avraham discusses this at length and notes that the Beit Yosef in siman 495 cites the views of the Rishonim and inclines to prohibit. One should therefore be stringent. The Gra likewise concludes that Maharshal's view is primary, and in siman 495:3 explains that the prohibition against taking out a load on an animal on Yom Tov is because of the obligation that one's animal rest.
