Seif 3: He should declare it ownerless between himself and himself. NB: See what I wrote on this in my work Nedarim Zerizin, in my manuscript omissions, part 2, fol. 78, on the Gemara fol. 43, to the holy community of Krakow; see there, with God's help, and examine carefully. The Beit Yosef brought in the name of the Rokeach, siman 300, as follows: "If an animal of a Jew is in the hand of a non-Jew, and the non-Jew does work with it on Shabbat, since he does not take wages it is permitted; and if the Jew sees, it is permitted." The Beit Yosef wrote that the words of the Rokeach are not correct; see there, and see what the Taz wrote in explanation. See my novellae to Avodah Zarah 15, where I explained the words of the Rokeach. Now it appears that one may say simply as follows. Since we hold that one who borrows without permission is a gazlan, and regarding a gazlan we hold that if he stole and the owners did not despair, neither can consecrate it: this one because it is not his, and that one because it is not in his domain. The main derivation that neither can consecrate is from the verse, "when a man consecrates his house"; just as his house is in his domain, etc. It is thus proven that when another has stolen it, it is not called his house. But if he rented it or lent it to another, it is still his house and he can consecrate it. So too here, when the verse says, "you shall not do any work, you, etc., and your animal," that applies specifically when it is in his domain, but when it is not in his domain it is not "your animal." Therefore, in a case of borrowing or rental, where he can consecrate it and it is in his domain, so too he violates on Shabbat because of "you shall not do any work, etc., and your animal." But if a non-Jew stole it, or similarly if he stipulated with him not to work on Shabbat and he nevertheless does, he is a borrower without permission and is a gazlan; in that case the Jew certainly does not transgress through it and there is no concern. Even according to the Beit Yosef later, who holds that accepting all liability does not help, that is only because it is with the Jew's consent and the non-Jew does not have the status of a gazlan. But where he has the status of a gazlan, there is no issur at all upon the Jew. Only where the Jew later sees him must he protest, for otherwise he agrees to the matter; although the non-Jew had already become a gazlan, it reverts to being a deposit, as the Beit Shmuel wrote in Even HaEzer siman 28, that although neither can consecrate it, with the consent of both it becomes a deposit and the consecration takes effect. So too here, if he does not protest, it reverts to being a deposit and he violates through it. This is what the Rokeach meant: if the non-Jew does work with it on Shabbat, it is permitted, meaning that it is permitted to rent it to him in this manner, stipulating that the non-Jew not do work with it on Shabbat and that he not give him wages; then even if the non-Jew later works with it, there is no issur for the Jew. But if he sees, he must protest, for otherwise it becomes a deposit and returns to his domain. This resolves the matter; examine carefully.
There, in the gloss: But he may rent it or lend it and stipulate that he return it before Shabbat, but it does not help if he stipulates with the non-Jew that it rest on Shabbat, because the non-Jew is not believed about this. NB: I have room for analysis here, for one who examines the first chapter of Avodah Zarah 22 will see that it says there, "a non-Jew obeys; a Kuti does not obey." This seemingly proves that a non-Jew has credibility about this, that he will not do work with it on Shabbat. One cannot say that there it is specifically because the work is attached to the ground and public, so since he promised not to do work with it, if he later fails to keep his word and his falsehood becomes public, he will be disgraced and therefore he obeys; but here, with movable objects, where his falsehood will not become public, a non-Jew is not believed. This is not so. If the meaning of "a Kuti obeys" were that he obeys because it is shameful for him not to keep his word, then what does the Gemara answer, "a Kuti does not obey, for he says, I have learned more than you"? What difference does that make? Either the meaning is that at the beginning of the rental he does not listen to him and will not promise not to work with it, in which case he should indeed not rent to him, and why should renting to a Kuti be forbidden entirely even if he agrees to his words? Perforce, "a Kuti does not obey" means that even if he promises, we are concerned that later he will not obey and will not keep his word. If the meaning of "a non-Jew obeys" is that he is ashamed not to keep his word, then what difference is there between a Kuti and a non-Jew, and why would a Kuti not be ashamed not to keep his word? What does "I have learned more than you" have to do with not keeping his word? Rather, the meaning of "a non-Jew obeys" is that even where the Jew will not know, he will not do work with it and thereby cause the Jew to stumble; he is not suspected of causing a Jew to stumble. But a Kuti says, "I have learned more than you," and thinks he is not causing the Jew to stumble, because he says that it is permitted even for a Jew. If so, the same should apply to movable objects and an animal: a non-Jew should be believed about this, since a non-Jew obeys. At first glance this is not difficult, for that is the view of the Rashba, but since we rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who says that one may not rent a bathhouse to a non-Jew, it is proven that he holds that even a non-Jew does not obey; therefore we rule properly that the non-Jew is not believed about this. Yet it still requires study: why does the Gemara ask, "but a bathhouse to a Kuti is permitted"? Perhaps the novelty is that not only a Kuti, who does not obey, is certainly forbidden, but even a non-Jew, who does obey, is also forbidden to rent it to him. What, then, is the question? Therefore it appears that we say the logic that a non-Jew obeys only in a case such as a field, where there is not so much loss for the non-Jew; if he does not work today, he will not lose, for he can do it tomorrow, since the work has a set amount. In such a case he obeys. But with a bathhouse, where there is no fixed amount and he can work today as well as tomorrow, where the non-Jew would incur loss, he does not obey any more than a Kuti. I later saw this reasoning in the Maharsha, s.v. ha le-nokhri shari, and I was pleased that my view matched his; see there. Therefore there is no special novelty that a non-Jew does not obey by a bathhouse. And when the Gemara later asks, "but his field to a non-Jew is permitted," and explains the reason that he is working for his own share, why did it not give the reason that by a field a non-Jew obeys? Since by a bathhouse it is obvious that he does not obey, one who rents him the bathhouse is like one who explicitly rents it to him to work on Shabbat. This implies that a field in such a case, even when he rents it to him also for Shabbat, is permitted even if he does not stipulate that it rest on Shabbat; otherwise it should have taught that a field is forbidden to rent to him ordinarily where there is concern he will work with it on Shabbat, and we would know automatically that a bathhouse is forbidden even with a condition, for by a bathhouse he certainly will not obey where he has a loss. That is obvious and need not be taught, for where the non-Jew has a loss he does not obey, no better than a Jew, because he has no credibility in that matter. This appears to be the intent of the sugya. Examine carefully. Accordingly, one may say that specifically if he rents him an animal to do with it constantly as he wishes, so that even if he works with it today he can also work with it tomorrow, the non-Jew has a loss and will not obey. But if he rented the animal only for a particular task, to do such-and-such with it, in a manner where the non-Jew has no loss because if he does not do it today he can do it tomorrow, then he may rent it to the non-Jew and stipulate that it rest on Shabbat. In such a case the non-Jew obeys, since the Gemara states explicitly that in that case a non-Jew obeys, and we do not find anyone who explicitly disagrees about that; it is preferable to minimize dispute. Thus one may say that everyone agrees that in this case a non-Jew obeys. As for the field, the reason needed to be that he is working for his tenancy is because in that case no stipulation is required at all. Since the matter is rabbinic, we listen to leniency. This appears correct to me in practical halacha. Examine very carefully.
