Seif 3: He should declare it ownerless privately. Note: See what I wrote on this matter in my work on vows of exhortation, in my manuscript addenda, part II, p. 78, on the Gemara, p. 43, for the holy community of Krakow; see there, with God's help, and examine it carefully. The Beit Yosef cites the Rokeach, siman 300, as follows: “If an Israelite's animal is in the possession of a non-Jew, and the non-Jew performs labor with it on Shabbat, since he is not receiving rental payment it is permitted; and if the Israelite sees him, he must protest.” The Beit Yosef writes that the Rokeach's ruling is incorrect; see there. See also what the Taz wrote in explaining it, and see my novellae on Avodah Zarah, p. 15, where I explained the Rokeach's words. At present, it appears that they may be explained simply as follows. We rule that one who borrows without the owner's knowledge is a robber. Regarding a robber, we rule that when an item was stolen and the owners have not despaired of recovering it, neither party can consecrate it: this one because it is not his, and the other because it is not in his possession. The primary derivation that neither may consecrate it is from the verse, “If a man consecrates his house”: just as his house is in his possession, so too any consecrated object must be in his possession. It follows that when another has stolen the item, it is not called “his house”; but when he rented or lent it to another, it remains his house and he may consecrate it. The same applies here. The verse states, “You shall not perform any labor, you...and your animal,” which refers specifically to an animal in his possession. When it is not in his possession, it is not deemed “your animal.” Therefore, where it was lent or rented, since he can consecrate it and it is considered in his possession, he likewise violates “You shall not perform any labor...and your animal” if it works on Shabbat. But if a non-Jew stole it, or similarly if the owner stipulated with him not to work it on Shabbat and he nevertheless did so, he is a borrower without permission and thus a robber. In that case, the Israelite certainly commits no violation through the animal and there is no concern. Even according to the Beit Yosef below, who holds that acceptance of full liability does not suffice, that is only because the arrangement was made with the Israelite's consent and the non-Jew therefore does not have the status of a robber. But where he does have the status of a robber, the Israelite incurs no prohibition at all. Nevertheless, if he later sees the non-Jew working it, he must protest, for otherwise he is consenting to the matter. Although the non-Jew had already become a robber, the animal then reverts to being a deposit. As the Beit Shmuel writes in Even HaEzer, siman 28, although neither party can consecrate stolen property, with the consent of both it becomes a deposit and the consecration takes effect. So too here, if the Israelite does not protest, the animal reverts to being a deposit, returns to his possession, and he violates the prohibition through it. This is what the Rokeach means: if the non-Jew works it on Shabbat, it is permitted, meaning that the Israelite may give him the animal under an arrangement that he not work it on Shabbat and receive no rental payment. If the non-Jew subsequently works it, the Israelite has committed no prohibition; but if he sees him, he must protest, for otherwise it becomes a deposit and returns to his possession. This resolves the matter well; examine it carefully.
Ibid., in the gloss: But he may rent or lend it and stipulate that it be returned before Shabbat; however, it does not help to stipulate with the non-Jew that it rest on Shabbat, because the non-Jew is not trusted regarding this. Note: This requires study. One who examines the first chapter of Avodah Zarah, p. 22, will find the statement, “A non-Jew obeys; a Samaritan does not obey.” This apparently proves that a non-Jew is trusted not to perform labor with it on Shabbat. One cannot answer that there the non-Jew obeys only because the labor concerns attached real property and is public, so that if he promised not to work and then broke his word, his falsehood would become known and disgrace him, whereas here, with movable property, his falsehood would not become known and he is therefore not trusted. That cannot be correct. If “a Samaritan does not obey” meant that at the outset he refuses to promise not to work, then one simply would not rent to him, and there would be no reason to prohibit renting to a Samaritan who expressly accepts the condition. We must therefore understand that even when he promises, we fear that he will later disobey and fail to keep his word. If the reason a non-Jew obeys were merely embarrassment at breaking his word, why should a Samaritan differ, and what relevance would his claim, “I know more than you,” have to his failure to keep his word? Rather, “a non-Jew obeys” means that even when the Israelite will not discover the matter, the non-Jew will not perform the labor and thereby cause the Israelite to transgress; he is not suspected of deliberately causing an Israelite to stumble. A Samaritan, however, says, “I know more than you,” and believes that he is not causing the Israelite to transgress, because he considers the act permitted even for an Israelite. If so, the same should apply to movable property and animals: the non-Jew should be trusted, because a non-Jew obeys. At first glance, this is not difficult, for that view follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, whereas we rule that one may not rent a bathhouse to a non-Jew, apparently because even a non-Jew does not obey. Accordingly, our ruling that the non-Jew is not trusted is understandable. Yet the Gemara's question remains difficult: “But a bathhouse may be rented to a non-Jew.” Perhaps the teaching means that not only may it not be rented to a Samaritan, who certainly does not obey, but it may not even be rented to a non-Jew, who ordinarily does obey. Why, then, is this a difficulty? It therefore appears that the principle that a non-Jew obeys applies only to a field, where refraining from work that day causes him no substantial loss, because he can perform the fixed amount of work tomorrow. In a bathhouse, however, there is no fixed quota; he can operate it both today and tomorrow. Where the non-Jew will suffer a loss, he no longer obeys any more than a Samaritan. I later saw this reasoning in Maharsha on the words “But it is permitted to a non-Jew,” and my understanding accords with his; see there. There is consequently no special novelty in the bathhouse case, because with respect to a bathhouse the non-Jew is no more likely to obey. The Gemara later asks, “But his field may be rented to a non-Jew,” and explains that the tenant is working for his own share. Why does it not answer that a non-Jew obeys regarding a field? Since it is obvious that with a bathhouse he does not obey, renting it to him is equivalent to expressly renting it for Shabbat work. The implication is that a field under comparable circumstances may be rented even when the agreement includes Shabbat and no condition was made that he rest. Otherwise, the teaching should have stated that one may not rent a field without qualification where there is concern that he will work on Shabbat, and we would automatically know that a bathhouse is prohibited even with a condition, because where he faces a loss he certainly does not obey. That obvious point required no teaching, for where the non-Jew suffers loss he is no better than an Israelite with respect to credibility. This appears to be the intent of the passage; examine it carefully. Accordingly, one may distinguish. If one rents an animal to a non-Jew for unrestricted, continuing use, then refraining from work today causes him a loss, because he could work it both today and tomorrow; in such a case a non-Jew does not obey. But if he rented the animal only for a specified task, so that the non-Jew loses nothing by postponing the task until tomorrow, the Israelite may rent it to him and stipulate that it rest on Shabbat. In such circumstances the Gemara expressly states that a non-Jew obeys, and we find no authority who explicitly disputes that point. It is preferable to minimize disagreement; therefore, all may agree that in this case a non-Jew obeys. The reason the Gemara needed the principle that a tenant farmer works for his own share is that in a field no stipulation is required at all. Since the matter is rabbinic, we follow the lenient conclusion. This appears correct as practical law; examine it very carefully.
