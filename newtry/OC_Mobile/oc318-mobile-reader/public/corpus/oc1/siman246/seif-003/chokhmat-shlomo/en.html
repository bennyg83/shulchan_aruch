Seif 3. He declares it ownerless between himself and himself. NB: see what I wrote on this in my work on vows Zaruzein in my manuscript notes part 2 daf 78 on Shabbat daf 43 for Krakow community; with God's help examine. And behold Beit Yosef brought in the name of Rokeach siman 300, and this is its language: an animal of Israel in a non-Jew's hand — if the non-Jew does melacha in it on Shabbat, since he does not take rent it is permitted; and if a Jew sees it is permitted — end. And Beit Yosef wrote that Rokeach's words are not found; see there; and see in Taz what he wrote in his explanation; and see in my chiddushim on Avodah Zarah daf 15 what I wrote explaining Rokeach's words; see there. And now it appears one may say simply thus: since we hold one who borrows without consent is a robber, and regarding a robber we hold stolen and owners did not despair — both of them cannot consecrate this, because it is not his and because it is not in his possession; and therefore the main teaching that both cannot consecrate is from what is written "when a man consecrates his house" — his house is in his possession, etc.; therefore it is proven where another robbed it is not called his house, but if he rented or lent to another it is still his house and he can consecrate; therefore here too, since scripture said "you shall not do any melacha — you, etc., and your animal" — only when it is in his possession; but when not in his possession it is not "your animal"; therefore in loan and rental where he can consecrate and it is in his possession — likewise he transgresses on it on Shabbat for "you shall not do any melacha, etc., and your animal"; but if a non-Jew robbed it — and likewise if he stipulated with him that he will work on Shabbat and he will do so — he is a borrower without consent and a robber, and in this certainly the Jew did not transgress in it and there is no concern in this; and even per Beit Yosef below who holds accepting all liability does not help — this is only since it was with the Jew's knowledge and he does not have the name of robber; but where he has the name of robber there is no prohibition on the Jew at all, only where he sees afterward he must protest, for otherwise he agrees to the matter; and even though he already became a robber, it returns to being a deposit, as Beit Shmuel wrote in Even HaEzer siman 28 — even though both cannot consecrate with both's consent it becomes a deposit and consecration takes effect; therefore here too if he does not protest it returns to being a deposit and he transgresses on it; see there. And this is Rokeach's intent: if the non-Jew does melacha in it on Shabbat it is permitted — meaning it is permitted to rent it in this manner that the non-Jew will not do melacha in it on Shabbat and he will not give him rent, and then even if afterward he does there is no prohibition for the Jew; but if he sees he must protest, for otherwise it becomes a deposit and returns to his possession; and it works well; examine. In the hagahah there: but he may rent or lend it and stipulate that he return it before Shabbat; but it does not help if he stipulates with the non-Jew that it rest on Shabbat, for the non-Jew is not trusted for this. NB: I have room for inquiry here, for one who examines chapter 1 of Avodah Zarah daf 22 — it says there: a non-Jew is trusted, a kuti is not trusted — therefore it appears a non-Jew has trustworthiness regarding not doing melacha in it on Shabbat. And one cannot say there it is specifically trusted since the melacha is attached and publicized — and therefore since he promised not to do melacha, if afterward he does not stand by his word and it becomes known he lied, disgrace will be his, therefore he is trusted; but here in melacha of movable objects where his lie will not be publicized the non-Jew has no trustworthiness — this is not so, for if the intent of kuti is not trusted is because of disgrace that he does not stand by his word — then what does it answer that kuti is not trusted who says "I am more learned than you" — in any case if the intent at the start of rental he does not listen to promise not to do melacha — then he should not rent to him truly, and why is it forbidden entirely to rent to a kuti even if he agrees to his words; perforce the intent of kuti is not trusted is even if he promises we fear afterward he will not be trusted and not fulfill his word; and if the intent of what "non-Jew is trusted" is disgrace of not standing by his word — then what difference between kuti and non-Jew, and why is there no disgrace for a kuti not standing by his word, and what relevance is "I am more learned than you" to not standing by his word; perforce the intent of non-Jew is trusted is even when it will not be known to the Jew, he will not do melacha in it to entrap the Jew, and he is not suspected of entrapping the Jew; but a kuti said "I am more learned than you" and holds he does not entrap the Jew in this because he says it is also permitted for the Jew. Therefore per this reasoning likewise for movable objects and animals there would be non-Jew trustworthiness on this since non-Jew is trusted. And behold at first glance it is difficult, for this is Rashba's view, but since we hold like Rabban Gamliel who said one may not rent a bathhouse to a non-Jew — it is proven he holds even a non-Jew is not trusted; and we hold like Rabban Gamliel, and therefore they ruled correctly that the non-Jew is not trusted for this. But still requires study what the Talmud challenges: behold a bathhouse for a kuti is permitted — perhaps it teaches a greater matter that not only kuti who is not trusted is certainly forbidden to rent, but also even a non-Jew who is trusted is forbidden to rent — and why does it challenge; and therefore from this it appeared we do not say the reasoning non-Jew is trusted except such as in a field where the non-Jew does not lose so much that if he does not work today he will not lose and will do tomorrow — this has a limit, therefore in this he is trusted; but in a bathhouse that has no limit and he can work today also tomorrow — whenever loss reaches the non-Jew he is not trusted more than a kuti; and again I saw this reasoning in Maharsha on "behold for a non-Jew it is permitted" — my intent in this; see there. Therefore there is no side of a greater matter that in a bathhouse the non-Jew is not trusted more. And what it challenges afterward "behold his field for a non-Jew is permitted" and explains the reason that he works his tenancy — why did it not give the reason that in a field the non-Jew is trusted, namely since in a bathhouse it is obvious he is not trusted — the one who rents him the bathhouse is like one who rents explicitly that he will work on Shabbat — and it implies "behold a field" likewise even if he rents also for Shabbat it is permitted even if he does not stipulate with him to rest on Shabbat, for otherwise it would teach us a field where it is forbidden to rent stam where there is concern he will work on Shabbat — and we would know in a bathhouse it is forbidden even with a condition, for in a bathhouse certainly he is not trusted where he has loss, and this is obvious — it need not teach us where the non-Jew has loss he is not trusted, for he is no worse than an Israelite who has no trustworthiness in this. So appears the intent of the sugya; examine. And since so, one may say specifically if he rents the animal to do work in it constantly as he wishes — then even if he does melacha today he will do tomorrow, and the non-Jew has loss here and the non-Jew is not trusted; but if he rented the animal only for specific work to do thus and thus in a matter where the non-Jew has no loss — if he does not do today he will do tomorrow — in this he may rent to a non-Jew and stipulate with him that it rest on Shabbat, for in this case non-Jew is trusted, since it is explicit in the Talmud that in such a case non-Jew is trusted and we do not find anyone who disagrees explicitly; and to minimize dispute is preferable; and therefore one may say here all agree non-Jew is trusted. And what was needed in a field "he works his tenancy" — because here even a condition is not needed, and since it is a rabbinic matter we listen to be lenient — as in my humble opinion correct for practical law; examine well.
