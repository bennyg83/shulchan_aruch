It is forbidden to rent. If one sold his animal to a non-Jew, fixed a price, and delivered the animal to him for examination, with the understanding that if he finds a defect he may return it but if he wants it he will retain the purchase, then so long as the buyer has not revealed that he does not want it, the animal is presumed to belong to the buyer and there is no issue of the Jew's animal resting. Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger in the name of the Ran, first chapter of Avodah Zarah. See Tosafos Yerushalayim, who challenges this from an explicit Yerushalmi.
His animal to a non-Jew. This refers to a large animal. A small animal, which is not ordinarily used for labor, is not included in this concern, as Rambam states explicitly. Know further that Rambam does not distinguish, regarding a large animal itself, between a horse and other animals, as he does regarding the sale of a large animal, where he permits selling a horse. The reason is that the prohibition of sale is only a decree lest one come to lend, rent, or allow the animal to be tested. Since a horse ordinarily serves for riding, which according to most poskim does not violate the requirement that one's animal rest because a living being carries itself, the Sages did not prohibit its sale, as stated in the Gemara. Actual lending or rental, however, directly concerns a Torah prohibition, and one must certainly fear that the non-Jew may place a load upon it or use it to pull a wagon. This is especially so in our countries, where horses are also designated for carrying loads, unlike their places where donkeys were designated for loads and horses only for riding. It is therefore clearly forbidden to rent even a horse. I found that Olas Shabbos rules this explicitly in the name of Ralbach; Eliyah Rabbah cites it without dissent, and Knesses HaGedolah likewise rules so. Baer Heitev's statement that there is no need for concern when a non-Jew rides a Jew's animal does not mean that one may therefore rent him a horse. It refers instead to the case discussed in siman 305, seif 22. See there and Peri Megadim. It appears that Peri Megadim did not see what Knesses HaGedolah, Olas Shabbos, and Eliyah Rabbah cite explicitly in the name of Ralbach.
The resting of his animal. The same applies to one's wild animals and birds, and even to aquatic species. Chapter 5 of Bava Kamma, cited in Chiddushei Rabbi Akiva Eiger. One is commanded that they too rest.
