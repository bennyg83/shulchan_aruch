Rashi explains the baraita according to Beit Shammai: labor of an Israelite performed in an Israelite's utensil, such as a vat set up on Friday in which the labor continues on Shabbat, is biblically prohibited according to Beit Shammai. Labor of a non-Jew in an Israelite's utensil is rabbinically prohibited; therefore on Wednesday or Thursday it is a rabbinic doubt whether the non-Jew will use it on Shabbat, and it is permitted. According to Beit Hillel, whom we follow, it is permitted even on Friday, because there is no obligation, even rabbinically, that utensils rest. This is the Mechaber's first, unattributed opinion, following Rambam and Rif, who omitted the prohibition. See Bach. I do not understand his words, for according to Beit Shammai the resting of utensils is prohibited even where a fixed price was set, even through lending, and even where the non-Jew uses them without the owner's knowledge; the Israelite must protest. See Baal HaMaor to Shabbat 19 and Avodah Zarah 15. Some say that even according to Beit Shammai an animal may be rented on Wednesday or Thursday, but this is not the view of the poskim. This will be explained in seif 3.
Know. According to those who explain Rav Yosef in Shabbat 18 as holding that the resting of utensils is prohibited even according to Beit Hillel, one must explain how he understands this baraita. Under Rabbeinu Yonah's explanation, items not used for labor may be rented, while utensils used for labor would be prohibited even on Wednesday or Thursday. One may answer that the baraita concerns utensils that perform labor but periodically rest, such as a vat, beam, and press, whereas ordinary plowing implements would be prohibited. Tosafot on Shabbat 19 did not establish the case as a garment or cloak; see also Tosafot 18 and Netziv. Yet according to Rabbeinu Yonah, the rule is not limited to a plow; even a vat is prohibited, while only a garment or cloak is permitted. The Taz explains that rental, because it yields profit to the Israelite, makes the non-Jew appear as his agent, whereas lending is permitted. Levush implies that two concerns combine: people may say that he takes Shabbat wages, since he transfers it on Friday, and they may say that the non-Jew is his agent. Lending, where only the second concern exists, was not prohibited for detached objects whose use is not public. See simanim 243 and 244: where the prohibition is only rabbinic, an act done privately is permitted. The same may apply here in a sparsely populated place, though household members may still create suspicion unless none are present; see siman 247.
