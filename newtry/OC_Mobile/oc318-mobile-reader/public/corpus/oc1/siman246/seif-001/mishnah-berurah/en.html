It is permitted to lend, etc. This is the view of the Rif and Rambam. They hold that the baraita stating that one may not rent utensils to a non-Jew on Friday, but may do so on Wednesday or Thursday, is based on the obligation that one's utensils rest on Shabbat, and follows Beit Shammai, who maintain that a person is commanded regarding the resting of his utensils just as regarding the resting of his animal. Beit Hillel, however, hold that a person is not commanded regarding the resting of his utensils. It is therefore permitted to lend or rent one's utensils even on Friday, including utensils used for labor. Nevertheless, they too hold that utensils may be rented only through inclusion in a broader rental period, but not for Shabbat alone, even if the rental begins earlier in the week, because that is payment for Shabbat, as explained below according to the alternative view; no authority is found to permit this. The alternative view is that of Rabbeinu Yonah. He explains that the baraita did not prohibit the rental because of the resting of utensils, but applies according to all opinions. When one gives utensils used for labor to a non-Jew on Friday and the non-Jew will immediately use them on Shabbat, it appears as though he performs the labor as the Israelite's agent, which is prohibited because instructing a non-Jew is a rabbinic prohibition. Although siman 252:2 permits giving hides to a non-Jewish tanner near nightfall where a fixed price was agreed, because the non-Jew works for his own benefit, that case differs: the Israelite gains nothing from the non-Jew's working on Shabbat. With rented utensils, it is known that if the Israelite stipulated that the non-Jew cease using them on Shabbat, the non-Jew would pay less rent. Since the Israelite profits from the ordinary rental under which the non-Jew uses the utensils on Shabbat, it appears as though the non-Jew is his agent. If he gives them on Wednesday or Thursday, however, this appearance is not as strong, and it is permitted.
And the like. For example, millstones and all craftsmen's implements. See Peri Megadim, who writes that the same applies to utensils that do not themselves perform labor, such as a vat in which beer is made or a pot in which food is cooked. Since the non-Jew nevertheless performs labor with them, it appears that he is the Israelite's agent. Rabbeinu Yonah excluded only a garment, cloak, or similar item not ordinarily used to perform labor, which may be rented even on Friday.
By inclusion. But taking payment specifically for Shabbat is prohibited according to all opinions, even for an object not used for labor or for renting a room in which to live, as explained below in siman 306:4.
And to lend it to him, etc. Since the Israelite gains nothing from the non-Jew's labor, people will not say that the non-Jew is his agent. It is therefore permitted to lend him even for Shabbat alone, including utensils with which the non-Jew will perform labor.
And we do not say, etc. Although he does not lend it entirely gratuitously, but in exchange for the other party lending him an item later, it is nevertheless permitted, because it is not structured as an actual rental in which he says, 'I will give you this amount so that you will lend me your utensil.'
