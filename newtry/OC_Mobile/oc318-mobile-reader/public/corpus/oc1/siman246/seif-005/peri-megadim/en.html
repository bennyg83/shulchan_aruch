Even so. See Taz. The Mechaber's words are from Rivash. On Shabbat the non-Jew has no lawful right to the animal, since the agreement ended before Shabbat and his retention is unauthorized. Biblically the Israelite can reacquire it from ownerlessness. The Beit Yosef similarly explains Rabbeinu Peretz in Semak, who permits renting by inclusion and declaring the animal ownerless, while another person cannot acquire it because the non-Jew has possession of it on Shabbat. After Shabbat, although the non-Jew's right has ended, the Israelite owner reacquires it immediately by declaration, since it was made ownerless only until the conclusion of Shabbat. The Beit Yosef therefore writes that the ownerlessness is not permanent. The Tur, however, appears to hold that the ownerlessness was made only to escape the Shabbat prohibition, and therefore another person cannot acquire it even where the non-Jew has no lawful possession on Shabbat. This is the Magen Avraham's intent, though Eliyah Rabbah questions him. Levush appears to understand it this way. The Mechaber and Rama here seem to reverse their positions from Choshen Mishpat 273. There the Mechaber holds that biblically ownerlessness must be declared before one person and rabbinically before three; accordingly here one should at least declare it before one. Rambam holds that biblically even a private declaration works and only rabbinically are witnesses needed, so for avoiding a Shabbat prohibition a private declaration suffices. Tosefet Shabbat notes this and suggests the present case is one where no other person is available; it is therefore better at least to satisfy the view that a private declaration works. The Mechaber lists private ownerlessness or transfer to the non-Jew as two emergency methods. Levush reverses the order and prefers ownerlessness before three. The reason initial reliance is prohibited may be appearance as Shabbat wages, or because the ownerlessness is not wholehearted but only intended to avoid the prohibition, which is inadequate initially. The Taz cites a certain rabbi, and Tzemach Tzedek likewise permits such a device to avoid a biblical Shabbat prohibition and, similarly, interest. Eliyah Rabbah objects that the Talmudic proof concerns only rabbinic interest; for a biblical prohibition a complete sale is required. With chametz, see siman 448 and Masat Binyamin. Interest may differ because where the transaction is not truly a loan and has the form of a sale, the Torah did not prohibit it. But sale of chametz and Shabbat are comparable and require a complete legal sale. The view cited by Olat Shabbat is sound: during the animal's labor no other Israelite will acquire it, because doing so would cause him to violate the resting of his animal; after the labor, it is no longer ownerless. This can explain Rivash's case, where the non-Jew retained it contrary to the agreement. Even if the non-Jew had no legal right, he remains liable for accidents until returning it, and thus has possession sufficient to prevent another from acquiring it on Shabbat; after Shabbat the owner's rights return.
See. Tevuot Shor, in Bechor Shor to Pesachim 21, considers that a sham sale of chametz may avoid a biblical prohibition because the owner also nullifies and declares it ownerless. According to this, after Pesach another Israelite cannot acquire it, because the ownerlessness was only for the days of Pesach, and the sale was sufficient at least to remove the rabbinic obligation of physical destruction. This may work for a rabbinic prohibition, as stated above in the name of Eliyah Rabbah. The matter will be explained in the laws of Pesach. In my view, however, nullification does not help unless it is wholehearted; with property of substantial value, one certainly does not renounce it wholeheartedly. A complete sale valid according to Torah law is therefore required. This is not the place to elaborate.
