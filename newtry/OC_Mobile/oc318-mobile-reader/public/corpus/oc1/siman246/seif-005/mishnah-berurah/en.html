Although they stipulated afterward. This wording implies that if they made no stipulation at all, it is certainly prohibited. The Beit Yosef, however, considers permitting a case where, even after becoming partners, they did not say, 'You take Shabbat,' but simply divided the week's total earnings equally. He then receives Shabbat wages by inclusion, and there is no prohibition concerning the animal's rest because the non-Jew works on Shabbat on account of his own share, though the Israelite acted improperly initially by entering the partnership without stipulating. The Shulchan Aruch's wording indicates that it did not rely on this in practice. Even according to the Beit Yosef, one should permit an equal division afterward only in a case of major loss, in accordance with Rama in siman 245:1; the Magen Avraham indicates that one may rely on this.

They are legally valid. The best of all these arrangements is to transfer the animals to the non-Jew during the week by a complete sale, receive from him a token payment, record the remaining price as a debt, and designate the animals as security for the Israelite so that his money is protected. It is proper to execute the sale before the local beit din, both so that it is performed according to Torah law and so that it becomes public and no concern of appearance remains.
Whichever of them he wishes, even, etc. Even merely having the non-Jew accept responsibility, the final method stated by the Mechaber, suffices here, although the non-Jew may not sell the animal, because it stands in his domain regarding increases and decreases in value. The Shulchan Aruch rules like the permissive opinion in seif 4. It is nevertheless better to make a complete sale, as stated above, to satisfy the stringent opinion, because according to that view a biblical concern exists. If all these arrangements are difficult and it is a pressing circumstance, such as one who owns fields that cannot be leased to a non-Jewish tenant farmer unless he also provides oxen for plowing and cannot prevent their use on Shabbat, he may rely on the Bach, who permits declaring them ownerless before three people prior to Shabbat, provided the matter is public and the declaration is wholehearted. Without pressing need, one should not rely on ownerlessness, as stated above.

There is no permission for the non-Jew to perform the Israelite's work on Shabbat, even if the animal was sold to him by a complete sale, because he is still performing the Israelite's work on his behalf. Accordingly, if the horses belong to an Israelite and the non-Jew is also his general employee for whatever work the Israelite needs, selling the horses to him does not help, for the Israelite may not allow him to perform his work. If, however, he was hired only for this specific task, to travel with the horses and transport merchandise whenever needed during that year, it is permitted according to Rambam, as stated in siman 244:5. Even then, the Israelite may not instruct him to travel on Shabbat; if he travels on his own, the Israelite need not stop him.
Only that he publicize, etc. Biur Halachah explains that one should not rely on any of these arrangements except in pressing circumstances. This excludes those who intentionally sell their oxen to a non-Jew so that he will work them on Shabbat while the labor remains associated with the Israelite. Although one need not protest against those who are lenient, since they have authorities on whom to rely, a conscientious person should not do so except in pressing circumstances and under a scholar's ruling. If an Israelite owns a horse and a government official who knows him demands to borrow or rent it, and the Israelite cannot refuse because of hostility, he should sell it to him for a small sum. The official may then use it as he wishes. If he later returns the horse and also pays rental money, these may be treated as gifts.
