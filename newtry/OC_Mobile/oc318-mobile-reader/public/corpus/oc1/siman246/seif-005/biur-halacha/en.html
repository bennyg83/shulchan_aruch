Knesses HaGedolah cites Maharam Alashkar in the name of Rav Yosef Gaon that it is forbidden for a Jew to employ a legal device with a non-Jew by selling him an animal, and concludes: “All these matters are wearisome, and I do not see a straight path for a person to choose in this matter. Nevertheless, in places where the practice is to permit it and they rely upon the lenient authorities, leave Israel alone; but every conscientious person should be stringent for himself.” Ralbach writes similarly, and Eliyah Rabbah cites him. Tzemach Tzedek, siman 35, also protests that people brazenly sell their horses specifically to evade the Shabbos prohibition. He writes that Jewish wagoners who own wagons and horses and transport loads for payment may not sell the horses to a non-Jew or declare them ownerless in order to transport loads on Shabbos, especially where the Jew remains constantly with the horses, travels with them during the week, bears their maintenance, the non-Jew is merely his annual employee, and the entire business remains known by the Jew's name. This also creates maris ayin. For a public mitzvah, however, such as transporting esrogim so that the public can fulfill the mitzvah at its proper time and the journey must be expedited, declaration of ownerlessness is permitted, because one should not be stringent where a mitzvah is involved. Eliyah Rabbah likewise rules stringently and questions why Magen Avraham did not cite Tzemach Tzedek. Eshel Avraham, seif katan 9, however, inclines toward leniency. Since the animal is not rented for plowing or similar work, but merely to carry loads, one may rely upon ownerlessness, a Shabbos sale, or a stipulation that the animal is acquired by the non-Jew, and certainly upon a complete sale. He adds, however, that perhaps the sale is effective only when made on Wednesday or Thursday, not on Friday; see his extended discussion. Tosafos Shabbos rules similarly. In our case, involving oxen designated for plowing, which is Torah-prohibited labor according to all opinions, where the business is known by the Jew's name and the non-Jews are merely his employees, it appears that even Eshel Avraham would agree with Tzemach Tzedek that leniency is difficult. Among the other later authorities, some incline toward leniency and hold that where one executes a document with them and publicizes the matter, there is no maris ayin. Responsa Shoel U'Meishiv, first edition, part 2, siman 67. Others are stringent. Tov Taam VaDaas, siman 163, writes that regarding the sale of an animal for work on Shabbos, which involves a Torah prohibition, the sale is ineffective because it is merely an evasion. Therefore, one should not be broadly lenient except in pressing circumstances, and even then the document must be halachically valid, prepared under a Torah scholar's direction, and publicized, as Rama writes. Fortunate is one who trusts in Hashem, fulfills the Torah's plain command, “so that your ox and your donkey may rest,” and devises no Shabbos evasions. The Holy One, blessed is He, will surely grant him success in his property during the six weekdays in reward for this.
