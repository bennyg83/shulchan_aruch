He returns. See Taz. The positions of the Rif and the Rosh are known. The Rif does not distinguish between a pause caused by coercion and one not caused by coercion, but only between the Amidah and Krias Shema. The Rosh does not distinguish between Krias Shema and the Amidah, but only between a pause caused by coercion and one not caused by coercion. The Rama’s silence here indicates that even where the coercion did not arise through the person himself, he returns to the beginning. This also appears from siman 103, seif 2, where the Rama refers to siman 85, indicating that the Amidah is treated like Krias Shema. The Levush likewise writes in siman 85, seif 1, that the Amidah is equal to Krias Shema, and that without coercion one does not return. The Bach cites Tosafos, Berachos 33a, s.v. “but a scorpion,” as discussing a case in which he paused, since without such a pause no authority on page 23a would require returning. At first glance this is difficult, for Rav Ashi concludes there that even without such a pause, etc. The intent of Tosafos is that since the Gemara merely says that he interrupts and does not say that he returns to the beginning, one should not mistakenly infer that he does not even return to the beginning of the blessing. That inference would be incorrect. One may also answer that there the person was halachically disqualified from continuing according to Rashi, and according to the Rosh as well while the water was flowing, whereas here there is no basis to say that he returns to the beginning without having paused the required time. It must therefore concern a case in which he paused, and the reason is that it was not an unavoidable interruption arising from his own condition. The Taz rejects this and explains that the case is one in which he did not pause, following the Rosh’s explanation there. The Perishah writes here that the minister of Coucy disputes the Rashba, and that the Rama rules like the minister of Coucy. See Beit Yosef here in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah on Berachos 33a, that according to the Raavad the person must be disqualified from continuing, while another form of coercion does not require returning to the beginning. The words of the minister of Coucy appear in the Rosh on page 23a and are cited by the Beit Yosef in seif 5, implying that whenever one is compelled he returns to the beginning. The Rama likewise rules here that even one compelled by an external circumstance returns to the beginning. Accordingly, the Bach’s position is the position of Rabbeinu Yonah just described. See Magen Avraham, seif 5, seif katan 2, for the practical ruling.
