“If not, he returns, etc.” See Derech HaChaim, who writes that this applies specifically when the interruption involved speech. A mere pause, even when caused by circumstances beyond his control, is different. He brings proof from the case of urine flowing upon one’s knees, where the ruling is that he returns to the place where he stopped, and no mention is made of the beginning of the blessing. Lechem Chamudos, chapter Mi Shemeiso, section 76, however, writes explicitly that the Gemara means the beginning of the blessing. Derech HaChaim further supports his view from the fact that the discussion there in the Gemara concerns speech. Although one who studies Biur HaGra will find that according to the Rosh’s explanation the passage does not concern speech alone, since walking is also considered an interruption, nevertheless his ruling appears correct, because perhaps only there, where there was also some interruption through walking, must he return, unlike a mere pause. Peri Megadim, however, implies that even for silence alone one must return to the beginning of the blessing. This requires study. Rabbi Akiva Eiger’s novellae to siman 107 also appear to leave this question unresolved. It further appears to me that we say he returns to the beginning of the blessing after a pause only when the pause resulted from a circumstance that rendered the person or place unfit, such as urine flowing upon his knees or another comparable circumstance. Since, had he paused long enough to complete the entire prayer, he would have had to return to the beginning, a shorter pause causes him to return to the beginning of the blessing. If he paused willingly, however, then unless the pause lasted long enough to complete the entire prayer, and occurred without compulsion, it is likewise only one level less severe and he does not return to the beginning of the blessing. One should not object from the Bach, who writes that a scorpion is not considered compulsion, yet the Mechaber rules that if he did not pause long enough to complete the entire prayer he returns to the beginning of the blessing. One may answer that the Mechaber follows his own view, according to which in prayer one who pauses long enough to complete the entire prayer returns to the beginning even without any compulsion. Therefore, in his view, one who pauses for a shorter period returns to the beginning of the blessing in every case. This does not apply according to our ruling.
