If one spoke. See Taz, who discusses this at length. Regarding his difficulty that the Raavad’s rulings contradict one another, since here he rules that one who deliberately spoke returns to the beginning, while in siman 65 he follows the Rosh that even if one paused long enough to complete the entire prayer, when the pause was not caused by coercion he does not return to the beginning: it is not correct to define every pause not caused by coercion as deliberate speech. One who studies Rabbeinu Yonah on page 33a will see that the Raavad requires a person to be halachically disqualified from continuing, but where he was not so disqualified he does not return, although the circumstance may still have been unavoidable. There is also a simple distinction between speech and a silent pause. The Hagahos Maimoniyos, cited by the Beit Yosef, states that silence is like speech only with respect to pausing long enough to complete the entire prayer. Where that measure was not reached, there is certainly a distinction. If one spoke, even without pausing long enough to complete the entire prayer or even that blessing, Tosafos on page 33a and the Mechaber in seif 5 require him to return to the beginning of the blessing. With silence, however, even if he paused long enough to complete that blessing, he does not return to the beginning of the blessing, and certainly not when the pause was shorter. Otherwise, what measure could be assigned to such a rule? Speech and silence are therefore plainly not equivalent for every purpose. The Taz also challenges from siman 690, seif 5, where the Rama rules that one who spoke, meaning deliberately, does not return to the beginning even if he paused long enough to complete the entire Megillah. The Rama rules in simanim 65, 78, 88, and 84 like the Rosh and the Raavad, that no distinction is made between Krias Shema, the Amidah, Hallel, and the Megillah, but only between an unavoidable pause and another pause. Why, then, does he rule concerning the Megillah that deliberate speech does not require returning to the beginning? This question remains even if the Mechaber here is understood as discussing inadvertent speech. The Rashba there may be understood as distinguishing between Krias Shema and the other cases, but that does not resolve the matter according to our ruling. Magen Avraham, seif katan 6, distinguishes between Krias Shema and the Amidah, and the same distinction applies to the Megillah: deliberate speech does not require returning to the beginning of the Megillah, unlike the Amidah. The Taz appears to repeat his earlier question concerning the Raavad. He concludes that if one deliberately spoke between one blessing and the next, all agree that he begins the next blessing and nothing more is required. Even if he spoke in the middle of a blessing, it suffices to return to the beginning of that blessing. This is unlike siman 114, seif 7, because there he intentionally omitted “Retzei” in the blessing of Avodah and completed the blessing, and therefore they penalized him by requiring him to return to the beginning of the Amidah. Here, however, if he spoke in the middle of a blessing, returning to the beginning of that blessing suffices, for even if the speech were treated as though he had not recited that blessing at all, repeating it is enough. The question addressed to Rabbeinu Gershom concerned one who deliberately spoke in the middle of a blessing, completed that blessing, and continued praying. In that case they ruled that he must return to the beginning of the Amidah, because it then resembles the case in siman 114, seif 7. If the speech was inadvertent, then after he has moved his feet he returns to the beginning of the Amidah, while if he has not moved his feet he returns to the beginning of that blessing.
