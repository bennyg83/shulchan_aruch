“And even a snake, etc.” The reason is that in most cases it does not bite. This is stated by Rashi and Rambam in their commentaries to the Mishnah. Where there is concern that it may kill, a snake has the same law as a scorpion. Magen Gibborim in the name of the poskim.
“He should not interrupt.” See Mishnah Berurah, where we explained that this means interruption by speech. I explained the matter this way in accordance with the commentators on Shulchan Aruch, who understood it from the plain meaning of the gloss, which says that he may walk, etc. This interpretation, however, prompted the leading authorities, namely Taz and several other commentators, to object that the Mechaber contradicts himself. In seif 2 he writes that in another circumstance one may not leave his place, implying that walking is also considered an interruption. Magen Avraham answers that there it is considered unnecessary movement, although it was also somewhat for the sake of a mitzvah, namely bowing and prostrating during the prayer. Biur HaGra takes a different approach. In his view, the Tur and Shulchan Aruch do not accept the gloss, but hold that walking itself is also called an interruption, as written in seif 2 that in another circumstance one may not leave his place. Wherever movement is forbidden, even walking alone is forbidden. What is written here, that even for a snake he should not interrupt, means that he should not even walk from this place to another, since there is no danger. In practice, Biur HaGra does not decide which view is correct. It appears that one should act as I wrote in Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 10, that where there is a great need one may rely on those who are lenient and hold that walking is not considered an interruption, for several later authorities agree with them.
