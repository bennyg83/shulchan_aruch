Seif 3, “But he may walk, etc.” For we do not find that walking is called an interruption. This is the view of Rabbeinu Yonah, who explains “he should not interrupt” to mean by speech. He follows his own position, for he writes: “And in the Yerushalmi it says that even if a snake was coming toward him, he should move aside, and this is not an interruption.” The correct reading there is: “If it was threatening and coming toward him, he moves it away from before him, provided that he does not interrupt his prayer.” The word “moves it away” is used as in “from the side of the Mishkan.” This means that the ruling concerns specifically a snake already coiled around him, where walking will not help because it is already coiled around him. But if it is approaching him and he can move aside, he should do so, because walking is not an interruption. This is not because of danger, since an ordinary snake does not constitute danger. This is the meaning of “provided that he does not, etc.” Regarding Rabbi Akiva’s bowing there, he follows Tosafos’ explanation. Although he agreed with the Rosh’s explanation that this occurred after the prayer, because he found the prohibition of bowing difficult, as Tosafos did, according to the Rosh’s explanation cited in the preceding siman it would indeed constitute an interruption. Tosafos on 33a and the Rosh explain the Yerushalmi differently, that the animal was enraged and dangerous, as they wrote in Shulchan Aruch, “and likewise a snake, etc.” The phrase “provided that he does not, etc.” means that he can avoid it by walking, as stated there regarding a donkey and wagon, and as explained above regarding gentile kings.
