However, he may walk, etc. The Beit Yosef writes in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah that nowhere do we find walking defined as an interruption. This is difficult in light of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, which state that for another purpose one may not leave his place until he completes his Amidah. They further explain that the incident of Rabbi Akiva, when he prayed privately and a person would leave him in one corner and later find him in another because of his many bowings and prostrations, occurred during the supplications after the Amidah. The Rosh writes likewise. This implies that walking is an interruption. Rabbeinu Yonah must therefore disagree, and according to the Tur one should not be permitted to walk in order to cast away a snake when there is no danger. How, then, could the Rama cite Rabbeinu Yonah as the practical ruling? One cannot prove that Rabbeinu Yonah agrees with the Tur merely because he too explains Rabbi Akiva’s conduct as occurring during supplications after the Amidah. Rabbeinu Yonah gave that explanation because of another difficulty, namely that one may not prostrate oneself in the middle of the blessings, not because he inferred that one may not move during the Amidah. Tosafos likewise cite this answer to that difficulty and reject it because the passage refers to the Eighteen Blessings. They answer instead that one may bow in the middle of a blessing. Their words also imply that one may move during the Amidah for the purpose of prostration. The Rivash, responsum 332, adopts the position of the Tur and the Rosh and prohibits the chazzan on Yom Kippur from moving during the Amidah for the prostrations in the Avodah service, and the Rama cites this below. It is therefore astonishing that here the Rama rules like Rabbeinu Yonah that walking is not an interruption in the Amidah. This requires study. See what I wrote in siman 8, seif 13.
