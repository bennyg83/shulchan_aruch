However. See Taz. Rabbeinu Yonah follows Tosafos, Berachos 31a, that Rabbi Akiva bowed with his hands and feet extended, and therefore necessarily moved from his place. This would indicate that moving is permitted even during the Amidah, were it not for the rule that one may not bow at every point. Yet the Mechaber in siman 113, seif 1, rules that one may bow in the middle of a blessing, and nevertheless here in seif 2 he explains, following the Rosh, that one may not walk during the Amidah. Rabbeinu Yonah explained that the incident occurred after the Amidah because one may not bow at every point, and it is possible that Rabbeinu Yonah agrees with the Rosh. See there. See also siman 621, seif 4, and Taz, seif katan 3. Nevertheless, one may say that moving without need, merely in order to prostrate oneself, is forbidden during the Amidah. Likewise, a chazzan on Yom Kippur may not move from his place, and therefore a lectern is positioned for him, as is customary, so that he need not move. For a need, however, such as avoiding a snake, it is certainly permitted, as Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 3. Rabbeinu Yonah’s statement that walking is nowhere treated as an interruption means an interruption like speech that would require returning to the beginning of the blessing, as in seif 5. Rabbeinu Yonah follows his own view that walking is not called an interruption at all, unlike our ruling. See siman 8, seif 13, Taz, seif katan 11, and Magen Avraham, seif katan 17, that only moving from one house to another constitutes an interruption, but not moving within one house. Here it may be said that even moving from one house to another is not an interruption, with regard to avoiding a snake, in the same manner as speech.
