(s.k. 1) King, etc. — on account of monetary loss one need not interrupt — see siman 93, where Magen Avraham, s.k. 3, brought in the name of Semag that one interrupts only when there is danger to life; and so he brought these words of Semag above in siman 92, s.k. 2, and siman 108, s.k. 8, that one does not pass the time of prayer on account of monetary loss; and Magen Avraham holds the same regarding interrupting prayer — its law is thus.

In Avodah Zarah 57 there is a dispute among the poskim on the prohibition of "let him transgress and not be killed" — whether one may be stringent upon himself to be killed and not transgress.

And the incident of that pious man in Berachot 32b — a certain pious man prayed; an officer came and gave him peace; he did not respond; the officer waited until he finished his prayer; afterward they asked why he did not respond — is he not liable for his life? They appeased him with words and he was appeased, and the pious man departed in peace. Nevertheless, it is difficult for the poskim who hold one may be stringent upon himself in such a case — if so, why did he not respond? And perhaps the officer would not wait until after prayer to appease him, but would kill him immediately or not accept appeasement.

It should read: he was sure, etc. — meaning he recognized that officer, his nature and essence, and that he would not kill him until he asked why he did not respond; and he was intelligent in his nature and would accept appeasement — see there in Maharsha in Chiddushei Aggadot, who wrote a different explanation.

And so the plain custom, etc. — meaning regarding the prayer leader, as explained below, siman 123: when he prays in a loud voice he does not say at the end of prayer "May they be acceptable," etc., for he relies on "May their prayer be accepted" that is said in Kaddish after prayer; if so, all that the prayer leader says until he says Kaddish is like the supplications after prayer, and its law is like an individual's "Guard my tongue" that he says after prayer before "May they be acceptable"; and nevertheless the custom is that the prayer leader uproots his feet when he says Tachanun and sits — as explained in siman 131, seif 2, that it is fitting to sit at the time of saying Tachanun (and the same applies to one who goes up with the Torah scroll to the bimah).
