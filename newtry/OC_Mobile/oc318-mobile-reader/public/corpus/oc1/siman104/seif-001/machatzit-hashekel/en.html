(Seif katan 1) A non-Jewish king, etc. One need not interrupt because of monetary loss. See seif 3, where the Magen Avraham cites the Semag in seif katan 3 that one may interrupt only where there is danger to life. He likewise cites these words of the Semag above, siman 92 at the end of seif katan 2, and siman 108 at the end of seif katan 8, that one may not allow the time for prayer to pass because of monetary loss. The Magen Avraham maintains that the same rule applies to interrupting during prayer.
See Yoreh Deah, beginning of siman 157, where the poskim dispute whether, in the case of a transgression for which the law is that one should transgress rather than be killed, one may be stringent upon himself and allow himself to be killed rather than transgress.
Regarding the incident of that pious man in Berachos 32b, a pious man was praying when an official came and greeted him, and he did not respond. The official waited until he completed his prayer, afterward asked him why he had not responded, since he had endangered his life, and the pious man appeased him with words. The official was appeased, and the pious man departed in peace. Nevertheless, according to the poskim who maintain that one may be stringent upon himself in such a case, it remains difficult why he did not respond, since perhaps the official would not wait until after the prayer and allow him time to appease him, but would kill him immediately or refuse to accept his explanation.
It must be that he was confident, etc. Meaning, he knew the nature and character of that official, that he would not kill him before asking why he had not responded, and he also understood his temperament and knew that he would accept the explanation. See Maharsha, Chiddushei Aggados, who gives another answer.
And this is the widespread custom, etc. This refers to the chazzan. It is explained below, at the end of siman 123, that when he prays aloud he does not say at the end of the prayer, “May the words of my mouth be accepted,” because he relies upon “May their prayers be accepted,” which he will say in the Kaddish after the prayer. Accordingly, everything the chazzan says until Kaddish is comparable to the supplications after prayer, and its law is like that of an individual reciting Elokai Netzor after prayer, before “May the words of my mouth be accepted.” Nevertheless, the custom is that the chazzan moves his feet when reciting Tachanun and sits, as explained in siman 131, seif 2, that it is proper to sit while reciting Tachanun. The same applies when he ascends the bimah with the Sefer Torah.
