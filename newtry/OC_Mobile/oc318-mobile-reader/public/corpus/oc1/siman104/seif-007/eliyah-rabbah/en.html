[7] “If he paused long enough to complete, etc.” In Eliyahu Zuta I objected that the Bach disagrees with Shulchan Aruch here. He rules that one returns to the beginning only when the interruption resulted from a condition affecting the person himself, such as a place unfit for recitation because excrement was present, or urine flowing upon his knees. Since he should have taken care initially not to come to such a situation, he is regarded as negligent. Here, however, where the interruption arose from an external circumstance such as oxen or a wagon, he need not return to the beginning, because this is not called compulsion. Olas Tamid cites him this way. This is difficult, because above in siman 25 the Bach explicitly rules that there is a distinction between Krias Shema and prayer, and that in prayer one returns to the beginning even if the interruption was not caused by compulsion. Nachalas Tzvi there also rules in his name this way. This requires great study. The proof the Bach brings here from Tosafos, Berachos 33, who write that one does not return to the beginning, is also no proof in my humble opinion. Tosafos may follow the French rabbis cited by Beit Yosef in siman 65, who hold that even in prayer and even under complete compulsion one does not return to the beginning. Taz was later printed, and I saw that he objected to the Bach because Tosafos discuss a case where he did not pause long enough to complete the entire prayer, concerning which there is a dispute on page 23 according to Rav Ashi’s answer. I do not understand his reasoning. There, the view requiring one to return to the beginning is based on the fact that the person was unfit when he began to pray because he needed to relieve himself, as Rashi explains. Here, in the case of a scorpion, this does not apply, and Tosafos did not need to prove at all that he does not return to the beginning. This also resolves the objection of Levushei Yom Tov against Rama and Levush, who rule here without qualification, whereas in siman 68 they distinguish between an interruption caused by compulsion and one that was not. Lechem Chamudos also objects on pages 24, 27, and 40. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty, because there is a distinction between prayer and Krias Shema, and they rely upon the authorities who hold that in prayer one returns even without compulsion. I later saw that Levush writes in siman 85, regarding Krias Shema, that the same law applies to Hallel, prayer, and Megillah. This shows that he requires specifically an interruption caused by compulsion. Accordingly, it is obvious that Lechem Chamudos and Levushei Yom Tov are not objecting to Levush, for it is clear that here too he discusses compulsion and relies upon what he wrote above, as Levush explicitly writes in his glosses to simanim 65 and 85. Rama’s words are also resolved, for in siman 65 he explicitly writes, “And so it is in siman 104.” He therefore did not need to add a gloss here that the ruling applies only where there was compulsion. One could nevertheless say that his reference concerns how the duration is measured according to the reader. There is also proof from his writing in siman 23, seif 2, “See above, siman 85,” where he refers from the law of prayer to the law of Krias Shema, which implies that one returns to the beginning only where the interruption was caused by compulsion. Accordingly, one must say that here he discusses a condition affecting the person himself, as above in siman 65, whereas oxen and a wagon are not compulsion, as the Bach rules here. I saw, however, that Derishah writes that the Semag cited by Beit Yosef in siman 65 shows that anything not done voluntarily is called compulsion, unlike Beit Yosef’s citation of the Rashba that only where the person himself was disqualified is it called compulsion. According to this, Levush and Rama may indeed be speaking here even of oxen and a wagon, and one would return to the beginning. In truth, this requires study, for these are the words of Tosafos in Berachos 22, and there it is clear that the matter depends on whether the person was fit. This is also implied by the Tosafos rulings there, as well as by the Tur and Rabbeinu Yerucham. The language of the Rosh in the chapter Mi Shemeiso, however, implies the Derishah’s view, as does Abudraham. It further appears to me that even if we say that the matter depends upon the person’s fitness, one should nevertheless be stringent in prayer with every involuntary interruption, because according to the Rif and Rambam, if one paused long enough to complete the entire prayer he returns to the beginning even without compulsion. I found that Magen Avraham, siman 65, rules this way, especially according to my explanation there that this is also the Raavad’s view.
