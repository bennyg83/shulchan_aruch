(Seif katan 6) If he spoke. See siman 114, seif 7, that if he spoke deliberately, he need not.
And see Bach, meaning that the Beit Yosef disagreed with the responsum of Rabbi Gershom ben Shlomo. It is cited by the Taz, who ruled that speech constitutes an interruption, and he likewise ruled this way in the Shulchan Aruch. For the Beit Yosef himself ruled below, siman 114, that if one deliberately said “He causes the wind to blow” in summer, or “Grant dew and rain,” he returns to the beginning. Is saying “He causes the wind to blow” or “Grant dew and rain” less serious than ordinary speech? Therefore, the Bach disagreed with the Beit Yosef and ruled that one who deliberately spoke must also return to the beginning, and wrote that this was likewise the view of Rabbi Gershom ben Shlomo. Certainly, however, the Beit Yosef here maintains that even one who deliberately spoke does not return to the beginning, as is evident from what he wrote in his longer work regarding the words of Rabbi Gershom ben Shlomo. See there. Regarding the incident of Rabbi Abbahu, Berachos 24b, Rabbi Abbahu was walking behind Rabbi Yochanan and reciting Krias Shema. When he reached filthy alleyways he stopped until he left the alley. He then asked Rabbi Yochanan, “To where should I return?” Rabbi Yochanan answered, “If you paused long enough to complete all of it, return to the beginning.” Yet even had he not paused that long, he had spoken, since he asked where to return. It follows that during Krias Shema, even deliberate speech does not require returning to the beginning.
In Yoreh Deah, siman 99, seif katan 9, we rule that one may not intentionally nullify a forbidden substance. If he nullified it inadvertently it is permitted, but if deliberately, it is forbidden. If he thought it was permitted to nullify it and did so, he is considered inadvertent. See there. See also Makkos 7b, where the Sages taught concerning those liable to exile that the Torah states “unintentionally,” excluding one who acted deliberately. The Gemara asks that this is obvious, since such a person is liable to death, and Rava answers that it refers to one who says the act was permitted, for such a person is close to deliberate, and therefore the baraisa calls him deliberate. Tosafos explain that although regarding forbidden fat and Shabbos the Torah also says “unintentionally,” one raised among non-Jews who believes the act permitted is liable, since ordinarily one who says it is permitted is considered inadvertent. Exile is different because there is an additional verse excluding him. Thus, in general, one who believes an act permitted is considered inadvertent.
And in the Chiddushei Rashba at the beginning of the second chapter of Berachos, etc. He brings proof that regarding Krias Shema, even if one interrupted and paused long enough to complete it all, he has fulfilled his obligation. He writes that this is also implied by the incident involving Rabbi, Berachos 13b, who recited the first verse of Krias Shema and then taught his students. When he finished teaching, he returned and completed Krias Shema, according to Rabbi Shimon son of Rabbi. Yet even if the delay itself was not an interruption, the speech involved in teaching his students was certainly an interruption. It follows that speech during Krias Shema does not invalidate it.
Perhaps this applies specifically where he spoke words of Torah. These are his words concerning the incident of Rabbi Abbahu cited above, though one can distinguish somewhat between the two cases.
