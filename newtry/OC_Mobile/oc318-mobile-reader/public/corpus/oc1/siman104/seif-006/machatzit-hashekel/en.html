(s.k. 6) If he spoke — and see siman 114, seif 7: if he spoke intentionally, he need not.

And Bach — meaning the Mahari Abuhav argues on the responsum of the Rashba. It is brought in Taz that he ruled that speech is an interruption, and so he ruled in Shulchan Aruch. For the Mahari Abuhav himself ruled below in siman 114: if in the summer months he intentionally said "Who makes the wind blow" or "Who makes the rain fall," he returns to the beginning; and is saying "Who makes the wind blow" or "Who makes the rain fall" less than speech? Therefore Bach disagreed with the Mahari Abuhav and ruled that even speech intentionally requires returning to the beginning; and he wrote that such was the view of the Rashba. But certainly the Mahari Abuhav holds here that even if he spoke intentionally he does not return to the beginning, as is apparent from his words that he wrote here in his long work per the Rashba — see there. In the incident of R' Elazar, etc., 24b: R' Elazar would follow R' Yehoshua and read Keriat Shema; when he reached filthy alleys he would be silent until he exited. He said to R' Yehoshua: to where should I return? R' Yehoshua said: if he waited in order to finish it entirely, he returns to the beginning. And we learn: even if he did not wait in order to finish it entirely, it is speech; for they asked him "to where should I return" — perforce in Keriat Shema, even intentional speech, he need not return to the beginning.

Yoreh Deah 99, s.k. 9: we hold it is forbidden to cancel a prohibition ab initio; and if he cancelled unwittingly, it is permitted; and if he cancelled intentionally, it is forbidden. And if he thought it was permitted to cancel and cancelled, it is called unwitting — see there. And see in Makkot, beginning of chapter 2, 7b: the rabbis taught regarding those liable for exile, it is written "unwittingly," excluding intentional; and the Gemara challenges: it is obvious — he is liable for death. And Rava answers: perhaps one would say it is permitted — close to intentional; therefore the baraita called it intentional. And Tosafot wrote there, etc.: it is difficult regarding milk and regarding Shabbat it is written in both "unwittingly," and they are liable for a child captured among the nations, which is a case of saying it is permitted; and one may say here regarding exile is different, for it is written "unwittingly" extra to exclude saying it is permitted — until here. Behold, in general saying it is permitted is unwitting; and exile is different because there is an extra verse.

And in the chiddushim of the Rashba, beginning of Berachot, etc., that he brought proof that in Keriat Shema, even if he interrupted and waited in order to finish it entirely, he fulfilled; and these are his words: and so it is implied in that which is stated regarding Rabbi, 13b: he was reading one verse of Keriat Shema and teaching his students; and when he finished his lesson he returned and completed Keriat Shema — per R' Shimon ben Rabbi — until here. And it is difficult for him: the waiting is not an interruption; nevertheless the speech with which he taught his students is an interruption; perforce in Keriat Shema speech is not an interruption.

And it is possible specifically when he spoke words of Torah — and these are his words that he wrote above in the incident of R' Elazar; and one may distinguish somewhat between them.
