Seif 6. If one spoke during prayer, the law concerning whether he must repeat it is the same as for other interruptions, etc. Note: Beit Yosef understood the view of Abudarham to be that if one spoke, he returns to the beginning of all eighteen blessings. He wrote that most poskim disagree. Taz explains Abudarham's words at length. I am astonished at all of them, for how did they overlook the Gemara in Taanis 3b? There Rabbi Chanina states that in the rainy season, if one omitted 'Who makes the wind blow,' he is not required to repeat; if he omitted 'Who causes rain to fall,' he must repeat. Moreover, even if he said, 'Who removes the wind and drives away the dew,' he is not required to repeat. This is difficult. Even granting that his request is treated as nonexistent because he did not restrain the rain, nevertheless, since he mentioned words unrelated to the prayer, this should constitute an interruption in the middle of prayer. It is no less than ordinary speech, which is an interruption. Necessarily, if one spoke in the middle, even during the first three blessings, he does not return to the beginning, and certainly not during the other blessings. This is a clear proof. While considering this, I observed that the Rif records this statement, but Rambam and the Rosh omit the conclusion of the Gemara that if he said, 'Who removes the wind and drives away the dew,' he need not repeat. Why did they omit it? This requires study. A possible resolution is that they agree with Abudarham that one who speaks returns to the beginning, but they maintain that this depends on the dispute in Berachos concerning one who paused long enough to complete the entire prayer. According to the view that where the delay was caused by circumstances beyond his control he returns to the beginning after pausing long enough to complete the entire prayer, one who speaks returns to the beginning even without such a delay. Actual speech constitutes a direct interruption even without a prolonged pause, just as a silent pause long enough to complete the entire prayer constitutes an interruption. The two are equivalent. When one merely pauses without action, the pause counts as an interruption only if it lasts long enough to complete the whole prayer. Without that duration, it is not regarded as a pause. But when one makes an actual interruption through speech, even a shorter period constitutes an interruption. Therefore, according to the opinion that even a pause long enough to complete the whole prayer does not require returning, although the full measure of interruption occurred, interruption does not invalidate the prayer. Consequently, speech also does not require returning. According to the opinion that a pause long enough to complete the prayer requires returning, interruption does invalidate in the middle. A shorter silent pause is harmless only because it is too brief to count as an interruption. Actual speech, however, certainly constitutes an interruption, and therefore he returns even without a prolonged delay. Rambam and the Rosh accordingly understood that Rabbi Chanina followed the opinion in Berachos that even after pausing long enough to complete the entire prayer one does not return. The same would then apply to an interruption by speech. According to our ruling, however, that one does return after a pause long enough to complete the entire prayer, the same applies when he interrupts through speech even without such a pause. They therefore omitted this ruling, because he must in any event repeat due to the interruption. Abudarham thus has support from Rambam and the Rosh. The Rif, by contrast, follows the poskim who hold that one who spoke does not return, and therefore records the ruling. Although the Rif rules that regarding Krias Shema one does not return even after pausing long enough to complete it, he nevertheless holds that regarding prayer one does return to the beginning after such a pause. He decided the practical halachah between Rabbi Abbahu and Rabbi Yochanan by distinguishing that in prayer one returns, whereas in Krias Shema one does not. The disputants themselves, however, make no such distinction. According to the one who says he does not return, even in prayer he does not return, and according to the one who says he returns, even in Krias Shema he returns. The Rif adopted a compromise for practical halachah, that in Krias Shema one does not return but in prayer one does. This is well explained.
