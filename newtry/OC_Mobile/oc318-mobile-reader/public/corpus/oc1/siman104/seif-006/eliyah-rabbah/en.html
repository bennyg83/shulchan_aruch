[8] “If he spoke, etc.” Levush writes, “If he transgressed and spoke, etc.,” which clearly refers to intentional speech. This is also implied by Beit Yosef’s statement that this was an unauthorized interruption. It is therefore difficult that Knesses HaGedolah explains the case as one where he spoke because of compulsion, such as when he saw a king. How does he reconcile Beit Yosef and Levush? Perhaps he understands them to mean that he could have shortened the prayer or moved aside, as at the beginning of this siman, in which case he was forbidden to speak but nevertheless did so. It is still called compulsion, though this explanation is strained. In any event, we learn from his words that one who speaks with full intent must return to the beginning even if he did not pause long enough to complete the entire prayer, as stated below in siman 114, seif 6. The Bach also rules this way, and this is the primary halachah. According to my explanation, all of Taz’s objections against Beit Yosef and Bach are resolved, and his lengthy discussion was unnecessary. Examine this carefully. Taz’s extended explanation of Abudraham, that the case is one where the person did not begin again from the start of the blessing but resumed where he had stopped, completed the prayer, and stepped back, and must therefore pray again from the beginning, is also forced. Nor does this appear correct as a matter of halachah. The Rashba disputes the basic ruling that one must return to the beginning of the blessing, holding that he need return only to the place where he stopped, as Beit Yosef writes, just as regarding Krias Shema in siman 64. We certainly should not be more stringent and require him to return to the beginning of the entire prayer on that account. Taz also objects from siman 114 and says that the reason will be explained there, but he does not explain it. Olas Tamid writes that the case there is worse because he changed the order of the prayer. This is unconvincing, for the Tur and Shulchan Aruch there write, “Whenever they said that he returns to the blessing in which he erred, this applies when the error was inadvertent, etc.” Perhaps the phrase “in which he erred” does not, in his view, include ordinary speech during prayer. Olas Tamid further rules that even after intentional speech one need not return because in cases of doubtful blessings we are lenient. This is difficult. The reason for leniency in doubtful blessings is that blessings are D'Rabbanan, yet he himself writes below in siman 107 that prayer is D'Oraisa and therefore a doubt is treated stringently. See Lechem Chamudos, page 23, and his proofs there. This requires study. If he interrupts between one blessing and another, it is clear that he resumes from the place where he stopped.
