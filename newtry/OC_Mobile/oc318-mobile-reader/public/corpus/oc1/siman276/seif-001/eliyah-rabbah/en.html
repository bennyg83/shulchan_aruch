[Levush] Techumin are d'rabbanan, etc. Magen Avraham adds that what lies outside one person's techum may lie within another person's techum, unlike other d'rabbanan prohibitions. He overlooked Rabbenu Yerucham, netiv 12 part 12, who rejects this and concludes that with all d'rabbanan prohibitions another Jew may benefit. Ran's view here is also a solitary one. See Beit Yosef.
[Levush] Whether he fixed a price, etc. Beit Yosef states this plainly in the name of Sefer HaTerumot, Semag, and Hagahot Ashri. This requires study, because Ran in the first chapter of Shabbos and Shibbolei HaLeket permit where a fixed price was set, as I wrote in siman 252 seif katan 9. I also found in Maharshal's commentary to Semag that there is no proof from the Gemara and many disagree, permitting when the payment was fixed; on this basis the people practice leniency. I am surprised at his claim that many disagree, because Mordechai and Agudah in the first chapter of Shabbos, Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Kol Bo follow Semag and Sefer HaTerumot. Moreover, when the work is done in the Jew's house, a fixed price helps according to no opinion, as I concluded at the end of siman 244. This also answers Maharshal's objection that there is no basis in the Gemara, because the case of work in the Jew's house is explicit in the Yerushalmi, as Beit Yosef writes in simanim 244 and 252.
He did it under a standing arrangement, etc. Meaning the non-Jew was hired generally to light a lamp whenever needed. A salaried arrangement means he is paid a set amount each day for the Jew's needs. If he regularly uses evasions, it is prohibited, as Piskei Tosafot in chapter Kol Kitvei writes.
[Levush] Some say the same applies, etc. Bach argues at length against Beit Yosef and Shulchan Aruch, who understand Sefer HaTerumot and Semag to permit benefiting from a bonfire made for a non-Jew or a sick person, just as they permit benefiting when a non-Jew added many logs for himself. Bach says this is incorrect: where a permitted fire was already burning before Shabbos we do not suspect he will add more, but when he prepared a fire for his own need we do suspect he may add for the Jew, and it is prohibited according to all. Taz labors to defend Beit Yosef with strained distinctions. I am surprised that these great authorities misunderstood Beit Yosef. Sefer HaTerumot siman 252 explicitly writes, after the law of a non-Jew who added: similarly, if a non-Jew made a fire for a child to warm himself or for a sick person, a healthy person may also benefit. Semag says likewise. Mordechai in the first chapter of Shabbos goes further: even those who prohibit where the non-Jew added for the Jew permit where he made the fire for a sick person. The reason appears that in the first case we actually see that he added for the Jew, while in the latter we do not suspect he will add merely because the Jew benefits. Bach in seif katan 4 understands Mordechai as speaking where the non-Jew added specifically for the sick person, and Magen Avraham follows him, but this is clearly not the text. Sefer HaTerumot, Semag, the simanim of Sefer HaTerumot, and Kol Bo all say simply "if he made," confirming my explanation. Bach also proves from Maharam, who was stringent when the non-Jew made a fire yet permitted if a fire was lawfully lit before Shabbos and the non-Jew added on Shabbos. I do not understand the proof. Maharam explicitly speaks where the non-Jew made the fire for a Jew, which is prohibited by the Mishnah. If he made it for himself or for a sick person, Maharam may well permit. See seif katan 12. Shibbolei HaLeket siman 30 cites Rabbenu Tam: if a non-Jew made a bonfire and increased the flame so a Jew could warm himself, it is prohibited because every stirring of the coals for the Jew is forbidden. This sounds as though the non-Jew first made the bonfire for himself and then increased it for the Jew. Precisely because the increase is prohibited, the Jew may benefit before he increases it.
By a bonfire, etc. Beit Yosef asks from siman 325 seif 10, where benefit is permitted when the non-Jew does not know the Jew. He answers that here, because adding heat requires only the slight effort of stirring coals, we are concerned even when they are not acquainted. He further distinguishes that there the Jew waters his animal afterward, whereas if both animals drank simultaneously, comparable to Jew and non-Jew warming together here, there would be concern. This is difficult, because then the Gemara in Shabbos 122 should have stated this distinction: perhaps the permission there is specifically when the Jew waters afterward, while simultaneous use would be prohibited; the lamp case would not teach this. Bach, Magen Avraham, and Nachalat Tzvi hold that here too it is permitted when the non-Jew is known not to be acquainted with the Jew, and this appears primary. I found the same in Tashbetz siman 33 and Kol Bo page 31. Olat Tamid and Magen Avraham write that the concern is specifically with a bonfire, but if the non-Jew heated an oven everyone agrees that a Jew may enter the heated room, like the rule that one lamp serves a hundred.
