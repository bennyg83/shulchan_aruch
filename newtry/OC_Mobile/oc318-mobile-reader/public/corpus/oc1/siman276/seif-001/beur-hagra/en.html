"Even for one," etc. This is analogous to the opening case, where one may make use of the light because it was not kindled for him. See Tosafos there, and Rashi and Tosafos in Beitzah 25a. "And there is no distinction," etc. This is analogous to the case of a non-Jew who comes to extinguish a fire, where the Sages say that the non-Jew acts on his own initiative. It must therefore be understood that here the prohibition is because the Jew derives benefit from the melacha itself, and even when the non-Jew acts on his own initiative it is forbidden. Independently of this point, contracting for work performed in a Jew's house is prohibited, as stated in the Yerushalmi cited by Tosafos at the beginning of Shabbos and by other authorities. See Hagahot Mordechai, beginning of Shabbos, siman 24, at length. "Since the Jew," etc. Meaning that this differs from sending a letter, where the Jew does not derive benefit from the act of transportation itself, but only from the letter reaching its destination. "Or for the need of an ill person." See Chullin 15b concerning slaughtering, and likewise concerning a person who is ill but not dangerously so in Eruvin. The principal ruling is like the final approach there, as in Chullin concerning food cooked for an ill person. Tosafos in Gittin explain that only for brit milah did the Sages permit increasing the quantity; see there. "Or for the need of small children." See Ran at the beginning of Yoma, who explains that this is because such food is their ordinary need, unlike Rashba, who sought to prove from there that one may always directly feed children a rabbinic prohibition. See below, siman 343. The phrase "for the need of small children" means what they genuinely need in significant measure. Otherwise there is no such permission, as appears in Eruvin. "And some prohibit." This follows the distinction stated there, that the leniency applies where the non-Jew does not know the Jew. The first view holds that, regarding a fire, there is no distinction between a fire kindled for 1 and for 100. "However, if he did," etc. When the Jew sees the non-Jew doing the melacha, he must protest. Even in the non-Jew's own house the Yerushalmi says that if the Jew sees him performing the work he must protest when it is being done without payment. Here there is no distinction between gratuitous work and paid work, as explained above. This is all the more evident because in the extinguishing case, when the Jew does not see the non-Jew, benefit is permitted and he need not tell him not to extinguish, since the non-Jew acts for himself. Here, by contrast, because the Jew benefits from the melacha itself, he must protest. See Hagahot Mordechai there, where this is discussed at length.
