(15) If the majority are non-Jews - For presumably he acts with the majority in mind, even if afterward Jews became more numerous and were added to them, or the non-Jews left; and likewise the reverse when the majority are Jews.
(16) Half and half - The reason is that it is impossible to determine whether he did it for the non-Jew or for the Jew. Some say because when it is half and half, presumably it was done for both.
(17) For the need of a non-Jew - Meaning for his own need, as he concludes at the end. The same applies if there is proof that he lit it for some other non-Jew. If we know that he did it also for a Jew, it is forbidden; so wrote Magen Avraham. See Biur Halachah, where we discussed this at length.
(18) That he uses - Meaning immediately.
(19) Although, etc. - For presumably when he lit it initially, he too acted primarily with himself in mind, and a lamp for one is a lamp for a hundred.
(20) For the Shabbat meal - This means he is sitting in darkness and has no lamp at all with which to eat. But if he finished his meal, even though he has not yet recited Birkat Hamazon, or if he has one lamp, it is obvious that it is forbidden to command a non-Jew to light for him, for it is a shevut not in a mitzvah context. However, if he has some lamp and transgressed and commanded a non-Jew to light another lamp for him, he may benefit from it while the first lamp is burning, as written in seif 4.
(21) Amirah to a non-Jew - But a shevut performed by a Jew himself is forbidden according to everyone, even for a mitzvah.
(22) For a mitzvah - Regarding building a synagogue on Shabbat through a non-Jew, Magen Avraham wrote that it is forbidden even according to Ba'al HaItur. See Peri Megadim, who concludes that this is because it is a mitzvah that does not pass, since he can build it on a weekday. See siman 244 in Magen Avraham, that it is forbidden to do this even by contract work unless there is concern that, Heaven forbid, because of this the synagogue construction may be entirely canceled; then one may be lenient with contract work. Perhaps even by hiring it is permitted in such a case if the non-Jew does not want to accept contract work, as Peri Megadim writes.
(23) And one does not protest - It is better that they be inadvertent and not intentional, as Bach and Magen Avraham write.
(24) For there is not, etc. - Shelah was stringent even for a great need, and wrote that he saw this practiced in important communities, where they sit in darkness on Motzaei Shabbat even at a wedding until the congregation says Barkhu. So too the Gaon Rabbi Shmuel practiced when they ate seudah shelishit at his home. Peri Megadim inclines to be lenient to light through a non-Jew on Motzaei Shabbat for a mitzvah need even while it is still bein hashmashot, and all the more on Friday during bein hashmashot for a mitzvah need, which is certainly permitted, as below in siman 342; see there.
(25) For the majority, etc. Nevertheless, it is permitted to tell a non-Jew to repair an eruv that was ruined on Shabbat so that many not come to stumble. Ba'al HaItur is worthy to rely upon to permit the shevut of amirah even for a Torah-level labor in the context of a public mitzvah, as Chayei Adam writes in the name of Maggid Mishneh. Similar to this is the view of Peri Megadim above at the end of seif katan 22.
(26) And see below - Meaning that there too it is explained not like Ba'al HaItur.
