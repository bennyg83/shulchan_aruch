But after it is extinguished, etc. According to the authorities who permit the case mentioned immediately above, where one adds to a fire that was initially kindled permissibly, they certainly do not accept this distinction. Rather, here too it is permitted even after the first light is extinguished. Regarding a fire, it is certainly impossible to know when the original fire has been consumed after additional fuel was added and it continues burning. We must therefore say that since it was initially permitted, that permission is not subsequently impaired, because we follow the initial state. The Tur and the other authorities who prohibit do not follow the initial state, and they hold that a prohibition now confronts us after the addition. Accordingly, here the permission lasts only while the first light continues burning. In this case there is a definite point at which the prohibition begins, because everyone can see when the first light is extinguished. This is unlike a fire, as explained above. Therefore, regarding a fire they prohibit even before additional fuel is added, since a future prohibition is already before us and we are concerned for it, because there is no definite and ascertainable point at which the prohibition will begin. It follows that there are two disputes here. First, regarding bonfires, those who prohibit do so even while the original permission still exists, whereas those who permit hold that no prohibition arises at all, since the fire began permissibly. Regarding a lamp, those who prohibit hold that permission lasts only while the first light burns, whereas those who permit allow benefit from the second light even after the first is extinguished. These two disputes share one underlying reason, namely, whether we follow the initial state. There is accordingly a difficulty with the Shulchan Aruch. In seif 1 it cites a dispute, whereas here it rules without qualification like the prohibiting authorities. The Beit Yosef likewise writes that after the first light is extinguished, all agree that it is forbidden, since the lamp was lit for a Jew. This is astonishing. Why should this differ from a fire, where it is impossible to know when the original fire has ended, yet the permitting authorities allow benefit? Moreover, the permitting authorities never mention that a prohibition begins after the first light is extinguished. They certainly would not have remained silent had they intended to prohibit it. Hagahos Maimoniyos writes: I remember that when I was with my teacher in the tower at Wurzburg on erev Shabbos, we made a fire so that we could warm ourselves beside it on Shabbos. After we had sat there until the fire was almost spent, the servants came and enlarged it, expressly stating that they had done so for our comfort. We remained seated beside it and rejoiced over the matter. End quote. The teacher mentioned there was Maharam, who is among the permitting authorities cited above. I copied this because a great authority acted permissibly in practice. Therefore, although we rule like the prohibiting authorities, as the Shulchan Aruch rules here in accordance with the Tur regarding lamps, that benefit is forbidden after the first light is extinguished, nevertheless, in a case of great need, where Shabbos enjoyment depends upon it, one who is lenient regarding lamps even after the first light is extinguished, and likewise regarding a bonfire, without being concerned that the non-Jew may add fuel, has authorities upon whom to rely. This appears correct to me. Even according to the Tur, who prohibits after the first light is extinguished, this applies only when the sole basis for permission was the burning of the first light. If there is an independent basis for permission, benefit is permitted at all times, even after the needs of the non-Jew or of a sick person have already been met.
