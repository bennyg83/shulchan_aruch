[4] From six hours, etc. Since I have seen a dispute among major early authorities that Beit Yosef did not mention, I must record it. Rokeach, siman 54, writes that one fulfills the three meals with a dessert or side dish eaten during the morning meal that one does not ordinarily eat on weekdays, such as fish or fruit, even before midday. Mordechai and Agudah cite Raavyah similarly. Behag and Ran write that only Birkat HaMazon constitutes a sufficient interruption, but they nevertheless agree that one can fulfill the third meal before midday, provided bread is eaten. Kol Bo, p. 41, Teshuvot Maharam, siman 23, Shibbolei HaLeket, and Tanya cite this view in the name of Targum Yerushalmi. Shibbolei HaLeket, however, records a custom that one does not fulfill the obligation before four hours, adducing the Mishnah that when Erev Pesach falls on Shabbos one leaves food for two meals. Sefer Yere'im writes that "two meals" means two quantities sufficient for satiation, and those two quantities should be divided into three meals. This is surprising, since Shabbos 117b teaches that on Friday night one rescues food for three meals. Why does it not similarly say food for two meals? Perhaps even this view agrees that initially one should eat the third meal after six and a half hours, corresponding to three distinct periods of satiation, and only when this is impossible may one divide the morning meal. More likely, Sefer Yere'im holds that the Erev Pesach source is inconclusive, but the Mishnah in Shabbos itself provides decisive proof, for its latter clause says that at Minchah one rescues food for one meal and washes utensils then, showing that the third meal's proper time is at Minchah. This resolves Tosafot on Shabbos 118a. Rosh, Rabbenu Yerucham, Tzedah LaDerech, and Abudraham therefore rule that one does not fulfill it before Minchah. Shibbolei HaLeket likewise cites Rabbi Yaakov of Marvege, who posed a dream question and received the answer from the verse, "Moshe said: Eat it today, etc." Just as the days are distinct from one another, so too the hours of the three meals must be distinct. Sefer Chasidim, siman 861, states that one who has food for only two meals, and dividing it into three would leave him unsatisfied at each meal, should rather eat two meals, one at night and one by day; alternatively, when eating during the day, after eating enough to require a blessing he should recite Birkat HaMazon and then eat again. The words "or he should eat during the day" imply that he should not eat at night but reserve both meals for the day. This requires study, since the nighttime meal is preferable to the third meal: according to all opinions it requires bread, and if one errs in Birkat HaMazon at night he repeats it, unlike at the third meal, as stated in siman 188, seif 8. It is also difficult to explain the phrase "after eating enough to require a blessing." Perhaps it is stating the law that Birkat HaMazon creates an interruption, following Behag, but this is strained. It appears more likely that Sefer Chasidim means that preferably he should not divide the meal, but if he wishes to do so, after eating an olive sized or egg sized amount, sufficient to require Birkat HaMazon, he should bless and then eat again, as Sefer Yere'im states. Magen Avraham quotes this briefly without noting these issues. He also disagrees and says it is preferable to divide the meal. It appears to me that Sefer Chasidim's reason is that it is prohibited to divide the morning meal in half. This also appears to be the position of Tosafot, Rosh, and their school, as is explicit in Sefer HaManhig, siman 63, contrary to Magen Avraham's understanding that according to all opinions there is no prohibition in dividing the morning meal and that the only concern is the preference to eat the third meal after Minchah. That is incorrect. What Magen Avraham writes at the beginning of siman 444, that on Erev Pesach one should divide the meal before the fourth hour and after Minchah eat fish or fruit, as Bach also writes, attempts to satisfy all opinions. According to my explanation, however, this stringency produces a leniency because it entails dividing the morning meal. Rama and Levush at the end of this siman and beginning of siman 444 also imply that one should not divide it. I further found in the margin of a parchment manuscript of Mordechai Katan a full responsum from his teacher Rabbi Yosef Bechor Shor. Its essence is that one should primarily rely on the side dish eaten at the morning meal. The proofs cited above that imply the meal is eaten at Minchah concern one who fulfills the third meal with bread, for he cannot do so before Minchah even if he interrupts with Birkat HaMazon, since all is considered one meal together with the morning meal. It is also not an honor to Shabbos to divide the meal, and the blessing is unnecessary when they have not actually finished their meal. Although we follow Rosh and the authorities I cited, this nevertheless supports Sefer Chasidim as I explained, and the approach of Tosafot and Rosh. See Hagahot Tashbetz, siman 23. Magen Avraham further writes that according to all opinions, if one began before midday and continued eating past midday, he fulfills the obligation because he at least ate after midday. In my humble opinion this is not clear, because once he begins before midday it is considered part of the morning meal. We also rule in siman 188 that the status follows the beginning of the meal, and this is implied by Tosafot and Rosh's proofs from the rule that at Minchah one rescues food, etc. Nevertheless, immediately after six hours it appears that he fulfills the obligation, as implied by Rokeach at the end of siman 54. Magen Avraham also writes that if it is known that his meal will continue past midday it is prohibited, as stated at the beginning of siman 471. At first glance that case is different because there the concern is the evening meal. If his reason is because of Minchah, this too requires study. See siman 232, seif katan 4.
[5] [Levush] His hands immediately, etc. Maharil, Hilchot Sukkah, writes that he should rise, walk about, and wait a little so that he mentally disengages from the first meal; otherwise it is considered a single meal. Malbushei Yom Tov and all the later authorities write likewise. Maharshal says that he should interrupt briefly with words of Torah or a short walk. Shibbolei HaLeket writes that his father would make a slight interruption by reciting a short chapter of Tehillim, such as "Hashem is my shepherd, I shall not lack."
