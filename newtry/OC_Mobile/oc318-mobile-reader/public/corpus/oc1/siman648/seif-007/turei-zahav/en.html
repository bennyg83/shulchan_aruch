Its dad was removed. In the Gemara: if its pitom was removed, it is invalid. Rabbi Yitzchak bar Elazar taught: if its pestle was removed. Rashi explained: its pestle, because it is sharp and made like a kind of pestle; and if its stem was removed, it is valid, meaning its tail. These are the words of my elder teacher, Rabbeinu Yaakov. But Rabbi Yitzchak HaLevi explained that its pitom and its stem are both at the tail: its stem, where the wood outside the depression in the esrog was removed, is valid; its pitom, where the stem was removed from within the esrog and made it deficient, is therefore invalid. This is what Rabbi Yitzchak bar Elazar taught: its pestle was removed, meaning that which enters into the esrog like a pestle that enters and strikes in a mortar. The language of Rabbeinu Yaakov appears correct to me. End quote. The explanation of Rabbeinu Yaakov's words appears to me to be as follows: the dad of the esrog is sharp and thin and made like a pestle, meaning that it has the shoshanta at its head, and this is like the pestle of a mortar, which has at its head a kind of round knob with which one strikes. The Rosh wrote: according to Rabbeinu Yaakov's explanation, there is no invalidity in the stem at all, even if it was torn away and its place remained a depression, because nothing was missing from the body of the esrog; and according to Rabbi Yitzchak HaLevi's explanation, there is no invalidity in the pitma at the head of the esrog. Rabbeinu Tam explained that both are at the head of the esrog, where a kind of protruding stem emerges from the point of the esrog, hard like wood. 'Its pitom was removed' means its pestle, that is, that stem was removed from its place of attachment and there remained a kind of depression at the head of the esrog. According to this there is no invalidity in the tail of the esrog. In the Aruch, in the name of Rabbeinu Chananel, he explained: pitma is dad, and it is the nose, and if its pestle was removed, this means the wood placed in the esrog. It implies that its pitma and its pestle are two matters: one at its head and one at its stem, and Rabbi Yitzchak came to add to the mishnah, not to explain it. So wrote the Rif and the Rambam. We therefore practice to invalidate in both cases when there was a pitma at the head of the esrog and it was removed, etc.; and likewise, if the stem was removed and none of it remained in the esrog, it is invalid, but if any amount of it remained, it is valid. End quote. The Beit Yosef wrote: since the Rosh distinguishes regarding the stem between whether some of it remained in the esrog and whether none remained, but does not distinguish regarding the pitma, it implies that whenever a little of the pitma was removed, even though the depression remained full, it is invalid. I do not recognize this implication, for the Rosh wrote: and likewise, if the stem was removed and none of it remained at all in the esrog, it is invalid, but if any amount of it remained, it is valid. End quote. This implies that the removal of both is equal. The reason he did not mention by the pitma that none of it remained at all in the esrog is because it is not a fixed matter that there is a depression there; many times there is no depression at the head of the esrog, but it is sloped and sharp, and at its head the dad, which is the pitma, is attached. Plain 'removed' means all of it. Furthermore, by the stem there is certainly removal in every esrog, for they cut it there from the tree, and therefore he needed to mention until what point the removal invalidates. So the Beit Yosef wrote explicitly in the name of Orchot Chayim: but if the shoshana alone was removed, or the stem alone, or both, and the two pestles remain, it is valid; so it is proper to rule and to act, and no more. End quote. The Beit Yosef also wrote that this is implied by the words of the Ran. So too the words of the Rambam, which are the language of the Shulchan Aruch here, 'its dad was removed,' imply that plain 'removed' means all of it. So too the language of the Maggid Mishneh, who wrote: from the language of our master it implies that it is not invalid through removal of the shoshana alone, but through removal of the dad, which is the wood upon which the shoshana is; and this is primary, and so it is proper to rule, etc. End quote. This implies that he means removal of the whole dad, for if it were invalid when only part was removed, then it would be invalid when the shoshana was removed, since the shoshana is part of the body of the dad; if it is removed, some of the dad has been removed. Why should removal of a little of the dad after the shoshana be more severe than that part upon which the shoshana sits? Rather, certainly he is speaking of removal of the entire dad as invalidating, not removal of part of it. So too is the implication of the Rema's words here, that he invalidates when the dad is removed, meaning all of it, as it appears to my humble opinion.
And some are stringent that if the shoshanta was removed, etc. There is no explicit opinion about this, except that the Rif wrote: the explanation of its pitom is the dad of the esrog, and it is its nose, as we learned regarding the pitma of a pomegranate, and it is the shoshanta. End quote. The Ran wrote that according to the Rif the pitma is the shoshana itself, but this does not appear correct to me, etc. The Beit Yosef wrote that even according to the Rif, the invalidating matter is the dad itself and not the shoshana, for he says 'dad,' and it is its nose, and the shoshana is not called dad, but nose. Therefore, that which the Rif said, 'and it is its shoshanta,' means that the dad itself is called its shoshanta because it has a shoshana, and it is called by its name. So too one can explain the language of the Tur, who wrote: its pitma was removed, and this is the flower at its head, etc.; meaning the dad itself is called by the name of the flower that is at the head of the dad. This also appears from his language in the remazim, where he wrote: if its pitma was removed it is invalid, and it is the flower at its head that protrudes from the head of the esrog, etc. If he meant the flower itself, which is the shoshana, he should not have said 'that protrudes from the head of the esrog,' but should have said 'that protrudes from the head of the dad.' Rather, certainly he is speaking of the dad itself and calls it flower. It appears to my humble opinion that if we have before us an esrog that is beautiful in its other qualities, except that it lacks the shoshana, or a little of the upper dad was removed, and another esrog that is not beautiful in its other qualities but has a shoshana, one should not take that shoshana into account. All the more so where there is doubt whether it ever had a shoshana at all. Since the Ran, the Maggid Mishneh, and the Beit Yosef in the name of Orchot Chayim wrote that this is the proper practical ruling, they certainly are fit to rely upon for practical halachah.
