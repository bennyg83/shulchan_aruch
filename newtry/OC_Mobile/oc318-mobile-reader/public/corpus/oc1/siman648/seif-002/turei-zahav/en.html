If any amount is missing, even if the hole does not go through, it is invalid. The 'some say' is the Ra'avad, who holds that we require two defects: a through-and-through hole and deficiency, for without deficiency it is valid in every case. Only where there is deficiency is there a distinction: if it is through-and-through, it is invalid even with any amount of deficiency; and if it is not through-and-through, specifically the size of an issar. We rule stringently except in pressing circumstances, as the Rema writes afterward.
Even though there is deficiency in them, for this is their manner of growth. He derives this in Terumat HaDeshen from the tereifah law of a lung: if there is a deficient lung that has a wrinkle and a crack, if it rises with inflation and the skin and flesh remain intact, it is valid. This is like an esrog that became this way at the time of its growth, with its skin and flesh intact, and it is valid. But anything missing from it after it was detached cannot have its skin and body intact. End quote. His intent is clear: we see that the skin of the esrog has formed over the place of that deficiency, only that there is a deficiency and depression there; in this case he permitted it. But if we see that it has not formed over, but rather there is a hole and deficiency there, even though it was made by thorns in its manner of growth, it is invalid. This is why he concluded: however, if he sees, etc. Those thorns neither raise nor lower anything regarding the law of a hole; he mentioned them in Terumat HaDeshen only because then it is common that the skin remains intact in that hole. The masses err in this, thinking there is a leniency when the hole was made by thorns.

This implies that if there is doubt whether the flesh remains intact, we go leniently, since the latter view, which is the Ra'avad, holds that even if there is certainly a deficiency it is valid whenever it is not through-and-through and the deficiency is not the size of an issar. We may therefore properly rely on it in a case of doubt at least. For this reason it appears to me that the Rema wrote here, 'and in pressing circumstances one may be lenient like the latter view,' and did not write it immediately after the dispute, in order to teach here that there is prohibition only when one sees that the flesh is not intact; but in a case of doubt it is valid. For in pressing circumstances we rely on the latter view even when the flesh is certainly not intact. It appears obvious to me that this speaks where, in any case, it was not punctured until the seed chambers; for otherwise, even if the flesh remains intact, there is invalidity because of a through-and-through hole, as nearby. And if there is doubt whether it reaches the seed chambers, it is also valid, since there is no deficiency at all, for the reason I mentioned: according to the Ra'avad it is valid even with a through-and-through hole whenever there is no deficiency at all, as it appears to me in this matter.
