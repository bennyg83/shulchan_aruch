(There, se'if 11) If it is half and half in one place, etc. Note: see what I wrote in the Taz, that the inferences in the mishnah are difficult because they contradict each other: at first it states that if it arose on most of it, it is invalid, implying that half and half is valid; and afterward it states that if it arose on a minority of it, it is valid, implying that half and half is invalid; see there. In my humble opinion it seemed possible to resolve this as follows. A deficient esrog is valid on the other days, and there are differing views about this below. In the view of the Ran brought in the gloss below, it is proven that he holds that specifically if most of it remains intact it is valid, but not when half and half remains. From the words of Mahari that the Magen Avraham brought, it implies that he holds that even if only a minority remains intact it is valid. In my humble opinion it would seem to decide that if most of it was removed and only a minority remains intact, it is invalid like the view of the Ran, for in that case his reason applies well, that it is not called an esrog but only pieces of esrog. That is, since most of it was removed and only a minority remains, it is called only a piece of esrog, and therefore it is invalid. But if half remains, since in any event most of it was not removed, it is not called a piece from an esrog, since the majority was not removed; in this the law follows Mahari, that it is valid. Therefore it works well to say that there is a distinction here between the first day of Yom Tov and the other days. On the other days we require specifically that it arose on most of it. But with half and half, even if from the standpoint of boil-like discoloration it invalidates even at half and half, nevertheless it is valid, for it is no worse than if it were removed, and is considered in any event as though it were absent. Since if it had been removed from the esrog it would be valid, even though it has not yet been removed it is valid, for anything in one's power is not considered lacking an act, as the poskim wrote regarding a Torah scroll on Shabbat, that if it is in his power to repair it, it is valid. So too here. But on the first day of Yom Tov, when a deficient esrog is invalid, then whichever way you view it: if we judge it as it is now, even at half and half it is not beautiful; and if we judge it as though it were removed, it is again deficient and invalid. Therefore it is valid only when it is on a minority, for then the name of boil-like discoloration is not upon it. Therefore the mishnah stated the clear case: if boil-like discoloration arose on most of it, it is invalid even on the second day of Yom Tov, for this is invalid whichever way you view it, as above; and if it arose on a minority of it, it is valid even on the first day of Yom Tov. But with half and half there is a distinction: on the first day of Yom Tov it is invalid, and on the second day of Yom Tov it is valid. Although the implication of the poskim does not appear to make this distinction, nevertheless the reasoning indicates as I wrote. It would further appear possible to say that since the laws of esrog are compared to tereifot, as stated in the Talmud and Tosafot on daf 36, if so one can say the same here: since by tereifot, two defects render it a tereifah, here too by esrog it is so. Therefore, if boil-like discoloration arose on most of it, it is invalid even without another defect joining it. But on a minority it is valid if there is no other defect. If there is another defect, it is invalid even when that joins with a minority of boil-like discoloration. Therefore at first it states that if boil-like discoloration arose on most of it, it is invalid even without another defect; and afterward it states that if boil-like discoloration arose on a minority of it, it is valid, even though there is another defect, namely that its stem was removed, for even with all these defects joined it is valid. But at half and half, if there is no other defect it is valid, but together with another defect it is invalid. Now Tosafot wrote in this sugya, daf 35b, s.v. alatah chazazit al rubo, that from the Yerushalmi it sounds somewhat that it is invalid specifically on the first day of Yom Tov, as I explained at the beginning of the first chapter. I am puzzled why they brought this Yerushalmi, since our Talmud there implies the opposite, from that which it asks: on the contrary, in two or three places it appears spotted and is invalid; and it concludes that it refers to the latter clause, etc. It is difficult: since in the end one must emend and reverse the reading from valid to invalid, why must it further answer that it refers to the latter clause? It could have remained as at first, that it refers to the first clause, only that the intent is as follows: since the mishnah mentioned specifically boil-like discoloration together with punctured and deficient of any amount, it implies that it is invalid only on the first day of Yom Tov, like punctured, and as Tosafot wrote at the end of the chapter. Therefore it properly said: they taught this only in one place, for then it is invalid only on the first day of Yom Tov and valid on the second day; but in two or three places, it is spotted and invalid even on the second day of Yom Tov. For from the language of the Talmud it is proven that two or three places are worse than one place on most of it, since it asks with the language 'on the contrary.' If so, it should have answered this way, that in two or three places it is invalid even on the second day of Yom Tov. Since it does not answer this way, it is proven that it was obvious to it that boil-like discoloration on most of it is invalid all seven days. If so, why did Tosafot bring the words of the Yerushalmi here? This requires investigation. According to the above, it appears to explain the language of the Yerushalmi, which says: Rabbi Yosi said in the name of Rabbi Shmuel: all invalidations invalidate only on the first day of Yom Tov. There they say: and most of it on one side, etc. At first glance it seems from his words that in two or three places it is better and is valid, while in our Talmud it implies the opposite. See Shiyarei Korban, who addressed this and pressed himself in his commentary there to resolve it. According to what I wrote, it works well: this continues from above, since all invalidations invalidate only on the first day of Yom Tov, but on the second day of Yom Tov they are valid even with boil-like discoloration. Therefore it properly says that when it is on most of it on one side, it is invalid only on the first day of Yom Tov; but in two or three places it is invalid even on the second day of Yom Tov, for it is like spotted. This works well and is correct. I saw that the Rosh brought the above Yerushalmi and explained it according to the Korban HaEdah's above explanation; why did he not explain more simply that the intent is that even on the second day of Yom Tov it is invalid in two or three places? I saw there that he disagrees with the Yerushalmi on logical grounds. This is puzzling: why does he not prove more strongly from our Talmud that it necessarily does not hold this way, since it does not explain the words of Rabbi Chanina in such a manner? This requires investigation.
