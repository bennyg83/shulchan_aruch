1) [Seif 1] A dried esrog is invalid. Although we expound, ‘the fruit of a beautiful tree,’ as a fruit that remains on its tree from year to year, namely the esrog, nevertheless the word hadar does not depart from its plain meaning, which implies actual beauty. If it is not truly beautiful, it is invalid. Therefore, a dried esrog is invalid. Levush.
2) Ibid. A dried esrog is invalid. The invalidation of dryness in an esrog applies only when most of it is dry, as with a lulav and hadas, where dryness invalidates only when it affects most of the leaves. If, however, the dryness is at its chotem, the Rosh and Ran wrote that just as even the slightest blister invalidates at the chotem, so too does dryness, and likewise all invalidations that disqualify an esrog because it lacks beauty. Mateh Yosef, vol. 2, Likkutim to Shulchan Aruch, 62b; Beit HaShoevah, sec. 5. See below, seif 12, and our words there, with G-d's help.
3) Ibid. A dried esrog is invalid. If it was withered but had not fully dried, it is valid. Rambam, Hilchos Lulav 8:1. This is derived from the Gemara, Sukkah 31a; see there. The Rosh cites it in the chapter Lulav HaGazul, and this is also the view of the Ran and Riaz, cited in Shiltei HaGibborim there in that chapter. So too wrote Rabbeinu Yerucham, R' Yitzchak ibn Giat, the Ittur, and Shibbolei HaLeket, that a withered esrog is valid. Regarding what Responsa Maharil, siman 5, and the Rokeach wrote, that a withered esrog is invalid, see what the Magen Avraham wrote about their words at the beginning of the siman. He concludes: Nevertheless, it appears that one may be lenient, since all the poskim wrote that a dried esrog is invalid, which implies that a withered one is valid, provided that he examines it. End quote. Birkei Yosef, sec. 1, agreed, and responded to the words of Eliyah Rabbah, sec. 1, who challenged the Magen Avraham; see there. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, secs. 1 and 2, likewise ruled in accordance with the Magen Avraham. Mishna Berurah, sec. 2.
4) Ibid. He should examine it by passing a needle through it, etc. This is difficult, for it will then have a through-and-through hole, which is invalid according to the Rosh and Tur, and so the Shulchan Aruch ruled in seif 2 according to the first opinion. One can answer that a through-and-through hole invalidates only when it was pierced from one side to the opposite side, or into the seed chambers. If, however, the hole passes through only one side, meaning through the thickness of the peel, it certainly does not invalidate according to any opinion when nothing is missing. The laws of an esrog are derived from the laws of treifos in an animal, and regarding treifos, if there is a hole through the thickness of an organ, such as the heart or intestines, but it does not reach the cavity, it certainly does not render it a treifah. The same applies here to an esrog. Taz, s.k. 1. However, Machatzis HaShekel explains the words of Magen Avraham, s.k. 1, that even if one pierces only the thick peel from one side to the other, it is called through-and-through. Therefore, one must reverse the needle and not insert it by the eye. Rather, he should insert the needle together with the thread from the side of the eye, without passing the needle from side to side, and then remove the needle with the thread from that same side and see whether the thread contains moisture. In that case there is no through-and-through hole; see there. So too wrote Eshel Avraham, sec. 1, and Levushei Serad. One must take care that the needle not enter the seed chambers. Eshel Avraham there. Regarding an esrog formed in ridges, if one of the protrusions was pierced, one can say that according to all opinions it is valid, because ‘from side to side’ means from one side to the opposite side, not merely through one protrusion. Mishbetzos Zahav, sec. 1; Mishna Berurah, Shaar HaTziyun, sec. 7. This applies when nothing is missing. Mishna Berurah there.
5) Ibid. He should examine it by passing a needle through it, etc. This examination prescribed by the Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch can hardly be performed without removing even the slightest amount and thereby invalidating it. It is therefore preferable to insert a needle only slightly and withdraw it from the same side into which it was inserted. If the hole fills again after the needle is removed, it is moist; if not, its moisture has already ceased and it has dried. This resembles what is stated in the Yerushalmi at the end of the second chapter of Sheviis regarding gourds that hardened during the Sabbatical year, etc. This is a simple method and does not cause even the slightest deficiency in the esrog. Mor U'Ketziah; Machazik Berachah, sec. 1; Ikkarei HaDat, siman 33, sec. 29. However, Beit HaShoevah, sec. 4, wrote that perhaps an esrog is different, for even if the hole does not refill, its moisture may still not have entirely ceased; see there. The Raavad, in Temim De'im, siman 232, wrote that one should pass a needle through it, and if it contains moisture, the moisture will immediately emerge through the hole; see there. Although it is impossible that inserting the needle will not remove the slightest amount of the esrog's peel, one can say that since that minute amount is pushed to the sides by the pressure of the needle or enters inward, it is not considered missing. This resembles what is written below regarding one who inserted a peg, that if the hole is not through-and-through and is smaller than an issar, it is valid; see there.
6) Ibid., Rama's gloss: An esrog from the previous year is certainly dried, etc. No examination helps for it, because our Sages were certain that it no longer contains moisture. Bach; Eliyah Rabbah, sec. 3; Chemed Moshe, sec. 1. However, Taz, s.k. 2, wrote that if we see that its beauty remains, the thread test is effective. Bikkurei Yaakov likewise wrote: I personally saw an esrog that had remained for more than a full year from the time it was detached from the tree. It had been protected from exposure to air by being sealed in a metal vessel and kept in a somewhat cool and moist place. Through this its beauty and moisture were preserved. I examined it and found abundant moisture. In such a case, it appears to me that one may permit an esrog from the previous year through the thread test, provided that it did not shrivel and lose its beauty; see there. Mishna Berurah cites his words in Shaar HaTziyun, sec. 8; see there.
7) Ibid., Rama's gloss: It is certainly dried and invalid. Regarding an esrog placed in honey to preserve its moisture, one should not rely on this to validate it without examining it with a needle and thread. Bach; Shiyarei Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, sec. 4. However, Taz, s.k. 17, wrote that it is invalid because it is pickled, and so ruled Shulchan Aruch HaRav, sec. 25; Beit HaShoevah, secs. 6 and 67; Mishna Berurah, sec. 54. With congealed honey, however, we do not say that something pickled is like something cooked, as written in Yoreh De'ah, siman 104, regarding a mouse. Machatzis HaShekel, s.k. 22; Mishna Berurah, Shaar HaTziyun, sec. 61. See further below, seif 15, and our words there, with G-d's help.
8) If one took an esrog in his hand to recite the blessing over it, and afterward they brought him a more beautiful esrog, he is not obligated to set aside the first and recite the blessing over the more beautiful one. Shevus Yaakov, vol. 1, siman 37, and vol. 2, siman 30; Responsa Yad Eliyahu, siman 72, agreed. However, Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 45, disputed the aforementioned Shevus Yaakov and concluded that it is clear that regarding an esrog, Chanukah lights, and every mitzvah in the world, one recites the blessing over the more beautiful and choice item and thereby fulfills his obligation, even if it arrived later; see there. Nevertheless, if he already began the blessing, he should not set it aside and take the other. Likewise, on the first day, if the less beautiful esrog belongs to him and he took it in his hand to recite the blessing, and meanwhile his fellow came and wished to give him a more choice esrog as a gift on condition that it be returned, it is preferable to perform the mitzvah with one that is actually his own. So wrote Shaarei Teshuvah below, siman 643, sec. 1; see there. In my humble opinion, on the first day it is preferable to recite the blessing over his own esrog even if he had not yet taken it in his hand for the blessing, provided there is no concern of invalidity and the only issue is enhancement. A person prefers to perform a mitzvah with his own money, and there is also concern that the owner of the esrog may not give it wholeheartedly as a complete gift. I later saw that Mabit, vol. 3, siman 49, wrote likewise, and Eliyah Rabbah cites him below, siman 649, sec. 18; see there. See below, sec. 87, and above, siman 454, sec. 39.
