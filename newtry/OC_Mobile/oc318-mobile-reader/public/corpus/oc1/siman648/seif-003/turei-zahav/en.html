That it was punctured from one side to the other. This is the view of the Ba'al Halachot Gedolot in Tosafot, that it is called through-and-through only in that case. But the view of Tosafot is that if it was punctured until the seed chambers it is invalid even without deficiency. That which we require through-and-through means when it is not opposite those chambers; but if it was punctured in the place of the chambers, it is through-and-through, for those chambers are like the bronchi in a lung. This is the 'some explain' that he brings here. In the Ran it is written as follows: Some are stringent and say that the peel is a distinct entity, and whenever all of it was punctured from one side, it is called a through-and-through hole. End quote. This is the view of Tosafot that I mentioned, that the entire thickness of the esrog until the seed chambers is called peel. This is as is found earlier there in the Gemara regarding terumah, that one may not take an esrog of terumah because he ruins it. Rashi explained that the outer peel becomes repulsive through handling by the hands, and it is prohibited to ruin terumah. They say there: if he designated it as terumah except for its outer peel, then according to one opinion it is permitted. That means that the thickness of the esrog until the seed chambers is the outer peel. For we see that there is an inner peel in the seed chambers themselves: there is a chamber between each portion of the esrog's seeds and its fellow, and those chambers are called the inner peel, with the seed resting inside it. But the entire thickness of the esrog until the chambers from the outside is called the outer peel. A parallel is the gizzard, where they speak of its outer skin, meaning the entire thickness of the flesh of the gizzard. The same is literally true regarding an esrog. One should not err and say that outer peel refers to the thin glaze above, as I will write in the name of the Ran nearby regarding peeling, for that is very thin and is not part of the esrog; even if most of it was peeled from the esrog, it is valid, as is stated afterward. How could they say regarding this in the Gemara that he designated the esrog as terumah but not that thin peel? Certainly without this, that thin peel is not included in terumah in the sense that it is included in eating and that it is prohibited to ruin terumah, for what loss is there in it, since it is not at all part of the flesh of the esrog? And the Rambam writes in chapter 11 of Terumot that esrog peels, even though they have no food and householders discard them, are nevertheless prohibited to non-kohanim. He is certainly speaking of the thin peels that we mentioned; but the thickness of the esrog, which is included in the flesh of the esrog, certainly is not discarded. Rather, they eat it, for it has sharpness, as I will write nearby in the name of the Ran. Furthermore, if you say that the outer peel in the Gemara regarding esrog is the thin one, one will still come to ruin terumah, for since it becomes repulsive through handling, one who wants to eat it afterward will cut somewhat into its thickness and discard from there the handled place, and it will not suffice for him to cut off only a hairbreadth. If so, he will come to ruin terumah. Rather, certainly it is speaking of the thickness of the esrog, which is certainly from the flesh of the esrog, and he does not designate it as terumah. This is what the Ran is speaking about here: if that thickness was punctured until the seed chambers. It follows that three peels belong to the esrog. The first is the very thin one, which is like a glaze above; there is no invalidation in it except if all of it was peeled, as stated afterward. The second is called the outer peel, meaning the entire thickness of the esrog until the seed chambers, and it has a sharp taste when one eats it. Regarding this we say that if a little is missing from it, it is invalid according to the Rambam even without being through-and-through, and according to the Ra'avad through being through-and-through, as above. The law of splitting nearby also concerns this peel. The third is the inner peel, which is between seed and seed within the esrog itself. But in the outer peel, meaning the thickness of the esrog that we mentioned, there is no further distinction; all of it is one, for what boundary is there to divide it? I wrote all this because the Beit Yosef wrote regarding the Ran: perhaps the entire thickness of the esrog until the seed chambers is called peel, etc. End quote. He wrote with somewhat uncertain language in this. My father-in-law and teacher, of blessed memory, was drawn from this to say that in truth there is a distinction in the thickness of the esrog between peel and the rest of the thickness until the seed chambers. He wrote that one cannot explain that the entire thickness of the esrog until the seed chambers is called peel, as the Beit Yosef wrote, for the language does not sound that way at all; rather, the outer peel is that which has sharpness, and if all of it was punctured from one side, it is through-and-through. End quote. But he was not precise in this, for the entire thickness of the esrog until the seed chambers, aside from the thin peel, has sharpness, and it is not called through-and-through except until the seed chambers. There is no need to elaborate further, for it is clear.
