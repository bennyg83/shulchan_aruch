Forbidden. See Ba'er Heitev. See Shach, Choshen Mishpat siman 73 s.k. 11, who explains that an inclusive oath works to annul a mitzvah because in the middle of the year no obligation of matzah or sukkah has yet taken effect. See the Taz there in the gloss of Chacham Tzvi, who challenges the Shach and writes that if one said simply "matzah," even standing on Pesach night, he is forbidden to eat. See the later authorities there, who resolve the Shach's words, and see the kuntres acharon to Sefer Ketubah, Even HaEzer siman 118.
I wrote there that initially there is no difficulty from Tosafot in Shevuot. The Shach is not speaking about the actual taking effect of the oath, for regarding that, his distinction that there is no mitzvah now has no place; when the oath takes effect on Pesach night, it is already a mitzvah, and Tosafot therefore needed another distinction to explain why the oath takes effect. Rather, the Shach discusses whether one must keep the oath or the mitzvah upon which he is already sworn, though he will incur the oath's punishment; that depends on which came first. Since matzah is not presently obligatory, the oath precedes it, for now there is no obligation, and when the obligation arrives the oath has already taken effect. Therefore the Shach did not mention Tosafot in Shevuot, who address the actual validity of the oath. This is clear, as I explained in its place. What Ketzot HaChoshen wrote from the Raavad is not the Shach's intent, and what he wrote that R. Chasdai in Responsa Rivash agrees with the Raavad is not so, as is clear in the Rivash. I explained this well there, with God's help.
