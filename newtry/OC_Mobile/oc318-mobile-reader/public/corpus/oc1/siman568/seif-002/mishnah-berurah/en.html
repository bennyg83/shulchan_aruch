(9) For a mitzvah matter - such as for a brit milah or a siyum of a tractate, even if he has no personal connection to it. Nevertheless, in all such cases he must fast part of the day, a little longer than his usual time; for example, if he usually eats in the fourth hour, he should eat in the fifth hour. Some say that even if they pressed him to attend a friendly celebration it is permitted, because of the ways of peace. But from the wording of the Shulchan Aruch, which says "because of the honor of a great person," it implies that this applies specifically for the honor of one who presses him, but not for other people.
(10) He borrows and repays, etc. - And he does not need to annul the vow.
(11) At the beginning of the vow - Meaning, even though he accepted it yesterday at Minchah, that acceptance is not now considered the beginning of the vow; rather, the beginning was when he first resolved to fast on some day, and there he did not specify the day.
(12) This is called this fast and is not, etc. - See the Taz, whose view is that if he is distressed by his fast and has fasted part of the day, he may borrow and repay with another day even on this very day. But the view of the Magen Avraham and several later authorities is that he may borrow and repay only where he accepted a fast in general terms, and even there only if he has fasted part of the day, as above.
(13) Monday and Thursday the whole year - For this is considered a designated day, and he cannot exchange it for other days. Nor can he move it to another year, for since he said "this year," he meant this year specifically. But if he vowed to fast Monday-Thursday-Monday for half a year or a year and did not say "this year," it is not called "this day," and he may borrow and repay on other Monday-Thursday-Monday days after the first half-year or year has ended. But he may not exchange them for other days, such as Sunday, Tuesday, Wednesday, and Friday, for perhaps he was particular about these days specifically, since they are days of favor. The later authorities wrote that one who vowed to fast Monday-Thursday-Monday may exchange them and fast Thursday-Monday-Thursday, for his intention was only these days, which are days of favor.
(14) A fast for a dream - Or a yahrtzeit.
(15) Even during the Ten Days of Repentance - Meaning, since these days in particular are designated, one might have thought that eating is forbidden.
(16) A brit milah - Or pidyon haben, or another seudat mitzvah.
(17) A mitzvah, etc. - And he does not need to fast another day because of this.
(18) To eat - It is obvious that where it is permitted to eat at the meal, it is not a fast at all, and afterward he may drink and eat even at home. However, before the meal he may not eat or drink at home. The baalei brit, however, may eat and drink immediately, for it is their Yom Tov. In Eliyah Rabbah he is uncertain whether this is perhaps only after Minchah; see there.
(19) For they did not practice, etc. - If before Rosh Hashanah he knows that between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur he will eat at a brit milah or another seudat mitzvah, he is obligated to fast one additional day before Rosh Hashanah, for this is the reason they fast four days before Rosh Hashanah, corresponding to the four days on which they eat. All the more so if the seudat mitzvah occurs during the four days before Rosh Hashanah, he should fast one day in its place. However, if Rosh Hashanah falls on Thursday, so that there are only four days before Rosh Hashanah, he need not fast one day before Shabbat, for from the outset they accepted only to fast on the days of Selichot and not on other days.
(20) To fast, etc. - If the thirty-first day from his son's birth falls on Shabbat, so that the pidyon must be done on Sunday and is a delayed pidyon, the Magen Avraham is uncertain whether it counts as a seudat mitzvah and one may eat when it falls during the Ten Days of Repentance. It is not comparable to brit milah, which is a seudat mitzvah even when delayed, for there every hour is its time so that he not remain uncircumcised, unlike pidyon haben. Therefore he inclines that the meal should be made afterward at night. In Dagul Merevavah he wrote that it is better to make the pidyon and the meal on Motzaei Shabbat; see there. The Rama wrote that all this applies only in such a case, but on other fasts decreed by the community it is forbidden to eat even at a seudat mitzvah. Therefore, if a pidyon haben falls on a public fast day, although the practice is to redeem him on the fast day, the meal is not made that day but afterward at night, as the Magen Avraham writes. In the gloss of Chatam Sofer he inclines that even in this case the pidyon and the meal should be made on the night entering the thirty-first day; see there. The Magen Avraham further wrote that it requires study when they fast on 20 Sivan in the kingdom of Poland whether one may eat at a seudat mitzvah, for perhaps it is included among other public fasts where it is forbidden, as above. In our times, however, most such meals are made at night. Once 20 Sivan fell on Friday and there was a brit milah meal, and the rabbi ordered them to eat by day because people would not be found to go at night. In a city where they do not fast on 20 Sivan, and people from places that do fast are present there, if they are needed to complete a minyan they may eat even on all the days, as Chayei Adam writes.
(21) And if he accepted upon himself, etc. - Meaning that all this applies only when he did not accept the fast upon himself, but merely relied on the custom, or answered amen when Monday-Thursday-Monday was blessed, in which case he did not accept it as a vow and may choose not to fast. Therefore, even if he did not know that he would be invited to a meal and began to fast, he may nevertheless eat at a seudat mitzvah. But if he accepted the fast upon himself at Minchah, doing more than the custom, he must fulfill it and fast even at a seudat mitzvah, according to Levush. The Magen Avraham and some later authorities hold that even when he accepted it at Minchah it is permitted, since his acceptance was because of the custom; and the Rama is speaking of one who does not fast all Monday-Thursday-Monday or all the Ten Days of Repentance, but only one or two days, so he must by law accept at Minchah, and his status is like one fasting in the middle of the year, since it does not depend on custom, and therefore he must fast. From the Gra it seems that he explains like the Levush. Nevertheless, it appears that in such a case he may borrow and repay another fast, for in any event several Rishonim hold that even one who says, "I accept upon myself to fast tomorrow," may borrow and repay.
(22) With his fast - This means an individual's fast. But for a fast decreed by the community, redemption does not help unless the community stipulated so.
(23) He may redeem it, etc. - For we estimate that he accepted it with this in mind: the fast is considered tzedakah, and since he gives the value of his fat and blood to the poor, he has fulfilled it.
(24) With money - The measure of redemption for a wealthy person is according to his wealth, for the reason is that the distress of the money is weighed against the distress of the fast; therefore each person gives according to what he is.
(25) When he accepted a fast in general - Meaning that at the time of acceptance he did not specify a particular day. If he did specify, saying then, "I am in a fast tomorrow," his law is like one who vows for this day, as above in seif 2; see Biur Halacha.
