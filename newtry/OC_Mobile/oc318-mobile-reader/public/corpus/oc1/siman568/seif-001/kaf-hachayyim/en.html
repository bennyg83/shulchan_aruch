1) [Se'if 1] If one vowed to fast this day, etc. One accepted a fast at Minchah on Wednesday, thinking the next day, Thursday, was Erev Rosh Chodesh, and later learned it was not Erev Rosh Chodesh because Rosh Chodesh would occur on Shabbos. He does not require annulment, though it is preferable to annul the vow. Zera Emes, Responsa Orach Chaim, siman 83; Machazik Berachah, paragraph 1; Shaarei Teshuvah, paragraph 1. See what we wrote above in siman 417, paragraphs 24 and 21.
2) If one said, "I will fast on Thursday if I am relieved of a certain matter," and forgot to accept the fast at Minchah, he is nevertheless obligated to fast. Zera Emes there, siman 85; Machazik Berachah, paragraph 2; Shaarei Teshuvah there.
3) Whenever fewer than ten people fast on Erev Rosh Chodesh or the like, no Torah scroll is taken out and the shaliach tzibbur does not say Aneinu in the repetition, even in Shome'a Tefillah and even at Minchah. Zera Emes there, siman 86. An ancient pamphlet of novel rulings from the early sages of Yerushalayim, dating from the nasi's time in the year 5269, states that individuals were designated to fast in response to a threatened calamity. The nasi agreed that the shaliach tzibbur should say Aneinu in Shome'a Tefillah because it was not a communal fast; they should not take out a Torah scroll in the morning, but should take one out at Minchah, and Aneinu in Shome'a Tefillah should be recited as at Shacharis. The same pamphlet states that when the official decreed against wine in Yerushalayim, they fasted because it was a communal affliction. Machazik Berachah, paragraph 3; Zechor LeAvraham, paragraph 400. See above, siman 566, paragraph 29, where this law is explained. Consider carefully.
4) One who vowed to fast every Monday and Thursday for a year may not have the vow annulled until the first Monday arrives. Radbaz, volume 1, siman 277; Machazik Berachah, paragraph 6; Shaarei Teshuvah there. See Yoreh Deah, siman 228, se'if 17, and our discussion above in siman 417, paragraph 25.
5) There: And he forgot and ate, etc. Even if he deliberately ate a large amount, he should eat no more. Bach; Magen Avraham, paragraph 1; Ateres Zekenim; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1; Mishnah Berurah, paragraph 1.
6) There: He completes his fast. This means that he must complete that day and cannot discharge the obligation by fasting another day, because his vow specified this day; namely, he said the previous day, "I hereby fast tomorrow." Mishnah Berurah, paragraph 2.
7) There: He completes his fast. This explicitly implies that he need not fast another day in its place. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 156, likewise explains that what was already distorted cannot be repaired; another fast would serve only to atone for his inadvertent transgression. Rema's words in the gloss below also prove this. Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 1. See above, siman 549, paragraph 7.
8) There: Or if he was the shaliach tzibbur, etc. It appears that on a communal fast, even if he ate, he may say Aneinu. As stated in siman 565, he can truthfully say "on this day of the fast," because the Sages instituted fasting. On a private fast, however, although he must complete it, if he ate the amount that would have destroyed his fast had he not vowed this particular day, he should not say Aneinu. Nahar Shalom, paragraph 1; Mishnah Berurah, paragraph 3. See above, siman 565, paragraph 6.
9) There: Or his rav. It is a fine custom to fast on the day one's primary rav died, because a person is obligated in greater honor and fear of his rav than of his father. Shelah, folio 201a. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 15, questions this, because the fast on the day one's father died is not because of honor but because of danger, as his fortune is impaired; see there. See below, paragraph 75.
9) The Yerushalmi states at the end of Moed Katan: Rabbi Avahu said, "May such-and-such come upon me if I tasted anything throughout a day on which a Torah scholar died." When Rabbi Yonah heard that a Torah scholar had died, even if he had already eaten and drunk, he completed the remainder of that day as a fast. Eliyah Rabbah there cites this.
10) There: But if he vowed to fast one day, etc. This means that he initially accepted a vow to fast one day and later, at Minchah, accepted that fast for the following day. Despite the later acceptance, it is not called a vow for "this day." Beis Yosef, citing the Rosh. Therefore, if he forgot and ate a kezayis on the fast day, although he accepted it the previous day at Minchah, he may eat that day and fast another day, because his original vow did not fix a particular day. If he did not previously accept a general one-day fast but at Minchah accepted a fast for tomorrow, it is called a fast for "this day," as stated in the following se'if. Even if he ate a kezayis, he must therefore complete the fast and need not fast another day, as stated above, paragraph 7.
11) There: He forgot and ate a kezayis, etc. This applies only if he swallowed; if he merely tasted, it does not. That is, even if he vowed simply to fast one unspecified day, if he merely tasted but did not eat, he fasts and completes that day. It appears that "tasted" means he tasted up to a revi'is and expelled it, as stated in the preceding siman; otherwise it is eating. Bach; Ateres Zekenim. Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 2, disagrees with Bach and writes that "tasted" here does not mean tasting and expelling. It means that he only tasted a small amount, less than a kezayis; he then completes his fast. See below, paragraphs 13 and 14.
12) There: He forgot and ate a kezayis, etc. Ran, citing Raah, writes that the amount is a date-sized portion, like Yom Kippur. The Rosh, however, reads that if he forgot and ate a kezayis, he has lost his fast; Tur likewise rules. Shulchan Aruch therefore follows the Rosh and Tur. Levush and Bach rule likewise, and this is the consensus of the later authorities.
