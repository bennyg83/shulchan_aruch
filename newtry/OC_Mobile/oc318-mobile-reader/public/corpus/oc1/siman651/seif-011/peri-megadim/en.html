He rustles. See Taz. What he wrote according to the Ran refers to the second view, the view of the Rif and Rambam, that one rustles the leaves afterward, meaning after the outward motion he makes three rustlings, and after the inward motion three rustlings. According to the Rosh and Tur, three times for every matter means three outward motions and three inward motions, and no leaf-rustling is required at all. One can therefore say that the Rav was concerned to be stringent for both explanations. Also, the requirement that the lulav be four tefachim 'to shake' indicates rustling, while Rabbi Zeira's question indicates three outward and inward motions, as written in note 10. The second difficulty is that the Rav seems to contradict himself. He wrote that outward and inward movement is the shaking, in a manner that requires no small motions, namely no rustlings; yet above he wrote that one rustles the lulav. These appear contradictory, and this seems also to be the Magen Avraham's question in note 20, and with G-d's help it will be explained there. Maharshal concludes that one should do as follows: toward the east, make three large outward motions, perhaps because this is the meaning of three times for each matter, and because Rabbi Zeira's question is treated stringently. He should also rustle the leaves at least during one outward and inward motion, three rustlings outward and three inward, to be concerned for the second view. He should also, during one outward and inward motion, make six small paired movements in the form of outward and inward motions, to be concerned for the third explanation. But during the second and third large outward and inward motions toward the east, no shaking is needed, neither rustling nor small pairs, for this is merely extra according to all explanations, and he has fulfilled according to all views as stated. The Taz also writes stringently that the large inward motions should bring the lulav closer than where it was when he began the outward motion, for bringing it only back to its original place is not considered a genuine inward motion; since he took it out, he must bring it back to its place, so only bringing it closer to his heart is considered a proper inward motion. Perhaps this is hinted in Rabbi Zeira's question and in the Tur's version, according to the Sephardic reading, 'and brings it toward himself,' meaning closer than the lulav initially was. I have written at length and still have not fulfilled the obligation of explaining the matter; see Lechem Mishneh, chapter 4, halacha 10, in his explanation of the Maggid Mishneh, and Sefer Kappos Temarim.
