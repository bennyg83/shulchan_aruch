1) [Se'if 1] The mitzvah of the Four Species is for each person to take one lulav, etc. So too in the introduction to Tikkunei Zohar, Zohar, Pinchas 256a, Maggid Meisharim to Maran on Emor, and Shaar HaKavanos 105c. The latter gives a mystical reason: they allude to seven chasadim. The three myrtle branches correspond to Chesed, Gevurah, and Tiferes; two willow branches to Netzach and Hod; the lulav to Yesod; and the esrog to Malchus, the crown of Yesod. Shaar HaKavanos 106 further writes that lulav and its species are alluded to in the Divine Name Ban, the Name Havayah spelled with hehs: the three myrtles in the yud, two willows in the first heh, lulav in the vav (because it corresponds to Yesod and includes the six extremities) and esrog in the final heh, because it corresponds to the crown and its chesed. It further states that the numerical value of lulav is chaim, and hadas also equals chaim when the word itself is counted. Yerushalmi: 'fruit of a beautiful tree' corresponds to the one God; 'one palm branch' to one nation; three 'branches of a thick tree' to the three Patriarchs; and two 'willows of the brook' to the two Tablets. Eliyah Rabbah 1. See also the author's Yismach Yisrael, discourse on Emor, for additional allusions.
2) There: For each person to take one lulav, etc. On the first day of Yom Tov it is better to go to a place with a lulav than one with communal prayer. Magen Avraham at the siman's beginning. On the second day, since we know the calendar and it is not a Torah doubt, if lulav is uncertain but prayer certain, on the first day he goes where there is a lulav, but on the second he goes where prayer is certain. Eshel Avraham at the siman's beginning. This parallels the rule concerning shofar above in siman 595; see there and our words.
3) There: For each person to take one lulav, etc. One person may not take it on behalf of everyone. Sukkah 41b interprets 'You shall take' to require taking in each person's hand; Tosafos explain this from the plural rather than singular wording. This apparently applies on all days. Responsa Chasam Sofer 182 agrees and disputes a rabbi who claimed personal taking is unnecessary on later days. The later authorities cite this.
4) There: Two willows and three myrtles, etc. If the entire congregation took a lulav containing only two myrtles, they must take it again with three. This involves no muktzeh prohibition, but they should not repeat the blessing. Responsa Knesses HaGedolah, Orach Chaim II:47; Responsa Beis David 455; Birkei Yosef 2; Shaarei Teshuvah 1.
5) Responsa Beis David writes that one who took only one willow branch must take again with a blessing, because 'willows of the brook' is plural. Birkei Yosef cites this, but in Yosef Ometz 2 objects: other expositions derive from the plural willows growing in a field or mountains, or one willow for lulav and another for the willow ceremony. He therefore rules that if one discovered only one willow after taking, he should add another and take all Four Species again without a blessing, since several great authorities hold he already fulfilled the obligation. Yafeh LaLev II:2 likewise rejects Beis David and rules against another blessing because several poskim deem the obligation fulfilled. See below, paragraph 123.
6) A gentile brought a willow to a Jew on Yom Tov, having detached it that day because he thought it permitted, and the Jew placed it in his lulav: it may not be moved. Responsa Rashba 297. Maharshal of Damascus, Orach Chaim 22, permits it, but Rabbi Chaim Abulafia in Etz HaChaim 141c rejects his words and forbids it like Rashba. Yad Aharon cites them in Hagahos Tur. See further below, siman 655, paragraphs 12–13.
7) There: And three myrtles. Under pressing circumstances, when no others are possible, three clipped myrtles suffice and one may bless over them, as above siman 646, paragraph 59.
8) There, gloss: Where no valid myrtle exists, one unclipped branch suffices. Ramban holds even one clipped branch suffices, as Beis Yosef and Ran write, and may be relied upon under pressure. Magen Avraham 1. Magen Avraham's attribution also appears in Orchos Chaim cited by Beis Yosef in siman 646, as Birkei Yosef 3 and Erekh HaShulchan 1 write. Maggid Mishneh on Hilchos Lulav, chapter 7, says all the Geonim rule like Rabbi Yishmael concerning the number, requiring three myrtles, like Rambam; Beis Yosef cites this here. Bach likewise says that despite Ramban, all other authors agree with Tur that exactly three are required. Responsa Divrei Shmuel 84 discusses a congregation that found only one myrtle branch on the first day. After citing the dispute, it concludes that since old and new poskim disagree and the prohibition is uncertain, the good and proper course is to take that one branch with the other three species to fulfill the possible mitzvah without a blessing, since blessings are not indispensable and doubtful blessings are omitted. Shaarei Teshuvah 1 cites this. There appears no distinction between the first and later days, because according to one view the required measure is lacking, and doubtful blessings are omitted.
9) There: And it is a mitzvah to bind them, etc. Although we rule like the Sages that lulav does not require binding, it remains a mitzvah for beauty, as written, 'This is my God and I will beautify Him.' Sukkah 33a; Tur; Beis Yosef; Levush. Since everyone agrees binding is a mitzvah, before tying one should say: 'For the sake of the unification of the Holy One, blessed be He, and His Shechinah, etc., I am about to bind the myrtle and willow with the lulav, to rectify the root of this mitzvah and all mitzvos included within it in the supernal place. May the pleasantness, etc.'
10) There: With a complete knot, etc. A baraisa teaches that if its binding came undone on Yom Tov, he binds it like a bundle of vegetables. Thus, on a weekday it should be bound with a knot forbidden on Shabbos and Yom Tov, namely two knots one upon another. Mordechai, chapter Lulav HaGazul; Beis Yosef.
11) There: With a complete knot, etc. Responsa Agurah B'Ohalecha, page 12, cites Beis David's astonishment at the universal custom not to make a knot, but to fashion a holder from lulav leaves and insert the species. He disputes Beis David and permits it, because this too is called binding. Pischei Teshuvah and Mishnah Berurah 8 cite this. Nevertheless, it is proper to tie them together and only then insert them into a holder if desired, satisfying everyone. See below, paragraph 14.
12) There: With a complete knot, etc. It is a mitzvah to bind it so the willow branches do not spread in different directions. Mateh Moshe 926; Shelah 245b. This means they should touch the lulav. The same applies to the myrtle; willow is specified because its branches are softer and more likely to spread away.
13) There: With a complete knot, etc. Bind them in their direction of growth. One should untie the myrtle bundle to ensure correct placement, since gentiles sometimes put one branch's base beside another's top or bend the tops inward so leaves do not fall, leaving them contrary to their growth. Straighten the stems' tops and place all in their growth direction. Mateh Moshe and Shelah there; Eliyah Rabbah 2; Shaarei Teshuvah 2.
14) Bind the willow on the left, myrtle on the right, and lulav in the center. This appears in Ibn Shuaib's derashos as a mystical practice. Shelah there; Taz 650; Magen Avraham here 4. Rabbi Yaakov Tzemach, however, writes: place one of the three myrtles right, one left, and one center, as in Zohar, Tetzaveh 186b. Hold the lulav with its spine facing you, so its right is at your right and its left at your left. Incline the central myrtle rightward. Lulav is Yesod, drawing from Daas through the spinal cord; the right branch is Chesed, left Gevurah, and center Tiferes, which inclines toward Chesed, so incline it rightward. The three myrtles correspond to Chesed, Gevurah, and Tiferes; the two willows to Netzach and Hod, one right and one left. He heard this from Rabbi Tzemach in the name of Rabbi Chaim Vital from the Arizal. Pri Etz Chaim, gate 29, end of chapter 1; Nagid U'Metzaveh; Magen Avraham there. Radbaz I:170 writes that this is his practice.
15) Ibn Shuaib writes that it should be held face to face: the lulav's face toward the holder and spine outward. Tolaas Yaakov writes the reverse: the spine toward one's face and the lulav's face outward, mystically corresponding to 'You shall see My back, but My face shall not be seen.' Shelah cites both and says that since Tolaas Yaakov was a great kabbalist whose words follow Zohar, his practice should be followed. Eliyah Rabbah in siman 650 favors Ibn Shuaib, because several great authorities agree. Radbaz writes that he holds the lulav's back toward his face to fulfill 'You shall see My back,' as is proper mystically and found in kabbalistic works; even plainly, this is proper, and he saw the early kabbalists practice it. Birkei Yosef 1 cites him. Mishbetzos Zahav in siman 650 says universal practice follows Tolaas Yaakov. This also follows the Arizal, as in the preceding paragraph. Ben Ish Chai, Haazinu 13, agrees.
