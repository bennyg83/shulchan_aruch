A left handed person takes, etc. This was written based on the Tur, who wrote: “Even if he is left handed, he takes the etrog with his right hand, which is the left hand of every other person, but is his own right hand.” The Beit Yosef wrote that the other poskim hold that we follow his own right and left. I am astonished, for the Tur himself wrote in his abbreviated rulings in accordance with this, contrary to what he wrote in the Tur. At the very least, he should have cited the opinion of the Rosh, as is his practice. Furthermore, it is difficult for me: if he takes the lulav with his own left hand, which is the right hand of every other person, how can he shake the lulav with his own left hand? It appears obvious that there is a scribal error in the Tur, and the text should read that he takes the bound species in the left hand of every other person. The reading, “Even if he is left handed,” is correct, for it teaches that in this matter as well we follow his own right hand. I later found that the Maharshal also emended the words of the Tur, and his intent is the same as my emendation. Therefore, we certainly have only the opinion cited by the Rama as “there are those who say,” which is the opinion of the Rosh and the other poskim. I am astonished at what the Levush wrote, that the custom does not follow this opinion, for the Rama already testified that the custom is the opposite. Therefore, no consideration should be given to one who erred and practiced otherwise.
And if he reversed them, he has fulfilled his obligation. This means that if a left handed person reversed them and took the etrog in the left hand of every other person, he has fulfilled his obligation. In my humble opinion, it was unnecessary to write this. Even an ordinary person who reversed them, placing the etrog in his right hand and the bound lulav in his left hand, has fulfilled his obligation after the fact. The primary reason for placing the lulav in the right hand is that it contains three mitzvos, and this is only the preferred manner. After the fact, he certainly fulfills his obligation if he reversed them, since he nevertheless performed the mitzvah, and the Torah does not state that the lulav must specifically be taken in the right hand. This is demonstrated in the Gemara at the end of the chapter, where it asks, “But once he lifted it, he already fulfilled his obligation,” and Abaye answers that the case is where he held it upside down. The Gemara did not answer that he held it in his left hand. Likewise, the poskim who wrote that before the blessing he should turn it upside down, as stated below, did not say that he should take the lulav in his left hand. It is therefore obvious that if he reversed the hands, he fulfills his obligation after the fact. Accordingly, the Rama did not need to write here, “And if he reversed them, he has fulfilled his obligation,” because this wording could imply that this applies only to a left handed person, since there are opinions on both sides, but that an ordinary person would not fulfill his obligation if he failed to follow the preferred procedure. This is not so. Rather, the law is as I have written, and this appears obvious.
