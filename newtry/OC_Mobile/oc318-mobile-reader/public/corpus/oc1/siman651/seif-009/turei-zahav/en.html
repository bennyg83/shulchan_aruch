The shaking is, etc. In the Yerushalmi we say: it was taught that one must shake three times for each matter. There are opinions about this. What the Ran wrote is that Rav Yehudai Gaon holds that one need not rustle the leaves at all, only extend outward and bring inward, raise and lower. With this there are three times, for raising and lowering are one, since it is impossible to raise without lowering, and extending outward and bringing inward are counted as two. The Rif and the Rambam hold that one must rustle the leaves apart from extending outward and bringing inward, and regarding rustling the leaves it says that it should be three times. In each direction there is one outward movement and one inward movement, without rustling the leaves; and during that outward movement that he does, he should make three small motions by rustling the leaves, and likewise during the inward movement. It follows that in each direction there is one outward movement and one inward movement and six small motions. Raising and lowering are also done in this manner. A third opinion is that those small motions that we mentioned should themselves contain outward and inward movement. That is, in one direction he makes one large outward and inward movement and one raising and lowering, without rustling the leaves, and afterward he makes three small motions there with rustling the leaves, each motion being a small outward and inward movement; and so for every direction, and likewise raising and lowering. This is the substance of the Ran's words, although this is not his exact language. The Rif wrote: this shaking is apart from extending outward and bringing inward. The Rosh wrote as follows: "for each matter" means for each direction to which he extends the lulav, he must shake three times apart from extending outward and bringing inward. That is, the outward and inward movement does not suffice until he shakes three times with each outward and inward movement. It would have been proper to explain that he holds his hand in one place and shakes some minimal shaking three times, were it not for the question of Rabbi Zeira, etc. This implies that for each side he must extend outward and bring inward three times, and we ask, etc. It follows that three outward movements and three inward movements are required for each side, which are 36 in all, end quote. His words require explanation in their meaning. At first he said that it would have appeared that he holds in one place and makes a small shaking three times, implying that afterward he holds that holding in one place is not enough, but there must be a manner of outward and inward movement there. According to this, in each direction he would make only one outward movement, and there, after that outward movement, he would make an outward and inward movement as the shaking; and likewise with the inward movement that he makes each time, after the inward movement he would make shaking in the manner of outward and inward movement three times. This is also in the rulings of the Rosh. In the Tur it is written as follows: the shaking is that he extends his hand from opposite himself outward, and shakes there three times during the outward movement and three times during the inward movement. There is a missing phrase there, and it should read: "and brings it toward himself and does so," as the Beis Yosef testified that this is the text of the Sephardic books. This is the truth, to agree with what he wrote in the abbreviated rulings of the Rosh. What he concludes, a total of 36 times, means that each outward and inward movement that he shakes is counted as one. It follows that in each direction he first makes an outward movement without shaking, and after he makes the outward movement, while the lulav is there, he makes three shakings, each one outward and inward; and each time that he brings his hand downward he does three shakings in this way. Consequently each direction has six shakings. This is what the Beis Yosef wrote, that the words of the Rosh accord with the third opinion of the Ran, meaning that he too explains that after the outward and inward movement there are small motions. Although from the Ran there it implies that in each direction only three small pairs are needed, while according to the Rosh six small pairs are needed in the manner of outward and inward movement as I wrote, nevertheless in the basic explanation he agrees that there are small motions in the manner of outward and inward movement. But there is difficulty with what the Rosh concludes: it follows that three outward movements and three inward movements are required for each side. According to what I wrote, on one side there is only one outward movement and one inward movement; and if he counts the shakings, then there are six shakings, as I wrote. He should have said: three outward movements and three inward movements for each outward and inward movement on each side. One must say that in truth this is what he means: for each side, in each outward and inward movement that is on each side. Were it not for the words of the Tur, we would have explained the Rosh's words well as follows: the Rosh does not hold that the small motions should be in the manner of outward and inward movement, for this does not apply at all. Rather, this is what he means: those three times mentioned in the Yerushalmi would have appeared to refer only to shaking, that it should be three times, with no connection to the outward and inward movements. Rather, he holds his hand in one place and shakes three times, while the outward and inward movement are done only once in each direction. But if so, what was Rabbi Zeira's question, whether this way is one and that way is one? That refers to outward and inward movement, and here there is only one outward and inward movement, while the minimal shaking alone is three times. Rather, certainly the three times refers to the outward and inward movement, meaning that it must be three times in each direction. About this Rabbi Zeira asked whether in each direction three outward movements and three inward movements are required, with some minimal shaking in each outward and inward movement, or whether an outward movement, inward movement, and outward movement in each direction suffice, and this is called three times, with a small shaking in each one. We go stringently, that in each direction he should perform three outward movements and three inward movements, and each time make a small shaking. But the language of the Tur does not imply that he explains the Rosh this way, for he wrote: "and he shakes there three times during the outward movement and three times during the inward movement," implying that the shaking itself contains outward and inward movement. According to our books of the Tur, where it is not written "and brings it toward himself and does so three times," we can explain well that the phrase "three times" has no attachment to what precedes it, but to what follows it, and this is what he says: he extends his hand from opposite himself outward and shakes there, meaning whatever shaking, and does so three times in outward movement and three times in inward movement, meaning he does so three times in outward movement, extending three times and shaking each time, and likewise inward movement three times, shaking each time. What the Tur writes afterward, "and raises his hand upward and does so three times," means that he raises three times and each time shakes a little. However, in the abbreviated rulings of the Rosh he did not write this way. It appears that the version "and brings it toward himself," etc., can also be settled: three outward movements and three inward movements are required in each direction, but if he only extends outward and there makes three outward and inward movements, the inward movements are not counted, since it is impossible to extend outward unless he afterward brings inward, and we maintain that outward and inward movement are one. Therefore he must afterward make an inward movement after completing the outward movement, and in that inward movement he should also make three outward and inward movements. Only the inward movements are counted, because it is impossible to make an inward movement without an outward movement. It follows that we have only the three upper outward movements and the three lower inward movements on one side. According to this, one need make in one direction only one large outward movement with three small pairs in it, and likewise one large inward movement with three pairs in it, and no more. In Responsa Maharshal, siman 83, he wrote on the words of the Rosh as follows: the explanation of his explanation is whether he needs outward movement together with inward movement, or three times altogether, meaning outward, inward, and outward. About this he wrote that we go stringently and require three times outward movement with inward movement. According to his explanation, the shaking is the outward and inward movement, and so it is explicit in Orchos Chayim, end quote. But the Tur does not imply this at all, for he wrote that he extends his hand from opposite himself outward and shakes there three times, implying that outward movement and shaking are two separate matters. This is how the Beis Yosef understood it, and this is correct. Furthermore, the Rosh already brought the words of the Rif, who wrote that the shaking is apart from extending outward and bringing inward. According to the Maharshal's explanation, there would be no shaking here except the three outward and inward movements, which are the extending outward and bringing inward mentioned in our Gemara. In what manner, then, do you find shaking apart from extending outward and bringing inward?
He rustles the lulav, etc. I am astonished that the Rav mixed the opinions in this. This matter of rustling the leaves was written by the Ran, as I wrote above; and according to the Ran, extending outward and bringing inward is one matter by itself, and afterward come the small motions, which are the rustling of the leaves, as I wrote above. But if this follows the view of the Rosh that we mentioned, however the text in the Tur and its explanation may be, nevertheless not all the shakings require rustling of the leaves. So too it is astonishing what he wrote afterward, that the outward and inward movement itself is the shaking, etc., for this is what the Tur wrote in the name of Baal HaItur, and it is not according to the view of the Rosh. However, the Beis Yosef already answered that this refers to the three small outward and inward movements of each outward and inward movement, that they do not require shaking of the spine and the leaves. The Rema wrote this within the words of the Rosh and the Tur in a way that there is no need at all for small motions after the outward and inward movement. The Maharshal concludes there in his responsum that one who is stringent to make three outward movements and three inward movements, and also to shake with three forces during the outward movement and three forces during the inward movement, in accordance with the words of the Ran, is praiseworthy. Nevertheless, in the second and third outward and inward movement, no further shaking is needed according to any explanation, and one who adds is called an ordinary fool, end quote. It appears to me that to satisfy the version of the Sephardic books in the Tur that we mentioned above, which read "and brings it toward himself," etc., when one makes the inward movement he must bring it close to himself until near his heart. For when he merely returns and brings it toward himself until the measure where it was before him when he began the outward movement, it is not considered an independent inward movement, for it is only the return of the outward movement, as I wrote above. It follows that initially it is proper to take it in this way: he takes the lulav and extends it without shaking until opposite himself, and there makes three small motions in the manner of outward and inward movement. Afterward he brings it toward himself and brings it very close to his body without shaking, and after bringing it close to his body he shakes with small motions as during the outward movement. Afterward he makes outward and inward movement without any shaking two times. And so he should do when raising and lowering, as the Maharshal wrote. In this way he satisfies all the opinions.
And there are meticulous people who do not turn it over, etc. It appears in my humble opinion that this is the primary view. For we derive this from what we learned: the two loaves and the two sheep of Atzeres, how would he do it? He places the two loaves on top of the two sheep, places his hand beneath them, and waves: he extends outward and brings inward, raises and lowers. He extends outward and brings inward to the One to Whom the four directions belong; raises and lowers to the One to Whom heaven and earth belong. Rava said: and so too with lulav. If so, just as with the sheep he lowers without changing his hand from how it was at the time of raising, with the two loaves above the sheep, and only brings it downward, so too with lulav he should perform the lowering just like the raising. Furthermore, it appears to me that when he turns the lulav over to perform the lowering, he will do it diagonally downward, and then he would need to make outward and inward movements to the sides below at the time of lowering. But we say only that he first extends outward and brings inward to the sides, and afterward he does only raising and lowering above and below, and not a second outward and inward movement to the sides. Therefore it is better not to turn it over, but only to lower the lulav downward after raising. Furthermore, in this way he satisfies everyone; and if he turns it over, there is concern that perhaps the opinion that this is not the way they grow is primary. Therefore it is good to do according to what is agreed upon by all.
