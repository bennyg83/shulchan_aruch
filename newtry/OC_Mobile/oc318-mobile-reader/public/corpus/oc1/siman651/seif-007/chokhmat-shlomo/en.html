(There, se'if 7) If he made a handle and placed the lulav in it, this is proper, etc. Note: see the Magen Avraham, who wrote that this means if the entire lulav is outside his hand, etc.; but if the lulav is in his hand it is invalid because of interposition. However, if he fixed it with nails, it is not an interposition. See Tosafos, Yoma 44b, s.v. "every day," who wrote in the name of the Yerushalmi that if he fixed nails into it, it is not an interposition; see there. If so, the same applies here with lulav. According to this, there is no need to press the explanation as the Magen Avraham wrote, that it is speaking where the entire lulav was outside his hand. Rather, it is speaking where the lulav was in his hand, only the handle was fixed with nails and was not an interposition; nevertheless, it still needs to be in a respectful manner. This is fine; examine it. Now regarding what is explained in the Shulchan Aruch, "and if he wrapped a cloth around it and took it, or wrapped it around his hand and took it, there are those who say that he has not fulfilled his obligation," etc., see the Magen Avraham, who wrote that he fulfilled his obligation because it is nullified to the hand. See what I wrote to challenge this in my work on Orach Chayim, year 5612, page 15, in the laws of Nesias Kapayim, siman 128; see there and examine it.
(There in that same se'if, in the gloss) But by law there is no concern, since the entire hand is not covered by them, etc. Note: see the Magen Avraham, who wrote to settle the difficulty of the Bach, that what is wrapped on the hand is different because it is nullified to the hand. It appears that there is proof to his words from what the Rambam wrote in chapter 10, halachah 9, of Hilchos Klei HaMikdash: a kohen who was wounded on his finger may wrap reed-grass over it on Shabbos, or a cloth that is not three by three; and if he intended to draw blood it is forbidden, provided that the reed-grass or cloth should not interpose between his flesh and the vessel at the time of the service. The Raavad challenged him and wrote: his words contradict one another; he said that not in the place of garments, less than three does not interpose between the hand and the vessel, etc. The Kesef Mishneh wrote that the Rambam explains like Rashi, that "they interpose and they do not interpose" is speaking not at the time of the service, but only because of extra garments, etc. The Kapos Temarim brought this in this sugya and rejected his explanation. I will prove further, with Hashem's help, from the language of the Rambam himself that he does not hold like Rashi. For it is difficult: after he wrote that he wraps reed-grass over it on Shabbos, he should have said "even on Shabbos." Especially since the law of Shabbos has no connection here, and perforce what he mentioned here is because of what is relevant to the law of the Temple service; only incidentally he mentioned it as a novelty, that it is permitted even on Shabbos. If so, he should have written "even on Shabbos." Furthermore, in truth, this law is unnecessary here, for he already explained above that less than three does not interpose. If so, this law is unnecessary; why do we need both? And if it is because of Shabbos, he should have written it in Hilchos Shabbos, not here. And if his intent is to say that even initially it is permitted, and if his intent is to teach us that in any event it must not interpose between his flesh and the vessel, he should have written this above briefly and made it one law: that if it does not contain three, it is permitted even initially, provided that it not interpose between his flesh and the vessel. There is another difficulty: in the previous law the Rambam changed his wording three times. First he wrote: if he wrapped on his flesh a garment not in the place of the priestly garments, etc., if it was three by three, it interposes and invalidates. There he used both terms, "interposes" and "invalidates." Afterward he wrote: less than this does not interpose; he used only the language of interposition. Afterward he wrote: and if it was a small belt, since it is significant in itself, it invalidates, even though it is not three by three. Here he used only "invalidates" and did not use "interposes." Perforce, he does not explain like Rashi, but like the Raavad, that "interposes and does not interpose" means actual interposition between the hand and the vessel. Therefore he mentioned the novelty in each case: where it is three by three, it interposes between the hand and the vessel, and not only does it interpose, but it also invalidates because of extra garments. But where it does not contain three, he teaches that it is not even an interposition between the hand and the vessel, and we learn all the more so that it does not invalidate because of extra garments, for otherwise let him state it because of extra garments. Therefore he teaches this, that it does not interpose. But if it was a small belt, which is something significant, then he teaches the novelty that it even invalidates because of extra garments, and all the more so because of interposition. In particular, since once it is invalid because of extra garments there is no need for the issue of interposition. If so, the words of the Rambam explicitly prove like the Raavad. But if so, the difficulty of the Raavad remains: why did he write the opposite in the latter clause? From this there is proof to the words of the Magen Avraham, that where the whole hand is not covered, it is nullified to the hand. According to this, this applies specifically to something that is not needed by the hand; then it is considered as nonexistent, and is nullified as though it were not on the hand. But if the hand needs it for healing and he cannot do without it, and without it he would be unable to perform the service, then one cannot say that it is as though it is not there; rather, it is significant, and therefore it is not nullified to the hand. Thus, although this significance does not make it a garment so that there should be a disqualification of extra garments, since it is not significant in itself but only because of the hand, nevertheless one also cannot say that it is nonexistent and nullified. Since the hand needs it, it is not comparable to straps, for there the place of the mitzvah of tefillin is without the wrappings on the hand, and he can also take the lulav without the straps on his hand, so it is as though it is not there. But when he has a wound on his finger, he needs the reed-grass, and without it he could not perform the service; therefore it is not nullified to it, and therefore it is an interposition. Therefore the Rambam wrote it here. And therefore he wrote "he wraps reed-grass over it on Shabbos" and did not write "even on Shabbos," for, on the contrary, he did not mention Shabbos as a novelty that it is permitted to wrap reed-grass over it, since that has no relevance here. Rather, he mentioned Shabbos in the opposite direction, as a novelty of prohibition: that even on Shabbos we require that it not interpose between him and the vessel. One might have thought that on Shabbos, either way, since on Shabbos it is forbidden to intend for healing and it is only for guarding, one might have thought that then it is nullified to the hand and not considered needed by it. It would be like two contradictory sides: if it is needed by the hand it should be forbidden on Shabbos, and if it is not needed by the hand it should be permitted even if it interposes. Therefore he teaches that although it is permitted on Shabbos, nevertheless it interposes. Therefore, if he had written "even on Shabbos," it would have implied that he mentioned Shabbos as a novelty of permissibility. Therefore he teaches that this is not so; he mentioned Shabbos only because of the latter clause, as a novelty of prohibition, that even then it interposes between the hand and the vessel. Therefore the Rambam properly stated this law by itself. In my humble opinion, this is the truth in the words of the Rambam, with Hashem's help. There is proof for the Agudah and the Magen Avraham, unlike the Kapos Temarim, who wrote from here a proof to the opposite; examine it.
