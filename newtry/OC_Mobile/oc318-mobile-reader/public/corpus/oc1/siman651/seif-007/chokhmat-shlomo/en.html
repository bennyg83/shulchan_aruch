"If he made a handle and placed the lulav in it, this is proper, etc." NB: See the Magen Avraham, who wrote that this means if the entire lulav is outside his hand, etc.; but if the lulav is in his hand it is invalid because of chatzitzah. However, if he fixed it with nails it is not a chatzitzah. See Tosafot, Yoma 44b, s.v. bechol yom, who wrote in the name of the Yerushalmi that if he fixed nails in it, it is not a chatzitzah; see there. If so, the same applies to lulav. According to this, there is no need for the forced explanation of the Magen Avraham, that the entire lulav was outside his hand. Rather, it can speak where the lulav was in his hand, but the handle was fixed with nails and is not a chatzitzah; nevertheless, it must be in a respectful manner. This resolves it; examine carefully. Regarding what is explained in Shulchan Aruch, "and if he wrapped a scarf around it and took it, or wrapped it around his hand and took it, some say he has not fulfilled, etc.," see what the Magen Avraham wrote, that he has fulfilled because it is nullified to the hand. See what I wrote to challenge this in my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5612, fol. 15, in Hilchot Nesi'at Kapayim, siman 128; see there and examine carefully.
There, in that same seif, in the gloss. "But by strict law there is no concern, since the whole hand is not covered by them, etc." NB: See the Magen Avraham, who resolved the Bach's question by saying that something wrapped on the hand is different, for it is nullified to the hand. There appears to be proof for his words from the Rambam, Hilchot Klei HaMikdash 10:9: "A kohen who was struck on his finger may wrap it with reed on Shabbat, or with a cloth that is less than three by three, and if he intended to draw blood it is forbidden, provided that the reed or cloth not intervene between his flesh and the vessel at the time of service." The Raavad objected: his words contradict each other, for he said that outside the place of the garments, less than three is not a chatzitzah between the hand and the vessel, etc. The Kesef Mishneh wrote that the Rambam explains like Rashi, that "they interpose and they do not interpose" speaks not at the time of service but only regarding extra garments. The Kapot Temarim brought this in this sugya and rejected his explanation. I will prove more, with God's help, from the Rambam's own language, that he does not hold like Rashi. It is difficult: after writing that he wraps reed on it on Shabbat, he should have said "even on Shabbat," especially since the law of Shabbat has no relevance here. Perforce, it is mentioned here because it relates to the law of service, and only incidentally does it add the novelty that even on Shabbat it is permitted; if so, it should have said "even on Shabbat." Moreover, this law is actually superfluous here, for he already explained above that less than three is not a chatzitzah; why state it twice? If it is for Shabbat, he should have written it in Hilchot Shabbat, not here. And if his intent is to teach that it is permitted even initially, and to teach that in any event it must not intervene between his flesh and the vessel, he should have written this above briefly as one law: that where it is less than three, even initially it is permitted, provided that it does not intervene between his flesh and the vessel. Also, in the preceding law the Rambam changed his wording three times. First he wrote: if he wrapped on his flesh a garment outside the place of the priestly garments, if it was three fingers by three fingers, it interposes and invalidates; he used both terms, interposes and invalidates. Then he wrote: less than this does not interpose, using only the language of interposition. Then he wrote: if it was a small sash, since it is significant in itself, it invalidates even though it is not three by three; here he used only invalidates and did not say interposes. Perforce, he does not explain like Rashi but like the Raavad, that interposes and does not interpose refers to actual chatzitzah between hand and vessel. Therefore he used each phrase for its novelty: when it was three by three, it interposes between hand and vessel, and not only interposes but also invalidates as an extra garment. When it is less than three, he teaches that it is not even a chatzitzah between hand and vessel, and all the more it does not invalidate as an extra garment, for otherwise it would suffice to say it invalidates as an extra garment. But when it was a small sash that is significant in itself, he teaches the novelty that it even invalidates as an extra garment, and all the more because of chatzitzah; especially since once it is invalid because of extra garments, there is no need for chatzitzah. Thus the Rambam's words explicitly support the Raavad. If so, the Raavad's question returns: why in the latter clause did he write the opposite? From here there is proof for the words of the Magen Avraham: where the whole hand is not covered, it is nullified to the hand. This applies specifically to something the hand does not need; then it is considered as though it is not there and is nullified as if it were not on the hand. But if the hand needs it for healing and cannot do without it, and without it he could not perform the service, one cannot say it is as though it is not there; it is significant. Therefore, although this significance does not make it a garment so as to create extra garments, since it is not significant by itself but only because of the hand, nevertheless one cannot say it is as though it is not there and nullified. Since the hand needs it, it is unlike tefillin straps, where the mitzvah of tefillin is in their place without the wrappings on the hand, and he can also take the lulav without straps on his hand, so it is considered as though it is not there. But when he was struck on his finger, he needs the reed, and without it he could not perform the service; therefore it is not nullified and is a chatzitzah. This is why the Rambam wrote this here. And this is why he wrote "he wraps reed on it on Shabbat" and did not write "even on Shabbat"; on the contrary, mentioning Shabbat is not a permissive novelty that one may wrap reed, for that does not belong here, but a stringency in the latter clause: even on Shabbat it must not intervene between him and the vessel. One might have thought that on Shabbat, since he may not intend healing but only guarding, it would be nullified to the hand and not considered necessary, creating a contradiction: if it is necessary for the hand it should be forbidden on Shabbat, and if it is not necessary it should be permitted even if it intervenes. Therefore it teaches that although it is permitted on Shabbat, it nevertheless interposes. Thus, if he had written "even on Shabbat," it would imply that Shabbat was mentioned as a permissive novelty; therefore he teaches that it is not so, but that Shabbat is mentioned for the latter clause, as a restrictive novelty, that even then it interposes between hand and vessel. Therefore the Rambam properly stated this law separately. In my humble opinion, this is the truth in the Rambam's words, with God's help, and there is proof for the Agudah and the Magen Avraham, unlike the Kapot Temarim, who wrote from here a proof to the contrary. Examine carefully.
