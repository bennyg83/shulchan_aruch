(There, se'if 5) He should recite the blessing "on taking the lulav" and Shehecheyanu before he takes the esrog, etc. Note: see Magen Avraham subparagraph 12, who wrote that nevertheless one should not do this initially, for there are those who say mitzvos do not require intent, etc. From this it implies that he holds that lulav is comparable in this respect to all mitzvos, and according to those authorities who hold mitzvos do not require intent, lulav also does not require intent. In my opinion, at first glance one can distinguish between lulav and ordinary cases, based on what we read in chapter 5 of Yoma, 47b: Rav Pappa asked regarding what is between the fingers in a handful, etc., or perhaps the Merciful One said "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring," and this is not present. Rashi explained: perhaps it did not enter between the fingers, and he did not intend it, end quote. If so, it implies that the language "and he shall take" teaches that intent is required. If so, regarding lulav too it is written "and you shall take," and it is like there, "and he shall take," so without intent it does not help. If so, although the Gemara there remains with the question unresolved, this would again become a double doubt: perhaps with all mitzvos intent is required; and if you say that with all of them intent is not required, perhaps with lulav we require intent, since it is written "and you shall take." With a double doubt one is obligated to recite the blessing again, since a double doubt is like certainty. I reconsidered and saw that this is not so. For if so, with shechitah, where it is written "and you shall slaughter," we should say similarly that intent is required, yet we maintain that shechitah does not require intent. Perforce the intent of the Gemara in Yoma is as follows: with shechitah too we maintain that although we do not require intent for the sake of shechitah, intent for the act is required. But if he did not intend any act, it does not help, because the language "and you shall slaughter" concedes that at least intent for an act is required. This is also the case in that passage in Yoma. It does not say there in Yoma that intent for its own sake is required, for there is no proof for that from the word "and he shall take." Rather, there, regarding what is between the fingers, his intent is not even for taking, as Rashi wrote, that they entered by themselves. Therefore it properly says: or perhaps the Merciful One said "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring," and at least intent for taking is required. If so, with lulav too we require intent for taking, but intent for the mitzvah depends on the law whether mitzvos require intent. It emerges from this that if one took a lulav entirely without intent, for example he intended to take another item and took the lulav, or in another manner where he did not intend even an act of taking at all with the lulav and esrog, this depends on that question in Yoma. For us it is a double doubt and he is obligated to recite the blessing. But there is no proof from the Gemara for the words of the Magen Avraham, that lulav is comparable to other mitzvos and does not require intent, from the fact that it asks, "once he lifted it he fulfilled his obligation." For one may say that the question was only that, if so, let us resolve the question of Rav Pappa mentioned above in Yoma, that we require "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring." We find similar reasoning in several places. Therefore the Gemara rejects this, saying there is no proof from there, for one may say it is speaking of a case where he turned it upside down. But in truth, the law of lulav is like there, and it is doubtful like there, and for us it is a double doubt. However, we already wrote that there is no proof from Yoma, for there the intent is only that intent for taking is required, not intent for the mitzvah. Now at first glance this was difficult to me: according to this, why in Yoma does it remain unresolved whether intent for taking is required, while with shechitah it is plainly proven in the Gemara that if he did not intend for an act it is invalid? Why was it not doubtful there whether if it fell by itself it is invalid, while in Yoma he is uncertain? Therefore it appeared that in Yoma too it is obvious to him that "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring" implies that intent for taking and bringing is required. Only regarding what is between the fingers he is uncertain for another reason: since this is a common and usual thing, that it enters between the fingers, then even though he did not intend this, it is like he intended it, since it will certainly be so and he knows at the time of the act that it cannot be avoided. It resembles what we maintain regarding a psik reisha that will not die, where one is liable even though one who does not intend is exempt; perforce, in a psik reisha, one who does not intend is considered as one who intends. If so, here too, regarding what is between the fingers, the Gemara asks whether this is considered intent, whether the intent for the body of the taking also extends to what is between the fingers, or perhaps the intent for the taking does not extend to what is between the fingers, and since it is written "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring," it is invalid because he did not intend it. If so, according to this, with lulav, where "and you shall take" is also written, we also require intent for taking. Therefore, if he took it without any intent, where he did not intend for taking at all, he certainly has not fulfilled his obligation and must certainly recite the blessing, in a case where he did not intend for taking at all. Specifically intent for the mitzvah is not required, but intent for taking is required. However, if so, I have difficulty with the Gemara in chapter 3 of Rosh Hashanah, which says there that if Persians compelled someone and he ate matzah, he fulfilled his obligation, and it derives from there: learn from this that mitzvos do not require intent. This is difficult: what of it? Granted that intent for the mitzvah is not required, at least intent for eating should be required, since it is written "you shall eat matzos," and intent for eating should be required. How is this different from shechitah, where it is written "and you shall slaughter" and we require intent for an act in general, and likewise the Yoma case above, where it is written "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring," and we require intent for taking and bringing? If so, here too it is written "you shall eat," and if so intent for eating should at least be required. If they compelled him to eat, then under compulsion his intent is not considered intent, as is proven in the Gemara there. If so, there was not even intent for eating there, and why did he fulfill his obligation even according to the one who says intent is not required? At first glance this requires analysis. From this, however, there appeared to be proof for what I wrote in my book Sefer HaChayim, siman 589, that regarding matzah, even though it is under compulsion, the act is considered an act, since because of his compulsion and his enjoyment he resolves and consents. If so, it is fine: regarding the body of the act, he consented because of his enjoyment and compulsion, only to the mitzvah he did not consent; nevertheless, regarding this it helps that mitzvos do not require intent. This is correct; examine it. See what I wrote on this in my novellae to Orach Chayim, beginning Wednesday, parashas Eikev, year 5586, page 41a, in siman 639; see there and examine it. And regarding the wonder about the Rambam, that he did not copy the Gemara's answer that it is a case where he turned it upside down, see there in siman 639 what I settled about this with Hashem's help; examine it. Now regarding what is explained in the Shulchan Aruch, that he should recite the blessing "on taking the lulav" and Shehecheyanu before taking the esrog: see the language of Tosafos, which the Turei Zahav also brought. One must examine this, for their words contradict one another. From what they wrote, that it is not reasonable to recite the blessing while the lulav is still lying in the vessel, it is proven that if it were not in a vessel, even though it is not in his hand, it would still be relevant to recite the blessing over it. If so, why did they afterward write, and so too the Shulchan Aruch, that he should first take the lulav in his hand? Why is this necessary? Let it lie before him without a vessel, and that would also suffice. And if the fact that it lies before him does not help, and this too is too far removed, and therefore we require that he take it in his hand, why did they first write "and the lulav is lying in a vessel"? They should have written plainly that the lulav is not in his hand, or that the lulav is lying there. Why did they need to mention the vessel? Therefore their words require analysis; examine it. While I am on this topic I will mention what was reported to me regarding what the Magen Avraham wrote above in the name of the Mateh Moshe, that it is proper to take the esrog first and afterward the lulav. I was told that the Dagul Merevavah, brought in Shaar Ephraim on Orach Chayim, disagrees with this and wonders at the Magen Avraham from the words of Tosafos and the Shulchan Aruch here, and from the Magen Avraham himself, who wrote that one should take the lulav first. When I heard this I wondered, for certainly the words of the Magen Avraham and the Mateh Moshe are correct. Certainly, according to the practice of the Talmud and the early authorities, where they did not practice turning the esrog upside down but took it in its order, it is proper to do as Tosafos and the Shulchan Aruch wrote. Otherwise, either way there is a problem: if he also takes the lulav, he has already fulfilled his obligation and the blessing is not before the performance; and if he does not take the lulav, it is a great advance preparation and too far removed, since it is not yet in his hand. Therefore an arrangement is needed, to take the lulav first without the esrog. But the Mateh Moshe and the Magen Avraham are speaking according to what we now practice, to take the esrog upside down, with its stem upward. If so, even though the lulav is also in his hand, he still has not fulfilled his obligation, since the esrog is upside down. Therefore it is proper to take the esrog first, as a sign corresponding to the tefillin of the hand and of the head, and afterward the lulav. Nevertheless, it is also like the words of Tosafos and the Shulchan Aruch, since he holds the lulav in its proper manner and the esrog upside down, and after the blessing he turns the esrog to its law. This is exactly like taking the lulav first and afterward the esrog, for he does not hold it in its proper manner until after the blessing, while the lulav is already held in its proper manner beforehand. This is precisely like the words of Tosafos and the Shulchan Aruch, and the words of the Magen Avraham do not contradict one another. Therefore, certainly the custom of Yisrael is Torah, and one who fears Heaven satisfies both the words of Tosafos and the Shulchan Aruch and the words of the Mateh Moshe and the Magen Avraham. Leave Yisrael to their custom, and this is fine with Hashem's help; examine it. Regarding the reason why we recite the blessing specifically over the lulav more than all the species, see what I wrote about its reason in the derashah for Sukkos of this year, 5613, in my commentary on the Torah. Now it appears further that the shaking of the lulav resembles accepting the kingship of Heaven in Kerias Shema. In Kerias Shema, one coronates Him, may He be blessed, above and below and to the four directions of the world; and so too the shaking of the lulav is to the four directions and upward and downward, to accept His kingship as in Kerias Shema. The difference is only that in Kerias Shema we coronate Him above first and afterward below, and therefore the letter ches in Echad precedes the dalet, as the authorities wrote, for the ches indicates that He is One over seven heavens and the earth, and the dalet over the four directions. But with lulav it is the reverse, for first we shake to the four directions and afterward upward and downward, meaning that we begin with an awakening from below first and afterward above, for this is the essence of the matter. It is known that the lulav hints that Yisrael prevailed in judgment on Rosh Hashanah. And how did they prevail? As our Sages of blessed memory said in tractate Rosh Hashanah: coronate Me over you so that your remembrance should rise before Me for good. If so, through coronating Him, may He be blessed, below, and saying "King over all the earth," making Him King below first, therefore they merited in judgment. Since the lulav hints that they prevailed in judgment, we shake it first to the four directions and afterward upward and downward, to hint that through this, through accepting the kingship of Heaven, we merited in judgment. Since the main shakings are with the lulav, and through it it is recognizable that we merited in judgment and by what we merited, therefore we recite the blessing over it. With this our custom is supported, to shake first to the four directions and afterward upward and downward, unlike the custom of the Chassidim. This is clear in my humble opinion with Hashem's help; examine it.
