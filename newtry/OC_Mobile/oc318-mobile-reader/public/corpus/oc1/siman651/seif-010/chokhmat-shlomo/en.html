(There, se'if 10) He should go around toward the right when shaking: east, south, west, north. Note: I have a great wonder here. From all the authorities it seems that the rule "all turns that you turn should be only toward the right" applies even to what is not done by foot but by hand; and in the laws of Chanukah it also seems so. At first glance, with all due respect, an explicit sugya in Yoma 59a was hidden from all of them. It says there that if you say they hold that encircling means by foot, everyone agrees that we derive the inner altar from the outer altar, and here they disagree about this: one master holds that encircling is by hand, and one master holds that encircling is by foot, etc.; see there. From this it is explicitly clear that when it is by hand and not by foot, this law of going around toward the right does not apply. It is already known that the Rambam always rules like the "if you wish, say" answer. If so, it is proven that this reasoning is according to everyone, that where it is by hand we do not require going toward the right. The Talmud concludes there that the correct explanation is as we answered initially, that one master holds encircling is by hand and one master holds encircling is by foot. What it says afterward, "if you wish, say, they disagree about this," is only to say about what reasoning they disagree, whether one encircles by hand or by foot. But this remains there in the conclusion, that they disagree whether it is by hand or by foot; so it is proven to one who examines the sugya there. If so, it is proven in the conclusion that by hand we do not require going toward the right. How, then, do all of them hold regarding lulav and Chanukah, which are not done by foot but only by hand, that going toward the right is required? This requires much analysis now. At first glance, it occurred to me to answer this difficulty against the authorities, so that they not be so astonishing, and not to say, Heaven forbid, that an explicit sugya was hidden from them. One can say as follows: one who examines the Talmud there sees that it first wanted to say that the reason of the one who says he places first on the northeast corner is that since it is the corner he encountered, he should have done it first because one does not pass over mitzvos. Only because it is written, "and he shall go out to the altar," etc., therefore when he returns, he returns to that corner first. If so, it is proven from the Talmud that something for which one already became obligated, and which was proper to do there, is preferable to the advantage of turning to the right. The practical difference for lulav is that if one forgot to extend it to the east or to the south and extended it to the west, and immediately remembered, then he should return to the east or the south, even though he goes from west to south and east and it is not toward the right, since that is where he should have begun, only it was not possible because of some matter; if afterward it is possible, he returns to it. Likewise with Chanukah lights: if he forgot to light the rightmost candle, or it was not possible then to light it, he should return to it and not be concerned that it is not toward the right. If so, although the Talmud retracts and wants to say another reasoning regarding what they disagree about, whether encircling is by hand or by foot, the intent is also this: certainly, where there is no other advantage opposing it, even by hand one should incline to the right. What difference does it make that it is by hand? Whether he turns right or left, all will be done by hand; and since the right has an advantage to turn toward it, even by hand it is proper to do so. Where there is no opposing advantage, the right is preferable even by hand. But where there is an opposing advantage, as here, where he should have placed on the northwest corner first, only it was not possible, if the encircling is by foot, since the actual essence of right is in walking, that is preferable to the advantage of what should have been placed first, and he should turn to the right. But if it is by hand, since he is not truly uprooting the rightward turn, because he stands in one place, in this case it is preferable to do what should have been done there first, and therefore he turns to it. This is what the Talmud says: if you wish, say that one master holds encircling is by hand. That is, according to the first wording, the reason was only because what should have been placed there first is preferable, even if encircling is by foot. The practical difference for the altar would be that if it happened that he forgot and did not place on the corner where he should have placed first, he would turn to it first and not take account of turning right, but would return behind him. Afterward it says that if you wish, say: actually, turning to the right is preferable to the advantage of what should have been placed initially. Therefore, if encircling is by foot, the right is preferable. Thus, on the outer altar, if he forgot and did not place on the corner where he should have placed first, he should not turn back behind him, but only turn to the right. Here, however, they disagree whether it is encircling by hand, for since he stands in one place, his right is not so strong, and in this case we return to the reasoning that he should turn back to the place to which he should have placed first. But in general, where there is no opposing advantage, the right is preferable even by hand. So it appears to explain this sugya. What emerges from what I wrote as practical law is that if there is no other advantage opposing it, the right is always preferable, whether by foot or by hand. But where there is another advantage, for example where he should have done this first and forgot or was prevented and did the second first, then if the act is only by hand, he goes back to what should have been preceded, even though it is by way of the left; but if the circling is by walking with the feet, he turns to the right in his ordinary manner and does not turn back behind him. So it appears in my humble opinion now, and there is no more time. Perhaps Hashem will grant me merit another time to explain this further; examine it well.
