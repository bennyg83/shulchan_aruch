The shaking. See Taz. In the Ran, citing the Yerushalmi, it says that one must shake three times for each matter. Rabbi Zeira asked whether this direction is counted as one and that direction as one, or whether this and that together are counted as one. R. Yitzchak Giyyas explained that the Yerushalmi follows our Gemara, Sukkah 38a, where Rava says that the same applies to the lulav, one raises and lowers it, brings it and takes it out. Rabbi Zeira's question about three times for each matter may mean each place where we shake, namely the blessing, Hodu, Ana, and the final Hodu, and those are the three times; or it may mean whether taking out and bringing back count as two, while raising and lowering count as only one, since once he raises it he necessarily lowers it afterward, and therefore there are three, not four. Or perhaps taking out and bringing back also count as one, and according to this one needs two outward motions with two inward motions, plus raising and lowering, making three. The matter was not resolved. Shaking means taking out and bringing back, and does not necessarily require rustling or fluttering the leaves. See Rashi, Menachos 62a, regarding harmful winds to the east, west, north, and south, and harmful dews above and below. According to the view that the waving is to the One to Whom the world belongs, perhaps only one outward and inward motion is needed. But according to the explanation that it is to restrain harmful winds, four outward and inward motions are needed for the four directions, as Rashi explained in Menachos. In my humble opinion, Rabbi Zeira's question may be explained homiletically: does this direction count as one and that direction as one, meaning outward and inward, while with raising and lowering the main intention is only the raising, since lowering is unavoidable, or are all the motions one expression that the one Creator created everything. Still, the explanations of the earlier authorities are certainly true, each according to his approach. The difficulty is that if so, for a lulav 'long enough to shake,' mere outward and inward movement does not require that measure. The Rif and Rambam therefore explain that the teaching means one must shake for each motion, namely for every outward motion, inward motion, raising, and lowering, one must rustle the leaves three times. Rabbi Zeira asked whether outward and inward are counted as one, and likewise raising and lowering as one, requiring six rustlings, or whether each is counted separately, requiring twelve rustlings; we rule stringently, because of love for the mitzvah, or because there is no burden in doing so. A third view is that the rustling itself should be in the form of small outward and inward movements. First one performs large outward and inward movements, and likewise raises and lowers, perhaps in each of the four directions and above and below, as Rashi explained in Menachos, and afterward in each direction one again performs three small paired outward and inward movements. The Rosh, after citing the Rif, writes that this shaking is besides the outward and inward movement, and explains that for every direction, besides the large motion, there must be small paired movements within it. It emerges that there are different methods: according to one, leaf rustling alone suffices; according to another, small paired movements are required; according to the Rosh's short rulings, after one large outward motion he makes three small paired motions, and similarly after the large inward motion, totaling thirty-six small pairs for the six directions. This remains difficult, for perhaps only rustling of the leaves is required and Rabbi Zeira's question concerns whether the large outward and inward movement counts as one.
And what. What the Tur wrote, etc., see Taz, who seeks to reconcile it with what appears in the short rulings of the Rosh. This refers to what the Tur wrote in the short rulings of his father the Rosh, which the Tur himself composed, as is known and as stated above. The Beit Yosef writes that the Rosh follows the third view in the Ran, but there is a distinction. According to the Ran's third view, 'for each matter' means at the blessing and at Hodu, and after one takes out, brings back, raises, and lowers, he makes three small pairs, while Rabbi Zeira's unresolved question is treated stringently. According to the Rosh, it can mean that within each large outward and inward motion one makes three small pairs, or perhaps six small pairs, three after the outward motion and three after the inward motion. In any case, they agree that at least small paired movements shaped like outward and inward motions are required. When the Rosh says that one needs three outward motions and three inward motions, he means small ones. That is, during the large outward motion there are three small ones, and after the large inward motion three more, six small pairs in all, as explained above. It emerges that in each of the six directions there is only one large outward and inward movement, and within it six small pairs in the form of small outward and inward movements, and perhaps no leaf-rustling is required. However, the Tur may be explained otherwise, according to the Ran, that for each side one performs three large outward motions and three large inward motions, all with leaf-rustling, for the phrase 'a lulav sufficient to shake' refers to rustling the leaves, since outward and inward motion alone does not need four tefachim, as the Ritva and Ran wrote. The words of the Tur, 'he should shake; he should move his hand and shake there three times outward and three times inward,' may be read as two clauses, meaning he shakes there with rustling during the outward motion and similarly three times in the large outward and inward movements. The Sephardic reading, 'and brings his hand toward himself,' can also be reconciled somewhat. But in the short rulings of the Rosh, composed by the Tur, it is explicit that there is only one large outward and inward movement for each direction, with six small paired movements within it. According to this, no rustling at all is required; the shaking in each direction is one large outward and inward movement containing six small pairs. The Maharshal in Sukkah explained the Rosh differently, that without Rabbi Zeira's question one might have thought that shaking three times means leaf-rustling in one place, but Rabbi Zeira's question proves that three outward motions are required. He therefore treats it stringently. According to this, no rustling and no small pairs are required, only three large outward and inward movements in each direction. The Taz writes that this is impossible in the Tur, and also impossible in the Rosh, for the Rosh cited the Rif's language that the shaking is besides the outward and inward motion, whereas according to Maharshal the outward and inward movement itself is the shaking. Rather, it must be explained as above, and with G-d's help it will be clarified further in the next note.
