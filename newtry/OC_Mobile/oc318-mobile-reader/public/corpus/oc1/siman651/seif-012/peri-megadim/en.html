First, it is comparable to the two loaves, and it appears so because Rava did not state a separate rule, 'a lulav is taken out and brought back,' but placed it in comparison to the two lambs; or because he did not say, 'and likewise a lulav,' as Rashi notes, but used the prefix meaning 'with,' which implies this. One may add that restraining winds and dews requires motion toward the six directions; see Rashi, Menachos 62, which I cited in note 10, and the same applies to lulav. See Tosafot, s.v. 'in order,' that on the festival we are judged regarding water. To my poor understanding, there is an allusion here: the shaking of taking out and bringing back is directed to the One to Whom the four directions belong, and also demonstrates that we are victorious, as explained by the Rosh and Avudraham. For this, leaf-rustling and fluttering are not needed; outward and inward movement suffices. But to restrain harmful winds and dews, one needs rustling and fluttering of the leaves, as the Rosh wrote from the Yerushalmi on the Mishnah, that shaking weakens the force of the accuser. See there. The blessing is the main act according to law, and at Ana one rustles to confound; see there. This is why the Mishnah says where they would shake, at Hodu and Ana, but not at Hatzlichah na, as Avudraham wrote: when we shake against the accuser's force, we ask at Hoshiah na to be saved from harmful forces, and at Hodu we give thanks to Hashem for saving us, but this does not apply at Hatzlichah. See there. Eliyah Rabbah writes that for this reason the Tanna said that since the lulav requires four tefachim for shaking, necessarily meaning rustling and fluttering, for outward and inward movement does not require four tefachim as stated above, the question is where we shake: only at Hodu and Hoshiah, not at Hatzlichah, like Beis Hillel. This is what Rashi meant, s.v. 'in order to restrain winds'; why not say like Rabbi Yosei that it is directed to the One to Whom the four directions belong? Rather, as we explained. For this reason the Mishnah uses the wording 'where' rather than 'in which place,' hinting to 'if so' in Aramaic, that in the Beis HaMikdash, where the main flow of blessing was, they poured water on the festival. Understand this. The Taz also wrote that if one turns it over, he must turn it diagonally, and the lulav necessarily inclines during the turning to one of the sides; he has already done this three times, and either way he has fulfilled.
