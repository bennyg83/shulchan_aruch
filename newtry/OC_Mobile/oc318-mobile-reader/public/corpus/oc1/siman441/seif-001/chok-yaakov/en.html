A non-Jew who lent money to a Jew upon his chametz. Taz rules that initially it is nevertheless forbidden to lend money to a non-Jew against chametz with the intention of redeeming it after Pesach, because during Pesach the Jew would violate the prohibitions of seeing and finding chametz. It appears to me that if, when the time for removal arrives, he states that he will no longer redeem it, he no longer violates the prohibition and there is no prohibition at all. This is implied by the Gemara and appears to be Bach’s conclusion. There is also no concern for legal evasion here, unlike below in siman 448, where we fear that one may falsely claim that he nullified the chametz in order to avoid prohibiting and losing it after the fact. Here, if he truly does not redeem it, there is no after-the-fact prohibition or loss. The chametz was retroactively acquired by the non-Jew, and his later conduct proves that when the time of removal arrived he no longer intended to redeem it. Why would he redeem chametz that has become forbidden to him?
He placed the collateral in his possession and said, “From now.” If he did not say “from now,” then even if the non-Jew delivered the collateral to the Jew, a non-Jew does not acquire a pledge from a Jew. Likewise, if he said “from now” but did not deliver the collateral, even if the due date was before Pesach, since the chametz remains in the Jew’s possession and under his liability, it is no better than chametz belonging to a non-Jew deposited with a Jew under the Jew’s liability, which must be removed, as explained in siman 440. All this is explicit in the Gemara and poskim. This is also the view of Rosh and Raavad. They disagree with Rambam only concerning Rambam’s requirement that he say “from now.” They hold that if the due date arrived before Pesach, the chametz is permitted even without saying “from now,” because the law of asmachta does not apply to a non-Jew. Delivery of the collateral is nevertheless required. The Gemara in Pesachim, chapter Kol Shaah, supports this. Rava holds that a creditor collects only from the time of actual collection. The Gemara challenges him from the Mishnah that where a non-Jew lent money to a Jew upon the Jew’s chametz, the chametz is permitted after Pesach, implying retroactive collection. It answers that the case is where the collateral was delivered to the non-Jew. The Gemara does not answer that the due date arrived before Pesach without delivery. According to Rosh and Raavad, it would then not even need to establish that he said “from now.” Rosh’s discussion likewise makes clear that delivery is necessary. Do not challenge this from Rosh in Nedarim 86, who explains the principle that chametz displaces a lien as follows: A Jew designated his chametz as specific security for a non-Jew, stipulating that if he did not redeem it by a set date after Pesach it would belong to the non-Jew. When the date passed and he did not pay, the chametz is forbidden for benefit because it remained in the Jew’s possession until the redemption date, and the prohibition of chametz displaced the non-Jew’s lien. This implies that the problem exists specifically where the date was after Pesach, but not if the date came before Pesach, even though the chametz was not delivered and was merely designated as security. Ran explicitly explains it that way, and Rashi in Kesubos 59 does as well. One may answer that although the prohibition does not fully displace the non-Jew’s lien when the due date came before Pesach, because the chametz then belongs to the non-Jew, the Jew must still remove it because it remains in his possession and under his liability. This is clear to anyone who examines the sources carefully. Bach interprets Rosh and Raavad differently in seif 2, but his explanation is not correct. The later authorities cite Shiltei HaGibborim that if the Jew transferred ownership to the non-Jew through a valid acquisition or together with real property, then even if it was not delivered until after Pesach it is permitted, provided liability no longer rests upon the Jew. For the methods by which a non-Jew acquires, see Choshen Mishpat, siman 123, the later authorities there, and below, siman 448.
And said, “From now.” Beit Yosef, based upon Maggid Mishneh, holds that most poskim agree that everything depends upon saying “from now,” whether the due date occurs before or after Pesach. If he did not say it, the chametz is forbidden. If he did say it, it is effective and is not considered asmachta. The phrase “from now” helps only where the collateral was delivered. Without delivery, “from now” does not remove the law of asmachta, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 207, by Sema. Magen Avraham writes likewise.
“From now it shall be yours.” Magen Avraham writes that it requires study whether, if he failed to say “from now” and the chametz is therefore prohibited, the Jew is obligated to redeem it from the non-Jew. He also discusses several distinctions. See what I wrote above in siman 440, seif katan 4. It is proper to be stringent and redeem it, because chametz displaces the non-Jew’s lien, and otherwise the Jew would be repaying his debt with property prohibited for benefit.
The due date arrived and he did not pay, it is permitted. We do not say that because he could still redeem it, he violates the prohibition during Pesach. Since redemption requires money, we do not apply the principle of potential ability. Ran writes this in the chapter Eilu Ovrin. It further appears to me that once the prohibited time passed and the chametz became forbidden to him if he redeemed it, we certainly do not say he could redeem it, because he will not redeem it merely to prohibit the chametz and cause himself a loss. Tosafos in Pesachim 46 distinguishes similarly. This is the same reasoning I wrote above in seif katan 5.
