[3] One must take, etc. And he blesses—in the Gemara at the end of the chapter "Lulav haGazul": a minor who knows how to shake is obligated in lulav; to wrap himself—obligated in tzitzit; to guard tefillin—his father buys him tefillin. The Bach explained: obligated in lulav to educate him, and likewise in tzitzit; but his father is not obligated to buy him a four-cornered garment, for even he himself is not obligated, as stated below in siman 24—all the more so lulav, whose cost is high; rather, with a lulav that the community buys in partnership, he and the minor bless. But tefillin, whose cost is not high, he must buy for himself, and likewise must buy for the minor—so far his wording. And I wonder that the words of Tosafos at the end of Arakhin escaped him—this is their wording: regarding tzitzit they do not use the language of "buying," for presumably he already has a tallit; and regarding lulav too he can fulfill with his father's lulav. Alternatively, because tefillin are costly, "buying" applies to them—so far their wording. And for the practical ruling: it is clear from Tosafos that even for costly items one is obligated to buy for one's son to educate him; and if he has no tallit, it appears he is obligated to buy him a tallit to educate him—and even per the Bach, now that everyone is careful to buy for themselves, one is obligated to buy for one's son. Maharil wrote in the laws of marriage: I was a bachelor and married a divorcee, and I prepared a new tallit for the wedding and wrapped myself in it before the wedding during the weeks of the festival to honor the festival; for I said, since I had become accustomed in other lands to wear tzitzit from the day I became bar mitzvah, I did not act like the sons of Rinus, who practice that even older youths do not wrap themselves in tzitzit until they marry a wife; and they relied on the verse "You shall make twisted cords for yourself," adjacent to "When a man takes a wife." After the festival Maharil told me that I had acted improperly, for a wedding day is preferable for a bachelor over any festival, and all new garments one should keep for the wedding—so far his wording. And it is implied in the remnants of Knesset HaGedolah that even the sons of Rinus practice with a small tallit, but not with a large tallit.
