[3] He must acquire, etc. And recite the blessing. The Gemara at the end of the chapter Lulav HaGazul states: a minor who knows how to wave is obligated in lulav; one who knows how to wrap himself is obligated in tzitzis; one who knows how to guard tefillin, his father acquires tefillin for him. Bach explains that “obligated in lulav” means obligated for purposes of education, and similarly with tzitzis. The father is not obligated to purchase a four-cornered garment for him, because even an adult is not obligated to do so, as stated below in siman 24. This is all the more true regarding lulav, whose cost is high. Rather, he recites the blessing over the communal lulav purchased in partnership, as does the minor. Tefillin, however, are not expensive, so one must purchase them for himself and likewise purchase them for the minor. I am astonished that Bach overlooked Tosafos at the end of Arachin: “Regarding tzitzis, the Gemara does not use the expression ‘acquires,’ because ordinarily he already has a tallis. Regarding lulav too, he may fulfill the mitzvah with his father's lulav. Alternatively, the expression of acquisition is appropriate regarding tefillin because they are expensive.” Thus, according to Tosafos, even expensive mitzvah objects must be acquired for a son in order to educate him. If he has no tallis, it appears that one must purchase a tallis for him for education. Even according to Bach, nowadays, when everyone is careful to purchase one for himself, one must purchase one for his son. Mahari Weil writes in Hilchos Nisuin: “I was an unmarried youth and married a divorcee. I prepared a new tallis for the wedding and wrapped myself in it before the wedding, on Shavuos, in honor of the festival. I reasoned that since I had become accustomed in other lands to wear tzitzis from the day I became bar mitzvah, and did not follow the custom of the people of the Rhine, where even older youths do not wrap themselves in tzitzis until they marry, relying upon the juxtaposition of ‘You shall make fringes for yourself’ to ‘When a man takes a wife,’ it was proper to do so. After the festival Mahari Weil told me that I had acted improperly, because a wedding day is more significant for a young man than any festival, and all new garments should be reserved for the wedding.” Shiurei Knesses HaGedolah implies that even the people of the Rhine wear a tallis katan; their custom concerns only a tallis gadol. Works of ethical instruction write that a small child should be dressed in a tallis katan once he knows how to speak, and at least from the age of three. The sign is the verse, “for man is the tree of the field,” just as a tree is orlah for three years. He should likewise be accustomed to the letters of Torah. Through all this he will merit a lofty soul in his youth.
