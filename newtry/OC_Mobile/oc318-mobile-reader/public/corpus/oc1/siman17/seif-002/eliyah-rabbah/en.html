[1] And it is not an obligation upon the person, etc. In Eliyah Zuta I explained that lulav is different, because a man is commanded to acquire a lulav for himself. Here, however, even a man is commanded only that if he owns a garment he must place tzitzis upon it, as stated below in siman 24. When Shulchan Aruch writes in siman 19 that tzitzis is an obligation upon the person, the intent is only to exclude an obligation upon the garment, so that one should not say that even when the tallis lies in a box it requires tzitzis. In truth, a man is not obligated to purchase a tallis in order to fulfill tzitzis. Levush appears to have found the two rulings in Shulchan Aruch contradictory. He therefore understood that there is a scribal error and that the text should read, “since it is only an obligation upon the person.” This forced him into all the strained distinctions he gives between lulav and tzitzis. Lechem Chamudos, page 103, understood it the same way. They did not analyze the matter correctly. Nachalas Tzvi and Levush himself at the beginning of siman 24 imply as I have explained. Levush further writes that it resembles a man's garment, and that Michal daughter of Shaul was different because she was the wife of a king and everyone recognized her. Nachalas Tzvi asks: from where did Levush know that Michal wore tzitzis? If it is because Eruvin 96a states that she wore tefillin, and Levush assumes that she also wore tzitzis, then according to Tosafos there, who explain according to the view that women may not voluntarily perform the mitzvah because tefillin require a clean body, why did the Sages protest regarding tefillin but not regarding tzitzis? I do not understand his difficulty. The Gemara asks only why they did not protest regarding tefillin. Regarding tzitzis there was truly no reason to protest. Nevertheless, Levush's explanation is unconvincing. First, as I wrote, the phrase “an obligation upon the person” comes only to exclude an obligation upon the tallis. Second, Yoreh Deah 182 teaches that even changing a single garment is prohibited, although the person is recognizable through the rest of the clothing. It is difficult to resolve this. There it will further be explained that the prohibition of a woman wearing a man's garment applies specifically when she wears it for adornment and beautification; see there. If a woman who wears tzitzis asks whether to recite a blessing, Bach writes that she should be instructed not to recite it. The same law applies regarding shofar if she asks.
