If he needed to relieve himself, etc. If a chazzan needs to relieve himself before the repetition of the Amidah, and he can restrain himself for the time required to walk a parsah, then since after the fact his prayer is valid, and since human dignity is involved, it is clearly permissible to rely here upon the Rif's lenient view, which permits this initially when he can restrain himself for a parsah. It appears that even those who are stringent would agree here because of human dignity. However, if he cannot restrain himself for the time required to walk a parsah, in which case all agree that his prayer is an abomination, the matter is uncertain. One cannot prove leniency from the Magen Avraham's agreement that if the urge arises while one is praying with the congregation, to the point that remaining would violate bal teshaktzu, he may nevertheless finish the prayer because human dignity overrides the prohibition of bal teshaktzu. Although the prohibition against praying when one needs to relieve himself also appears to be only Rabbinic, the cases are not comparable. There, the prohibition instituted by the Sages against praying while needing to relieve oneself, and their statement that such a prayer is an abomination, do not apply, because he checked himself beforehand and began permissibly. Indeed, even the Rama, who is stringent, agrees there that after the fact, if he did not interrupt, his prayer is valid for this reason, as the Olas Tamid writes. Here, however, he needs to relieve himself before beginning to pray, which is the case regarding which the Sages decreed and said that his prayer is an abomination. Consequently, all his blessings may be in vain and may involve the prohibition of taking Hashem's Name in vain. It is therefore possible that one may not be lenient because of human dignity. Perhaps, however, the Sages removed their prohibition in such a case, in which event the blessings would not be in vain. This requires great study. If this occurs to one called to the Torah after he has ascended the bimah, it is clear that he may read, because after the fact his reading is valid, as explained below. Certainly, if he already began reading and the urge then arose, he may complete the entire reading, as explained below in seif 2.
He must repeat the prayer. See Mishnah Berurah regarding urination. The first opinion, namely the Magen Avraham and Chayei Adam, maintains that after the fact he need not repeat the blessing. The latter opinion, namely the Eliyah Rabbah and Derech HaChaim, agrees that he must repeat the blessing. We lack the ability to decide between them. Although the Eliyah Rabbah cites Responsa Rashba, Responsa Rama, and Maadanei Yom Tov as supporting his view, see Magen Gibborim and Maamar Mordechai, who cite many Rishonim who support the Magen Avraham. According to them, even if he could not finish his prayer because urine began flowing down his knees in the middle, when the flow ceases and he must resume his prayer, he returns only to the place where he stopped, even if he knew beforehand that he needed to relieve himself. See there.
The time required to walk a parsah. See Mishnah Berurah. Regarding why the Mechaber did not explicitly state one hour and one fifth, Rabbi Akiva Eiger writes in his novellae, citing Ginas Veradim, that this teaches that even if, by sitting in one place, he could restrain himself somewhat longer, we assess him as though he were walking and could not restrain himself for the distance of a parsah. For the reason behind the measure of a parsah, see Bach, Olas Tamid, and Perishah. I saw a novel explanation in Chayei Adam: the Sages assessed that if he cannot restrain himself for this length of time, the food in his intestines has certainly already become excrement, and the verse states, “and all my innards shall bless His holy Name.” Therefore he must repeat the prayer. This is not convincing. According to him, how can one account for Responsa Rashba, which maintains that even regarding urination, after the fact his prayer is an abomination? Furthermore, if he assessed that he could restrain himself for a parsah, transgressed and prayed, and immediately after prayer relieved himself, would the prayer thereby be invalidated? We have not heard of anyone who maintains this.
But initially. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote that this applies even if he can restrain himself for more than a parsah. This is evident from the words of the Rosh and Rabbeinu Yonah. See there.
Even in words of Torah. See Mishnah Berurah regarding Krias Shema after the fact. It follows that this certainly applies to other blessings, even Bircas HaMazon. Although in siman 185, Mishbetzos Zahav, letter 2, it appears that the Lechem Yehudah considered it obvious that one must repeat Krias Shema after the fact if he could not restrain himself for a parsah, and was uncertain there only regarding Bircas HaMazon, the truth is as Peri Megadim writes here. With Hashem's help, I found this stated in Milchamos in the sugya concerning one who paused long enough to complete the entire prayer, Berachos 23. See there.
