Even though the prohibition of making oneself repulsive does not yet apply, in such a case Torah study is prohibited. See Levush, who reversed seif 3 and seif 2; see there. What the Rama wrote in seif 2 means as follows: The Mechaber wrote that both in prayer and in Krias Shema, if the urge arises in the middle, then during prayer one may not interrupt, while during Krias Shema he may continue reading in the ordinary manner, and if he wishes he may stop, as explained by the Levush. Before Krias Shema, however, if he cannot restrain himself for the distance of a parsah, he may not recite Krias Shema or study Torah, as stated in seif 1, except that after the fact he does not repeat Krias Shema. The Rama added that if the prohibition of making oneself repulsive applies, he interrupts, and so forth. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 2. Nevertheless, regarding Krias Shema, even the Magen Avraham certainly agrees that if his urge is so strong that the prohibition of making oneself repulsive applies, he must interrupt in the middle of Krias Shema. In Berachos 15a, according to the readings of the Rif and Rambam, this requirement applies only to Krias Shema, but for prayer one must go back in search of water, even if the prayer time will pass, and it is prohibited to pray without water. Tosafos there do not accept this reading. In their view, for prayer as well, if the time will pass one does not wait, while so long as the time has not passed one waits. Nevertheless, one does not go back at all for water lying behind him, even a short distance. For water ahead of him, too, if he is under constraint or is weak-hearted, he may pray after cleaning his hands with something that cleans. How, then, did the Mechaber rule here in accordance with both the Rif and Tosafos? One might answer that he adopted the stricter aspects of both opinions. Yet perhaps this creates an even greater stringency, since it would prohibit praying without water even if the prayer time will pass. In siman 233, however, the Mechaber rules entirely in accordance with Tosafos, writing that if water is readily available he should wash. This implies that when it is not readily available, even for prayer, he need not wash. Certainly, if the prayer time will pass he need not seek water, and even if the time will not pass he need not exert himself to obtain it. He is obligated only to return to water that is available to him, and so forth. One may distinguish between a traveler and one at home. While traveling, so long as the prayer time will not pass, he must go up to four mil ahead and one mil behind. At home, however, the Sages imposed no such burden. Alternatively, one may distinguish between hands that are actually dirty, for which he must seek water, and ordinary hands, for which he need not seek it. Or one may say that below, regarding Minchah, whose principal time according to the Mechaber begins at nine and a half hours, as explained by Magen Avraham and Taz there, the law of going four mil ahead cannot practically apply. Four mil take one hour and one fifth, and according to Rabbi Yehudah the entire remaining time for Minchah until plag haminchah is only that amount, less three minutes. Therefore, the Mechaber did not state this law there. Furthermore, according to the Mechaber in siman 106, prayer appears to be a Torah-level obligation. It is therefore improper to allow its time to pass because of the Rabbinic requirement of water. Although the Amidah text is only D'Rabbanan, the Mechaber nevertheless rules that once the time has passed one need not wait. In our novellae we wrote that there may be three positions in this dispute: that of the Rif and Rambam, that of Tosafos, and another position. See Eliyah Rabbah here. This is not the place to elaborate. In the Taz, the text should read that the Mechaber ruled in accordance with the Rif.
