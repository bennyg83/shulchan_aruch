6) There: Initially, he should not pray until he examines himself, etc. Bach writes that even if the prayer time will pass while he relieves himself, he may not pray first, even if he can restrain himself for a parsah. Magen Avraham 1 says one may perhaps rely in this case on Rif, who initially permits prayer when he can restrain himself for a parsah. Yad Aharon in Hagahos Tur and Beis Oved 1 cite this, and Eliyah Rabbah 3, Nehar Shalom 1, Kisei Eliyahu 1, Beis Oved 36, Shulchan Aruch HaRav 1, and Kitzur Shulchan Aruch 12:3 agree.
6) The same applies to a shaliach tzibbur who needs to relieve himself before the repetition: he may proceed because of human dignity. Likewise, if called to the Torah, he may ascend. Ammudei HaShulchan on Kitzur Shulchan Aruch there, in the name of Mishnah Berurah.
6) Eliyah Rabbah writes there that if he will miss communal prayer, he should first relieve himself. Chayei Adam 3:4, Chesed LaAlafim 2, and Ammudei HaShulchan agree. Chida, however, writes in Kesher Gudal 7:33 that even if the time for communal prayer will pass, one who can restrain himself for a parsah may pray. See Neveh Shalom there.
6) There, gloss: Anyone who needs to relieve himself is forbidden even in words of Torah, etc. Accordingly, the reference to prayer is not exclusive; the same applies to Krias Shema and its blessings. Olas Tamid 4. At times a person restrains himself out of human dignity while standing among a congregation; so as not to trouble the public to stand for him, or not to pass before the heads of the holy people, and the like. Praiseworthy is one who orders his ways and weighs the loss of one mitzvah against another. He should always examine himself early so he need not forfeit communal prayer or trouble the public. Carefulness leads to diligence, and diligence to cleanliness. Chesed LaAlafim 3. See Machazik Berachah, siman 3, paragraph 16.
