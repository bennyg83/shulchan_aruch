(Seif katan 2) In the middle, etc. “The Rashba writes, etc.” Since the Rama's ruling and distinction derive from his attempt to reconcile the Rashba's view with Terumas HaDeshen, Magen Avraham cites their words and refutes the Rama, for they necessarily disagree.
To interrupt. As explained above at the beginning of siman 78.
It appears to me that one may rely upon the Rashba. Meaning, since according to Magen Avraham the Rashba and Terumas HaDeshen disagree, and the Rashba maintains that even where bal teshaktzu applies he should not interrupt, whereas Terumas HaDeshen maintains that he should interrupt, Magen Avraham writes that one may rely upon the Rashba and not interrupt. “It is possible that even Terumas HaDeshen prohibited only regarding passing gas, where it is permitted, etc.” Meaning, passing gas does not constitute a complete interruption, but relieving oneself constitutes a complete interruption, and Terumas HaDeshen may therefore agree with the Rashba that one should not interrupt. If so, the converse may also be true: regarding passing gas, the Rashba may agree with Terumas HaDeshen. Magen Avraham did not write this in siman 103. See there for what I will write, with Hashem's help. This is the structure of Magen Avraham's words. At first glance, however, Terumas HaDeshen explicitly indicates the opposite. I likewise found that Eliyah Rabbah writes that Magen Avraham's statement that Terumas HaDeshen also agrees does not appear from Terumas HaDeshen, responsum 57. Terumas HaDeshen addresses the practice, even among meticulous individuals, not to walk four amos and recite the Master of the Universe prayer when one feels the need to pass gas during the Amidah. He reports that one of the great authorities said that when he felt the need to pass gas during prayer, he restrained himself. Terumas HaDeshen asks that one who delays relieving himself violates bal teshaktzu. He answers from Rambam's wording: if one sought to release gas from below and cannot restrain himself, he walks four amos, etc. This explicitly indicates that if he can restrain himself, he acts properly. Perhaps the reason is that bal teshaktzu applies only when one delays defecation or urination, but the Sages did not decree or prohibit merely delaying gas. However, regarding one who cannot restrain himself, Terumas HaDeshen did not find a satisfactory basis for permitting him not to walk four amos, except that he would be disgraced and embarrassed before the congregation when they notice. Accordingly, the Sages' requirement to move four amos and recite the prayer applies only to one praying privately. There is also some proof that for degradation and embarrassment of this kind we do not heed a Rabbinic prohibition, since several Amoraim delayed relieving themselves while sitting before their teacher. Because it would be degrading to leave and relieve themselves in their teacher's presence, they did not heed bal teshaktzu. The same applies here. Nevertheless, Terumas HaDeshen concludes that it is very difficult to invent distinctions based on reasoning when they are not indicated in the Talmud or rulings of the Geonim. These are his words, and I have copied most of them. Beit Yosef below in siman 103 also quotes them briefly. At the beginning, after citing Rambam as proof that one may restrain himself from passing gas, Terumas HaDeshen suggests that bal teshaktzu may apply only to delaying defecation or urination, but not merely to gas. He thus writes that regarding passing gas there is no bal teshaktzu, whereas defecation or urination certainly does involve it. How then can Magen Avraham write the opposite, that Terumas HaDeshen agrees regarding defecation and urination that he should not interrupt and should restrain himself, and prohibited restraint only regarding gas? In my humble opinion, Magen Avraham understood Terumas HaDeshen as follows. Although delaying defecation or urination certainly violates bal teshaktzu, Terumas HaDeshen nevertheless agrees with the Rashba that during the Amidah it is permitted to delay them. Relieving oneself would entail a major interruption, and the prohibition against interrupting the Amidah is more severe than the prohibition against delay, as Magen Avraham writes from Semag. It is therefore preferable to set aside the prohibition of delay rather than the prohibition against interruption. By contrast, when restraining gas, according to Terumas HaDeshen's initial assumption that bal teshaktzu applies even to gas, there is greater reason to be stringent and forbid restraint during the Amidah, because no competing prohibition is involved. Releasing gas during prayer does not constitute a substantial interruption, and walking four amos likewise does not constitute such an interruption. Why then should one be permitted to restrain himself and violate bal teshaktzu? Terumas HaDeshen therefore needed to prove from Rambam's language that delaying gas does not involve bal teshaktzu at all, and he may consequently restrain himself during the Amidah even though releasing it would not constitute a substantial interruption. Magen Avraham's statement that Terumas HaDeshen prohibited only regarding gas, etc., is difficult because this was only Terumas HaDeshen's initial possibility, whereas his conclusion permits even delaying gas based upon Rambam. It must be explained from Terumas HaDeshen's conclusion that there is some proof that for degradation, etc., one may be lenient, followed by his statement that it remains difficult to invent distinctions. Since Terumas HaDeshen records the practice not to move away in such a case, he did not challenge the practice as forbidden. Practice is decisive, and as the Yerushalmi states, when the law is uncertain, observe what the people do. The great authority's personal practice of restraining himself, however, was not established communal practice. Terumas HaDeshen therefore brought proof from Rambam, but did not regard it as conclusive, and added some further proof from the allowance to delay defecation or urination for human dignity. Certainly, then, one may delay gas. His concluding difficulty concerns this permission to restrain gas. Rama likewise appears to have understood him this way, since below in siman 103 Rama rules that in public one need not walk four amos. Had Terumas HaDeshen remained doubtful even about that, Rama should not have stated an unequivocal permission. Necessarily, Terumas HaDeshen's doubt concerned only the great authority's practice of restraining gas. Magen Avraham below in siman 103 also appears to understand the matter this way. Accordingly, Magen Avraham's words here are resolved: even in Terumas HaDeshen's conclusion, the permission to restrain gas remained doubtful. See below, siman 103.
