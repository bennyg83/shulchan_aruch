If the urge arose in the middle of his prayer, etc. See the novellae of Rabbi Akiva Eiger, who leaves the Rashba's proof as a difficulty because he considers it no proof. The central point of his question depends upon his assumption that if one prayed in the presence of urine, his prayer is valid after the fact. According to the many opinions cited above in siman 79 that disagree, however, the matter is readily resolved. Yet I have a difficulty with the Shulchan Aruch. Why did it cite the Rashba's ruling? His principal proof rests upon what he considered obvious, that one may not move from his place during the Amidah even if urine appears near him in the middle of the prayer, as cited by Magen Avraham and Biur HaGra. But the Shulchan Aruch rules in siman 90, seif 27, contrary to the Rashba on this point, as the Acharonim write, and the Beit Yosef retracted there from what he had accepted in siman 78. The Rashba's proof for this ruling therefore falls away. Why, then, did the Shulchan Aruch reproduce it here as law? Perhaps the Beit Yosef found the Rashba's reasoning independently compelling, since he began permissibly, even without the proof.
He should restrain himself until he finishes. See Mishnah Berurah, that by law the permission extends only through Krias Shema and its blessings, and no further. This is clear, for it resembles what we learned in Megillah 23: one does not lead the responsive recitation of Shema, pass before the ark, recite Bircas Kohanim, read from the Torah, or read the Haftarah with fewer than ten. See Ran there, who writes in the name of the Yerushalmi that each of these is a separate matter. Therefore, if one of them began with ten and some departed, although the law is that they finish, they may finish only that matter and may not begin another. From this one may infer similarly here. The Rashba holds that because he began that matter properly, having checked himself beforehand, he may finish that same matter, but may not begin another of the matters listed in the Mishnah or anything comparable. It is therefore clear that when the Rashba permits him to continue Krias Shema in the ordinary manner because he began permissibly, the permission extends only until the Amidah, but not to the Amidah itself, which is a different matter, as explained from the Mishnah.
He reads in the usual manner. It is clear that the same applies if one began listening to the Torah reading, did not need to relieve himself beforehand, and then a mild urge arose during the reading, in a manner that does not involve bal teshaktzu. He may listen to the entire Torah reading and answer “Blessed is Hashem, Who is blessed forever and ever,” until Maftir, which is considered a separate matter, as explained above in siman 55 in Mishnah Berurah, because he began permissibly. The same applies in the middle of Hallel, Megillah, Bircas HaMazon, and similar matters. He may finish that matter in the usual manner, provided his urge is not so strong that bal teshaktzu applies. It appears that the same is true of Torah study. If he is studying privately, did not need to relieve himself before beginning, and a mild urge arose in the middle of the subject, then even if he estimates that he cannot restrain himself for the time required to walk a parsah, he may nevertheless finish the subject. One cannot reject this by saying that Krias Shema and its blessings constitute one matter and may therefore be completed once begun, and the same is true of Torah reading and the other cases, whereas Torah study has no defined completion and therefore gains no advantage from having begun. That is incorrect, because even regarding Torah study we find an advantage in having already begun; see Shabbos 11a and Magen Avraham, siman 106, seif katan 6. At minimum, it appears to me that if interrupting now will cause subsequent neglect of Torah study, he may certainly complete the subject, especially where it is only a mild urge and he can restrain himself for a parsah.
But otherwise. See Mishnah Berurah, where I cited Magen Avraham regarding the Amidah. Regarding Krias Shema, even Magen Avraham agrees that if the urge is so strong that bal teshaktzu applies, he must interrupt and relieve himself, because interruption in the middle is permitted. This is stated by Peri Megadim. The same applies to all the matters mentioned above.
It is better to interrupt. If he interrupted, relieved himself, and thereby paused long enough to complete the entire Amidah, all agree that he returns to the beginning. The same applies to Krias Shema according to the Rama's ruling above in siman 65. If he did not pause that long, however, the matter is not fully clear to me. Seemingly, he should afterward return to the beginning. Even the opinion in Berachos 23a that when urine is flowing down his knees he returns only to the place where he stopped explains that this is because the person remains fit. The Rosh and Rashba explain in a responsum that even while the urine is flowing, he is fit by Torah law. This implies that otherwise, since he was disqualified for a time, he may no longer resume. In our case, while actually relieving himself, he is forbidden by Torah law to recite sacred words. He should therefore be considered disqualified according to all opinions. This is unlike walking through filthy alleyways, where one returns to the beginning only if he paused long enough to complete the entire prayer, as stated in siman 85, because there the person remains fit and only the place is unfit, as the Rosh writes. In truth, this can be rejected. The reasoning that the person is disqualified may apply only together with the fact that he should not initially have begun praying because he already needed to relieve himself, as is evident in the Rosh. Here, however, he checked himself beforehand. This answer may resolve the matter according to the Rosh, but not according to the Rashba, who interprets the baraisa concerning urine flowing down one's knees as a case where he had not needed to relieve himself beforehand and the condition arose only in the middle, yet the Gemara still requires the reasoning that he is fit by Torah law. One may further argue that Rabbeinu Yonah implies there that according to the opinion that one who paused long enough to complete the entire prayer does not return to the beginning, no Amora holds that one who paused for less than that returns to the beginning because of compulsion. The Rosh likewise appears to understand this. If so, the same applies here according to our accepted ruling. All this pressure is necessary according to their interpretation. According to Rashi, however, who explains that “the person was fit” means that he was fit when he initially began to pray, it is possible that even if he interrupted in the middle for a condition that, by Torah law, forbade him from reciting sacred words, he is nevertheless called a fit person. This also appears from Chayei Adam, who writes that one should not speak while relieving himself in the middle of prayer, implying that he does not return to the beginning. It appears that he derived this from the general language of the Poskim, who write that if one paused long enough to complete the entire prayer because of compulsion, whether because the person was unfit or the place was unfit, he returns to the beginning. They do not limit this to a case where the person's unfitness was only Rabbinic, like urine flowing down his knees. This implies that even where the prohibition is Torah-level, he does not return to the beginning unless the interruption lasted long enough to complete the entire prayer.
