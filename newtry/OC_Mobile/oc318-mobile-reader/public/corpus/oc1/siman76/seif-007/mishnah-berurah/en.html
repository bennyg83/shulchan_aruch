It is permitted to recite. If one finds excrement and does not know whether it is from dogs or other animals, or from a person, Magen Avraham holds that we follow what is most common. If young children are more common than dogs, it is forbidden even in a house. Conversely, in a refuse heap, if dogs are more common, we attribute it to dogs and are lenient. This applies specifically if he inspected the refuse heap and found no other excrement besides this. Otherwise, an ordinary refuse heap is presumed to contain excrement, because people commonly relieve themselves there. The later authorities rule like Magen Avraham.

For a refuse heap is presumed. This is not an absolute presumption, for if it were, even uncertainty regarding urine would be forbidden. Rather, excrement is commonly found there, so it is forbidden until he inspects it. I later found that Levush writes this as well.
Uncertain urine. If he knows that urine was present there but is uncertain whether a reviis of water or the like was poured into it, Chayei Adam writes that since the prohibition was established, we rule stringently even in a Rabbinic matter. Nevertheless, in a pressing circumstance, where this would cause him to miss Krias Shema or the Amidah, he may rely on the authorities who hold that even here we are lenient in a Rabbinic matter. If the same uncertainty concerns excrement, meaning that he knows excrement was present in the house but is uncertain whether it was removed, it appears that he may not recite or pray until the matter is clarified, because this is a Torah-level matter.
Even in a refuse heap, etc. Meaning, after it was inspected and no excrement was found there, and provided that no foul odor reaches him. Otherwise, it would be prohibited because of the refuse heap itself, as written in siman 79, seif 8.
Opposite the stream. Magen Avraham writes in the name of the Rosh that dripping is not considered opposite the stream and is permitted on a Torah level. Hagahos Rav Chaim Tzanzer questions this. See there.
