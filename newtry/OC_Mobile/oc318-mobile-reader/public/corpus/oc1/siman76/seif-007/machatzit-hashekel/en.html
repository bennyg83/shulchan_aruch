Seif katan 7, Its law is like excrement. This implies that if it is on his clothing and covered, it is permitted according to all opinions. If it is on his flesh and covered, it is permitted according to the first view. This is the correct reading. Magen Avraham then brings proof for both rulings, although proof is not strictly needed, since once he writes that its law is like excrement, we certainly should not be more stringent than regarding excrement itself. Nevertheless, he cites proof to exclude the view of Sefer Chassidim brought at the end of this seif katan.
And in Shabbos 117, etc. This is proof for the first ruling, that when it is on his clothing and covered it is necessarily permitted according to all opinions. The Gemara explicitly states this, and no authority can disagree. The case there concerns something covered. The Gemara states that Rabbi Chiya bar Abba said in the name of Rabbi Yochanan: Any Torah scholar upon whose clothing a grease stain is found is liable to death, as it is stated, "All who hate Me love death." Do not read it as "those who hate Me," but as "those who cause Me to be hated," because they make themselves repulsive in the eyes of others and thereby cause the Torah to be hated. Ravina says that the text refers to a semen stain. They do not disagree: the first statement concerns an outer garment, where even a grease stain is objectionable, while the second concerns an undergarment, where a semen stain is objectionable but a grease stain is not. Thus, the semen stain discussed there is on an undergarment and covered by an outer garment. Since the Gemara must invoke the concern that he causes the Torah to be hated, it follows that there is no objection regarding Torah study or prayer when the stain is on his clothing and covered.
And this is the wording of Hagahos Maimoniyos, etc. This is proof for the second ruling, that according to the first opinion, when it is on his flesh and covered it is permitted.
From the case of a kohen who experienced a seminal emission, etc., and wishes to recite Bircas Kohanim.
This implies that there is no prohibition, etc. This applies even though it is on his flesh. It necessarily follows that Hagahos Maimoniyos rules like the first opinion, that when it is covered it is permitted even on his flesh.
Only that he may wash it, etc. Meaning, since according to the strict law there is no objection, why is washing permitted on Yom Kippur, when washing is forbidden? Magen Avraham answers that this is comparable to one whose body is soiled with mud, who may wash it, because only washing for pleasure was prohibited, as stated below at the beginning of siman 613 and in the Rama there, seif 3. Rashi at the end of Yoma likewise implies this. However, Rashi does not write that he should scrub away the semen. Rather, he writes that he should scrub because of a chatzitzah. This refers to the baraisa: "The Sages taught: One who experiences a seminal emission on Yom Kippur descends and immerses, and in the evening he scrubs himself." Rashi explains that he scrubs because of a chatzitzah. The Gemara asks: In the evening, meaning after Yom Kippur, what has happened has happened. How does scrubbing then help regarding the chatzitzah that existed on Yom Kippur? Rather, read: Before evening he should scrub, meaning on Erev Yom Kippur each person should scrub himself with hot water so that if he experiences a seminal emission the next day he can immerse without a chatzitzah. Magen Avraham understands that Rashi's explanation according to the Gemara's initial assumption, that he scrubs after Yom Kippur because of a chatzitzah, necessarily means that the semen itself creates a chatzitzah. This implies that he scrubs only because of the chatzitzah, but not because of prayer, since it is covered even though it is on his flesh. One cannot answer that even according to the initial assumption the scrubbing was not specifically because of semen, and that the semen itself would prevent prayer, but that it would suffice to wipe only the affected area, whereas the baraisa's "scrubbing" means scrubbing the entire body lest some attached substance create a chatzitzah, as Rashi explains in the conclusion. Such an answer remains difficult, because from where did Rashi know that according to the initial assumption "scrubbing" meant the entire body lest some substance be attached? Perhaps it meant scrubbing only the area soiled with semen. Even then the Gemara could properly ask, "What has happened has happened," and the reason would be prayer rather than chatzitzah. Since Rashi nevertheless explains it as a chatzitzah, it necessarily follows that semen is not objectionable for prayer when it is covered, even though it is on his flesh. The reason must therefore be chatzitzah. Thus, Rashi also agrees with the permissive opinion regarding excrement on one's flesh.
However, Sefer Chassidim, etc. Even when it is on his clothing, etc. Magen Avraham's proof from the above passage in Shabbos, where the Gemara must invoke the concern that he causes the Torah to be hated, can be reconciled by saying that regarding prayer there is only a minor prohibition, without liability to death, whereas causing the Torah to be hated carries liability to death.
