That it not. See Taz. Excrement inside a glass vessel is permitted even when the vessel touches the excrement, because it is nevertheless a covering. A shoe that touches excrement is different, because it is his garment. Know that this is an unresolved question in Berachos 25b. The Rosh rules stringently and explains it as stated in Shulchan Aruch. Rabbeinu Yonah and Rambam, in chapter 3 of Hilchos Krias Shema, halachah 11, explain that when it is in a cavity it is permitted if the shoe merely touches it, and only when he stepped on it and it became attached is it forbidden. Nevertheless, this is not treated as a compounded uncertainty, because after the ruling of the Mechaber and Rama there is nothing further to consider. The same law that applies to a shoe applies to another garment or similar item, since the verse makes the matter dependent on covering, not on a separate domain. See what I wrote on Magen Avraham, seif katan 1. I do not know whether the Gemara's question concerns a Torah prohibition, or whether on a Torah level it is permitted because it does not touch his body and is considered covered, with the uncertainty only Rabbinic. Even so, we rule stringently, because one does not intentionally create a situation of Rabbinic uncertainty, as the Taz writes in siman 71, seif katan 3. Even were we to say that the Gemara's question concerns a Torah law, there remains a question whether, if he already recited the two blessings preceding Krias Shema and then put on his shoe and it touched the excrement, he would be exempt from reciting the blessings again, since in cases of uncertain Rabbinic blessings we rule leniently. Moreover, there is a compounded uncertainty: perhaps the law is lenient, and perhaps one does not repeat the blessings in such a case, as the Mechaber writes in siman 185. Accordingly, although when he subsequently recited Krias Shema there was a Torah-level uncertainty and he must repeat Krias Shema, and siman 65 states that whenever he repeats it he repeats it with its blessings, it may be argued that this case is different because he had already discharged his obligation regarding the blessings. The same may be said if, after he recited Krias Shema, he discovered excrement in a cavity. I discussed this at length in my novellae. I have now seen that, in any event, according to the Perishah's distinction in seif 7 between uncertainty whether one is obligated and uncertainty whether one performed the obligation, where we wrote that there is a presumptive status, the law here does not change because of that presumption. Proof may be brought from a case of improper concealment of the knife or a pause during the cutting of a minority of the simanim, which creates an uncertainty of neveilah.
