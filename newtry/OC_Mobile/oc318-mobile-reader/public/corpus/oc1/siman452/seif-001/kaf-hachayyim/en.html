1) [Se'if 1] One should be careful to kasher with boiling water, etc. The procedure is as follows. One takes a large cauldron filled with water and brings it to a vigorous boil, until the boiling water reaches every surface of the cauldron, inside and out. One places into it all the utensils one wishes to kasher, inserting them individually, one after another. Levush. Regarding a large utensil that cannot be inserted into the cauldron, see below, se'if 6.
2) Regarding how long a utensil must remain in the cauldron during kashering, Rif in the chapter Kol Sha'ah writes that it should remain there until it expels what it absorbed. Ran writes likewise there, as do Rambam, chapter 5, halachah 23; Raavad in Sefer Tamim De'im, siman 25; and Rashba in Toras HaBayis, bayis 4, sha'ar 4. Radbaz in Leshonos HaRambam, siman 241, demonstrates that this is Rambam's view. See there. Tur, however, writes that the custom is to insert the utensils and remove them immediately. He writes likewise in Yoreh De'ah, siman 121. See there. Mordechai, chapter Kol Sha'ah, siman 579, writes likewise, as cited by Beis Yosef. Levush, se'if 2, also writes likewise. Peri Chadash, at the end of se'if 6, writes that the Jewish people's custom has the force of Torah. Taz, subsection 2, however, writes that Tur's statement to remove them 'immediately' is not literal; they must remain at least briefly. Kol Bo likewise writes that one must be careful not to hurry to remove a utensil from the cauldron, allowing time for the boiling water to enter it and extract what it absorbed. Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 4; Ben Ish Chai, parashas Tzav, subsection 12, write likewise. See below, subsection 14.
3) One might ask: According to the opinion that intentionally nullifying a prohibition is forbidden under Torah law, how did the Torah permit kashering the utensils of Midian with boiling water? This appears to intentionally nullify the prohibition absorbed in them within the kashering water. The answer is that the rule against intentionally nullifying a prohibition applies only when one intends to mix a prohibited substance into permitted food in order to benefit from it. Here, one intends to kasher the utensil and derives no benefit from the prohibited substance, so it is permitted. Ran, chapter Ein Ma'amidin.
4) There. Before the fifth hour. At that time there is a secondary transfer of permitted flavor. Beis Yosef; Taz, subsection 1. How does this occur? The chametz imparts flavor to the utensil, the utensil imparts flavor to the water, and the water then imparts flavor back into the bowl, while the prohibition of chametz has not yet taken effect. Mordechai, chapter Kol Sha'ah, siman 576; Levush, se'if 3. Although some maintain that since it retains the designation chametz, it is considered to have absorbed a prohibited substance, and Maran took their view into account above in siman 451, se'if 4, here he relies upon the lenient authorities because some authorities independently permit all these matters. Ma'amar Mordechai, subsection 1. See above, siman 451, subsection 39.
5) There. Before the fifth hour. This means before the beginning of the fifth hour, namely, by the end of the fourth hour. Since chametz is at least rabbinically prohibited from the fifth hour, as stated above at the beginning of siman 443, its flavor is no longer considered a secondary transfer of permitted flavor. Peri Chadash; Mateh Yehudah; Mekor Chaim, subsection 1; Nehar Shalom, subsection 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 8; Chayei Adam, rule 125, subsection 33.
6) There. So that one will not need, etc. At that time, one need not ensure that the utensil was not used that day. Even if it was used that day and the water does not equal sixty times the utensils, it is permitted, because chametz is still permitted and this is a secondary transfer of permitted flavor. Tosafos, Chullin 108b and Avodah Zarah 76a; Rosh at the end of Avodah Zarah and in the chapter Kol Sha'ah; Mordechai, chapter Kol Sha'ah; Ran at the end of the chapter Kol HaBasar. Tur and Beis Yosef cite these authorities. Olas Shabbos, subsection 1; Magen Avraham, subsection 1; Chok Yaakov, subsection 2; Chok Yosef, subsection 1; Mekor Chaim, subsection 2; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 13; Chayei Adam, there, subsection 33, write likewise. Although the gloss above in siman 447, se'if 5, prohibits foods cooked in a chametz utensil and does not permit them because of a secondary transfer of flavor, here there are several successive transfers: from the food to the utensil, from the utensil to the water, and from the water to the second utensil. It is therefore permitted. Magen Avraham, there; Chok Yaakov, there; Mekor Chaim, there. All this is the strict law, but the custom is not to kasher any utensil that was used that day, as stated below in the gloss to se'if 2. See there. See also below, subsection 21, regarding whether the cauldron used for kashering must first itself be kashered.
7) An incident occurred in which a piece of bread was found in the cauldron used to kasher utensils before Pesach. The question was whether the utensils had to be kashered a second time. Beis David, siman 195, concludes that according to communities that do not apply the rule that a piece becomes intrinsically prohibited, it is obvious that the utensils are not prohibited, because the flavor expelled from the bread into the water was nullified in sixty. Even according to communities that do apply that rule, a second kashering is unnecessary. Their acceptance of the rule was observed only regarding a tangible substance, not mere flavor. Although they customarily kasher kneading utensils belonging to lenient communities, and so forth, that is only an initial stringency. Since this is a custom, we should not add further stringency. If it is clearly established, however, that their custom is to prohibit even after the fact because of flavor alone, then these utensils must certainly be kashered again. See there. Zechor LeAvraham, at the end of siman 451. See above, siman 447, subsections 59 and 76.
