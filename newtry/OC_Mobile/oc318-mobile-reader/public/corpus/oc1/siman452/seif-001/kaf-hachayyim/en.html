1) [Seif 1] One must be careful when performing hagalah, etc. The order of hagalah is as follows: take a large vessel filled with water and heat it until the water boils vigorously over the surface throughout the vessel, inside and outside. Insert the utensils one wishes to kasher, 1 at a time, one after another. Levush. Regarding a large utensil that cannot be inserted into the hagalah vessel, see below, seif 6. 2) Regarding how long the utensil should remain in the boiling water, Rif in Perek Kol Sha'ah writes that it should remain until it expels what it absorbed. Ran writes likewise, as do Rambam, chapter 5, law 23; Raavad in Sefer Tamim De'im, siman 25; and Rashba in Torat HaBayit, bayit 4, shaar 4. Radbaz, in Leshonot HaRambam, siman 241, also proves this from Rambam. Tur, however, writes that the custom is to insert them and remove them immediately, and writes likewise in Yoreh De'ah, siman 121. Mordechai, Perek Kol Sha'ah, siman 579, writes likewise, as cited by Beit Yosef; Levush, seif 2, likewise. Peri Chadash, at the end of seif 6, writes that the custom of Israel is Torah. Taz, seif katan 2, however, explains that Tur's word "immediately" is not literal: the utensil must remain at least a short time. Kol Bo likewise writes that one must not hurry to remove it before the boiling water has time to enter and expel what was absorbed. Eliyah Rabbah, letter 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, letter 4; Ben Ish Chai, Parashat Tzav, letter 12, write likewise. See below, letter 14. 3) One might ask: according to those who hold that one may not intentionally nullify a prohibition by Torah law, how did the Torah permit kashering the utensils of Midian by hagalah? Is he not intentionally nullifying the prohibited flavor absorbed in the utensils into the hagalah water? The answer is that the prohibition against intentional nullification applies where one intends to mix the prohibition into permitted food in order to benefit from it. Here his intent is to kasher the utensil and he derives no benefit from the prohibited substance itself, so it is permitted. Ran, Perek Ein Maamidin. 4) There, "before the fifth hour." At that time it is a secondary transfer of permitted flavor, even when the utensil is ben yomo. Taz, seif katan 1. The chametz imparts flavor to the utensil, the utensil to the water, and the water back to the bowl, while the prohibition of chametz has not yet taken effect. Mordechai, Perek Kol Sha'ah, siman 576; Levush, seif 3. Although some hold that because the designation "chametz" already rests upon it, it is considered prohibited absorption, and Maran took that view into account above in siman 451, seif 4, here he relies upon the lenient authorities because there are other authorities who independently permit these matters. Maamar Mordechai, letter 1. See above, siman 451, letter 39. 5) There, "before the fifth hour." This means before the beginning of the fifth hour, namely by the end of the fourth hour. Since chametz is at least rabbinically prohibited from the fifth hour, as stated above at the beginning of siman 443, it is no longer a secondary transfer of permitted flavor. Peri Chadash; Mateh Yehudah; Mekor Chaim, letter 1; Nehar Shalom, letter 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, letter 8; Chayei Adam, rule 125, letter 33. 6) There, "so that one will not need," etc. At that time one need not ensure that the utensil is not ben yomo. Even if it is ben yomo and the water does not contain 60 against the utensils, it is permitted, because chametz is still permitted and this is a secondary transfer of permitted flavor. Tosafos, Chullin 108b and Avodah Zarah 76a; Rosh at the end of Avodah Zarah and in Perek Kol Sha'ah; Mordechai, Perek Kol Sha'ah; Ran at the end of Perek Kol HaBasar. Tur and Beit Yosef cite them. Olat Shabbos, letter 1; Magen Avraham, seif katan 1; Chok Yaakov, letter 2; Chok Yosef, letter 1; Mekor Chaim, letter 2; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, letter 13; Chayei Adam there, letter 33, write likewise. Although Rama above in siman 447, seif 5, prohibits food cooked in a chametz utensil and does not rely upon secondary transfer of flavor, here there are several successive transfers, from the food to the utensil, from the utensil to the water, and from the water to the second utensil, and it is therefore permitted. Magen Avraham, Chok Yaakov, and Mekor Chaim there. This is the strict law. The custom, however, is not to kasher any utensil that is ben yomo, as stated below in Rama to seif 2. See also below, letter 21, regarding whether the hagalah vessel itself must first be kashered. 7) An incident occurred in which a piece of bread was found in the vessel in which utensils were being kashered before Pesach, and they asked whether the utensils had to undergo hagalah again. Beit David, siman 195, concludes that according to communities that do not apply chatichah na'asit neveilah, it is obvious that the utensils are not prohibited, because the flavor expelled by the bread into the water was nullified in 60. Even according to communities that apply chatichah na'asit neveilah, a second hagalah is unnecessary, because we have not seen that they accepted that stringency regarding mere flavor rather than tangible substance. Although they customarily kasher kneading utensils belonging to communities that are lenient, this is only initially. Since the entire matter is a custom, one should not add a new stringency. If, however, it is clearly established that their custom is to prohibit even after the fact because of flavor alone, then these utensils must certainly be kashered again. Zechor LeAvraham, end of siman 451. See above, siman 447, letters 59 and 76.
