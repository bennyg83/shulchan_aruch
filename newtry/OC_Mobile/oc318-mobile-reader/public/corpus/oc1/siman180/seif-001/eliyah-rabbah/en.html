[Levush] Prepared for a poor person, etc. For this reason the Sages say that one who prolongs his meal prolongs his days, because perhaps a poor person will arrive and he will give him food. One who feeds a poor person at his table or sends portions to one who has none is considered as though he offered a sacrifice on the altar. If no poor person is available, he should give the monetary value of the food. He should also speak words of Torah, because Torah atones even more than sacrifices. The Shelah, folio 86, writes that it is proper to give the best food on the table, especially to a distinguished poor person, so that it is evident he was received graciously. I praise communities that support young Torah students at their tables throughout the year like members of the household. In this way the host fulfills two matters, provision for the poor and words of Torah, because such a student is ordinarily poor and speaks words of Torah. Surely what is eaten at such a table evening and morning is considered like the two daily offerings, especially where the host honors the Torah student by placing before him the finest food on the table.
