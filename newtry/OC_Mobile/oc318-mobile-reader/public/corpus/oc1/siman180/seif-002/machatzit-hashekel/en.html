He likewise infers this from Rashi’s wording. Sanhedrin 92a states that Rabbi Elazar said: Whoever does not leave bread on his table will never see a sign of blessing, as it says, etc. The Gemara objects that Rabbi Elazar also said: Whoever leaves pieces of bread on his table is as though he worships idolatry, as it says, “Those who set a table for Gad,” etc. It answers that there is no contradiction: one case is where a whole loaf is present with them, and the other where no whole loaf is present. Rashi explains that the prohibited case is where a whole loaf is present, meaning that he brings a whole loaf after eating and places it on the table together with the pieces he left; where no whole loaf is present, the pieces are intended for the poor. See Perishah. Thus Rashi explains “where a whole loaf is present” as a case in which he brings it after eating, implying that if it was already present it is permitted. “And perhaps the Zohar,” etc. The wording is not precise, for the Zohar need not agree with the Gemara, and indeed preceded it by a long time. His intent is that although we generally do not follow the Zohar where it conflicts with the Gemara, here one may follow the Zohar because it can be understood not to conflict with the Gemara, and the Gemara too may agree with it. “And according to this the wording is precise,” etc., “for Rashi’s explanation is not settled,” etc. Meaning, although one could in any event reconcile the Zohar with the Gemara by explaining, like Rashi, that “a whole loaf is present” means that he brings a whole loaf, Magen Avraham prefers his own explanation, that one brings pieces. The phrase “a whole loaf is present” then implies that the whole loaf was already on the table, as stated above. This also accords better with the Zohar, according to which one should ideally prepare a whole loaf at the beginning of the meal for Bircas HaMazon, as Rema MiPano writes. According to Rashi, since the main prohibition is caused by bringing the whole loaf, even though its presence makes the act look idolatrous and one also transgresses with the pieces, as Derishah writes, nevertheless the whole loaf is the principal source of the prohibition. Why, then, did Rabbi Elazar not mention it at all? According to the Zohar, however, this is understandable. Presumably one follows the proper practice and prepares a whole loaf at the beginning of the meal for Bircas HaMazon, so Rabbi Elazar did not need to mention it. He mentioned only the pieces left at the time of Bircas HaMazon, which, according to Magen Avraham, are newly brought to the table and create the prohibition. “And on Shabbos,” etc., means that on Shabbos one should certainly follow the Zohar. Taz, at the end of Yoreh Deah 178, permits the custom of some to leave a whole loaf on the table throughout Friday night, unlike Levush, who prohibits it there. He writes that on Shabbos there is no concern of “those who set a table for Gad,” for this is done in honor of Shabbos, whose honor is expressed through two loaves. There is no prohibition, for even before Shabbos, immediately when the loaves are removed from the oven, they are placed upon the table in honor of Shabbos. See Shach there.
