Seif katan 3: Difficult, etc. “This requires study. Do Beis Hillel,” etc. At the beginning of Eilu Devarim in Berachos, Beis Shammai say that one sweeps the room and then washes the hands, meaning mayim acharonim, while Beis Hillel say that one washes the hands and then sweeps the room. On 52b the Gemara concludes that they disagree because Beis Shammai permit using an ignorant attendant. Since it is prohibited to destroy crumbs of a kezayis, such an attendant may leave on the floor crumbs of a kezayis. When one washes into a vessel, water may splash upon those crumbs and ruin them. One must therefore first sweep away all the crumbs. Beis Hillel hold that one may not use an ignorant attendant. Since he is a Torah scholar, he knows to remove crumbs of a kezayis, and we are not concerned about crumbs smaller than a kezayis. The Gemara says that this supports Rabbi Yochanan, who said that crumbs smaller than a kezayis may be destroyed by hand. The Gemara rules in this matter like Beis Shammai. Tosafos, s.v. crumbs smaller than a kezayis may be destroyed, writes: “Nevertheless, it causes poverty, as stated in Chullin 105b.” This prompted Magen Avraham’s question: Do Beis Hillel not take poverty into account?
As stated in Chullin, etc. “And Tosafos cites it,” etc. The Gemara there states that Abaye said: Initially I thought that sweeping food crumbs was required for cleanliness, but my master told me it was because failure to do so causes poverty. Tosafos proves from this that even crumbs smaller than a kezayis cause poverty, for Abaye must be discussing crumbs smaller than a kezayis. Crumbs of a kezayis may not be destroyed as a matter of law, not merely because doing so causes poverty. The Gemara then relates that the angel appointed over poverty pursued a certain man, seeking to impoverish him, but could not, because he was extremely careful with crumbs. One day the man ate bread while sitting in a grassy field, and crumbs fell among the grass where they could not be gathered. The angel said that now he would surely fall into his hands. After eating, the man took a spade, uprooted the grass together with the crumbs, and threw them into the river. He then heard the angel lamenting that he could not overcome him. It is clear from this that throwing crumbs smaller than a kezayis into water does not cause poverty. This is Tosafos’ intent, for otherwise one might ask why Tosafos needed to answer that it nevertheless causes poverty.
At the end of chapter 21 of Shabbos. The Mishnah on 143a states that on Shabbos one may remove crumbs from the table even if they are smaller than a kezayis, and they are not muktzeh because they are fit for animal food. The Gemara says that this supports Rabbi Yochanan, who said that even crumbs smaller than a kezayis may not be destroyed by hand. Rashi explains the inference: since the Mishnah says that one removes them by hand and does not throw them away. Tosafos challenges this reading from Berachos 52b, which says in Rabbi Yochanan’s name that crumbs smaller than a kezayis may be destroyed by hand, and therefore adopts another reading. According to Magen Avraham, however, there is no difficulty. Rabbi Yochanan made two statements, one permitting their destruction and one forbidding it, and both are true. The permission refers to throwing them into water; the prohibition refers to throwing them down where they will be trampled. Although the two formulations are identical, they must be interpreted differently. The Mishnah in Shabbos excludes only trampling them. For this reason it says “one removes them” rather than “one throws them,” since if thrown they may be trampled. It could equally have said that one may throw them into water, but chose the shorter expression “one removes them.”
