And he immediately remembered. See Mishnah Berurah regarding the ruling cited by Peri Megadim in the name of Bach and Derishah, and their reason for it. In truth, their words are not understood, since more than the time required to utter a brief phrase has elapsed. Even if we say that his intent is to join the words ‘Who forms light’ to the word ‘Who,’ so that it is as though he said, ‘Who, by His word, forms light,’ his words still require great study. This reasoning applies only according to the Gemara's initial assumption, when it sought to resolve the question, according to the Rosh's text and explanation, see there. The question concerned one who held a cup of beer, thought it was wine, began, ‘Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe, Who creates the fruit of the vine,’ then remembered and concluded, ‘by Whose word all things came into being.’ Does he fulfill his obligation because we join the words ‘by Whose word all things came into being’ to the Name and kingship stated initially? The Gemara sought proof from the baraisa stating that if one began in the morning with ‘Who brings on evenings’ and concluded with ‘Who forms light,’ meaning that he said, ‘Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe, Who by His word brings on evenings, Who forms light and creates darkness,’ and continued through the conclusion ‘Who forms the luminaries,’ he fulfills his obligation. This would show that we join his statement ‘Who forms light’ to the Name and kingship said initially, disregarding the intervening words ‘Who by His word brings on evenings,’ which are irrelevant. In that context Bach's reasoning applies, namely that the baraisa speaks only where he corrected himself within the time required to utter a brief phrase, for otherwise we certainly do not join the phrases, as Shulchan Aruch rules below in siman 209, seif 2. According to the Gemara's rejection, however, the question remains unresolved and generally we do not join the phrases. Here, since Rabbah bar Ulla said that the daytime attribute must be mentioned at night and the nighttime attribute by day, when he initially stated the blessing and kingship he had both in mind. Why, then, is correction within the time required to utter a brief phrase necessary? One cannot reject this by saying that since we in fact rule leniently on the unresolved question, as Rif writes, the baraisa must be understood simply on the basis of joining the phrases, and we no longer apply the reasoning that he initially had both in mind. This is incorrect. Rif's reason is that since the question was unresolved, we are lenient in a Rabbinic matter. Why, then, should we be stringent here? On the contrary, the same principle should lead toward leniency. Beit Yosef also implies that he fulfills his obligation even if he did not remember immediately. The language of Shulchan Aruch, which afterward says, ‘and he did not say “Who forms light,”’ also does not accord with Bach, for according to Bach it should have taught a greater novelty, that even if he said it, but not immediately, he does not fulfill his obligation. Peri Megadim's resolution is strained. The fact that Shulchan Aruch says ‘and he immediately remembered’ is best understood as describing the usual case, as Beit Yosef himself writes. Moreover, several Rishonim maintain that even if he never said ‘Who forms light,’ but concluded ‘Who forms the luminaries,’ he fulfills his obligation, as we write below. Their view should certainly be combined to rule leniently when he did not say ‘Who forms light’ immediately, as we wrote in Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 2, in the name of Derech HaChaim.
And he did not say ‘Who forms light.’ See Mishnah Berurah. All of this follows Shulchan Aruch and the later authorities, except Peri Chadash, who has a different approach throughout this matter. They require two elements to be proper: first, either the opening or the middle of the blessing; second, the conclusion. Biur HaGra challenges Shulchan Aruch's ruling with several proofs and rules primarily like Rashba, cited in Beit Yosef, that everything depends upon the conclusion alone. If he concluded properly, he fulfilled his obligation. Milchamos Hashem of Ramban, according to his text, likewise supports Rashba's view, that if one concluded ‘Who forms the luminaries,’ he fulfills his obligation even though at the beginning he said only ‘Who brings on evenings.’
