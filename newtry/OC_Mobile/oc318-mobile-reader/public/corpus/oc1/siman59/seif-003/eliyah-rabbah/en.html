Mateh Moshe writes that one should say ‘ma'aritzim u'makdishim,’ all with a final mem and not a final nun. Some have the practice of standing when the chazzan says, ‘and they proclaim as King the great and awesome God,’ because the initial letters of ‘the God, the great, the mighty, and the awesome’ have the numerical value of the Divine Name. This practice has no foundation or essential basis. Nevertheless, all now follow it. Regarding ‘Who illuminates the earth,’ one might ask what benefit the light provides to the earth. The answer is that the sun causes plants to grow and sweetens fruit. The text says ‘with mercy’ because He brings the light gradually, for one who rises from bed and immediately sees a great light cannot open his eyes. The siddur of Maharil based on the Arizal writes that in ‘laKel Baruch’ the lamed has a kamatz, and likewise in ‘u'leMelech Kel chai’ the lamed has a kamatz. Rabbi David Abudraham writes: It is surprising that ‘In Your abundant mercy, have mercy upon us’ was instituted in the blessing over the luminaries. The answer is that the word ‘me'oros’ in the verse is written without a vav because the luminaries cause croup to afflict children. They therefore instituted the plea ‘In Your abundant mercy,’ and so forth, in the blessing over the luminaries. ‘God, blessed, great,’ and so forth, follows the alphabet, through whose letters heaven and earth endure. In connection with the light, we also mention the creation of the angels, who are likewise called light. Furthermore, they are created each day, just as the light is renewed. Knesses HaGedolah writes that one should say ‘u'v'ne'imah kedushah,’ with the dalet carrying a cholam rather than a shuruk, and that one should say ‘u'v'ne'imos kol, kedushah u'tehorah.’ One should say ‘retzon Konam’ without a yud. A gloss to Magen Avraham writes that Yotzer Or, the Amidah, and Bircas HaMazon contain every letter of the alphabet except final peh, teaching that wrath does not rule over one who concentrates during these three. One must pause between ‘Yotzer’ and ‘or,’ so that they not sound like a single word. The initial letters of ‘mesharsim va'asher mesharsav’ spell mum, the final Name among the seventy-two-letter Name; one must therefore take care not to omit the vav of ‘va'asher.’ One should not say ‘u'Melech olam,’ but only ‘Melech olam’ without a vav. The initial letters of ‘ahuvim, berurim, gibborim’ spell Abag, one of the Names within the forty-two-letter Name. The initial letters of ‘osim b'eimah’ have the numerical value seventy-two. Whoever concentrates upon these Names will not be harmed throughout that day. Rabbi David Abudraham, however, writes that one should say ‘u'Melech olam.’ Shibbolei HaLeket writes that we say ‘all as one respond,’ rather than ‘all together respond.’ The Geonim wrote regarding Sandalfon, who is called ‘one,’ as in the verse, ‘I saw one wheel on the earth.’ Rabbi Elazar said: There is one angel who stands upon the earth and whose head reaches among the chayos. He is five hundred years' journey taller than his fellow, stands behind the Chariot, and binds crowns for his Creator.
[4] And when they respond, and so forth. Meaning, in public. This also appears from Levush, for regarding an individual it is explained in siman 132 that he says it quietly. See above, siman 61. Meaning, Rama writes there that the custom is to answer. The Tur likewise writes that this is no worse than responding to a greeting, for which one may interrupt. Shelah, page 253, writes that this does not appear correct to him. Rather, the Sephardic custom is not to answer Amen and interrupt. He adds as their reason that even if one recites Krias Shema with awe, fear, trembling, and perspiration throughout all 248 limbs, he must nevertheless pass from fear to love, so that the joy of faith in Hashem's unity enters his heart, and he is prepared to surrender his life and all his possessions. The blessing therefore concludes with love both in the morning and evening, followed immediately by accepting the yoke of the Kingdom of Heaven and then ‘You shall love,’ and so forth. One should therefore not interrupt. Rather, it appears that he should prolong his recitation so as to conclude the blessing together with the chazzan. Consequently, if he finished before the chazzan, it is proper to answer Amen in accordance with the Tur's view.
