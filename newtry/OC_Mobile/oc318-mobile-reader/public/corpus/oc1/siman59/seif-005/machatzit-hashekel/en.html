Seif katan 5, ‘with fewer than ten.’ Darkei Moshe writes so. Magen Avraham's intent is as follows. Rama writes that the chazzan discharges an individual even if he is proficient, but that the chazzan cannot do so with fewer than ten. This might imply that the requirement of ten applies specifically to one who is proficient, since Beit Yosef discussed one who is not proficient earlier, and one might have thought that the chazzan can discharge him even with fewer than ten. Magen Avraham therefore explains that this cannot be Rama's intent, because the source of this ruling is Rabbeinu Yonah, who explicitly states that even one who is not proficient cannot be discharged by the chazzan with fewer than ten. Rama must therefore mean that even one who is not proficient requires ten for the chazzan to discharge him.
Even though the people in the fields, and so forth. Beit Yosef comments on Tur's citation of a responsum of the Rosh that it is proper to recite Yotzer Or and the evening blessing quietly together with the chazzan, as ruled in Shulchan Aruch. The Rosh explains that a person cannot constantly concentrate silently upon the chazzan. Even if he initially concentrates, should his mind turn to other matters in the middle of the blessing, he loses the required concentration through that interruption. But when one recites the blessing verbally, even if he recites part without concentration, he fulfills his obligation. Beit Yosef asks that at the end of Rosh Hashanah, in the dispute between Rabban Gamliel and the Sages regarding the blessings of Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, the Sages hold that the chazzan discharges only one who is not proficient, while Rabban Gamliel holds that he discharges even one who is proficient. The conclusion is that the halachah follows Rabban Gamliel on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, when the blessings are numerous, meaning long and unfamiliar, but follows the Sages during the rest of the year, when the chazzan discharges only one who is not proficient. Why, then, did the Rosh need the concern that one may turn his mind elsewhere? By law, the chazzan does not discharge one who is proficient during the rest of the year. Beit Yosef answers that this may apply only to the blessings of the Amidah, which are requests for mercy, so each person should pray for himself and concentrates better when reciting them verbally. Regarding the blessings of Krias Shema and similar blessings, however, one fulfills his obligation by hearing them from the chazzan, provided that he concentrates upon all his words. Beit Yosef rules accordingly. Thus, according to Beit Yosef, the blessings of Krias Shema are more lenient than the Amidah, because throughout the year the chazzan can discharge even one who is proficient.
Rabbeinu Yonah, however, indicates the reverse, that the blessings of Krias Shema are more stringent than the Amidah in this respect. The Mishnah in Megillah 23b states that one does not recite the blessings of Krias Shema responsively with fewer than ten. Rabbeinu Yonah explains in the name of Rambam that although the people in the fields are discharged, as Magen Avraham cites here, those people were prevented by circumstances and could not enter the city. The chazzan discharges them even though they remained in the fields and did not hear the prayer from him. This differs from Rashi's explanation there. The Mishnah means that people who did not hear the blessings of Krias Shema from the chazzan because they were prevented from coming to the synagogue when he recited them may later be discharged by one who recites the blessings while they listen and answer amen, but only when ten are present. Rabbeinu Yonah asks that the rule concerning the people in the fields, who are exempt because of circumstances beyond their control, logically applies both on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur and during the rest of the year, unlike the Rosh, who limits it to Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. Why, then, must the blessings of Krias Shema be repeated for those who were absent? They should be discharged by the chazzan's original blessing even without being present, just as the people in the fields are discharged. The Sages certainly would not have instituted this arrangement for one who negligently failed to attend the synagogue. Rabbeinu Yonah answers that the blessings of Krias Shema are more stringent than the blessings of the Amidah. The chazzan cannot discharge the people in the fields for the blessings of Krias Shema until they enter the synagogue, hear them from him, and answer amen.
Therefore, the statement that one does not recite the blessings of Krias Shema responsively, and so forth, is understandable. Thus, according to Rabbeinu Yonah, the blessings of Krias Shema are more stringent than the Amidah regarding discharge by the chazzan, contrary to Beit Yosef's inference from the Rosh.
This implies that it concerns one who is not proficient, for otherwise even regarding the Amidah he does not fulfill his obligation. Meaning, according to Rabbeinu Yonah, the blessings of Krias Shema are more stringent than the Amidah regarding discharge by the chazzan, contrary to Beit Yosef. Rabbeinu Yonah states that one is discharged only by hearing and answering amen, which implies that hearing the chazzan can discharge him. Yet during the rest of the year we rule like the Sages against Rabban Gamliel that the chazzan does not discharge one who is proficient in the Amidah. All the more so would he not discharge one who is proficient in the blessings of Krias Shema, which Rabbeinu Yonah considers more stringent. We must therefore say that Rabbeinu Yonah discusses one who is not proficient. The chazzan can discharge such a person even during the rest of the year in the Amidah, and likewise in the blessings of Krias Shema. Even so, the responsive blessings of Krias Shema may not be recited with fewer than ten. Thus, even one who is not proficient can be discharged by the chazzan only in the presence of ten.
A learned person recites the blessing, and so forth. This means that two people ate together, and one is learned, meaning that he knows how to recite Bircas HaMazon, while the other is ignorant and cannot recite it, as explained at the beginning of siman 193. Blessings over enjoyment differ because they do not require ten.
And in Ran, and so forth. Ran therefore challenges the Geonim, who explain that the rule that the responsive blessings of Krias Shema are not recited with fewer than ten means that one person may not begin the blessings of Krias Shema so that the others fulfill their obligation through him unless ten are present. Their reason is the Kedushah contained in Yotzer. Although the evening blessings of Krias Shema contain no Kedushah, the Sages made no distinction. Ran writes: ‘I am astonished by this explanation, for it would imply that whenever ten are present for the Amidah, the chazzan discharges one who is proficient, whereas at the end of Rosh Hashanah it appears that even when ten are present, the chazzan does not discharge those who are proficient.’ Ran understood the Geonim to be discussing one who is proficient, and therefore challenged them. According to Magen Avraham's explanation that Rabbeinu Yonah discusses specifically one who is not proficient, the same can be said of the Geonim, and the difficulty disappears.
