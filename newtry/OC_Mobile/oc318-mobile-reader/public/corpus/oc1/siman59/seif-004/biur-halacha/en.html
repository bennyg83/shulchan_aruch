With the chazzan. See Mishnah Berurah. See Tur and Beit Yosef, who write in the name of a responsum of the Rosh that if one silently attends to the words of the chazzan, but in the middle of the blessing turns his mind to other matters, he has lost the necessary concentration by interrupting in its midst. Whoever relies upon the chazzan must concentrate upon everything he says and not turn his mind elsewhere, for otherwise he does not fulfill his obligation. When he recites the blessing himself, however, even if he recites part without concentration, he fulfills his obligation. This requires great study. Since we rule that hearing is equivalent to responding, it is as though he actually recited the words, as Tosafos proves in Berachos 20b, s.v. ‘as we find.’ Even if we regard his turning his mind elsewhere in the middle as though he omitted part of the blessing, why should that matter? Rashba writes in the first chapter of Berachos, and part of his ruling is cited in siman 187, that if one altered or omitted much in the middle of lengthy blessings, he fulfilled his obligation. The rule that one who alters the formula instituted by the Sages does not fulfill his obligation applies only when he alters the opening or conclusion. The rest of the text is not indispensable, except for specific words identified by the Sages, such as covenant, Torah, and kingship in Bircas HaMazon, and the mention of the Exodus, kingship, the splitting of the Sea, and the plague of the firstborn in Emes VeYatziv, and similar matters. One cannot say that we regard his turning his mind elsewhere in the middle as though he verbally interrupted, just as we regard the entire blessing as though he recited it. The Rosh's wording, ‘because he interrupted,’ might lend some precision to this. Yet this is not tenable. First, even if one actually spoke in the middle of a lengthy blessing, it is not clear that he must repeat the blessing, as discussed in Nishmas Adam, klal 25. Second, regarding the beginning and end of the blessing, which he heard from another who verbalized them and intended to discharge his obligation, it is understandable that we treat him as though he also verbalized them. But his turning his mind to other matters in the middle is merely an unrelated thought. Even according to the view that thought is equivalent to speech, that applies only when one mentally formulates words of Krias Shema, prayer, or the like, not when he merely thinks about something unrelated. Moreover, the Rosh himself rules in the chapter Mi SheMeiso that thought is not equivalent to speech. Perhaps the Rosh means only that he did not fulfill the mitzvah in its preferred manner, similar to what Beit Yosef writes in siman 58 in the name of Rashba regarding the statement that one who recites Shema together with the men of the watch has not fulfilled his obligation. See there.
Quietly. See Mishnah Berurah. It appears to me that the Rosh said this only in his era, when people still commonly fulfilled their obligation through the chazzan's blessing, as Biur HaGra writes. Since the Rosh was stringent with himself to recite it together with the chazzan, he had to say it quietly and not raise his voice, so as not to disturb the other people who listened and intended to fulfill their obligation through the chazzan's blessing. Possibly for this reason he also hastened to finish the blessing and answer Amen after the chazzan, as cited in Tur, so as not to separate himself from the congregation, since ideally they all had to answer Amen after the chazzan. It may even have been indispensable, for otherwise they would not fulfill their obligation through his blessing, as Rabbeinu Yonah writes in the chapter Mi SheMeiso and as cited in this siman by Magen Avraham, seif katan 5. This does not apply today, when the custom is that each person recites the blessings of Krias Shema for himself.
With fewer than ten. See Mishnah Berurah regarding what it writes about the morning blessings in the name of Levush. Levush's statement is at the end of siman 6 and is cited there by Magen Avraham, seif katan 10. Magen Avraham in this siman, seif katan 1, disagrees and writes that according to all authorities, regarding other blessings, one who is not proficient does not require ten. Peri Megadim there writes that perhaps Levush speaks specifically about one who is proficient. Even regarding one who is proficient, Peri Megadim in siman 8 questions the basis for distinguishing this from blessings over mitzvos, where we rule that one who has already fulfilled his obligation may discharge another's obligation without ten. See there. Chayei Adam, klal 5, rules without qualification like Levush, implying that Levush is stringent in every case. This indeed appears from Levush in siman 59, seif 4. See there also for his reason.
And he should not answer Amen. At first glance, we could say that Beit Yosef means specifically when one prays in exact unison with the chazzan, and likewise when he prays alone. His intent would be to exclude those who always answer Amen after the blessing ‘Who chooses His people Israel with love,’ as mentioned in Tur, siman 61. In that situation one should likewise not answer Amen after the blessing Yotzer Or, because he would be answering Amen after his own blessing. But if he finished before the chazzan, then even after the blessing ‘Who chooses’ he should answer Amen to the chazzan, and we are not concerned about an interruption. Birkei Yosef and Shaarei Teshuvah appear to understand it this way. This is also somewhat implied by Beit Yosef at the end of the paragraph beginning ‘And it appears,’ where he concludes with the view of Ramah. Rama's intent in concluding, ‘See,’ and so forth, would refer to what he writes there, that even when praying alone we are not concerned about an interruption. It is more correct, however, to say that since Beit Yosef rules without qualification in Shulchan Aruch, he intends to reject Ramah's view. Rather, Rabbeinu Yonah's words are understood literally: even if one finished ‘Who chooses’ before the chazzan, he should not answer Amen to the chazzan because it is an interruption, in accordance with Beit Yosef's first resolution. This concern applies only after the blessing ‘Who chooses.’ After ‘Who forms the luminaries,’ if he inadvertently finished before the chazzan, he should answer Amen. Ideally, however, he should finish together with the chazzan so that he need not answer Amen. Beit Yosef holds this as well, contrary to Rama's gloss here. Rama's words, ‘See,’ and so forth, mean that the common practice follows the Rosh, to finish even Ahavah Rabbah before the chazzan and answer Amen after him, as Darkei Moshe writes. Biur HaGra also appears to understand it according to this second explanation. In seif katan 12 he writes that there Rama rules like the Rosh and Ramah, implying that Beit Yosef in Shulchan Aruch disagrees even with Ramah. In my humble opinion, there is no proof in Beit Yosef that he himself agrees with Ramah. He merely cites a proof from Ramah for his own words. Beit Yosef holds that it is preferable to finish together with the chazzan in order to satisfy all views. This resolves Shaarei Teshuvah's question for one who examines the matter.
