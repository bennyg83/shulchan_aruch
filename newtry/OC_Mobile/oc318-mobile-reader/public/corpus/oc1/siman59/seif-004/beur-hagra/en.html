Se’if 4: “The blessing, etc.” The Rosh writes likewise, explaining that this was his practice because a person may fail to concentrate and thereby lose the entire blessing when he did not hear it. Regarding the reading itself, however, he does not lose his obligation, as stated at the beginning of the second chapter: “From here onward, the mitzvah is reading.” See Tosafot, Rosh Hashanah 16b, s.v. VeIyun, etc., which cites the Jerusalem Talmud, etc.
“Quietly, etc.” This is implied by the Tosefta cited above according to the Hagahot Maimoniyot, which explains “they respond” to mean that they say it aloud. The Rosh, however, does not explain it this way, for his words imply that were it not for the aforementioned concern, the individual would not recite the blessing. This indicates that in his time individuals did not recite the blessings; rather, everyone listened to the chazzan, and the concern he mentioned caused our custom to develop. The Tosefta is understood according to its plain meaning, as stated there: “He would say all these,” etc., implying that the others did not recite them. The reason he recited all of them is as I wrote above in siman 49 in the name of the Rosh: one cannot discharge another’s obligation in an oral recitation in that manner, as stated in the Tur there. He nevertheless recites it quietly, because the primary rule is to listen to the chazzan.
“And he should hurry, etc.” As I wrote above at the beginning of siman 51.
“Nevertheless, etc., the blessings, etc.” As stated in the Tosefta. Even R’ Yehuda would recite only Kedushah. The Ran writes there in Megillah in the name of the Geonim that “one may not divide, etc.” means that one may not begin the blessings of Keriat Shema in a manner in which one person recites the blessing and the others fulfill their obligation through his blessing unless ten are present. This is unlike other blessings, such as Kiddush, Havdalah, and similar blessings, where even among two people one may discharge the other’s obligation, except for Birkat HaMazon, which requires three; see there for the reason. The requirement here is because of the Kedushah contained in these blessings. The Sages did not distinguish in their enactment even at Maariv, etc. This means that one who knows the blessing may discharge one who does not know it with fewer than ten, except for the Kedushah within it. This is like what is stated regarding Birkat HaMazon in the seventh chapter of Berakhot. Rabbeinu Yonah likewise writes in the third chapter of Berakhot that, because of the difficulty stated above, he holds that an individual may recite this Kedushah. He therefore explains the rule to mean that the listeners fulfill their obligation only by answering Amen, and that one person may discharge the others only in the presence of ten. This is also alluded to by the statement in the third chapter of the Jerusalem Talmud: “It is not reasonable regarding Keriat Shema that each person should review it with his own mouth.” See above, siman 594, in the Rama’s gloss, and the Magen Avraham there.
“And he should not answer, etc.” As explained, the reason is that one who answers Amen after his own blessings is considered an ignoramus, because one may not interrupt between a blessing and the matter over which the blessing is recited.
“And see above, etc.” There he rules in accordance with the Rosh and the Rama that one should answer Amen. The reason is that these blessings are not blessings over Keriat Shema itself, as explained above. The Rambam, however, writes that after Ahavah Rabbah one should not answer Amen after one’s own blessing. This should be explained as being because of an interruption, as above. See Beit Yosef.
