[Levush] The Mishnayot of Bameh Madlikin, etc. For the explanation of this chapter see Kol Bo and Abudarham. The order of the Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and Levush makes clear that Bameh Madlikin is said after kiddush. Bach writes that matters connected with welcoming the day take precedence and says the world's custom to say Bameh Madlikin first is mistaken and should be protested. I found the same explicitly in Kol Bo, Abudarham, and Rokeach siman 50. Responsa Maharshal siman 85 writes that it should be said before kiddush because Bameh Madlikin corresponds to the lamp and the lamp precedes wine kiddush. This is surprising against all the authorities cited above. Moreover, Bameh Madlikin is not recited because of the lamp mitzvah itself but to remind people to avoid invalid oils and wicks. Responsa Maharshal siman 64 also implies it is said after kiddush. It appears that his phrase "it would be proper to say" states an ideal thought, but not the actual custom. I wrote similarly in siman 267 seif katan 8. Olat Tamid cites Maharshal for the opposite arrangement, and it seems he confused Maharshal with Bach. A later work cites Eliyah Zuta in Maharshal's name while ignoring my disagreement and repeatedly attributes matters inaccurately.
[Levush] Since there are still, etc. Raavan siman 42 writes that it is said so people will know to separate from prohibited oils and wicks for the coming Shabbos. If one asks why it is not said before Minchah so they can immediately correct matters, the answer is that most people are not yet in the synagogue. Kol Bo says it is also said to delay those who did not pray because of danger from harmful forces. Maharam bar Yosef would say after Bameh Madlikin: "It was taught, Rabbi Chananya said: A person must examine his garments on Erev Shabbos near dark lest he forget and go out; Rav Yosef said: this is a great law of Shabbos," and then "Rabbi Elazar said, etc.," as Kol Bo and Shelah record. Maharshal siman 64 ordinarily omitted "Rabbi Elazar said, etc." entirely, explaining that it is only aggadah recited so Kaddish can follow, whereas we say Aleinu before Kaddish anyway. He therefore preferred omitting it so as not to delay hearing Kaddish, especially since the congregation tends to rush through it.
