You can know this, for according to Rabbeinu Tam, etc. Beit Yosef cites Agur, who writes that his father would not recite a new blessing after a fixed daytime sleep because the poskim dispute whether one must do so. Beit Yosef objects that no such dispute is found and that everyone requires a blessing. He then suggests that Agur's father relied upon Rabbeinu Tam, who holds that even an entire night's sleep is not an interruption. Although we do not rule like Rabbeinu Tam regarding nighttime sleep, perhaps his view should still be considered regarding daytime sleep. This is the alternative opinion cited in seif 11. Magen Avraham explains that according to Rabbeinu Tam, since even nighttime sleep is not an interruption, the reason Birchas HaTorah is recited each day must be that the Sages instituted it anew every day, like the morning blessings. According to him, one who rises before dawn would not recite it until dawn. It is therefore possible that Agur's father and the alternative opinion in seif 11, which take Rabbeinu Tam's view into account, accept his basic principle that neither daytime nor nighttime sleep is an interruption, and that the Sages instituted Birchas HaTorah anew each day like the morning blessings. We disagree with Rabbeinu Tam only in this detail: one who rises before dawn must recite it immediately, as stated in seif 13. The prior blessing exempts only one day. Once he rises, even before dawn, it is considered a new day for this purpose. Accordingly, one who remained awake all night must at least recite the blessing when dawn arrives, because the prior blessing exempted only one day, like the other morning blessings. One might alternatively say that the alternative opinion does not fully accept Rabbeinu Tam. Perhaps it holds that a fixed daytime sleep is not an interruption and wakefulness at night is not an interruption, but nighttime sleep is an interruption. If so, one who remained awake all night would not recite a new blessing. Magen Avraham therefore says: “You can know this, for according to Rabbeinu Tam,” etc. Since Rabbeinu Tam certainly requires a new blessing because of the new day, it is preferable not to multiply the dispute between our view and his. We should adopt the first understanding: we agree with his general principle and disagree only regarding one who rises before dawn. Accordingly, one who remained awake all night must recite the blessing. In siman 494, Magen Avraham himself rules that one who remained awake all night should recite the blessing when day begins. If someone present slept during the night, he should listen to that person's Birchas HaTorah, with both intending that the listener fulfill his obligation. Eliyah Rabbah writes likewise. In siman 46, seif katan 12, Eliyah Rabbah writes that one who did not sleep all night should not recite Elokai Neshamah or HaMaavir Sheinah.
