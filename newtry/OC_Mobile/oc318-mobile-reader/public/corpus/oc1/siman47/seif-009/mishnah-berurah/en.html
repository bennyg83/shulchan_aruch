(19) That if he interrupted. Their reasoning is that only Ahavah Rabbah, whose principal institution is not Birchas HaTorah, exempts Torah study only if one studies immediately without interruption. The regular Birchas HaTorah, however, exempts the entire day, even if one does not study after Shacharis until midday. This differs from other blessings, where one may not interrupt between the blessing and the mitzvah, as explained in siman 206. Torah is different because the mitzvah is to contemplate it day and night, and therefore there is never a complete lapse of attention. Most later authorities disagree with the Mechaber and agree with the alternative view. They hold that Birchas HaTorah follows the ordinary law of blessings over mitzvos or enjoyment: if one interrupts immediately after the blessing, he must repeat it, even if he knows with certainty that he did not divert his attention, because the mitzvah had not yet begun and the blessing has nothing upon which to take effect. Peri Chadash and Gra agree, and Chayei Adam rules accordingly. Nevertheless, because some poskim hold that one need not repeat the blessing, it is better, if one interrupted after Birchas HaTorah, to intend afterward in Ahavah Rabbah to fulfill Birchas HaTorah and to study a little immediately after prayer. See Biur Halachah.
(20) And this is the custom. The reason for saying three verses is that they wished to arrange the passage in the manner of one reading from the Torah, and they selected these verses because they contain blessing. They may be recited even before dawn. This is unlike those who prohibit saying these verses then because the priestly blessing is not performed at night. That is incorrect, because we recite them for Torah study, not as the priestly blessing.
