If there is a distinction between Ahavah Rabbah and Birchas HaTorah, the wording is clear because it says this only regarding Ahavah Rabbah. If there is no distinction, four explanations may be offered. First, “studied” excludes Krias Shema. Second, “immediately” means that prayer does not constitute an interruption, as Levush writes. Third, Ahavah Rabbah exempts only the study performed immediately afterward, not later study after an interruption. Peri Chadash cites Raavad and Rashba in this manner. Levush appears at first to agree, as Magen Avraham, letter 6, implies, though in letter 7 I shall explain that this is not Levush's view. Birchas HaTorah, by contrast, exempts the entire day. Fourth, Bach, with whom Peri Chadash agrees, holds that one may not interrupt after Birchas HaTorah, just as with other mitzvah blessings. If he interrupted, he may need to recite the blessing again. Ahavah Rabbah exempts Torah study only where immediate study reveals that the blessing was also intended for Torah study. If he did not study immediately, perhaps his intention in Ahavah Rabbah was only for Krias Shema. Birchas HaTorah, however, was principally instituted for Torah. Even if he did not study immediately but recited the passage of the daily offering, one cannot say that he intended only for that passage and not for the entire day's study. According to Rashi, the passage of the daily offering is considered like public Torah reading. See Beit Yosef. For this reason French authorities also recite Eilu Devarim, the Mishnah Eizehu Mekoman, and the baraisa of Rabbi Yishmael. They hold that immediate study is required after both Ahavah Rabbah and Birchas HaTorah. This is our custom.
