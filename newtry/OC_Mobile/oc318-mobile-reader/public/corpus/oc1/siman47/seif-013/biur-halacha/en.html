"One who rises early," etc., recites Birchot HaTorah. See Mishnah Berurah regarding the case where he went back to sleep afterward. The principal question is whether sleep by day or a second sleep at night creates a new interruption requiring another blessing. The authorities dispute this because Birchot HaTorah resemble Birchot HaMitzvot in some respects and Birchot HaShevach in others. If one slept a fixed sleep by day, many authorities hold that he should recite Birchot HaTorah again upon rising, because a fixed sleep is a genuine interruption. Others disagree and hold that the morning blessing continues for the entire day until the next principal night's sleep. Since the matter concerns a doubtful blessing, the usual practice is not to recite a new blessing after a daytime sleep. One who wishes to satisfy all opinions should have in mind when reciting Ahavat Olam in the evening to discharge any renewed obligation and then study immediately afterward. If one rose in the middle of the night, recited Birchot HaTorah, studied, and then returned to sleep until morning, there is a substantial dispute. Magen Avraham holds that the later sleep constitutes an interruption and requires a new blessing in the morning. Several later authorities disagree. Pri Chadash and others maintain that once he recited the blessing after his principal nighttime awakening, he need not repeat it. In practice the doubt should be avoided where possible by hearing the blessing from another person or by intending to be discharged through Ahavah Rabbah and studying immediately afterward. The rule is different when the first awakening was merely temporary and the person clearly intended to return to sleep. In such a case it is preferable, where feasible, not to recite Birchot HaTorah until the final awakening. If he needs to study Torah during the temporary awakening, however, he must not study without a blessing, and he should recite it then. Whether he must repeat the blessing after returning to sleep remains subject to the dispute just mentioned. The later authorities also discuss one who remained awake the entire night. Since he did not have the ordinary interruption of sleep, some hold that he should not recite a new Birchot HaTorah in the morning. Others hold that the obligation renews each day. The accepted practical method is to hear the blessings from someone who slept, answer Amen, and then study. If no such person is available, the various customs follow the rulings of the later authorities as explained in Mishnah Berurah. All this concerns actual Torah study. Mere contemplation does not require a blessing according to the principal ruling, although writing Torah as part of learning may be different, as explained above.
