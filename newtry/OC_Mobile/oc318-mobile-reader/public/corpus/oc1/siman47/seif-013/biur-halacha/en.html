One who rises early, etc., recites Birchas HaTorah. See Mishnah Berurah regarding one who later returned to sleep. This comes from Derech HaChayim, who writes that if one later went back to sleep, whether by day or by night, he need not recite another blessing. His intention is even where he returned to a fixed sleep before dawn, as he himself concludes, excluding Peri Chadash, who requires a new blessing in that case. He does not mean to exempt even a fixed sleep at the beginning of the following night, despite what Peri Megadim's wording at the end of Eshel Avraham, letter 12, may superficially imply. It appears more correct that Peri Megadim agrees. His suggestion that Derech HaChayim may exempt for a full twenty-four-hour period was meant only to exclude Peri Chadash's view that the first blessing lasts merely until the person rises in the morning. It is nevertheless obvious that a fixed sleep at the beginning of the following night constitutes an interruption. This is also implied by Beit Yosef at the end of the paragraph beginning “Agur further writes.” We should not disregard the clear rulings of so many great authorities merely because of Peri Megadim's uncertainty. Beit Yosef, Peri Chadash, Gra, and Eliyah Rabbah all require a new blessing even after a fixed daytime sleep. Although in that case we are stringent like a minority view, after a fixed sleep in the following night one certainly must recite the blessing.
One who rises before daylight, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 30. After the fact, if he already recited Elokai Neshamah and HaMaavir Sheinah properly the first time, Peri Chadash holds that he repeats both blessings when he rises from bed a second time. Chayei Adam agrees regarding HaMaavir Sheinah. Mishmeres Shalom in siman 6 implies that he should not repeat them, and Derech HaChayim likewise implies this. Because of doubt concerning blessings, one should be lenient and not repeat them. If sleep merely overcame him unintentionally, everyone agrees that he does not repeat them. Peri Chadash, siman 46.
He recites the entire order of blessings except, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, where I wrote “the later authorities.” This refers to Magen Avraham in the name of Zohar, Gra, Peri Chadash, and Chayei Adam. What I wrote, “but some say,” comes from Chayei Adam. Shaarei Teshuvah records that some are lenient in this matter as well. According to Baer Heitev and Derech HaChayim one should be stringent initially even if he heard a rooster. I did not rule without qualification like them because Peri Chadash and Gra personally acted leniently, as recorded in Peri Chadash and Maaseh Rav. Moreover, Peri Megadim implies that before daylight one may recite the blessing even if he did not hear a rooster. Only after midnight and before dawn would hearing a rooster be required. Chayei Adam's words appear at first to contradict Magen Avraham's conclusion in seif katan 13. Upon careful examination, however, Chayei Adam can be understood as reading Magen Avraham literally. Magen Avraham refers to siman 46, seif 8, where Rama permits the blessing even without hearing a rooster because the Gemara refers to the world's regular order. Accordingly, perhaps the Zohar's phrase “when the rooster calls” also means the world's regular order rather than actual hearing. Magen Avraham then concludes, “Nevertheless, it is good to be careful initially,” meaning to be stringent where he did not hear it. If he heard it, one may be lenient even initially.
