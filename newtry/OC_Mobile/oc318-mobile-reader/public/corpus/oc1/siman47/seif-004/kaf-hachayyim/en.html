6) [Se'if 4] One who merely thinks words of Torah need not recite a berachah, because thought is not equivalent to speech. Beis Yosef; Taz 3; Magen Avraham 2; Shulchan Aruch HaRav 2; and Chayei Adam 9:10. See Nishmas Adam 4 regarding the words of Gra, and Pri Megadim, Mishbetzos Zahav 2. Accordingly, those who study a text analytically must take care to articulate some words of Torah after the berachah. Taz, ibid. This applies if one does not recite the passage of Birkas Kohanim immediately after Birchos HaTorah, for there is concern that he might interrupt with mundane speech before uttering words of Torah. One should be stringent, as Maran writes in se'if 9 that it is proper not to interrupt between them. If, however, he recited Birkas Kohanim immediately after Birchos HaTorah, he need not take this precaution. See Pri Megadim, Mishbetzos Zahav 3.
7) There: One who merely thinks words of Torah need not recite a berachah. Minchas Aharon 5:34 writes that analytical study from a book is not mere thought, since it is virtually impossible for one not to utter something while analyzing the text. If he articulates it, he certainly must first recite the berachah. Rabbi Beis Oved, in his handwritten novellae on Orach Chaim to this siman, agrees; Pischei HaDevir 2 cites him. See there.
8) There, in the gloss: The same applies in that one may issue a ruling without giving the reason for it. The later authorities rule likewise: Solel Boneh 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, ibid.; Chayei Adam, ibid.; and Ben Ish Chai, Vayeishev 12. Levush writes that the reason, which constitutes the essential words of Torah, remains only in his thoughts. This implies that if he states the reason for the ruling aloud, he may not do so until he recites the berachah. Chesed LaAlafim 1 and Ben Ish Chai, ibid., rule accordingly.
9) There, in the gloss: The same applies in that one may issue a ruling, etc. Minchas Aharon 5:34 concludes that Rama's reference to issuing a ruling means specifically a ruling concerning what is forbidden or permitted, and not monetary law. Rama himself states this explicitly in Darkei Moshe, siman 85, in the name of Ran, and refers to it in Darkei Moshe to this siman. He explains why, in monetary law, whether at the beginning or conclusion of a case, one may not issue a ruling before Birchos HaTorah even without giving a reason: this itself is Torah. On this basis, he disagrees with Mateh Yehudah 4, who understood Rama to refer to monetary law. He writes that it is forbidden to adjudicate monetary law between litigants. Moreover, the license concerning forbidden and permitted matters applies specifically to a simple ruling that requires no textual research. If the matter requires substantial study through consulting a text, it is not mere thought and requires a berachah. Pischei HaDevir 3. See Yafeh LaLev 3, who writes that both types of ruling are permitted without stating a reason; see there. It appears that, wherever possible, one should be stringent in accordance with Minchas Aharon.
