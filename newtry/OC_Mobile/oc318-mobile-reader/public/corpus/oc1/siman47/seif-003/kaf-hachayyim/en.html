5) [Se'if 3] One who writes words of Torah must recite the berachah even if he does not read them. Taz 2 questions this ruling: How does writing differ from thought, regarding which the next se'if rules that no berachah is recited because thought is not equivalent to speech, as Beis Yosef writes at the end of this siman? Writing, too, involves only thought. He concludes that 'one who writes words of Torah' means one who writes or copies them intending to learn; such a person properly recites the berachah. A scribe who copies a book merely as paid work, however, should not recite a berachah beforehand; see there. Magen Avraham 1 writes similarly, but concludes that even one who writes as a mode of study requires further consideration, for it is nevertheless only thought; see there. Solel Boneh 2 and Yad Aharon, Hagahos Beis Yosef, rule likewise. Levush 4, however, explains that a writer recites the berachah even though he does not read because he performs an action through writing. Pri Megadim, Mishbetzos Zahav 2, writes likewise; see there. According to this, even if he does not intend to study what he writes, he must recite a berachah because he performs an act of writing. Mateh Yehudah 2 similarly writes that the unqualified language of Shulchan Aruch implies no distinction: whether one writes to study or copies for pay, writing is equivalent to speech and requires a berachah; see there. Chayei Adam 9:11 explains that a writer ordinarily articulates what he writes and might utter it unintentionally. He writes that this applies all the more to one writing a Sefer Torah, tefillin, or mezuzos, who is obligated to articulate the words and therefore must recite the berachah. Amar Shmuel 1:2 likewise writes that one may not write sacred scrolls before reciting Birchos HaTorah, because he is obligated to articulate what he writes; see there. In practice, to avoid every doubt, both a scribe writing sacred scrolls and a scribe copying a book as a mode of study, or anyone writing words of Torah, should first recite Birchos HaTorah and then read the passage of Birkas Kohanim, as is customary. His berachah will then primarily apply to Birkas Kohanim, after which he may write whatever words of Torah he wishes. One who listens to words of Torah from others should do likewise: he should recite Birchos HaTorah, read the passage of Birkas Kohanim, and then listen, because this matter is disputed. See Machazik Berachah 4 and Erech HaShulchan 2. If, however, one merely writes several words from verses in a friendly letter for elegance of expression, no berachah is required, as Taz and Magen Avraham write there. See above, siman 46:56, and below, paragraph 7.
