Seif 3: One who writes words of Torah, etc., must recite the blessing, etc. The Mechaber's words require study. Here he clearly holds that writing is superior to mere thought. Yet in the laws of Megillah, siman 690, regarding one who was writing the Megillah, it is evident that he fulfills the mitzvah only if he also reads it aloud. Writing alone does not suffice. This is explicit in Megillah 18a. One does not fulfill the mitzvah through writing alone, but only through verbal recitation. Why should this be so? Without the derivation connecting “remembering” to “remembering,” thought alone would have sufficed. Since the Torah says, “You shall not forget,” forgetting of the heart is already forbidden. What, then, does “Remember” add? It teaches verbal recitation. Even so, perhaps writing should suffice, since writing is superior to thought. If “You shall not forget” alone would have required only mental recollection, and “Remember” teaches that thought is insufficient, perhaps writing should still qualify. The Gemara itself is difficult. How does it know that the case of one writing the Megillah means that he also read it orally? Perhaps writing itself fulfills the mitzvah. We are therefore forced to conclude like Taz and Magen Avraham that writing is equivalent only to thought. This appears very difficult. It seems to me that the matter can be resolved as follows. The Gemara states that according to all opinions the entire Megillah must be placed before him. According to the initial assumption that thought would suffice, the case would be that the complete Megillah lies before him and he reflects upon it. The derivation from “remembering” teaches that reading by heart does not fulfill the mitzvah. This does not contradict the requirement that the entire Megillah be before him. Before the derivation, oral reading by heart may have sufficed without the Megillah before him. After the derivation, the reading must be from a complete written Megillah lying before him. Accordingly, if he reads aloud and afterward writes, the verbal reading is from the complete Megillah placed before him, and he fulfills the mitzvah. If he does not read aloud and seeks to fulfill the mitzvah through the act of writing itself, then while writing he is not looking at the complete Megillah placed before him, but only at the words he is presently writing. One cannot direct his eyes elsewhere and write at the same time. Since he is looking only at the section being written, the complete Megillah is not before him during the act that is supposed to fulfill the mitzvah. Writing therefore cannot fulfill the mitzvah. It remains possible that writing is superior to thought. This appears correct, and the difficulty is resolved. Examine this carefully.
