Permanently. If one made a pouch for tefillin, then even if he placed them inside intending later to remove them from that use, the pouch is forbidden for mundane use. The same applies if he had an existing pouch and added something to it for the sake of tefillin, and then placed tefillin inside intending later to remove them. It remains forbidden until he removes what he added. If he placed tefillin inside once with the intention that the pouch be used for them permanently, it is forbidden even if he had not previously designated it. Magen Avraham. See there. Responsa Shaar Ephraim, siman 21, concludes that any portion extending beyond the space needed for the tefillin is not considered necessary for the tefillin. He therefore permits placing the tefillin in the lower compartment of a double pouch and money in the upper compartment. See there.

It is obvious that a person cannot forbid property that is not his. If one stole cloth and made it into a tefillin pouch, he acquires it through the physical change and thereby renders it forbidden. It is permitted. Without an explicit stipulation, it is forbidden. Therefore, those who use their tefillin bag for other items while traveling do not act properly. Nevertheless, one may defend the custom by saying that such people presumably act as though they explicitly stipulated this use. The same applies to Rama's ruling here that parchment processed for tefillin may not be used for mundane writing. We see daily that scribes write kesubos, gittin, and other non-sacred documents upon their parchment. Presumably, when they initially prepare it, they intend it for either sacred or mundane use. Accordingly, it is certainly permitted to write a get upon parchment processed for a Sefer Torah, as is also implied in Even HaEzer, siman 124, seif 2. Magen Avraham and Taz. See Bach and Olas Tamid. If one already began writing sacred material upon parchment or paper, he may not afterward write mundane matters upon it, and a condition does not help. All this concerns sacred objects such as tefillin and a Sefer Torah. For accessories of sanctity, a stipulation is effective even to permit their use while they retain sanctity, as explained in siman 154, seif 8. A stipulation does not permit a degrading use, as explained there. Objects used for a mitzvah, such as tzitzis, shofar, lulav, and Chanukah lights, do not become forbidden through designation. Mundane use is permitted even without a condition. See there.
