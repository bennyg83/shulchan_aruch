(Se'if katan 1) To sunset, etc. This means the end of sunset, etc. His intent is that the Tur wrote as follows: Its time is close to sunset, when the sun is at the tops of the trees. The Bach explained that close to sunset means half an hour before sunset, as we say regarding close to Minchah, meaning half an hour before Minchah. In any case, it is clear from the words of the Tur that close to sunset and when the sun is at the tops of the trees are one time, and their times are equal. Therefore the Bach challenged the language of the Shulchan Aruch, which wrote that the time of the Ne'ilah prayer is when the sun is at the tops of the trees, so that he will complete it close to sunset; but according to what was said above, these are the same time. See the Taz, who tried to force an explanation of this. Therefore the Magen Avraham answered that the Tur is speaking of the beginning of sunset, whereas the Shulchan Aruch is speaking of the end of sunset, which is a long time after the time when the sun is at the tops of the trees, which is half an hour before the beginning of sunset. From the beginning of sunset until the end of sunset is the measure of four or five mil, each authority according to his view, as is found in Pesachim 94.
As Tosafos wrote in Avodah Zarah 34a, s.v. 'mis'anin,' etc., that sunset can be said to mean the end of sunset, namely the emergence of the stars; see there.
See Beis Yosef, siman 562, who explains this way the words of the Talmud, 'Any fast over which the sun did not set,' etc., meaning the end of sunset, which is the emergence of the stars. He also brought the words of Tosafos mentioned above.
And the Bach wrote that nowadays the practice is, etc. For in the Yerushalmi, Rav and Rabbi Yochanan disagree, since for this reason they called this prayer Ne'ilah, after its time. Rav holds that its time is at the time of the closing of the gates of heaven; the Rambam wrote at the beginning of Hilchos Tefillah that this means that the gates of heaven are locked before the sun and it is hidden, namely at night. Rabbi Yochanan holds that its time is at the time of the closing of the gates of the Sanctuary, which is during the day. The Bach concludes that their dispute concerns the time of the end of the prayer, which is the main matter; according to Rabbi Yochanan, the conclusion of the prayer must be by day, namely when the sun is at the tops of the trees. That is the beginning of the prayer according to Rav, and the end of its time according to Rav is at night. Nevertheless, Rav agrees that initially, if possible, one should begin the prayer during the day, only that its conclusion should be at night.
The Bach considers primary the authorities who rule like Rabbi Yochanan, that the conclusion too must be by day, and about this the Bach wrote: And nowadays, when the practice is, etc. For they hold like the Rif and the Rosh, meaning that they rule like Rav. Seemingly, the Magen Avraham's bringing the words of the Bach is unnecessary, because the Beis Yosef in the Shulchan Aruch ruled explicitly like the Rif and the Rosh, according to the Magen Avraham's explanation that 'sunset' written in the Shulchan Aruch means the end of sunset. One must therefore say that he brought it only because of the end of the Bach's words, that one should say the stanza 'the day turns' before the sun sets. However, regarding the time of the prayer too he needed to bring the words of the Bach, because in the Shulchan Aruch it says that he should complete it close to sunset. Although the Magen Avraham explained that this means the end of sunset, namely the emergence of the stars, nevertheless he wrote 'close to sunset,' which means half an hour before the emergence of the stars, as the Magen Avraham also wrote in se'if katan 4, that it is still day or bein hashmashos. The Bach, however, was lenient on the basis of the custom even if the conclusion of the prayer would be actually at night.
But one must begin, etc. For in this even Rav agrees, if possible, as above. He wrote that they should be careful not to hurry and thereby confuse the Minchah prayer; 'the wise man has his eyes in his head'; see there.
