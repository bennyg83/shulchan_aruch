(1) Written matters. A blind person may recite Scripture by heart because, “It is a time to act for Hashem; they have violated Your Torah.” The same applies to one imprisoned where he cannot obtain a Chumash.
(2) You may not. Radbaz, part 1, siman 135, writes: “Regarding reading the weekly portion by heart while the chazzan reads, I personally am careful, but I do not protest against others.” Many poskim hold that the prohibition applies specifically when one discharges others through such a recitation. Ateres Zekeinim and Biur HaGra write likewise.
(3) To recite them by heart. One who delivers a public discourse containing many Torah verses, and for whom repeatedly searching in a Chumash would burden the congregation, may perhaps be lenient. See Yoma 70a, Tosafos Yeshanim, s.v. “And on the tenth,” in both explanations. Biur HaGra agrees in practical halachah with the latter explanation.
(4) And fluent. If it is not fluent in everyone's mouth, it is forbidden even if it is fluent in his own mouth.
(5) Such as Krias Shema. The same applies to Pesukei DeZimrah.
(6) And the passage of the daily offering. Magen Avraham writes that one who studies Beit Yosef will see that there are differing explanations for why these passages may be recited by heart. One should therefore be careful not to recite anything by heart except what is mentioned here in Shulchan Aruch. Responsa Chavos Yair permits reciting the entire book of Tehillim by heart. Since it is recited to awaken Hashem's mercy, it has the status of prayer. It appears to me that one may rely upon him. In any event, Ateres Zekeinim and Biur HaGra follow the poskim who hold that the prohibition applies specifically when one seeks to discharge the public.
(7) It is permitted. When one recites Scripture to discharge others, Magen Avraham inclines to be stringent and not recite it by heart under any circumstances. Biur HaGra's words likewise imply this.
