(1) To be careful. A brief introduction: bodily immersion requires that no interposition adhere to the body, preventing water from reaching that place; otherwise the immersion is invalid. By Torah law an interposition invalidates only when it covers most of the body. Rabbinically, even a minority is forbidden as a decree because of a majority, but only where people ordinarily object to leaving it there, even if this person does not presently object. Something people do not mind is an interposition only when it covers most of the body, which is rabbinically forbidden. Netilas yadayim is rabbinic, but the Sages modeled their enactments upon Torah law. Thus, an interposition over most of the required washing area invalidates even if people do not mind it; over a minority it invalidates only if people ordinarily object. The Shulchan Aruch's instruction to avoid an interposition means removing before washing anything people ordinarily object to. If an interposition is found afterward, whether on a minority or majority, we may assume leniently that it appeared later. Initially, however, one should inspect his hands before washing.
(2) Filth beneath the nail, etc. This means nail grime that ordinarily accumulates there. Actual excrement, even opposite the flesh, logically invalidates the washing if found remaining there.
(3) Not opposite the flesh. For example, where the nail extends beyond the fingertip. Most people object to the repulsive grime beneath that extension, so it interposes and must be removed before washing. People object to dough even opposite the flesh. Yoreh De'ah 198 lists additional interpositions; only common ones are listed here. Eliyah Rabbah cites Sefer Chasidim that for this reason it is proper not to grow long nails.
(4) And a bandage. A rag spread with fat, wax, or another ointment and placed upon an ache [Aruch].
(5) Upon his flesh. Below, siman 162:10, one need not wash the bandaged place at all: since it cannot be washed, it resembles a severed finger, and only the rest of the hand requires washing. That case involves a wound, so we do not fear he will remove the bandage and touch food with that flesh, because it hurts. Here there is only discomfort at a place requiring washing, without pain from washing; he may remove the bandage whenever he wishes. We fear he might remove it during the meal and touch food, so he must remove it and wash. Washing while it remains would leave an interposition.
(6) And mire, etc. Meaning cistern mud, like potters' clay, which adheres strongly and prevents water reaching beneath even when wet. Other mud, when dry and adhering to flesh, likewise interposes, whether on the hand or beneath a nail, even opposite the flesh. This excludes a quantity so slight that people do not object, which does not interpose wherever found; the same applies to excrement and dough, as explained in Biur Halachah. One whose occupation involves mud, such as a brickmaker or potter, certainly does not regard it as an interposition wherever found, since he does not object, as below in se'if 2; unless it covers most of the washing area.
(7) But, etc. This returns to the beginning of the se'if and the general law of interposition: one need not be concerned about an interposition on a minority of the hand when he does not object. Tur holds that even concerning the items previously listed, to which most people object, it does not interpose if this person does not object. Magen Avraham and the other later authorities hold that for all those items we follow most people, not the individual.
(8) On a minority, when he does not object. If most of the hand (meaning most of the washing area) is covered, it interposes even when he does not object.
(9) The mud. Magen Avraham limits this to mud opposite the flesh, even mire and potters' clay, because in these countries people do not object there, making it a minority to which they do not object. Beyond the flesh, most people certainly object even to excrement, so it interposes even if this individual does not. Chayei Adam and Derech HaChaim adopt his view. Some are lenient regarding excrement and ordinary mud.
(10) He objects. This implies that although most people do not object, since he does, we follow him and it interposes. This appears limited to potters' clay or dry mud. One need not be stringent with wet mud or dirt beneath a nail opposite the flesh, which does not adhere strongly; especially if he ordinarily objects only when the time for washing arrives.
