[1] Levushei Serad: Long, etc. Therefore, a person should not grow his fingernails long. Sefer Chassidim, siman 58.
[2] Levushei Serad: Because the excrement is not firmly attached, etc. This is somewhat difficult, for he later writes that what is opposite the flesh does not constitute an interposition because it is not firmly attached. One may answer that here people are ordinarily particular about it, whereas opposite the flesh people are ordinarily not particular, except occasionally; therefore, since it is not firmly attached, it does not constitute an interposition. Yet this requires study, for in Yoreh Deah, siman 198, seif 3, he cites an opinion that anything loose enough for water to enter beneath it never constitutes an interposition, even where people are ordinarily particular. Perhaps one may distinguish between something loose and something merely not firmly attached. In truth, in Yoreh Deah there, seif 18, Levush writes without qualification that what is not opposite the flesh is something people are particular about and constitutes an interposition, while what is opposite the flesh is not ordinarily a matter of concern and does not constitute an interposition. The law of dough also requires study. Here he gives the reason that it is firmly attached, whereas in Yoreh Deah he writes: “Dough beneath the fingernail, even opposite the flesh, is something people are ordinarily particular about and it constitutes an interposition.” Perhaps he gives the reason that it is firmly attached because if it were not firmly attached, objection would not make it an interposition, as he wrote earlier. However, according to my distinction between something people are ordinarily particular about and something they mind only occasionally, this cannot be said, and the matter requires study. Perhaps his meaning is that since it is firmly attached, people are ordinarily particular about it.
[3] Levushei Serad: A large amount that obstructs, etc. Olas Tamid writes that Levush implies this applies even when one does not object to it, and he expresses surprise, since a minority that one does not mind does not constitute an interposition. He did not examine Yoreh Deah there, where Levush explains that the reason is that one does mind it. This is also evident from the phrase here, “But if it is a small amount,” etc., and from what he later writes, “like dough, if he is particular,” etc. It is thus clear that if there is only a small amount of dough that people do not mind, it does not constitute an interposition. Beit Yosef writes likewise. The reason Levush did not explicitly write that people are ordinarily particular has already been explained. See below, siman 162, seif katan 8.
[4] A bandage on his flesh, etc. As for the ruling at the end of siman 162 that it suffices for him to wash the rest of the hand, excluding the place of the bandage, one may answer that there the case involves an actual wound on his hand, whereas here it involves only a minor ailment. Since there is concern that he may remove the bandage and touch food, he must therefore wash the entire hand. Bach and the later authorities write likewise. Maadanei Melech, folio 224, however, distinguishes that below the case is one who does not mind the bandage.
