The Shabbos before Pesach. Magen Avraham writes: Maharshal would teach that every person should eat a small amount before Pesach from the flour prepared for Pesach, because perhaps some chametz became mixed into it and it may be attributed to what he ate. It is proper to give some of it to the poor. This is from Mateh Moshe and Shelah. He questions this, because that principle applies only to an important dry item mixed with another dry item, when one of them was eaten, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 109. Flour mixed with flour is considered a liquid mixture. If there are sixty parts, everything is permitted; if not, everything is forbidden. He concludes that the practice must be merely an additional stringency. In my opinion, Maharshal stated the practical law. Although we rule that flour mixed with flour is treated as a liquid mixture, some leading poskim hold that it is treated as a dry mixture, as explained by Shach in Yoreh Deah, siman 109, seif katan 3, and in Minchas Yaakov, part 2, page 84a. According to them, the prohibition would revive during Pesach. Therefore, wherever possible, we correct the matter initially according to all opinions by eating some beforehand and attributing the possible prohibited portion to what was eaten. Although Yoreh Deah states that one may attribute the prohibition to what was eaten only when it was consumed inadvertently or fell away by itself, here the act occurs before Pesach, while everything is still permitted, and there is no concern.
Because of the miracle that occurred on it. Tur writes that the lamb for the Pesach offering in Egypt was taken on the tenth, as Scripture states. The Exodus occurred on Thursday, as stated in Seder Olam. Thus the tenth of the month fell on Shabbos. Each person took a lamb for his Pesach offering in order to slaughter the Egyptians’ deity, and the Egyptians could not object. Because of that miracle it is called Shabbos HaGadol. This is indeed stated in the baraisa in Shabbos 87 and in Tosafos there. However, Tur’s attribution to Seder Olam requires study. Seder Olam explicitly states that Israel left Egypt on Friday. The Gemara in Shabbos 88 likewise cites Seder Olam to that effect. This follows the Sages, who hold that the Torah was given on the sixth of Sivan, on Shabbos. Rabbi Yose, who holds that it was given on the seventh of Sivan, maintains that they left Egypt on Thursday. Since our ruling is that Shavuos is always on the sixth of Sivan, and our practice is to say “the day of the giving of our Torah” in accordance with the Sages, it is difficult that Tur and the other poskim give a reason for Shabbos HaGadol that follows Rabbi Yose rather than the halachah. There is another difficulty. If the Exodus occurred on Thursday, then Friday, the second day of Pesach, corresponds to the first day of Shavuos according to the calendar mnemonic. Counting fifty days in our manner would place the giving of the Torah on the fifty first day, although all agree that it was given on Shabbos. Asarah Maamaros, cited by Magen Avraham below in siman 494, noticed this difficulty and answered it with difficulty. I also wrote an answer there in seif katan 1. According to the tanna of Seder Olam, however, the Exodus occurred on Friday, Shabbos was the second day of Pesach, and the Torah was given on Shabbos, which was the fiftieth day of the count. The reckoning therefore aligns with halachah and custom. See the later authorities for many other reasons why it is called Shabbos HaGadol. Taz writes that many ask why the greatness was not attributed to the tenth of the month even when it falls on a weekday. He heard from Rabbi Moshe Charif that another miracle occurred on the tenth, namely the splitting of the Jordan. People might therefore have thought that the distinction of the date came from the Jordan miracle. It was therefore called Shabbos HaGadol, because the day on which they emerged from the Jordan was not Shabbos. Taz presented this to his father-in-law, who praised it.
