And cabbage, etc. This is called strunk according to Orchot Chaim, while Bach identifies it in German as kohlkraut. Regarding all these vegetables, even if they are also good raw, we must say that most people do not normally eat them raw, as written in siman 202 seif 12. This refutes Magen Avraham's comments in seif katan 3 and seif katan 9. Nevertheless, raw parsley and maror receive Borei Pri HaAdamah, because cooking them by themselves diminishes them. See below, siman 473.
And leeks, etc. Maadanei Melech writes that Aruch explains kartei as pori in a foreign language, and he heard that they are a type of onion shaped like a half-circle. Targum on Beha'alotcha, however, translates chatzir as kartei, and Orchot Chaim writes that it is called aschlich in German.
One recites Borei Pri HaAdamah, etc. Bach rules Shehakol, but the later authorities write that his view does not appear correct. Taz distinguishes that when garlic and onions are still tender one recites Borei Pri HaAdamah, but when they become old one recites Shehakol, since then they are not fit to eat raw without bread. Olat Tamid writes concerning legumes called peas: whether eaten raw or slightly roasted in smoke, as is common in Bohemia, or cooked and eaten in pea soup even if somewhat dissolved, or cooked and then drained of all hot water and seasoned with salt and pepper and eaten whole as a dessert, or dried and soaked in cold water until their skins loosen and then roasted, while still moist, on a finely perforated pan over coals without any liquid, in all these cases one recites Borei Pri HaAdamah. Orchot Chaim similarly describes drained cooked peas and peas roasted without water. Olat Tamid adds that if they are thoroughly mashed, such as mashed peas, one recites Shehakol; see below, siman 208 seif katan 11. He further writes that one who eats dried legumes raw recites Shehakol because they are not fully good in that state. Olat Tamid says this ruling requires study. In my humble opinion, it is explicit in Shibbolei HaLeket, siman 44. See Magen Avraham at the beginning of this siman.
Even if he cooked them, etc. Olat Tamid discusses at length that this appears to contradict siman 202 seif 13, where a nut fried in honey receives Borei Pri HaEtz even though the honey improves it. In my humble opinion, the distinction is that there the honey is subordinate to the nut, whereas here the vegetables are subordinate to meat or other ingredients, and therefore the blessing is Shehakol. So appears from Magen Avraham and Maadanei Melech, page 47. Taz also discusses this at length and derives several new laws, but one should not rule accordingly. The simple rule is that vegetables fried in fat or honey receive Borei Pri HaAdamah, because they remain primary.
[Levush] And it appears to me that even, etc. He overlooked Beit Yosef in siman 202, who cites Rashba himself, whose ruling above is that one recites Shehakol. The reason here is that since these vegetables are ordinarily eaten after boiling, their cooking water has the same status as the vegetables. Above, however, the case concerns something not normally cooked or pressed but eaten as is, and we do not say that its liquid has the same status as the fruit. So rule all the later authorities. Responsa Rosh, klal 4 siman 15, writes that regarding vegetable broth, since the principal cooking is for the vegetables, once they impart flavor to the water the blessing follows the flavor. Even if he cooked the vegetables specifically for their water as medicine, since people generally cook them for eating, the blessing on the water does not change on account of his particular purpose. This implies that herbs which are never cooked for eating but only for the beverage produced from them receive Shehakol, as do mulberry juice and apple wine that are intended for drinking.
