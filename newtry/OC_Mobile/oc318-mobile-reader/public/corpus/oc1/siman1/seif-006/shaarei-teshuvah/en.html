See Shev Yaakov, siman 1, who writes that for rising at midnight one should reckon twelve equal hours from midday, when the sun stands at the center of the sky for that location. Twelve hours later, half of a full day, is midnight, when the north wind blows. This is also the view of Shalmei Tzibbur. The same calculation applies to the hour of a seasonal transition. A conscientious person may be stringent and also take account of the hours reckoned from the actual beginning of the day or night. The times of Krias Shema and prayer, however, depend upon the actual day and night. One who wishes to pray at the times when the watches change, as the Tur writes, should divide the period from tzeis hakochavim until alos hashachar into three watches, both in summer and winter. Eshel Avraham cites part of this. See Responsa Devar Shmuel, simanim 213 and 221, and what I wrote in Yad Ephraim at the beginning of siman 58. Even HaShoham, siman 23, writes that people who vowed to rise at midnight to pray and plead for mercy intended halachic midnight. He maintains that it should be calculated six hours after the beginning of the night, whether the night is long or short. It has already been explained, however, that this is not the view of Shev Yaakov and Shalmei Tzibbur, and their view appears primary. Shevus Yaakov, volume 3, siman 44, writes that Tikkun Chatzos should not be performed publicly, but privately, as the verse states, “Walk modestly.” Therefore, when a few exceptional individuals perform it in a synagogue with a minyan while the entire congregation does not, there is a concern of ostentation. Nowadays, however, the general custom is to do so, and there is no concern of ostentation. Birkei Yosef writes similarly upon the words of Shev Yaakov: “Our ears have heard and our eyes have seen that in many towns the custom has spread contrary to what he wrote.” Birkei Yosef further writes that where a Tikkun Chatzos group has a fixed room near the synagogue and the communal leaders wish them to recite it in the synagogue so that they will also constitute the first ten, it is proper to do so. If, however, some young men among them occasionally engage in idle conversation and will not restrain themselves from speaking even in the synagogue, it is preferable that they maintain their fixed gathering in the separate room. Iggeres HaRamaz, meaning Rabbi Moshe Zacuto, states that Tikkun Chatzos should not be recited at the beginning of the night, even in summer, nor at the beginning of the second watch, but only from midnight onward. One should sleep at the beginning of the night so that he can rise at least half an hour before daylight. In pressing circumstances, even if alos hashachar has already arrived, there is still some basis for reciting the Tikkun. In siman 15 there he writes that in places where extensive selichos are recited during the pre-dawn watch on Erev Rosh Hashanah and Erev Yom Kippur, together with nefilas apayim, one who regularly recites Tikkun Chatzos throughout the year should recite it on Erev Rosh Hashanah as well. See siman 16 there: one who regularly recites Tikkun Chatzos but rises during the pre-dawn watch when the congregation has already begun selichos, and lacks time for both, should recite Tikkun Chatzos in accordance with the order of the Arizal, for it is a greater principle than selichos. Birkei Yosef cites Rabbi Avraham Azulai, author of Chesed LeAvraham, that after completing Tikkun Chatzos one should say: “I believe with perfect faith in the consolation of Zion. Our God, in His mercy, will comfort us and renew His kingdom over us speedily.” See there.
