There: 'Unless he became impure, and if so it is not called voluntary.' In my humble opinion this is difficult. Whether a sin offering or a guilt offering is involved, he has not presently vowed to become a nazir and has incurred no offering. We therefore allow the prayer only where one can voluntarily bring that very offering, such as a burnt offering or peace offering; there he can properly say, 'May it be Your will,' because he could volunteer to bring it. This differs from an offering that cannot itself be brought voluntarily except through a vow of naziriteship, when the person knows that he did not vow to become a nazir. What difference is there between not knowing whether he sinned and knowing that he did not vow naziriteship? It therefore appears that the intent is certainly not that his 'May it be Your will' refer to a nazir's guilt offering. Rather, the view is that since some other sin can be atoned through a guilt offering occasioned by his voluntary resolve, he may properly say, 'May it be Your will, etc., as though I had brought a guilt offering.' A nazir's guilt offering, for example, incidentally atones for another sin in his possession. The offering's primary cause nevertheless arose from his voluntary resolve: he vowed to become a nazir and afterward became impure through circumstances beyond his control. Another sin is thereby atoned incidentally. He may therefore say, 'May it be Your will, etc., as though I had brought a guilt offering' occasioned by his voluntary resolve, through which an incidental sin is atoned. This cannot be said of a sin offering. Other sin offerings cannot be caused by his voluntary resolve and desire, but only if Hashem brings a sin to his hand. A nazir's sin offering, since it does not come for atonement, atones for no sin at all. This is the intent of the renowned gaon R' Yitzchak of Pozna, of blessed memory. Regarding Magen Avraham's objection that a sin offering requires prior awareness, my revered grandfather writes in Tevuot Shor that a guilt offering likewise requires prior awareness, and the later authorities discuss this at length. In my humble opinion there is no difficulty. Regarding the possibility that he has no obligation at all, it is understandable that he should not say the prayer, since the offering cannot be donated. When he recites it conditionally, however, his lack of awareness is no impediment. This recitation does not require every condition and detail stated regarding a sin offering, because it is not an actual sacrifice. He merely recites the subject of the offering and prays that it be accepted to atone for any sin he committed. What difference does it make that the sin is unknown? The sin would indeed not be atoned through an actual offering; the Torah's wisdom decreed that a sin offering may not be brought without awareness. But this person is not attempting to offer a sacrifice without awareness. He recites the passage and requests mercy: if he sinned but does not know it, so that the burden of sin nevertheless hangs upon his neck, may this recitation be accepted as though the sin had become known to him and he had offered the sacrifice according to its law, in which case it would properly atone. May he now likewise be atoned through his good intention; that had he initially known and been permitted to bring an offering, he would have done so. As stated above in Rabbeinu Bachya's name, this thought is counted as an act: it is as though his sin had become known to him and he had become obligated to bring an offering. This appears correct in my humble opinion.
