(ibid.) “If not when he became impure” — and if so it is not called nedavah. In my humble opinion there is room to wonder: both regarding a chatat and regarding an asham, nevertheless now at least he did not vow nazir and is not obligated to bring a sacrifice; and therefore we only say this where one can voluntarily offer that sacrifice itself — such as an olah and shelamim, he may properly say “May it be His will that it be a nedavah to offer.” Unlike from this case, where the sacrifice itself does not come as nedavah unless when one vowed nazirus — and he knows himself that he did not vow nazirus. And what difference does it make if he does not know whether he sinned or did not vow nazirus? Therefore it seems that certainly the intention is not that he should intend in “May it be His will” for a nazir asham. Rather he holds that since it is possible he may be atoned for some sin through an asham sacrifice that his nedavat lev caused him, it is proper to say “May it be His will,” etc., as if I offered an asham — such as a nazir asham, for through its offering he is atoned for a concealed sin he has in hand; nevertheless the essence of the sacrifice comes from nedavat lev that he vowed nazir and later became impure through constraint. And similarly in other cases where automatically he is atoned for some other sin through it. Therefore it is proper to say “May it be His will,” etc., as if I offered an asham through nedavat lev, and thereby his sin that he has concealed is atoned. Unlike a chatat — one cannot say thus, for other chatat offerings cannot cause them through nedavat lev and will, except if Hashem placed some sin in his hand. And a nazir chatat, since it does not come for atonement, no sin is atoned through it at all. And this is the intention of the famous Gaon Moharar Yitzchak of Pozna, z”l: And in what Magen Avraham challenged — for a chatat requires prior knowledge, and the Arizal in Tanya Shlomo wrote that an asham also requires prior knowledge, and the Acharonim elaborated on this. In my humble opinion there is no difficulty: regarding “perhaps he is not obligated at all” — there is no reason he should not say it, since it does not come as nedavah. But as a condition it does not prevent that he lacks prior knowledge, for this recitation is not the actual offering requiring all the conditions and details stated regarding a chatat — for this is not actual sacrifice, only that he says the matter of the sacrifice and prays it be accepted favorably to atone for him for what he sinned. And if so, what of it that it was not known? — nevertheless the sin will not be atoned for him, except that thus decreed the wisdom of Torah not to offer a chatat without prior knowledge. And he is not coming to offer sacrifice without knowledge — only he says the parashah and seeks mercy that if he sinned but it was not known to him, nevertheless the collar of guilt hangs on his neck — this statement should avail him as if it were known to him to offer the sacrifice according to law, when then he would be atoned according to law; so too he should be atoned now through his good thought that he thought if he had known from the start and been permitted to bring sacrifice he would have brought it. And as stated in the name of Bachye — this thought should be considered as deed, namely as if his sin-offering obligation were announced to him and he were obligated to bring sacrifice — so in my humble opinion.
