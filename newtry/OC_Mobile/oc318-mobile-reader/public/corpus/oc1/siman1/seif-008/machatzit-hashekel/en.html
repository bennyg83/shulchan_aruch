Se'if katan 8: 'A burnt offering, etc. (I was asked, etc.) as an advocate.' An advocate means one who intercedes. The Gemara compares a sin offering to an advocate and a burnt offering to a gift. If a person sins against a mortal king, it is improper to bring him a gift before the king has forgiven his sin, because the king will not accept him. He first needs an advocate to appease the king, and afterward he brings the gift. Likewise, as long as one has not brought the sin offering and the sin remains with him, he cannot bring the gift (the burnt offering) until after he has brought the sin offering and achieved atonement. The Gemara on the cited page states that a sin offering atones incidentally for failure to fulfill a positive commandment. The sin offering's primary obligation arises when one unintentionally violates a negative commandment whose intentional violation carries kares. If one brings such a sin offering and already owes a burnt offering because he violated a positive commandment or a negative commandment remedied by a positive commandment, for which a burnt offering is brought (and likewise for sinful thought and haughtiness) that sin offering also atones for the positive-commandment violation, but only incidentally. Rashi explains that this is not complete atonement, comparing the term to the verse 'the iron floated,' translated 'the iron rose to the surface.' A burnt offering, by contrast, atones for a positive-commandment violation directly and completely. Tosafos therefore ask: How can the burnt offering be called a gift and for that reason come after the sin offering? It atones for a positive-commandment violation more fully than the sin offering does.
Magen Avraham writes the answer: 'Although when it is brought, etc.' This means that relative to a sin offering, a burnt offering is nevertheless called a gift. Before the sin offering was brought, the person bore both a negative-commandment violation whose intentional transgression carries kares and a positive-commandment violation. Even if he brought a burnt offering, the debt from the negative commandment carrying kares would remain. It is therefore preferable to bring the sin offering first, achieving complete atonement for the negative commandment and at least incidental atonement for the positive commandment. Although part of the positive-commandment sin remains because it was not fully atoned, the burnt offering is now called a gift. Regarding our recitation, however, it is like one reading Torah. Reciting the sin-offering passage is of uncertain effect and may atone neither for a negative-commandment violation (for perhaps he incurred none) nor, consequently, for a positive-commandment violation, because it is mere Torah reading, and one may not bring a sin offering for a positive-commandment violation alone. A burnt offering atones for failure to fulfill a positive commandment, as stated on that same page of the Gemara. That is, perhaps he likewise owes no burnt offering, although a burnt offering can be donated. Nevertheless, since it is uncertain whether either recitation atones, one might think the sin offering should come first, because precedence chiefly depends upon which achieves greater atonement. Magen Avraham therefore had to say that there is no person who does not owe a burnt offering. The burnt-offering passage therefore certainly atones, whereas it is uncertain whether the sin-offering passage atones, as explained. The burnt-offering passage should consequently precede the sin-offering passage.
