In Magen Avraham, se'if katan 4: 'The night into twelve hours.' This means that after the night's twelve hours have passed, the time is counted as day in winter; in summer the reverse applies. This concerns midnight: after six hours from the beginning of the night have passed, it is midnight. My revered grandfather in Tevuot Shor does not hold this view; see there and what I wrote in siman 47. See Bechor Shor on Berachos, who writes in the name of Likkutei HaArizal that both in summer and winter midnight is the midpoint of the darkness of night. He agreed with this, unlike Shaarei Tziyon. Shalmei Tzibbur, however, cites the holy master and scholar R' Shalom Mizrachi Sharabi, of blessed memory: In Gan Eden day and night are equal. The relevant time is after six of the night's twelve hours. These are measured from midday, when the sun is directly overhead, for that is certainly midday everywhere, in Gan Eden and in every climate. From when the evening shadows begin to incline until twelve hours are complete is certainly actual midnight. In winter the night borrows hours from the beginning and end of the day, approximately two thirds of the added time from the beginning of the day and one third from its end, as stated in Zohar, Vayakhel, etc. The afternoon tamid was likewise offered from when the evening shadows began to incline, in both winter and summer. Although in summer more than six hours elapse from that point until sunset, the excess beyond six hours belongs to the beginning of the coming night; in winter the reverse is true. Thus, after six of the aforementioned twelve hours of night it is midnight. One must immediately strengthen himself like a lion and rise from sleep, taking great care that actual midnight not pass while he sleeps, because he then tastes the taste of death, etc. If, however, after awakening at midnight he recites the blessings and Tikkun Chatzot and then sleeps, there is no concern. The impure spirit no longer rules over him and he does not taste death after awakening at midnight to draw the mochin to Zeir Anpin, etc. See there, where he elaborates with pleasant words. The gaon, author of Shav Yaakov, writes likewise. See Shaarei Teshuvah concerning this, citing other responsa.
