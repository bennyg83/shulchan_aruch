“The Binding.” Bachye, Parashas Tzav, writes that mere recitation is not enough; one must explain the passage and recognize God’s wonders. So too he explains why one who recites Tehillah leDavid three times is assured of a share in the World to Come.
An olah. After the passages of the olah, shelamim, and todah, one should recite the passage of the libations found in Parashas Shelach, for no such offering is brought without libations. A chatas and an asham, however, do not require libations. The passage beginning Tzav Es Aharon does not relate to an ordinary olah, but to the daily offering, as stated in seif 9. One should also recite at the end of Vayikra the passage beginning “If a person hears the voice of an oath,” which concerns an oath of expression, an oath concerning testimony, a definite asham, and an asham talui. I was asked why the Tur and Beit Yosef in Shulchan Aruch instruct one to recite the olah before the chatas, when we rule in the tenth chapter of Zevachim that a chatas precedes an olah. Rashi writes likewise in Parashas Tazria; see Re'em there. It appears to me that Zevachim 7b explains why a chatas precedes an olah by comparing them to an advocate and a gift brought before a king: first the advocate appeases the king, and afterward the gift enters. Tosafos explain that an olah is the gift, even though it atones more fully for failure to fulfill a positive command than a chatas does. A chatas provides incidental atonement for failures concerning positive commandments, while an olah provides direct atonement. Nevertheless, once the chatas has first atoned for the kares-bearing transgression and incidentally for positive commandments, the olah that follows becomes a gift. In our recitation, however, the Tur writes that one should not say Yehi Ratzon after the passage of the chatas, lest he not actually be liable for a chatas, in which case he is merely reading Torah. An olah, by contrast, atones for failure to fulfill a positive command, and the Gemara states that no person is free of such liability. He should therefore recite the olah first, for it provides atonement. If he knows that he is liable for a chatas, he should recite the chatas first.
