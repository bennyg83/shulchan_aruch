[6] The passage of the Akeidah, etc. It is recited to invoke before Hashem the merit of the Patriarchs and to remember that one is obligated to surrender his life to Hashem. The passage of the manna is recited so that one will believe that all of his sustenance comes through Hashem's providence. Sefer HaKavanos writes that it should be recited before the passages of the offerings and that it should be recited even on Shabbos. Shelah, page 202, writes that on Shabbos and Yom Tov, among the passages of the offerings one should recite only the daily offering and all that accompanies it, the additional offerings, and the incense, because these are offered on Shabbos and Yom Tov. The other passages of the offerings should not be recited at all, even with the intention merely to read Torah. In Eliyahu Zuta I cited Lechem Chamudos, page 84, that one who has the ability to understand and learn should preferably learn Torah and suffice with the established prayers; instead of supplications and passages of offerings, he should study. Kitzur Shelah cited my words and objected that the Gemara distinguishes between the time for Torah and the time for prayer. This is not a valid objection, because the Gemara there refers to Shemoneh Esrei and Krias Shema. It is also difficult that he contradicts himself, for in the laws of washing the hands he ruled in accordance with my words. I also found this in Piskei Tosafos: one who is not burdened by business and is not a Torah scholar should not omit the daily ma'amados, words of supplication, and other verses printed in siddurim. One who is occupied with business should omit them, and a Torah scholar should preferably study in their place. Piskei Tosafos further writes that it is forbidden to recite ma'amados and supplications during Pesukei DeZimra or while the chazzan repeats Shemoneh Esrei. The precise authorities further write that one who does not recite ma'amados every day should at least recite the psalm for the day immediately after the passage of the daily offering in the morning. Even on Shabbos he should recite the psalm for Shabbos immediately after the verse concerning the Shabbos offering. Afterward he should recite, “You are He before Whom our fathers burned the incense,” the passage beginning “Hashem said to Moshe,” and the order of the incense as printed in the ma'amados. He then need not recite it again in Musaf, even on Shabbos or Yom Tov. I likewise saw in Taz, siman 132, that the incense should be recited before prayer. He reasons that the passage of the daily offering corresponds to its blood, while prayer corresponds to its limbs. Otherwise, why would both be needed? He therefore writes that the incense should be recited after the passage of the daily offering, so that it is read between the blood and the limbs. This reasoning is surprising, for when the daily offering was actually brought they afterward prayed, as stated in Sukkah 53a. Moreover, Rabbeinu Yonah at the beginning of the chapter Tefillas HaShachar explains that prayer principally corresponds to the blood. Nevertheless, the practical ruling is correct, not for the reason given, but because it is found in Responsa Menachem Azaryah, siman 14; see his explanation there. Seder HaYom writes that he saw a custom to introduce the daily psalm by saying explicitly, “The song that the Leviim recited in the Beit HaMikdash on the first day was,” and similarly on the other weekdays. Responsa Menachem Azaryah, siman 25, indicates that this wording should not be said; see there for the laws of these psalms. Levush HaOrah on Parashas Va'eschanan writes that the omission of Tachanun on Shabbos and Yom Tov refers to supplication that breaks a person's heart. A request that the Holy One, blessed is He, grant him abundant gifts is permitted. This is why requests were arranged in the Shabbos ma'amados.
[7] The Ten Commandments, etc. In Eliyahu Zuta I wrote that Maharshal was accustomed to recite them before Baruch She'amar, because the word “blessed” appears ten times from the beginning until “Blessed are You, God.” His grandfather, meaning Maharshal's grandfather, was accustomed to recite the first passage of the Ten Commandments in the morning and the second passage, in Parashas Va'eschanan, at night after reciting Krias Shema upon his bed. Gan Nata misunderstood my words concerning his grandfather; see there. The students of the Arizal write that he recited four remembrances every day: the giving of the Torah, the eradication of Amalek, the remembrance of Miriam becoming a metzora, as it is written, “Remember what Hashem your God did to Miriam,” and the remembrance of the Exodus from Egypt. Shelah, page 128, writes that the petition arranged after the seventy-two verses in our siddurim, which concludes with the blessing, “Blessed are You, Hashem, Who hears prayer,” is printed in error. The same applies to several other supplications and petitions printed elsewhere with such conclusions. This blessing is not mentioned in the Gemara or poskim, and one who recites it pronounces a blessing in vain. Rather, he should say, “Blessed are You, Who hears prayer,” without mentioning the Divine Name. See below, siman 46, seif 7, and Taz at the end of siman 131.
