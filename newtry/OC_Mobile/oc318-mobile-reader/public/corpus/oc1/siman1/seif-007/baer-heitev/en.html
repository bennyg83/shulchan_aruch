As a voluntary offering. But after chatat and asham, from the words of the author it appears that one should not say Yehi ratzon, because these do not come as voluntary offerings. Even after a outstanding asham he should not say it, as explained in Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De'ah, siman 5, where he ruled there to be stringent that it does not come as a voluntary offering; see Shach there, section 7. The Bach and Nachalas Tzvi wrote in the name of Maharshal that one may say Yehi ratzon even after chatat and asham, only that he should say it in conditional language, namely, "May it be Your will that if I transgressed a sin for which one is liable to bring a chatat, this be considered as if I offered a chatat," see there. Magen Avraham and Taz questioned him, see there. Magen Avraham raised that one should practice that after the passage of asham he say, "May it be Your will that if I became liable for an asham," etc., for an asham does not require prior knowledge, but after the passage of chatat he should say nothing, see there. Olas Tamid, and Eliyahu Zuta, and Sefer Bnei Chayyai, and Yad Aharon all agreed with Maharshal that he should say: if I became liable for a chatat, may this be considered as if I offered a chatat, and if not, behold I am like one who reads in the Torah, and it is fine, see there. And see Sefer Zevach HaShelamim, siman 1. And in the responsum of Emunas Shmuel, siman 53, he wrote in the Shelah that on Shabbat and Yom Tov one should not say Yehi ratzon, for a voluntary offering does not come then, but there is no loss in reciting the passages like one who reads in the Torah; nevertheless, if he is a Torah scholar it is better that he engage in the daily parashah. Solet Belulah wrote that other offerings he should not say even if his intention is like one who reads in the Torah, and Baer Heitev before me copied this; see there, and I do not know why.
