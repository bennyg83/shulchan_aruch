He should not leave. This means that he should not stop two verses before a section break in the Torah, lest people say that he did not read until the break because he intends to call another person who will read until the break. It appears to me that where a section break occurs in the middle of a verse, as at the beginning of Pinchas, one may conclude even with the verse adjacent to it. There is no concern that the next reader will read only until the break, for it is known that one does not conclude in the middle of a verse, even where a section break occurs. If he erred and concluded two verses before the break, the next reader should begin with an earlier verse. Agudah. Hagahos Ashri writes that where the subject has ended, such as the festival sections, and the fourth reader erred and left only two verses, the fifth should begin one verse earlier because it is impossible to continue further. Tosafos writes similarly. This implies that ordinarily he should read the two verses from this section and three from the following one. This is also explicit in the Gemara’s answer, “There it is different because another solution is possible.” One should not say that the only distinction is that the section contains only five verses and beginning earlier would leave two verses after the section break, for the first Tanna is not concerned with that. Necessarily, we do not repeat merely for this reason. The reason is that we are not concerned that those leaving will say that the second reader will read only two verses, because leaving during the reading is prohibited and is allowed only between readers. Tosafos’ statement that there is concern for those entering, who may think it is permissible to begin two verses before a section break, reflects only the questioner’s initial assumption. Even according to Ran, who is concerned with uprooting the Sages’ enactment, this applies only when establishing the reading initially, as explained in siman 423. When an error occurred, however, the Sages did not require repetition for this reason, as the Gemara concludes, because errors are uncommon. Therefore, if he erred and concluded two verses after the section break, the next reader likewise does not begin earlier. See what I wrote at the end of siman 137.
He should not begin. This means that he should not conclude for one reader two verses after a section break, because the next reader would then begin two verses after the break, and those entering would say that the first reader read only two verses. Otherwise, why did he not conclude upon reaching the break? The same applies if the end of the sidrah contains a section of five verses: the maftir should not begin two verses after the break, but should read all five. Rokeach, Hilchos Shabbos. This is also implied by Tosafos at the beginning of Megillah 22a regarding Vehikravtem. Although everyone knows that the maftir repeats earlier verses, in Talmudic times the maftir began where the previous reader ended, as Rosh writes, and it was then forbidden to begin two verses after a section break. The enactment was not annulled even now, and there is also concern that those entering may not know that he is the maftir. Darchei Moshe cites Or Zarua that one may begin Vayechal for the kohen, although it is two verses before a section break, and this is the practice. Agudah writes likewise. Mordechai explains that these sections are universally known, everyone knows that no one read before him, and there is no concern. This reasoning applies to Vehikravtem, which is two verses after a section break, and likewise to Vayechal. Tosafos, Megillah 22a, writes that these readings are universally known and there is no room for error. Likewise on Rosh Chodesh, when the third reader reads Uveyom HaShabbos, there is no concern that those entering will think that he read only its two verses, because the arrangement is universally known. They also should realize that, were this so, he should not have stopped when he reached the next section, since that too concerns the day. This requires study, for if so, why does the Gemara deliberate how to arrange the Rosh Chodesh reading? It could simply have instituted leaving two verses before a section break, and this arrangement would become universally known. It appears to me that permission rests on both reasons together. I further wonder at Terumas HaDeshen, who writes that a section of only two verses may be read independently, as ruled here, and cites Uveyom HaShabbos and Tosafos. Tosafos appears to permit only Uveyom HaShabbos because it is universally known. Perhaps Tosafos also means that whenever there is a section break in the Torah, everyone understands that it is appropriate to conclude there. If a reader stops somewhere that is not a section break, people may ask why he did not continue to the break and conclude that he began at the break and read only two verses. But where he concludes at a section break, there is no unusual feature. Tosafos’ further statement, “They also should realize,” means that if it were permissible to read only two verses, the second reader should not have stopped at Uveyom HaShabbos but should have continued through Uverashei Chodsheichem, and then the third and fourth readers could have read afterward. In my view, Tosafos means that there is no basis to assume he read only two verses when more remains to be read, since the entire section of Uverashei Chodsheichem, also relevant to the day, still lies ahead and only the fourth reader remains. Why would he have stopped there? Necessarily, he had already read three verses. In our Torah scrolls, however, there is no section break adjacent to Vayechal or Vehikravtem, as Shiltei HaGibborim, the punctuator in Hagahos Semag, and Responsa Maharil 160 write. This also resolves the old siddurim that conclude the kohen’s reading in Ve’Atah Tetzaveh one verse after the section break. There is no concern, for everyone knows that the kohen begins at the start of the sidrah. Recently, however, some altered the locations without sufficient precision. In Parashas Vayeilech too, they conclude with “in their ears,” although it is two verses before a section break, for everyone knows that no one reads after the third aliyah on a weekday. This is unlike newer siddurim that added two verses.
With a good matter. It appears to me that the good of non-Jews is not called good, as Tosafos writes in chapter 4 of Sanhedrin. The same applies to their misfortunes. See siman 591, seif 5, and Tosafos, Berachos 31a.
And he should conclude. Likewise, one should not conclude with a person who committed an evil deed. Rambam, end of Yadayim. A blind or lame person should not be called for a section concerning blindness or a broken limb, and similarly in all comparable cases, so that he not be embarrassed. Likewise, when the chazzan reads blessings, he should not intend to bless his friend. Sefer Chassidim, siman 770.
