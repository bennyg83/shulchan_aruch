Seif katan 1: He should not, etc. “Because he intends to call another person.” Meaning, he intends to call one more person to the Torah, and that person will read the two verses remaining until the section break.
And if he erred, etc. “He should begin with an earlier verse.” Agudah stated the ruling without giving a reason. Magen Avraham presently understands that although the first reader’s error of concluding two verses before the section break cannot be corrected with respect to those entering, there is nevertheless another concern, namely those leaving. After the second reader begins, some may leave and mistakenly think that he will read only the two verses until the section break. Magen Avraham rejects this, for the reason he gives immediately afterward, supported by Hagahos Ashri, Tosafos, and the Gemara.
Such as the sections of the festivals, which the Sages instituted to be read on that Yom Tov because the subject matter of that section pertains to the festival.
Because it is impossible to read more. Meaning, it is impossible to add for the fifth reader from the section following the festival section, because what follows has no relevance to the Yom Tov.
This implies that ordinarily, etc. Meaning, where it is possible to add beyond the two verses left in the section by continuing into the following section, as on Shabbos when the entire sidrah remains available, he should read those two verses and continue onward. We are not concerned for Agudah’s issue. Otherwise, why would the reason be needed that the subject has ended and it is impossible to read more? It should suffice to say that there is concern for those who leave during the reading. Necessarily, we are not concerned for this.
And so it is explicit in Megillah 22a. The Gemara discusses Rosh Chodesh, when four people must read. There are three sections: Vayedaber has eight verses, Uveyom HaShabbos has two, and Uverashei Chodsheichem has five. If the kohen and levi each read three verses from Vayedaber, two verses remain, and one may not leave fewer than three verses in a section. If they each read four, the third reader begins Uveyom HaShabbos, which has only two verses, and must add one or two verses from Uverashei Chodsheichem while leaving three for the fourth reader. But one may not begin a section with fewer than three verses. The Gemara therefore concludes that the kohen and levi together read five verses. The third reads the three remaining verses of Vayedaber and also Uveyom HaShabbos, and the fourth reads Uverashei Chodsheichem. How are the five verses divided between the kohen and levi, since each must read three? Rav says that the levi repeats: the kohen reads three verses, and the levi goes back and repeats the third verse, then adds two more. Shmuel says that they divide a verse: the kohen reads the first half of the third verse, for a total of two and a half verses, and the levi reads the latter half of the third verse plus two additional verses. The Gemara asks why Rav does not agree to dividing a verse, and answers that any verse not divided by Moshe may not be divided. Why does Shmuel not agree to repeating? Because of concern for those entering. If the levi repeats one verse, he begins two verses after the section break, and those entering may think that the kohen read only those two verses from the beginning of the section until the place where the levi began. Rav must hold that where no alternative exists we are not concerned for those entering, because one who enters will ask those present what the kohen read and they will tell him. The Gemara then cites a Baraisa: a section of only five verses is read entirely by one reader. If the first reader read only three, the second reads the remaining two and one verse from the next section; others say he reads the remaining two and three from the next section, because one may not begin a section with fewer than three verses. The Gemara asks: according to the view that one repeats, why not repeat instead of reading from the next section? It answers: here it is different because another solution is possible. This disproves Agudah’s concern. Even though another solution is possible, the second reader should have repeated because of those leaving during his reading, who may think he will read only two verses until the section break. One cannot answer that if he repeated there would remain only two verses after the section break, causing concern for those entering, because the first opinion is not concerned about that, as explained there. Necessarily, one does not repeat merely because of those leaving, for it is forbidden to leave during the reading, as stated below in siman 146.
For the first Tanna is not concerned about this. Although the alternative opinion in the Baraisa is understandable because it is concerned for those entering, the first Tanna is not concerned, since he allows the second reader to read the two remaining verses and one verse from the next section. He is not concerned that those entering will think that the second reader read only that single verse. Therefore, according to the first Tanna, the second reader should have gone back because of those leaving during the reading, even though leaving then is prohibited, as explained below in siman 146.
And what Tosafos wrote, s.v., etc. Meaning, perhaps Agudah’s reason is not concern for those leaving in the middle of the reading, but rather the concern stated by Tosafos, s.v. i isa, etc.
Regarding Rashi’s reading of the Gemara’s question, “According to the one who says repeat, let him repeat,” and not, “According to the one who says divide, let him divide.” Tosafos ask that the questioner should also have asked according to the view that divides: let him divide and repeat. Shmuel, who holds that one divides, would also agree that repetition is valid in principle, but rejects it only because of concern for those entering. Here, where the first reader read three verses and left two, the second could repeat half of the third verse. Tosafos explain that this is not done “lest those entering err, since this reader began two verses before the section break.” Magen Avraham explains that they may think the Sages’ enactment has been uprooted, because those entering, unaware that an error occurred, will conclude that one may initially begin two verses before a section break, meaning that the preceding reader may leave two verses before the section break. Tosafos answer that according to the view that divides, there is no difficulty, because where it is possible not to divide, one certainly should not divide. But according to the view that repeats, the question remains. This means that even the questioner understood that where another option exists one should not both divide and repeat, despite the concern that those entering may think the Sages’ enactment was uprooted. According to the view that repeats alone, however, the questioner thought repetition would still be proper because of that concern. This may also be Agudah’s reason for requiring the second reader to begin earlier, lest those entering think the enactment was uprooted.
That was only the questioner’s initial assumption. Although the questioner thought repetition was proper because of this concern, the answer, “Here it is different because another solution is possible,” necessarily holds that even because of concern for those entering one should not repeat. Agudah’s ruling is therefore rejected.
And even according to Ran, who is concerned, etc. Ran asks according to Rav, who permits repetition and is not concerned for those entering where no alternative exists: why not arrange that the kohen and levi each read four verses from Vayedaber, the third read the two verses of Uveyom HaShabbos plus one or two verses of Uverashei Chodsheichem, and the fourth read the remainder? One might object that beginning Uverashei Chodsheichem with only one or two verses would raise concern for those entering, but Rav is not concerned for them where no alternative exists. Ran answers that if this were done, not only those entering but even those present would mistakenly think that one may initially begin a section with fewer than three verses, thereby nullifying the Sages’ enactment, because they would not know that this unusual arrangement was adopted only from necessity. By contrast, when repetition occurs, everyone sees an unusual act, for many verses remain available, yet the reader goes back. They will ask why and be told that one may neither begin nor leave fewer than three verses in a section, and that repetition was therefore necessary. Thus even at the Gemara’s conclusion there is concern lest the Sages’ enactment be uprooted, but the Sages did not wish to establish a fixed arrangement that would cause this. See Magen Avraham in siman 423, where his citation of Ran is imprecise.
