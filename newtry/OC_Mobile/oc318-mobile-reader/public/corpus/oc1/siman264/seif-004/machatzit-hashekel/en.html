Paragraph 4: After the fact; and it appears to me that if there is, etc. Rema did not wish to rely upon the permission when a valid lamp is present if the valid lamp is not next to the invalid one but merely in the same or another room; he therefore permits only when necessary. If the valid lamp stands beside the invalid one, however, it is permitted even without necessity. Siman 276 states that if a non-Jew lights a lamp for a Jew, benefit is forbidden, but if the Jew already had a burning lamp, he may use the non-Jew's lamp as long as the Jewish lamp remains lit. This is not considered benefit from the non-Jew's lamp, since he already had his own light, even though the non-Jew's lamp adds illumination. The same applies here.
He follows his own reasoning, etc.; there is no permission through a shamash beside Chanukah lights, but according to us it is plainly permitted. At first glance, he means that Bach holds that even when a shamash stands beside the Chanukah light, using the light there is forbidden. Although he has another lamp (the shamash) his use near the Chanukah light is still called benefit from it. The same would apply here. According to us, who permit use near a Chanukah light when a shamash is present, it would also be permitted here. This is incorrect. Rema writes below in siman 673 that the shamash should be made longer than the other lamps, so that if one comes to use light, he will use that lamp. This implies that despite the shamash, Rema agrees that use by the Chanukah lights themselves is forbidden. Magen Avraham there, paragraph 4, likewise cites Milchamos Hashem: It is nevertheless forbidden to use their light, because an observer will say that all of them were lit for his needs, as sometimes happens. Furthermore, Chanukah lights do not resemble our case. There the concern is that an observer will say they were lit for personal use; on Shabbos this reason does not apply, because one may use the lamp's light, as Magen Avraham writes in a similar context above in siman 263, paragraph 22. Rather, Magen Avraham's somewhat awkward language refers to another matter. Bach below discusses Terumas HaDeshen's case, cited by Beis Yosef, where one Chanukah lamp became mixed with two shamash lamps. It is not nullified in the majority because it is an item counted individually, and Shulchan Aruch rules accordingly. Rema writes that if two lamps are lit, it is known that one is permitted, since only one Chanukah lamp was mixed in; one permitted lamp certainly burns, and their light may be used. Although even with a shamash present one may not use the Chanukah lights because observers may say they were lit for him, that applies while they burn at the mitzvah time for the mitzvah. In this mixture they do not burn for the mitzvah and truly were lit for his needs. Bach disagrees there and forbids even this case, against Rema. Magen Avraham refers to that ruling. Since Terumas HaDeshen's case involved shamash lamps, Magen Avraham also says "shamash," though his wording remains imprecise. Tosefes Shabbos challenges Magen Avraham from Tosafos, Shabbos 27a, s.v. "Rabbi Yishmael." The Gemara says one may not light with tar because its odor is foul. Tosafos asks why that reason is needed: Rabbi Yishmael holds that anything derived from wood may not be used because it does not draw after the wick, and tar derives from wood. According to Magen Avraham, what is their question? The foul-odor reason is needed to prohibit it even when a valid lamp burns beside it, whereas the reason that it does not draw after the wick would permit that situation. In my humble opinion, Magen Avraham permits only after the fact; initially he agrees that one may not rely upon the other lamp. His wording indicates this. Rabbi Yishmael discusses initial lighting, so Tosafos properly asks why the bad-odor reason is needed; failure to draw after the wick suffices to prohibit initial lighting even beside a valid lamp. Tosefes Shabbos argues that if Magen Avraham permits using it after the fact, all the more should initial lighting be permitted: using it involves the single decree lest one tilt, yet it is permitted, while initial lighting would involve a double decree; lest one use it and then tilt. In my humble opinion, this does not follow. Rema writes, "If he lit, etc., it is forbidden to use," from Responsa Rashba, implying that one might have thought initial lighting forbidden but after-the-fact use permitted. The proposed double decree can be answered: concern that he will use the lamp is not a mere decree, because it is nearly certain, like Tosafos on 23b, s.v. "a decree," concerning the decree lest one tilt. Yet according to this, how does Magen Avraham know Bach disagrees, since Bach's wording indicates an initial case? One can answer this, and it is straightforward.
