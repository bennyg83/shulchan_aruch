For the Shabbos lamp. On Yom Tov all wicks and oils are permitted except oil of terumah that became impure and must be burned; see the Gemara on 24a. At first glance, tar should also be prohibited on Yom Tov. We rule above in siman 263, se'if 5, that a blessing is recited over Yom Tov lighting, indicating that it too is obligatory; there is therefore concern that he may leave the room and depart. Tar is different, because its sole prohibition is concern that he may leave the room; one should not abandon a certainty because of a doubt. Pri Megadim.
It is forbidden to use. Responsa Rashba explains that since the entire prohibition against lighting is lest one tilt, that concern likewise applies now when using the light. Regarding melted tallow, which in fact draws after the wick and is prohibited only as a decree because of unmelted tallow, the after-the-fact law requires study.
One may be lenient after the fact. See Mishnah Berurah for Magen Avraham's view. Magen Avraham follows his own reasoning that the first opinion's leniency applies even when the valid lamp is in another room; he therefore permits whenever there is such a lamp, even without pressing circumstances. Vilna Gaon, however, explains that the leniency derives from Chanukah lights, whose light may be used when the shamash stands beside them. There the shamash is actually adjacent, yet Rema permits here only for a Shabbos need. This is because even the Chanukah ruling itself is uncertain according to Rashi and Ran, as Vilna Gaon writes, so for Shabbos we are stringent unless there is a Shabbos need. Alternatively, Rema agrees with Eliyah Rabbah's distinction: our case does not resemble siman 276 or the Chanukah lights of siman 673. Here there remains concern that he may tilt even though he can see by the valid lamp, because a person wants every lamp to burn well. There the law depends on benefit; since he can use another light, it is not considered benefit.
