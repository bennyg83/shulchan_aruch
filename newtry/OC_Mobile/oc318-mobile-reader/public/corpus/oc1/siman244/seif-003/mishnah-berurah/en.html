(19) In violation. Meaning, it was contracted work within the techum, forbidden only because of maris ayin, since the non-Jew actually works for himself. If they were day laborers, the work is forbidden by law after the fact, not merely improper. Magen Avraham and Bach imply this, and Chayei Adam rules that public work performed by day laborers is permanently forbidden to the Jew and to others for the time needed to perform it after Shabbos, as in siman 325:14. Some are lenient and prohibit it only for the time needed after Shabbos. See Mishnah Berurah there.
(20) It is proper to be stringent. The later authorities write that after the fact one may rely upon Rabbeinu Tam regarding contracted work and live in the house even oneself. Likewise, if stones were cut and beams prepared under contract, they may be set into the building after the fact.
(21) They should not enter it. Ever, even others. Nevertheless, one may derive other benefit from it or sell it to a non-Jew.
(22) Against his will. Meaning, the non-Jew violated the condition made with him beforehand. The Jew need not be concerned and, according to Mordechai, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and Biur HaGra, need not even protest while the work is being done. Magen Avraham strongly questions this because attached work is public, and observers do not know about the stipulation. Eliyah Rabbah likewise struggles with Mordechai's ruling. Therefore, one should not be lenient, but should protest and prevent the work. Eliyah Rabbah writes that where the condition was made initially and accepted, the Jew need not pay them to stop. This implies that if he failed to stipulate initially, protest alone is insufficient and he must spend money to stop them, because he was negligent. The practical ruling requires study.
