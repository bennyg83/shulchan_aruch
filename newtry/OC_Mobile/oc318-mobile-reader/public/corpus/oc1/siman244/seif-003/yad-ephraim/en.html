Magen Avraham, seif katan 18: When he hires the gentile under a contracted arrangement, it is permitted as a matter of law because the gentile acts for his own benefit, receiving a share of the customs revenue itself. This is comparable to giving hides to a tanner, which is permitted when a fixed price was set. Although here the Jew profits from the customs collected by the gentile on Shabbos, which resembles a bathhouse or mill as explained above in siman 243, seif katan 2, the Jew’s profit itself is not the concern there, just as there is no concern with a mill operated under sharecropping. The reason for prohibition there is that we do not say the gentile acts for himself, since even if he does not work on Shabbos he must still pay the fixed rent, as Magen Avraham writes and as I explained there at length. Where the gentile receives a fixed share of every dinar collected, however, it is comparable to a mill under sharecropping. The only concern is that here the Jew assigns the work specifically to Shabbos. The Sages nevertheless permitted this because of financial loss. Thus there is no prohibition from the Jew’s appointing the gentile to collect the customs, and the gentile does not appear to be his agent. Nor is there a prohibition in the customs revenue itself entering the Jew’s possession because of Shabbos wages. The Jew did not purchase only the customs rights of Shabbos, but the customs rights for the entire year, including Shabbos. He acquired the Shabbos income inclusively together with the weekday income, and when he receives it, it constitutes Shabbos wages included within a broader payment. If, however, he leases to the gentile only the profits of Shabbos, then even though there is no agency on behalf of the Jew, the income itself entering the Jew’s possession is separate Shabbos wages and is forbidden. The Sages did not permit that because of financial loss. Their leniency applies only where the concern is that the gentile appears to act as his agent because the Jew specifically assigns him to Shabbos, as explained in siman 243 regarding an oven and bathhouse. So it appears to me. I have seen some explain Magen Avraham’s statement that the Jew receives Shabbos wages inclusively to mean that the gentile is also obligated to collect during part of Friday, so the arrangement resembles leasing it to the gentile for a fixed payment while the gentile keeps the remainder, and the Jew’s Shabbos wages are included with weekday wages. In my humble opinion this is incorrect. Where the underlying enterprise belongs to the Jew and the gentile receives a fixed amount, it is not a lease but sharecropping. Rather, the explanation is as I wrote. Magen Avraham’s statement regarding an oven and bathhouse, that sometimes there is financial loss, and “nevertheless on that day,” appears possibly to contain a scribal error. It should read: “Nevertheless, on that day,” and his intent is as follows. With an oven or bathhouse, one may sometimes lose more than the amount lost from one day’s customs revenue. One could answer that a bathhouse or oven differs: even though, if it had been constructed specifically for Shabbos, the loss of the money invested in it would be considered a genuine loss, nevertheless, since it can be operated during the weekdays, the fact that it is not operated on Shabbos is treated merely as loss of profit. Customs rights are different because the Jew paid the king for a fixed number of months. If he does not collect on a particular day, he loses money from his own pocket. Magen Avraham responds that one cannot make this distinction, because even regarding customs there may be cases where failing to collect on Shabbos does not cause an out-of-pocket loss. We must therefore say that the loss is assessed day by day. But if so, the same should apply to an oven or bathhouse. Although it can be used on weekdays, since he spent money constructing it and its entire purpose is to be operated each day and earn rental income, failure to operate it on Shabbos should likewise be considered a loss. To this Magen Avraham writes, “Therefore it appears,” etc. If there is no scribal error in the phrase, “and one could say, nevertheless,” the words “therefore it appears” should perhaps be emended to “indeed it appears.”
