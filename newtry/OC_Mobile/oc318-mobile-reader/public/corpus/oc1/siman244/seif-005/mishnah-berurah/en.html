(24) That he write for him. Magen Avraham explains Rambam as referring to a nobleman who retains a designated scribe or tailor. Whenever the nobleman needs writing or sewing, the worker must do it, and when there is no need he sits idle. It is therefore permitted for the worker to write or weave on Shabbos, because he may work whenever he wishes, and the Jew did not tell him to work on Shabbos. If he wishes, he can do it tomorrow, and the Jew gains nothing from its being done on Shabbos. If, however, the Jew hired him to weave constantly throughout the year or to write constantly, even Rambam forbids work on Shabbos, although the Jew does not object if he misses a day, because the Jew benefits from the work. Taz implies that even such employment is considered contracting according to Rambam so long as the Jew does not object if he is idle for a day. If he insists that he not sit idle on any day, all agree that it is forbidden, as though he explicitly instructed him to work on Shabbos. Taz further writes that if he hired him to finish a book or garment by a certain deadline, it is considered insistence when the work cannot be completed without Shabbos labor. Even if he could force himself to work nights and finish during the week, it is still as though he was instructed to work on Shabbos. Peri Megadim explains Taz this way and agrees against Eliyah Rabbah.
(25) Or that he weave for him, this is, etc. This is the correct reading.
(26) He does not account with him. Meaning, he does not scrutinize him if he misses a day of work.
(27) In the Jew's house. There even fully contracted work is forbidden, as stated below in siman 252.
(28) And some forbid. This is Raavad's opinion. According to Taz, the reason is simple: even if the Jew does not object to an idle day, he benefits from the work. According to Magen Avraham as well, Raavad holds that the Jew benefits from work done on Shabbos because he may need another piece of writing tomorrow and the worker would be unable to perform both tasks.
(29) For a particular task. In such a case, the Jew may derive no benefit from the work on Shabbos, since he may not need so much writing or weaving. If the worker is hired for all tasks, however, it is nearly certain that the Jew will need some other task tomorrow, so he benefits from the Shabbos work. Therefore, it is forbidden even if he does not account with him day by day.
(30) For all tasks. For this reason, one must protest when household servants perform their employer's work on Shabbos, even outside the Jew's house and without his instruction, because they are hired for all tasks. All the more so is it forbidden in the Jew's house, where even one hired for a specific task may not work. Taz cites Rabbeinu Simchah as forbidding a maidservant even to perform her own work in her employer's house, because observers may say she works for the Jew. Eliyah Rabbah cites Sefer HaTerumah as permitting her own work and maintains that Rabbeinu Simchah also agrees. Peri Megadim reconciles Taz. Chayei Adam writes that all agree she may mend her own clothes in the employer's house when it is recognizable that she works for herself. See there further and what we wrote in seif katan 35.
