For members of his household. See Baer Heitev. Responsa Penei Yehoshua, manuscript, discusses a community whose members pledged to build a synagogue. Before beginning construction, they purchased land from which to take earth and clay for bricks and hired a non-Jew to make and fire the bricks, paying him a fixed amount per thousand bricks. He writes that one may not permit this merely because some people customarily contract such work out; it is permitted only where the majority customarily use contractors. He suggests that perhaps the prohibition of attached work, even outside the city, applies only after the Jew has begun construction on his own house. Even though stones are cut in the non-Jew's house, perhaps observers may see them, and so forth. Here, however, no work on the synagogue itself had yet begun. The conclusion of the responsum is missing, but its opening indicates that he rejected this leniency as well, for he writes, “I reviewed every possible basis and found no permission,” etc. See there. According to Magen Avraham's ruling that communal property is not subject to suspicion, there would have been grounds for leniency here as well. Magen Avraham writes that the great authorities did not wish to permit construction of a synagogue by contract “in these times,” but that reason does not apply where the work is performed outside the city. Eliyah Rabbah writes, however, that perhaps even communal activity is subject to suspicion regarding a shvus. This requires study. See Responsa Tiferes Tzvi, siman 10, concerning a community building a synagogue by means of a non-Jewish contractor who wished to build on Shabbos. If they did not allow him, the project might be cancelled, or they would at least lose more than two hundred reichsthaler, which they would have to pay him without receiving work, to compensate him for the wages of his laborers. He cites Tosafos in Shabbos in the name of Baal HaIttur, who permits where there is concern that the project will be cancelled, and Peri Megadim writes likewise. Although the question stated that cancellation was not certain, he writes that one must also consider that people may cease donating, which would itself cause the project to be abandoned. There was also an additional basis for permission: they had purchased the land from a non-Jew and it was not yet publicly identified as Jewish property. He therefore permitted them to sell the entire property and everything needed for construction to the non-Jew until the walls were completed. Once the walls and roof were finished, they would buy it back and perform no further work on Shabbos. The walls and roof do not visibly identify the structure as a synagogue. The ceiling and other features, however, are recognizable as synagogue construction and unlike an ordinary building. These could not be permitted through the device of a sale, since their purpose is evident to all. Therefore, after completion of the walls and roof, no work should be done on Shabbos. See there.
