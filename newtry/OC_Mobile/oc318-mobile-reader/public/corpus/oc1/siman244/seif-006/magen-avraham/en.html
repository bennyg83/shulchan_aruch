By contract. This is permitted by strict law, as stated in siman 252. Although one may not designate Shabbos as the time for the work, here it was permitted because of loss. If it were not permitted, the Jew might personally collect the customs and come to a Torah prohibition by writing, as explained in siman 266. For this reason, it is permitted even though the matter is public. This implies that where customs collection involves no writing, such as collecting foodstuffs, it would be forbidden. Nevertheless, it appears permitted because of the financial loss. Although some forbid a double Rabbinic prohibition in a case of loss, as stated in siman 307, seifim 5 and 19, here it is permitted because it resembles rescuing property from their hands, meaning preventing the money from becoming permanently lodged with the non-Jew, as Mordechai writes based on the first chapter of Avodah Zarah. A Jew who leased salt production may hire workers by contract, paying a fixed amount for a fixed quantity of salt. See Responsa Maharshal 100 and siman 245:3.
To lease the customs. Hagahos Ashri at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah writes: If a Jew and non-Jew jointly received the right to mint coins from the king, and they did not stipulate initially regarding Shabbos, a wise Jew may hire the non-Jew to perform his share on Shabbos, because the non-Jew works for himself. The rule is that when the non-Jew works under a fixed arrangement for himself, it is permitted, though the Jew may not benefit from the work on Shabbos; without such an arrangement it is forbidden because the non-Jew acts as his agent. The obvious meaning is as Mordechai writes here: the Jew says, “When you collect one hundred dinarim for me, I will pay you such-and-such.” This is why Hagahos Ashri concludes that under a fixed arrangement the non-Jew works for himself. Although the work is designated for Shabbos, it is permitted because of loss. “The Jew may not benefit from it on Shabbos” means that even where a price was fixed, he may not benefit from that work on Shabbos. Hagahos Ashri writes likewise at the end of the first chapter of Shabbos, cited in siman 252:4. Beit Yosef and Bach in siman 245 struggled unnecessarily with this. A question remains: when the non-Jew is hired by contract, his Shabbos wages are absorbed within the larger payment, whereas if the profits of Shabbos alone are leased to him, this appears to be unabsorbed Shabbos wages, which are forbidden even in a case of loss, as stated in siman 243 regarding an oven and bathhouse. Sometimes the loss there may exceed that of customs, since customs may involve only lost profit, not an out-of-pocket loss. One may answer that customs entails a loss on that particular day, since each day is assessed independently. More fundamentally, however, Shabbos wages do not apply here at all. The non-Jew pays no rent; he purchases the customs revenue from the Jew, as though buying merchandise. It is therefore permitted by strict law when the non-Jew takes all the profit. The only issue is maris ayin, because people may say that he works for the Jew, who hired him to write and collect customs on Shabbos. This is Rama's meaning when he says that in a case of loss such as this we are not concerned. Rama thus does not follow Beit Yosef's view in siman 245 that this constitutes Shabbos wages. It resembles one who knows non-Jews will bring merchandise on Shabbos and sells it before Shabbos to another non-Jew who will receive it then, which is plainly permitted. Regarding coinage as well, the material belongs to the king, and the Jew sells only the royal license granted to him.
Its law is like, etc. One may give silver to a non-Jew to mint coins, because there is no audible activity publicly identified as belonging to a Jew, and there is no concern since the non-Jew works for his wages. Hagahos Maimoniyos.
In his work. This is forbidden not only when the customs operation belongs to the Jew, but even when he leased it to the non-Jew for Shabbosos and has no involvement in it, lest people say that the non-Jew works for his benefit. It requires examination whether a Jew may personally accept food collected as customs, provided it did not come from outside the techum and involves no concern of trapping or attached produce. It appears forbidden because of “pursuing your affairs,” as stated in siman 306. See also the end of siman 307.
