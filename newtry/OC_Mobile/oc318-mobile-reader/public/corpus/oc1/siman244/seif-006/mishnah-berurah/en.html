(31) And he hires a non-Jew for him. The wording is imprecise. It means that he hires the non-Jew. Later authorities.
(32) If it is by contract. It is then permitted by strict law, like hides given to a tanner at the beginning of siman 252, where one may give them before sunset under a contract because the non-Jew works for himself. Although it is forbidden there to designate Shabbos for the work, and this is also a public matter known to belong to a Jew, resembling work performed in the Jew's house, it was permitted here because of loss. A person is anxious over his money, and if it is not permitted he may collect the customs himself and come to a Torah prohibition by writing a note, as tax collectors do. According to this reasoning, where customs collection involves no writing, such as collecting foodstuffs, it would be forbidden even through a non-Jew, because refusing permission would not lead to a Torah prohibition. Taz and Magen Avraham, however, conclude that even in such a case it is permitted through a non-Jew because of loss. The Jew himself may not receive even foodstuffs collected as customs, even if they were not brought from outside the techum and involve no concern of trapping or attached produce, because of “pursuing your affairs,” as stated in siman 306. Eliyah Rabbah. A Jew who leased salt production from the government may hire workers by contract, paying a fixed amount for a fixed quantity of salt, even though the tools and wood belong to the Jew. They must not bring wood from the Jew's domain on Shabbos, because contracted work is permitted only when everything needed was transferred before Shabbos. He may not hire a non-Jew for Shabbos or by the month, because the Jew benefits from his work. The only permission is contracting. Chayei Adam.
(33) I will give you such-and-such. Meaning, he does not mention Shabbos at all. He merely says, “If you collect such-and-such, I will pay you such-and-such.” He is therefore not a day laborer, and there is no issue of Shabbos wages, because the Jew already leased from the government the customs rights for the entire year, including Shabbosos. Peri Megadim.
(34) And the non-Jew will take, etc. This too is permitted by strict law because the non-Jew works for himself. Magen Avraham writes that there is no issue of Shabbos wages even if he leases it to him for Shabbos alone, because it is as though the non-Jew purchased the customs revenue. It resembles merchandise known to arrive on Shabbos that the Jew sells before Shabbos to a non-Jew who will receive it then, which is plainly permitted. Regarding coinage, the Jew merely sells him the royal license granted by the king, and the non-Jew takes all the profit. The only issue is maris ayin, because people may say that he works for the Jew, who hired him to write and collect customs on Shabbos. This is why Rama writes that in a case of loss we are not concerned, without mentioning Shabbos wages. See Biur Halachah.
(35) They were not concerned. All the poskim imply that even a major loss was permitted only in the forms described by the Shulchan Aruch, either contracting or leasing the actual profit to the non-Jew, where the non-Jew works for himself. An actual day laborer is forbidden in every case. Therefore, those who operate taverns and bring a non-Jew into the establishment before Shabbos to sell drinks on Shabbos act improperly. Even if lost Shabbos profit is considered a major loss, the poskim never permitted an actual day laborer, and perhaps this is merely lost profit, like an oven and bathhouse. At most, one may use the arrangements described by the Shulchan Aruch and Rama, or sell the non-Jew before Shabbos all the merchandise he will sell. Although the business remains associated with the Jew, this is permitted because of loss. According to Taz below, the Jew may sit at a distance and supervise so the non-Jew does not steal, but he must not participate in the business or speak with him about it. This is difficult to observe while sitting there. The common practice is lenient, and it is better that people remain inadvertent rather than deliberate transgressors. Tosefes Shabbos. Fortunate is one who trusts in Hashem and does not seek leniencies concerning Shabbos.
(36) Its law is like, etc. Therefore, one may give silver to a non-Jew before Shabbos under a contract, paying a fixed amount for a fixed quantity of coins, or the Jew may lease to the non-Jew the profit that will arise on Shabbos, as explained above, because of loss.
(37) The Jew should not sit there. This refers to the Jew who leased it to him or to his agent. Taz explains that it is forbidden to sit there in order to know how the non-Jew conducts the business and receive an account from him the next day, because this violates “pursuing your affairs,” as stated in siman 306:1. If he sits there only to ensure that the non-Jew does not steal from the customs operation, it is permitted. This explains the custom of those who lease mills from the government to station a Jew there even on Shabbos, provided he says nothing about the business. The other later authorities rule likewise.
