If it is under a contracted arrangement, etc. Mordechai writes in the name of Maharam: Even if one distinguishes ordinary contracting, where the Jew does not designate Shabbos specifically for the gentile’s work, from customs collection, where he assigns him specifically for Shabbos, it is nevertheless permitted because the Sages permitted certain matters to prevent financial loss. In the chapter Mi SheHechshich, they permitted conduct that would otherwise be forbidden lest a person carry four amos in a public domain. The same applies here, and all the more so where this saves the Jew from the authorities, for if it were not permitted he might lose the entire debt because of even a brief delay. Beit Yosef asks why Maharam did not permit it simply because customs collection involves no Torah prohibition. He answers that customs collectors ordinarily must write. He further answers that regarding instructing a gentile outside the context of a mitzvah, there is no distinction between a Torah and Rabbinic prohibition. Beit Yosef also asks that public work is forbidden even in the gentile’s house, as stated in seif 4, and answers that this case is different because of financial loss. He then asks that the Sages permitted the case in Mi SheHechshich only to prevent a greater Torah violation, namely carrying four amos in a public domain, whereas here customs collection involves only a Rabbinic prohibition. Why should it be permitted? He answers that customs collection too may involve a Torah prohibition because the Jew may write. According to his explanation, however, the leniency produces an undesirable stringency. It would follow that the leniency applies only where there is concern that the Jew may write. In places where no writing is done, especially in small communities where customs collectors do not keep written records, hiring the gentile would be forbidden because there is no risk of a Torah violation. The poskim should then have distinguished between such cases, yet they did not. I am also astonished at the holy words of the author of the Shulchan Aruch, for the chapter Mi SheHechshich shows that concern for financial loss can justify permitting a lesser Rabbinic prohibition in order to prevent a more severe Rabbinic prohibition as well. The Gemara had to find an arrangement the person would not otherwise be allowed to use, and states that the Sages were not concerned for a minor loss, implying that they were concerned for a major loss. Even in a courtyard there is no Torah prohibition. It is logical to arrange matters so that a person does not come to violate a more severe prohibition, even if both are Rabbinic, by allowing the lesser Rabbinic prohibition because of the loss. So too here: customs collection involves no Torah prohibition, but among Rabbinic prohibitions there is a severe one, namely that the Jew collect the customs himself, and a lesser one, namely that he lease it to a gentile. Since one of them will certainly occur, it is preferable to allow the lesser prohibition, for we can testify that otherwise he will commit the more severe one, even though both are Rabbinic. One may ask why siman 243 did not likewise permit leasing a bathhouse to a gentile because of financial loss, lest the Jew commit the more serious violation of employing the gentile as his worker. It appears that the Sages permitted this only where a major loss is involved, such as customs duties and minting, which generally involve substantial enterprises. They permitted the category generally even where a particular instance is small. A bathhouse and similar matters do not ordinarily involve such a large loss. Moreover, when the Jew initially established the bathhouse he knew that he could not operate it on Shabbos even through a gentile. There is therefore no serious concern that he will violate the more severe prohibition. This appears correct.
That the Jew should not sit beside the gentile. Meaning, the Jew who leased the enterprise to him, or the Jew’s agent. Mordechai derives this from the end of the chapter Bechol Me’arvin: a person may not walk through his field to determine what work it requires, and so forth. It appears to me that the prohibition against sitting there likewise applies only when he sits to observe the gentile’s activity so that he can receive an accounting the following day. If he sits merely to guard the gentile from stealing and to ensure that the customs receipts are deposited in the chest, it is permitted. A proof is the rule, “Guard for me the produce within your techum,” and so forth. Another proof appears in the chapter HaZahav, Bava Metzia 58a: one who hires a worker to guard the red heifer, or to guard seeds, does not pay him Shabbos wages. This shows that the guarding itself is permitted, but he may not receive separate payment for Shabbos because of the prohibition of Shabbos wages. The conclusion there states that if he is hired by the month, he may even receive payment for Shabbos. Thus sitting and guarding on Shabbos is permitted. The same applies here. This explains the custom of those who lease mills from the ruler and station a Jew there even on Shabbos, provided they are careful not to discuss the business at all.
