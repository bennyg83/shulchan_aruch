If. See Taz. His statement that public work is prohibited even in the non-Jew’s house is like the ruling in seif 4 concerning a ship. Magen Avraham, seif katan 13, writes that the prohibition applies specifically in a public place, not in the non-Jew’s private house; see seif 3 and what I explained there. Beit Yosef, however, writes that for this reason it is prohibited in “his house,” meaning the Jew’s house; see there. Taz further writes that even regarding a Rabbinic prohibition the Sages enacted safeguards lest one come to a more severe prohibition. In Shabbos 154b, regarding glassware, they did not enact a safeguard for a minor loss. See Magen Avraham, siman 266, seif katan 14. The Mechaber omitted the case of a sack of glass according to his own approach, but Ran writes that the permission applies even in a Rabbinic prohibition; see there. See also siman 334, Magen Avraham, seif katan 3. The Mechaber in siman 266, seif 1, rules without qualification, implying that it applies even nowadays; and in siman 334 he likewise presents the first view without qualification, permitting even a Rabbinic prohibition. This is difficult from siman 266, seif 9: why did he omit the case of a sack of glass? With Hashem’s help it will be explained there. Magen Avraham, seif katan 17, also agrees that where writing is not customary it is permitted. Levush implies that even where writing is not customary it is prohibited, and he also writes that dropping the burden is Rabbinic. Nevertheless, one could say that in a Rabbinic matter and the like, the established custom is lenient. Regarding the distinction between a major and minor loss, see Levush, who appears to agree. With Hashem’s help this will be explained further in Magen Avraham.
