He works for himself. Meaning, the Jew does not care whether it is done today, because if it is not done today it can be done tomorrow. See the end of siman 243.
Not everyone recognizes it. Although some people know that it is the Jew's work, it is permitted because it is done privately. This is obvious and also implied by seif 4. The gloss, however, discusses attached work within the techum, where we are concerned even about guests and members of his household, as explained above. This is obvious, unlike Olas Shabbos.
And says that so-and-so, etc. Meaning, he assumes that the Jew hired him by the day, which is forbidden by law.
His courtyard or his wall. Since the Shulchan Aruch did not simply write “to build him a house,” it implies that the prohibition applies specifically where it is known to belong to him. If it stands in a place where ownership is not known, it would be permitted, as stated at the end of siman 243. Nevertheless, it appears to me forbidden because neighbors who know it is his may suspect him, as stated in the gloss.
To harvest his field for him. Although fields are ordinarily worked by sharecroppers, so observers might assume that the non-Jew took it as a sharecropper, as stated in siman 243, that case is different. Upon investigation, it becomes known that he truly received it as a sharecropper. Here, when people see at harvest that the non-Jew takes no share of the profit, they will know that he was not a sharecropper and will suspect that he was a day laborer.
Guests. In chapter 3 of Sheviis, mishnah 1, Tosafos Yom Tov cites the Yerushalmi that we do not take passersby into account for maris ayin, but only the townspeople. We must say that they were lenient regarding Shemittah. See the end of siman 671.
Members of his household may suspect him. This is unlike the end of siman 243. There, they know that the non-Jew receives the profits of the bathhouse. Here, they may still suspect that the Jew hired him by the day and merely claimed that it was contracted work, as Ran writes regarding a field.
Outside the techum. There is no concern that Jewish guests will happen to arrive there, because Jews ordinarily spend Shabbos among Jews. In our city, the custom is to permit hiring a non-Jew by contract to remove refuse from the street, even though he works on Shabbos and this is Torah-level labor, since removing a mound and leveling the street constitutes building. Presumably, a great authority permitted it because there is no suspicion regarding communal property, as stated in Yoreh Deah 141:4. A day laborer is certainly forbidden. According to this, building a synagogue on Shabbos by contract might also be permitted. Nevertheless, I saw that the great authorities refused to permit it. Nowadays, the surrounding nations do not allow public labor on their festival days, and permitting it on our Shabbos would cause a desecration of Hashem's name. Street repair is not so strongly associated with the Jewish community. In a place where the custom is not to permit street work, however, one may not be lenient.
