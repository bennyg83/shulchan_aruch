Privately. See Mishnah Berurah and Biur HaGra, who cites the Yerushalmi above, which permits detached work specifically in the non-Jew's house. At first glance, this implies that he is stringent when it is done publicly, even though it is detached work and it is not known to belong to a Jew. The wording of the Shulchan Aruch, however, which says that not everyone recognizes it, but if it was known, indicates that the primary factor is whether the work is publicly identified as belonging to the Jew. The Gra himself writes likewise in his explanation of the gloss in seif 2. The Yerushalmi's permission when it is done in the non-Jew's house is because in such a case it is ordinarily not recognizable as the Jew's work. Even public work is permitted when ordinary observers cannot recognize it as belonging to a Jew. The same applies even if it is done in the public domain, as Beit Yosef writes there in the name of Rabbeinu Yerucham. It is also possible that the Yerushalmi mentions the non-Jew's house merely to exclude the Jew's house. It appears that this too is the Gra's intent, except that the word “etc.” is missing after “privately.” He refers to the Shulchan Aruch's statement that not everyone recognizes it, and cites the Yerushalmi's requirement that it be done in the non-Jew's house because there it is not recognizable as the Jew's work. The same is shown by the permission for attached work in another city. All these cases depend upon the same principle.
Or to harvest his field for him. See Mishnah Berurah, citing Taz, that even where the townspeople customarily hire workers as contractors, it is still forbidden, because observers may suspect them of being day laborers, which is also common. Peri Megadim understands that even if most of the town hires by contract, it remains forbidden so long as day labor is also common. In my humble opinion, this may be challenged from the Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah. Rabbeinu Tam permitted a Jew to hire a craftsman to build a house by contract. The Rosh objected that the statement that a sharecropper works for himself in a field is no proof regarding building contracts, because fields are ordinarily worked only through sharecropping or tenancy, whereas most houses are built by day laborers, so an observer assumes they are day laborers. This implies that if most of the town builds by contract, there is no need to be stringent even regarding houses. The Tosafos quoted by Taz are the words of Ri against Rabbeinu Tam, and the Rosh's statement likewise follows Ri. Old Tosafos to Shabbos 18a, however, supports Taz, holding that even where contracting is common it is forbidden if day labor is also common. Peri Megadim writes that according to Ran, who forbids even contracted field work because it appears like day labor, it is forbidden even when observers know that he is a contractor, since unless the non-Jew shares in the profit, people may come to permit actual day labor. According to that approach, even if the matter is publicized and the local custom is exclusively contracting, it remains forbidden. This is stricter than the Tosafos approach. According to the Shulchan Aruch's reason, however, that an observer does not know a price was fixed and assumes the Jew hired the non-Jew to work on Shabbos, it would appear permitted where it is publicized that this person hired by contract, or where the local custom is to hire workers by contract. Peri Megadim inclines toward leniency in that case. In my view, this requires much study, because Ran is not alone. Maharam in Tosafos to Avodah Zarah 21b holds that the distinction between sharecropping and contracting is not merely that houses are usually built by day labor, but that a sharecropper owns a share in the actual produce and resembles a partner, whereas a contractor receives money and therefore appears to be the Jew's hired worker. According to this, it is forbidden even where contracting is public knowledge. The same implication appears in Baal HaMaor, Hagahos Semak, Kol Bo, Hagahos Ashri, and Sefer HaTerumah. All make the essential distinction depend upon whether the non-Jew has a share in the object itself. Therefore, even if the town works only by contract, it remains forbidden according to Ran. This also explains why the Gemara in Moed Katan rules without qualification that contractors working within the techum are forbidden. On the other hand, Rambam's wording, adopted by the Shulchan Aruch, explains that an observer does not know a price was fixed. This implies that where most of the town hires by contract, even house construction may be permitted, as I demonstrated above at the beginning of siman 243 from Rambam himself, where a majority is required. Rabbi Akiva Eiger cites Responsa Mahari HaLevi as agreeing with the stringent view, though he himself permits where the local custom is contracting. Regarding a field, if it is publicized that this person hires by contract, or that this is the local custom, one should not protest against those who are lenient. Several Rishonim permit contracted field work because observers may assume sharecropping, and although we follow the stringent ruling of the Shulchan Aruch, their view may be combined with these circumstances. Regarding house construction, however, Rabbeinu Tam's lenient view is isolated, and Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that he himself later retracted it. It therefore requires study whether publicity suffices. I later found that Nishmas Adam, rule 3, is likewise stringent, and the practical ruling therefore requires study. What Mishnah Berurah writes, that it is considered contracting only when the entire building is contracted, but not when only the architect is the contractor and the workers beneath him are sometimes day laborers, follows the later authorities. It appears that this means where the owner himself sometimes hires the workers beneath the architect as day laborers, so the building can no longer be characterized as a contracted project. If, however, the owner has no involvement in the work and hires only the architect, while the architect himself sometimes hires day laborers beneath him, the project is still considered contracting.
