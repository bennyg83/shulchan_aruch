(1) A fixed payment. Meaning, contracted work, where he agrees that the non-Jew will perform a specified task for a fixed sum. If the non-Jew does it as a favor, see siman 247:4 and siman 252:2.
(2) It is permitted. Since the price was fixed, the non-Jew works for himself in order to finish more quickly. The Jew does not care whether it is done today, because otherwise it can be done tomorrow. If he specifically assigns the work for Shabbos, it is forbidden, as stated in siman 247:1.
(3) Privately. Meaning, it is work whose Jewish ownership is not generally recognizable, as the Shulchan Aruch explains. In truth, even if he performs it publicly, it is permitted so long as the work itself is not known to belong to a Jew.
(4) Not everyone recognizes it. Even if some people know it is the Jew's work, it is permitted. Although the gloss below says that one must be concerned about guests and members of his household, that case involves attached work, which is ordinarily associated with its owner, and the Sages were more stringent.
(5) Therefore, etc. Meaning, attached property is ordinarily treated as though its Jewish ownership is known and publicized.
(6) His courtyard or his wall. Even if it stands in a place where ownership is not publicly known, it is forbidden, because neighbors who know it is his may suspect that the non-Jew is his day laborer.
(7) To harvest his field for him. Although fields are ordinarily worked through sharecropping, and people might assume that the non-Jew received the field as a sharecropper, as stated in siman 243:1, this case is different. If people investigate there, they will discover that he truly receives a share of the produce. Here, when they see at harvest that the non-Jew takes no share of the profit, they will realize that he was not a sharecropper and may suspect that he was hired by the day. Taz is stringent even where the local custom is to hire by contract, because day labor is also common. Certainly this applies to house construction. Some are lenient where the entire town hires by contract. Contracting means that the whole project is contracted, not merely that the architect is a contractor while the assistants are sometimes hired by the day. See Biur Halachah. Regarding a field, one may rely on the lenient opinion in a case of loss. Regarding a house, it remains doubtful whether one may be lenient against several poskim. See Shaarei Teshuvah citing Noda BiYehudah, siman 12.
(8) In the city. Meaning, within the town, which is sometimes called a medinah.
(9) The techum. Because townspeople sometimes go there.
(10) To allow it. Sefer Chassidim, siman 348, relates that a man hired a non-Jew to build his house by contract, and the non-Jew built on Shabbos. People protested, but he disregarded them. Within a short time, the land remained neither to him nor to his descendants.
(11) If he lives, etc. Meaning, he lives outside the town's techum and no other town lies within the worksite's techum. Even so, it is forbidden because guests may happen to come to him and suspect him. The leniency below for work outside the techum applies where only the work is there, while the Jew himself lives in town.
(12) And there is no other town. The same applies if any Jew lives within the worksite's techum. It is forbidden, and he must instruct the non-Jew before Shabbos not to work on Shabbos.
(13) It is permitted. There is then no concern of maris ayin, and the non-Jew works for himself. From this seif it follows that one may not hire a non-Jew by contract to remove refuse from one's courtyard when he does so on Shabbos, because this improves the courtyard and constitutes attached work. In some places the custom is to hire him to remove refuse from a public street, even though he works on Shabbos. Magen Avraham explains that communal property creates no suspicion, though day labor is plainly forbidden. Later authorities dispute this distinction and hold that there is no difference between communal and private property. Magen Avraham himself concludes that where the custom is not to permit street work, one should not be lenient because it may cause a desecration of Hashem's name. Even if communal property creates no suspicion, Magen Avraham agrees that a synagogue may not be built on Shabbos by contract. Since the surrounding nations do not permit public labor on their festival days, allowing it on Shabbos or Yom Tov would cause a desecration of Hashem's name. Several later authorities nevertheless write that if there is genuine concern that construction of the synagogue will otherwise be abandoned entirely, one may allow a non-Jewish contractor to build it, provided it is public knowledge that the work is contracted.
