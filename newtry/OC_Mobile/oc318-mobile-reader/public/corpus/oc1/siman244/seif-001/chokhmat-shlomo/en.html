Seif 1: One may contract with a non-Jew for work. NB: I copy here briefly what I answered to someone from the holy community of Zaleszczyki. Regarding what he asked about his father-in-law's potash works: renting out his father-in-law's potash works certainly helps, since it was itself held by his father-in-law through rental. Either way: if rental does not acquire, it is not his and remains that of the first non-Jew; and if rental does acquire, the second non-Jew acquired it. Still, it is better to write that he sells him all his rights, because one can say this is not a true either way argument. Since his father-in-law has documents that are effective by dina demalchuta, even if rental does not acquire by halacha, he acquired by dina demalchuta; but the second non-Jew, who does not have such documents, did not acquire by dina demalchuta, only by Jewish law, and perhaps rental does not acquire. Therefore one should write language of sale, or write in German on stamped paper in a manner effective by dina demalchuta, in which case rental suffices. Regarding ash that has not yet come into existence, one should write that they sell all the ash when it comes into existence and into their domain; and for added strength they obligate themselves, their bodies and persons, to provide and transfer the ash to the non-Jew when the ash comes into existence and into their domain. This helps even for something not yet in existence. Also, in any case, although one does not acquire something not yet in existence, if he seized it we do not remove it from him. Here too, since in truth the sellers do not retract, and the non-Jew, because he works on Shabbat for himself, is like one who seized it, it is like his. Nevertheless, for added strength they should write as I wrote, according to what I answered the holy community of Zaleszczyki.
Seif 1, in the gloss: And even if he lives among non-Jews, one must be concerned for guests who come. NB: See Magen Avraham s.k. 8, who wrote that here in our city they practice leniency, etc. See what I wrote on this in a responsum to the holy community of Falticeni, in my work on Yoreh De'ah, mahadura 6, siman 220, to forbid this by law for several reasons, with proof for the view of the Eliyah Rabbah. See there, with God's help, and examine carefully.
Now regarding what is written in the gloss: And even if he lives among non-Jews, one must be concerned for guests who come there, or for members of his household, who will suspect him. NB: See the Magen Avraham, who asked from Sheviit and answered that regarding Sheviit they were lenient. I saw in Responsa Noda BiYehudah, first edition, Orach Chayim, that he explains the Magen Avraham's intent: the reason is that Sheviit is a lighter issur, and from this he learned that the same applies to Chol HaMoed, which is also a lighter issur, so we are not concerned for guests. In the newly printed Yeshuot Yaakov on Orach Chayim, he explains that the intent is that they were lenient because Sheviit is a long period; see there. At first glance, one might prove against the Noda BiYehudah from the Gemara. If the Sages distinguished between light and severe issurim, then since he himself wrote that work on Chol HaMoed is a light issur and resembles Sheviit, why does Avodah Zarah 21b ask: if a bathhouse may be rented to a Kuti, a Kuti also does work in it on Chol HaMoed? What is the question? Perhaps only regarding the severe issur of Shabbat and Yom Tov did they concern themselves for marit ayin, but regarding Chol HaMoed, a light issur, they did not. This would seem to disprove the Noda BiYehudah. But when one examines carefully, it appears that the Magen Avraham's intent must be because Sheviit is a light issur. One cannot explain like Yeshuot Yaakov, that the leniency is because it is a long period. Granted, if it were permitted to rent to a non-Jew for weekdays and only forbidden to rent for Shabbat itself, one could say that the loss of Shabbat is not as great as the loss of Sheviit. But since it is forbidden to rent it entirely because of concern for Shabbat, the loss is greater than by Sheviit. I later saw that Yeshuot Yaakov himself noticed this and was pressed in answering it. Therefore the main explanation appears to me to be that of the Noda BiYehudah. As for the difficulty from Avodah Zarah, why the Gemara asks from Chol HaMoed, one may answer that one who examines the Gemara there will see that Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel used the phrase, "and this non-Jew works in it on Shabbatot and Yom Tov." He included concern for Yom Tov labor. The main issur of a bathhouse on Yom Tov is only because of "that which every soul eats"; it is not something equal for every soul, and is therefore a negative derived from a positive, and regarding this we do not say that it returns to the original issur, as Tosafot wrote in Beitzah 12a similarly regarding "for you" and not for Heaven. This issur, which is not equal for every soul, is no more severe than Chol HaMoed, which is also Torah law according to several poskim. Therefore the Gemara asks properly: since you are concerned even for Yom Tov labor, though it is only a positive-command issur, then a Kuti also works in it on Chol HaMoed, which is equivalent to the issur of using a bathhouse on Yom Tov. But for us, where the Rama speaks only of Shabbat in contrast to Shabbat labor, Sheviit is certainly lighter, and the same applies to Chol HaMoed. Regarding bathhouse labor on Yom Tov, one may say that in truth we are also not concerned for guests. For this reason it is also well understood why the Gemara did not ask on the Mishnah itself that renting a bathhouse to a non-Jew is permitted; on the Mishnah one can say it forbade only because of concern for Shabbat, not Yom Tov. In this way one may say that they were lenient for Chol HaMoed, while Shabbat is severe. But Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel mentioned even Yom Tov, and bathhouse labor on Yom Tov is only a negative derived from a positive, so it properly resembles Chol HaMoed, which is also Torah law, as I wrote in Hilchot Chol HaMoed. Thus the Gemara's question is proper, and the Noda BiYehudah's ruling is resolved. Examine carefully. See my novellae to Hilchot Chanukah, where I brought a proof from elsewhere against the Noda BiYehudah, and see what I wrote in my novellae to Avodah Zarah about several errors I saw in Yeshuot Yaakov in this sugya. I also saw that Yeshuot Yaakov asked further on the Taz, who challenged the Rama: why does he permit where there are no observers, when wherever the Sages forbade because of marit ayin, it is forbidden even in private chambers? That author wondered why the Taz did not ask from the Gemara's statement that outside the techum it is permitted, since even in private chambers it should be forbidden. This is not difficult at all. The rule that even in private chambers it is forbidden applies only where observers are relevant in that place, but now there are none; even if he would not place observers there, it would only be because of this issur, since he wants to do this thing, but otherwise observers could be present in a permitted manner. In such a case the Rabbis did not distinguish and forbade in every case, as we say elsewhere, lest people say all patterned breads are forbidden but those of Baytus are permitted. But outside the techum on Shabbat and Yom Tov, it is impossible for observers to come there, and this is not because of this issur but because of another issur. Since the issur itself prevents the possibility of observers, in such a case the Rabbis did not enact, and the principle of not distinguishing does not apply. This is clear and simple. Examine carefully.
