[1] And he fixes a price, etc. The Shulchan Aruch states this without qualification in the name of Rambam. This is difficult, for Rambam derived it from the law of sending a letter in siman 247. Mordechai, in the first chapter of Shabbos, however, writes in the name of Sefer HaTerumos: “If a non-Jewish hired worker or maid lights a lamp or makes a fire for a Jew, it is forbidden. One may not permit this on the ground that a fixed price was set, for that permission applies to sending a letter, where the Jew does not physically benefit from the prohibited act itself. Here, however, the Jew benefits from the very object produced by the Shabbos prohibition, and if it was done for the Jew it is certainly forbidden.” Sefer HaTerumos itself, siman 222, explains further: “This is unlike a letter, where the Jew does not physically benefit from the act of carrying and transport.” Agudah, in the first chapter of Shabbos, Semag, and Kol Bo write likewise. Why, then, did the Shulchan Aruch rule here without qualification that it is permitted, even though the Jew benefits from the labor itself, such as fieldwork, stones, writing, and weaving a garment, all discussed in this siman? Hagahos Ashiri likewise derives from these authorities that even making a garment is prohibited. One cannot answer that all these authorities discuss only whether one may benefit from the completed work on Shabbos itself, while initially giving the work is permitted, for then why would they cite the law of a letter? I was later enlightened by Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 1 of Hilchos Shabbos, who also cites the foregoing authorities and writes: “Garments to a launderer, etc. But to perform a Jew's work in the Jew's house, setting a fixed price is ineffective. The same applies to any labor in which the Jew benefits from a prohibited act, such as a non-Jew who lit a lamp or made a fire. Even if a price was fixed, it is forbidden, and this is unlike a letter,” etc. At first glance this is self-contradictory, for if one benefits from the prohibited labor, such as garments given to a launderer, it should be forbidden even in the non-Jew's house. Sefer HaTerumos there is likewise very difficult; see there, as I have abbreviated. It therefore appears to me that in truth, when a fixed price was set, everything is permitted in the non-Jew's house, even though the Jew benefits from the prohibited labor. The prohibition applies only in the Jew's house, as with a lamp or fire, which the non-Jew ordinarily makes in the Jew's house or in his presence. The comparison to a letter is raised because the Jew's handwriting is recognizable and it is therefore like work done in the Jew's house; nevertheless, it is permitted because he does not benefit from the act of carrying itself. It now emerges that there is no dispute among the poskim. Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch permit where a price was fixed only in the non-Jew's house, as stated in seif 5 and by all the poskim, and in siman 252, seif 2. Hagahos Ashiri, who inferred from the foregoing authorities that even a garment made by the non-Jew in his own house is prohibited, did not reach this distinction, leaving room for clarification.
[2] That not everyone, etc. And as for what he afterward wrote, that because of the members of his household it is also forbidden: Magen Avraham wrote that here we are dealing with movables, which are permitted even when some people know. See below, seif 4.
[3] Levush: To build him a house, etc. Levush changed the wording of the Shulchan Aruch, which says “his house,” repeating the possessive in order to teach that even if it stands in a place where it is not known to belong to him, it is prohibited because of his neighbors. This is unlike the bathhouse at the end of siman 243, where the profits belong to the non-Jew. Perhaps the Shulchan Aruch also did not mean its wording precisely.
[4] To harvest his field, etc. The permission in siman 243 applies where he rents the field to a non-Jew and the produce belongs to the non-Jew. Here, by contrast, people see that the Jew takes the produce, and it appears to them as though the non-Jew is a day laborer. So writes Ran.
[5] For members of his household, etc. This is unlike the end of siman 243, because there they know that the non-Jew receives the profits of the bathhouse. Here, however, they may still suspect that he hired the non-Jew by the day and merely claims that he hired him as a contractor. These are the words of Magen Avraham. According to what I wrote in the name of Ran, that it appears to people as though he is a day laborer, perhaps the intent is that even if they know he is a contractor, it nevertheless appears to ordinary people like day labor, because they do not know how to distinguish. Magen Avraham writes that in some places the custom is to hire a contractor to remove refuse from the street, and the non-Jew works on Shabbos, because with communal property there is no suspicion, as stated in Yoreh Deah, siman 141. Nevertheless, the great authorities did not wish to permit construction of a communal synagogue because of chillul Hashem. Even repairing a street in a place where the permissive custom does not exist should not be treated leniently. The reason of chillul Hashem appears forced here. It is more plausible that they did not permit it because perhaps the rule that a public body is not suspected applies specifically to idolatry, but not to a Rabbinic prohibition of this kind. Shach in Yoreh Deah there writes that where two people are involved there is no suspicion. See siman 276, seif katan 14.
