1) [Seif 1] He fixes a price, etc. This means that he establishes a known sum for the work, and the non-Jew performs it whenever he wishes. Since the Jewish person does not care at what time the work is performed, the non-Jew may perform it even on Shabbos, because the Jewish person does not benefit specifically from the Shabbos labor and the non-Jew acts for his own benefit.
2) There: He fixes a price, etc. The same applies if he stipulates a particular payment per unit of work, so that the worker's compensation rises and falls according to whether his output is small or large. This too is considered a fixed price and is permitted. Shemen HaMishchah, folio 18; Petach HaDevir, paragraph 1.
3) There: This applies in private, when not everyone recognizes, etc. 'Everyone' is not literal. It is forbidden even if only some people know, for the gloss concludes that concern applies even regarding his household members and does not present this as a dispute. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 1. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2, however, writes that it is permitted although some know it is a Jewish person's work. The gloss concerns work on something attached to the ground within the techum, where we are concerned even about guests and household members, contrary to Olas Shabbos; see there. Maamar Mordechai, paragraph 1, however, writes that Olas Shabbos's words appear primary, because there is no distinction between detached and attached items; see there. Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 2, similarly writes that since the matter depends upon privacy and publicity, it is obvious that there is no distinction between work upon detached or attached property. Work on attached property outside the techum is permitted because it is private, while moving objects in a Jewish person's house is forbidden because it is public. Anything performed in a Jewish person's house is called public even if done in a secluded part of the house, since many people commonly enter and leave, and there is also suspicion by neighbors and even household members, as stated in the gloss; see there. Mateh Yehudah nevertheless writes there that in the non-Jew's house, even garments recognizable as Jewish clothing are permitted, even in a shop; see there. Ruach Chaim, paragraph 1, however, writes that when people give garments to tailors to sew, if Jewish and non-Jewish clothing are distinguishable, it is forbidden even under a fixed-price contract; see there. Therefore, it appears that one should rule stringently like Olas Shabbos and Ruach Chaim: the work must specifically be in the non-Jew's house and must not be publicly recognizable as belonging to a Jewish person. If, however, the non-Jew commonly makes Jewish garments from his own materials to sell, one may give him a garment to sew under a fixed-price contract, or purchase from him a garment to be made to the buyer's measurements. The non-Jew may perform it whenever he wishes, even on Shabbos and even in a shop, because an observer will say he works with his own material in order to sell it. See below, siman 252, seif 3.
3) This distinction applies specifically to movable utensils, permitting work under a fixed-price contract in a non-Jew's house when it is not publicly known to belong to a Jewish person. If, however, a building is constructed upon a Jewish person's house or shop, even if it is in the non-Jewish district and is not recognizable to the Jewish residents of the city, one cannot permit work upon the house or shop merely because its Jewish ownership is not publicly known. Since it is within the city or techum, some Jewish person will inevitably know, and one's friend has a friend. It is forbidden in every case, and this is clear. Ruach Chaim there. See Magen Avraham, paragraph 4.
4) There: This applies in private, etc. 'Private' means that Jewish people do not see that non-Jew while he performs the Jewish person's work. If non-Jews see him entering a non-Jew's house on Shabbos, this is not considered public. R' David HaLevi in Shemen HaSimchah, folio 17b. The author there, folio 20a, agrees; Petach HaDevir, paragraph 4.
5) There: An observer who sees the non-Jew, etc. This implies that if people know a fixed price was established, such as in a place where the custom is to build by contract, it would be permitted. This is incorrect, for Beis Yosef writes that in such a case, where the owners receive all the produce, it appears to people that the worker is a day laborer; see there. Mateh Yehudah, paragraph 3. See above, siman 243, paragraph 6.
6) There: And says that so-and-so, etc. This means that he hired him by the day, which is forbidden by strict law. Magen Avraham, paragraph 3.
