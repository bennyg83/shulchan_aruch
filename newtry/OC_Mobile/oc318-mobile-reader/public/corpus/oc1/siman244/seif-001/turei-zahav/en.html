To build his courtyard for him. Tosafos at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah writes in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that he permitted constructing a house through a gentile contractor, since the Jew gains no advantage from the work being performed on Shabbos. Just as regarding a field the sharecropper is performing his own sharecropping arrangement, so too here the gentile is performing his own contracted work. Rabbeinu Yitzchak forbids it for the reason explained below, and Rambam rules likewise, as stated in this seif. Rabbeinu Tam himself did not rely on his own ruling when he built his house. Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 1 of Hilchos Shabbos, writes in the name of Rabbeinu Simchah that one may derive from the Yerushalmi that maidservants may not perform work in a Jew’s house on Shabbos. The reason is that onlookers may say that she is performing the Jew’s work.
Or to harvest his field, etc. Although we stated above that a field is ordinarily worked through sharecropping and therefore may be leased under an inclusive arrangement, that case is different. The Sages permitted it only where this is in fact the arrangement. Onlookers attribute the matter to sharecropping, and if they investigate they will discover that the gentile indeed receives a share of the produce. Here, however, where the owner receives all the produce, it appears that the gentile is a day laborer. So writes Maggid Mishneh, and Beit Yosef states that this is also the view of Tosafos and the poskim. Tosafos at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah, when disagreeing with Rabbeinu Tam’s permission to build through a gentile contractor on Shabbos, write that house construction differs from field sharecropping: fields are commonly given out for one-half or one-third of the produce, whereas houses are ordinarily built by day laborers. An observer will therefore not say that the gentile is working under a contract, but that he is a day laborer. This appears to imply that contracted field work is permitted when the gentile undertakes to perform the Jew’s agricultural work. Yet this distinction was offered only as a logical response to Rabbeinu Tam. Tosafos and the other poskim subsequently cite the Yerushalmi, which permits contracted gentile work on Shabbos only with detached materials, not with anything attached to the ground, as the Shulchan Aruch rules here following Rambam. Even with detached materials, it is permitted only in the gentile’s house. It follows that on Jewish land it is forbidden even in a field, and not only in house construction, since the only permitted case is detached work. The words of Tosafos themselves can also be explained this way. At first glance their statement that houses are ordinarily built by day laborers is surprising, for we see that craftsmen are commonly hired to construct an entire house under contract. Rather, Tosafos distinguishes as follows. With a field there are two possible permitted arrangements: the gentile works for himself in return for a share, or he works entirely for the Jew under a contract. There is one forbidden arrangement, namely day labor. Since it is common for the gentile to work for himself, we judge leniently. House construction has no arrangement in which the gentile works for himself. He always works for the Jew. The only possible leniency is a contract, and opposite it stands the possibility that he is a day laborer. One must therefore be stringent even under a contract. If the truth regarding a field is that the gentile works entirely for the Jew under contract, the first basis for leniency is absent; it is then like a house and forbidden. Thus Tosafos’ intent agrees with Maggid Mishneh. The other poskim cited by Beit Yosef likewise conclude, based on the Yerushalmi, that all contracted work involving attached property is forbidden, whether a field or a house. Beit Yosef writes that Rabbeinu Yerucham’s permission to give a water mill to a gentile contractor follows the permissive opinions, but according to those who forbid work involving land, a mill is likewise forbidden, for there is no distinction.
