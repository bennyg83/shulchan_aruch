Or. See Taz. As written in siman 253, seif katan 2, Ran explains that it appears like a day laborer because people do not know how to distinguish between contracted work and day labor. Tosafos, however, writes that people will say they are day laborers, whereas fieldwork is ordinarily done by contract. See Bach. I have not fully understood their words. Rambam, whose language is also used by the Mechaber, writes that an observer will say that so-and-so is a hired laborer, which does not follow Ran, who writes only that it appears like day labor. Taz writes that Tosafos also supports Rambam, for they cite the Yerushalmi that craftsmen working on detached materials are permitted, implying that work attached to the ground is prohibited even in a field. Tosafos understand that stonecutting is likewise prohibited by the Yerushalmi. According to the view that the stonecutting case does not establish a prohibition for a mill, one could say that the Yerushalmi’s law concerning detached work refers specifically to stonecutting. See Bach and Magen Avraham, seif katan 9; with Hashem’s help this will be explained further. Taz further answers that although most such work is contracted, whenever there are not two independent grounds for leniency it remains prohibited; see there. Regarding his statement that a watermill is prohibited, see what I wrote in seif katan 1, and see siman 253 in Beit Yosef and Magen Avraham, seif katan 2. In such a case it may be prohibited by law, with the practical result that it would remain prohibited even outside the techum. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 5, and Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 5; with Hashem’s help this will be explained further there.
What the Taz wrote. In seif katan 1, that the reason is because of onlookers, is challenged by Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 11, for there it is explained that a non-Jew’s own work is permitted even in a Jew’s house; see there. At the very least, Hagahos Mordechai must be prohibiting the non-Jew’s own work, for if it referred to the Jew’s work it would be obvious that craftsmen are no better, especially since the Jew benefits from their Shabbos labor. Hagahos Mordechai’s statement that the non-Jew’s own work is permitted privately within a Jew’s house presents no concern of onlookers. Whenever there is only a Rabbinic prohibition, the Sages did not prohibit it in private, as Taz writes in siman 243, seif katan 3. See there, where Maharshal writes in the name of Hagahos Maimoniyos that a Jewish maidservant is prohibited because onlookers may say she is doing the Jew’s work. The statement of Hagahos Mordechai at the end of chapter 14, that one need not protest a non-Jew from outside, implies that one must protest one’s own maidservants. This requires study.
See. Nechalas Tzvi, who writes that according to Rashba’s explanation of the Yerushalmi, first chapter of Shabbos, halachah 8, even contracted fieldwork is prohibited. I do not understand this. It is true that Rashba prohibits even sharecropping of a field in Avodah Zarah 21b. Nevertheless, some say that according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, whose view we follow regarding sharecropping of a field, contracted fieldwork is likewise permitted; while regarding contracted construction of a house, the halachah follows Rashba. In practice, however, even contracted fieldwork is prohibited. If it is publicly known that the field is worked by contract, one could say it is permitted, because the Mechaber gives the reason that people will say they are day laborers, not merely that it appears like day labor. Contracted work involving an oven, bathhouse, or mill, however, remains prohibited even when the arrangement is known, because the Jew benefits from the Shabbos labor, as Mahari Abuhav writes, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 253. See Magen Avraham there, seifim katanim 1 and 2, and what I wrote there in Peri Megadim.
The law. Concerning a Jew’s mill on Shabbos, see siman 450, Taz, seif katan 6, who writes that there is no remedy except a sale for half a year, so that the Shabbos payment is absorbed into the larger payment. At first glance, where there is a sale the issue of Shabbos wages should not apply, since on Shabbos it belongs entirely to the non-Jew, and after Shabbos the non-Jew withdraws from the purchase and gives the Jew a gift so that he will not sue to enforce the sale. It also appears that “sale” is not precise, for the same should apply to a sharecropping arrangement on Shabbos, as explained in siman 243. On Pesach, however, an actual sale is required. With Hashem’s help this will be explained further in Hilchos Pesach; this is not the place to elaborate.
