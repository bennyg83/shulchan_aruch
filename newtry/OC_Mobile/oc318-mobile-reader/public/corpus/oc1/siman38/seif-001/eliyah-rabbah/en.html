1) “If he is suffering, etc.” The Levush’s use of the word “a sick person” implies that he means two cases: one who is ill, or one who is suffering even though he is not ill. This is also indicated by his conclusion, “they are exempt, etc., because their minds are not,” etc., in the plural. If he meant only a sick person, he should have written in the singular, “he is exempt because his mind is not settled.” This explains why the Levush omitted from this siman an entire se’if of the Shulchan Aruch, whose wording is: “One who is suffering, and one whose mind is not settled and properly composed, is exempt, because it is prohibited to divert one’s attention from them.” Rather, it appears to me that in this se’if the Levush agrees with what is written here concerning one who is suffering, etc. Nevertheless, it appears that he also concedes that a sick person who is not suffering is obligated, since he writes, “and one with an intestinal illness, even if he is not suffering,” etc. This implies that another sick person is exempt only when suffering. He holds, however, that an ordinary sick person generally suffers, and an unqualified statement is understood accordingly. The Magen Avraham writes that one who suffers even because of the cold is exempt, and that “whose mind is not settled” means a case in which he cannot settle his mind. A person with an intestinal illness is exempt even while walking through streets and markets, and he is prohibited from acting stringently, except during Keriat Shema and prayer. Responsa Menachem Azaria, siman 60, however, implies that even outside the time of Keriat Shema and prayer he may put on tefillin when he has a suitable opportunity, removing them when his distress returns. If he was compelled and did not remove the hand-tefillin, he committed no prohibition. The later authorities write that the same applies to one who cannot guard against passing gas, except that when he must remove the head-tefillin he should remove the hand-tefillin as well. Sefer Chassidim, siman 609, writes: a person wearing trousers who is naked from the trousers upward should not put on tefillin, because his heart is exposed and he may not recite the blessing; nor is it proper that he be naked while wearing tefillin. A person who can hear but cannot speak should put on tefillin, as should one who can speak but cannot hear. Thus far his words. At first glance his statement “nor is it proper,” etc., appears superfluous. It seems, however, that he means that even in a case in which no blessing is required, it is still improper to be naked while wearing tefillin. Knesset HaGedolah writes that a deaf-mute who can neither hear nor speak is exempt from tefillin, since he is exempt from all mitzvot; nevertheless, if he wishes to put them on, one should not prevent him. The Beit Yosef writes that the Rosh cites Sefer HaIttim, which exempts a blind person from tefillin because the verse states “and you shall see it.” The Rosh disagrees and writes that this verse concerns tzitzit. The Shulchan Aruch therefore omits this view. I saw, however, that Rabbeinu Yerucham rules like Sefer HaIttim, and Sefer Barukh SheAmar likewise rules this way in the name of the Rosh. One can answer the Rosh’s objection by saying that Sefer HaIttim derived the law from the verse concerning tefillin, “And all the peoples of the earth shall see,” as Maadanei Melekh writes on page 96. This requires further study.
