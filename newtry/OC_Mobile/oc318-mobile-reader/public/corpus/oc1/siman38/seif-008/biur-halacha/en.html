Writers of Torah scrolls, tefillin, and mezuzos, etc. This is the correct text; it appears this way in the Gemara and all the Rishonim.
They and their merchants, etc. See Mishnah Berurah for what we wrote in the name of Magen Avraham. In my humble opinion, this requires study from Bava Metzia 82b. One opinion holds that the lender performs a mitzvah by making the loan; the other holds that he does not perform a mitzvah because he intends his own benefit. See Rashi there, s.v. "with a lender, it is necessary," who writes that nevertheless there is a mitzvah. Rabbi Akiva, whose view is accepted, thus holds that even when a person also intends his own benefit, he is considered engaged in a mitzvah and is exempt from giving bread to a poor person, thereby becoming a paid custodian. Perhaps the Gemara's statement that one opinion considers the loan a mitzvah means that the lender presumably intends to fulfill his Creator's commandment and also intends his own benefit, whereas Rabbi Eliezer holds that he intends solely his own benefit. This resembles Rashi's explanation in Pesachim 9b, s.v. "this person is completely righteous," that he also intends, etc., and on 8a, s.v. "and in such a case"; see there. If we know that his sole intention is profit, he is not called engaged in a mitzvah. Nevertheless, it appears to me that if both intentions are equal, he is called engaged in a mitzvah, like Eliyah Rabbah's ruling in siman 589, paragraph 8, that one who blows shofar both to practice and to fulfill the mitzvah has fulfilled his obligation. Know further that concerning the writing of sacred texts itself, where writers commonly intend to earn wages, I am inclined to say that it is nevertheless included in engagement in a mitzvah. Although this initially appears identical to the case Magen Avraham derived from Rashi, it seems to me that there is a distinction, which also resolves Magen Avraham's question. Writing itself is unquestionably the actual mitzvah, as it is written, "You shall write them." It is merely not the completion of the mitzvah before the tefillin are tied on, as Menachos 41b proves. It is therefore always included in engagement in a mitzvah. Even if we know that his initial reason for beginning to write was solely for pay and he would not otherwise have begun, we say that now, while writing, he has no particular intention beyond writing ordinarily lishmah for the mitzvah of tefillin, as the law requires. Rashi's explanation, "those who purchase them in order to make them available," applies only to the merchants. Trading in tefillin is nowhere defined as a mitzvah. Even if selling them to someone who needs tefillin is considered engagement in a mitzvah, when he purchases the tefillin from the scribe for commerce, the act itself does not yet contain the mitzvah. Since the Gemara exempts merchants in every case, Rashi explains that they purchase them in order to make them available to those who need them; their mitzvah-oriented thought makes the act engagement in a mitzvah. Returning a lost item, writing sacred texts, and similar activities differ, because the act itself constitutes engagement in a mitzvah.
If they were, etc. See Olas Tamid, Magen Avraham, Vilna Gaon, and the other later authorities. Rema rules according to the Babylonian Talmud rather than the Yerushalmi, and therefore exempts them even from Krias Shema and prayer themselves, unlike the Mechaber, who obligates them in Krias Shema and prayer in every case. Olas Tamid and Magen Avraham imply that the halachah follows Rema.
They must perform their work, etc. See Mishnah Berurah, paragraph 26, where I copied Levush's practical ruling. This also appears at first glance from Rema's word "must." It is difficult, however, because Rema follows Ran, as he himself concludes, and according to Ran one is exempt in every case once he has begun writing, as Beis Yosef proves. Perhaps when someone wishes to depart by sea or with a caravan and will be unable to obtain tefillin for several days, one may initially begin writing even after the time for Krias Shema arrives, though this will cause that time to pass. This is nevertheless unpersuasive: why must the scribe place himself in this situation by beginning to write or sell, thereby becoming engaged in a mitzvah and removing from himself the positive obligations of Krias Shema, tefillin, and the like? Do we tell a person to sin, etc.? This requires further study. Magen Avraham's words at the end of paragraph 10 ("all this concerns," etc.) also do not imply this, though one can say that he does not discuss Levush's case. Were it not for Levush, one could explain Rema's phrase "if they must" to mean that the scribe estimates that many buyers will arrive that day and therefore begins early and hurries. Otherwise, a scribe presumably would not write continuously day and night, leaving no time for any mitzvah, including accepting the yoke of Heaven and putting on tefillin. Avodas HaYom also implies this.
Then they are exempt. See Mishnah Berurah for what we wrote in the name of Magen Avraham. After the fact, even if one began when prohibited, he need not interrupt, because he is nevertheless engaged in a mitzvah. Thus siman 72 states that once they began carrying the deceased out, they do not interrupt, even if the time for Krias Shema has arrived, as Pri Chadash writes there.
For anyone engaged, etc. See Sukkah 26a. Emissaries performing a mitzvah (such as one traveling to greet his rav or redeem captives) are likewise included among those engaged in a mitzvah and are exempt from all other mitzvos.
If it requires exertion. See Mishnah Berurah. Ran writes likewise. Although Tosafos, the Rosh, and Rabbeinu Yerucham are stringent, Beis Yosef and Rema appear to accept Ran's practical ruling, as do Hagahos Ashri in the name of Or Zarua, because of their powerful proofs. Magen Avraham likewise cites Maggid Mishneh that Rambam and the Geonim hold that one is exempt even when he can perform both mitzvos. Ran explains that the Torah did not obligate one occupied with the work of Heaven to exert himself to perform other mitzvos, even though he could then perform several mitzvos. One digging a grave for a deceased person is therefore exempt from them all even while briefly resting, for during the rest he is still called engaged in the mitzvah, since it strengthens him to resume digging. He is exempt at that time even though he could give a coin to a poor person standing near him. One wearing tefillin, however, though he fulfills a mitzvah, is called engaged in it so as to be exempt from all mitzvos only while putting them on, not afterward. The same applies to guarding a lost item, as I wrote in Mishnah Berurah. All this follows from Ran and Or Zarua. Rema's words, "but if, etc., without exertion," mean that the second mitzvah adds no exertion at all, but the same effort serves both. If he can fulfill both in the ordinary course of the first mitzvah, he should certainly do so, for one should not be called bad when he can be good. Ran writes this there, and it follows from the Gemara proof he cites; otherwise the Gemara's question there would present no difficulty. See there.
