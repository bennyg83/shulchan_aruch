Because sometimes, etc. Tur writes that Rabbi Yehudah HaChasid practiced this. Beit Yosef challenges the custom from the Yerushalmi, where Rabbi Acha wished to protest against those who did so. Rabbi Yehudah HaChasid apparently relied upon the Babylonian Gemara, which is primary. Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi recited Shema during his study session with his students and later recited it again with its blessings, as Beit Yosef explains at length. Maharshal, responsum 64, writes that when he reaches the Krias Shema of Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi, he recites the entire first paragraph through “and in your gates.” Ri writes likewise in his novellae to Berachos. He further says that the blessing afterward, Mekadesh Shimcha BeRabim, from the Yerushalmi, appropriately follows. Just as Hashem sanctifies His Name publicly, we are obligated to sanctify His Name publicly, which is included in “with all your heart,” etc. His words imply that Mekadesh Shimcha BeRabim should be recited as a formal blessing with Hashem's Name, saying, “Blessed are You, Hashem, Who sanctifies,” etc. Beit Yosef likewise includes it in the count of blessings. Semag, Tur, and Tosafos, Pesachim 104b, also treat it as a blessing. This is surprising because it is not mentioned in the Babylonian Gemara. In siman 157, Beit Yosef derives from Borei Nefashos that Modim DeRabbanan should not conclude with Hashem's Name because it appears only in the Yerushalmi. Rambam likewise does not mention Hashem's Name here. We can only answer as above: since everyone has already adopted this blessing, the custom remains binding. Tur's text reads, “You are He before the world was created.” This is likewise the text in Tosafos, Pesachim 104b and Berachos 46, Rambam, all old siddurim, the Yerushalmi, and Yalkut VaEschanan in the name of Eliyahu. Sephardic siddurim read, “before You created the world.” No one should alter ancient texts. A recent grammarian changed our text to match the Sephardic version, and he did improperly. Some object that “before the world was created” could imply, Heaven forbid, that another created it. The opposite is true. The altered text merely says that Hashem existed before and after He created the world, which is obvious because every actor exists before and after his act. If the intention were to identify the Creator, it should simply say, “You created the world.” The original text testifies to Hashem's eternity: He always exists, both before the world's creation and afterward, as in Yigdal, “Primeval before all that was created.” One who changes this or other ancient prayer texts is at a disadvantage.
