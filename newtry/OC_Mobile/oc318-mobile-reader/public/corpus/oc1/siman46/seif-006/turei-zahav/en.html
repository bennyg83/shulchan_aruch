The Gemara says: when he sits up, he recites Matir Asurim; when he straightens, he recites Zokef Kefufim. When he sits and stretches his limbs, he thanks Hashem for moving limbs that had been bound during the night. When he stands upright, he thanks Hashem for straightening his posture. Rav Amram holds that if he first recited Zokef Kefufim, he should not afterward recite Matir Asurim, because movement of the limbs is included in straightening the body. Tur disagrees and asks why, since the Gemara assigns a separate reason to each blessing. Everyone agrees that if two possible blessing arrangements exist, one with a single blessing and one with two, the single blessing should ordinarily be preferred to avoid multiplying blessings. Here, however, each blessing was instituted at a distinct moment. When he first sat up, Zokef Kefufim was not yet relevant. Only Matir Asurim applied. When he later stood, a new act created the obligation of Zokef Kefufim. Rav Amram holds that although today we recite all the blessings together in the synagogue and could theoretically recite only Zokef Kefufim, we should not alter the Talmudic order. But if he happened to recite Zokef Kefufim first, he is exempt from Matir Asurim. Even in Talmudic times, if he failed to recite Matir Asurim when sitting and later recited Zokef Kefufim, he would not repeat the included blessing. Tur holds that because each blessing had its own original moment of obligation, the separate obligation remains even after Zokef Kefufim. My father-in-law asks why Rav Amram did not similarly rule that one who first recited “Who did not make me a woman” should omit “Who did not make me a slave,” and certainly “Who did not make me a non-Jew.” The Gemara asks why both slave and woman are necessary and answers that a slave is more degraded. There is no difficulty. A slave or non-Jew may later become fully obligated through emancipation or conversion, while a woman can never become obligated in all mitzvos. Therefore the blessing concerning a woman does not include the other two. The blessings concerning a non-Jew and a slave are likewise both required. Bava Kama 15 teaches that each has a distinct inferior status. A slave lacks lineage, while a non-Jew is outside the mitzvah system. Each also has an advantage over the other. A person must therefore thank Hashem separately that he was not created in any of these three states. Matir Asurim, however, is truly included in Zokef Kefufim. Rav Amram's ruling is therefore correct, and Shulchan Aruch properly follows it because of doubt concerning blessings. Rokeach lists Zokef Kefufim before Matir Asurim, as do old siddurim. This appears contrary to the Gemara. Perhaps Rokeach interpreted “when he straightens” as lifting his head while still sitting in bed, and “when he sits upright” as the later movement of separating and stretching his limbs. His version of the Gemara may also have had the clauses in a different order. In any event, the primary order is Zokef Kefufim before Matir Asurim.
