1) [Seif 1] When he awakens from his sleep, etc. How good and pleasant it is if a person is able to refrain from speaking anything at all, so that the first words he speaks upon rising from sleep begin with a mitzvah, blessing the Creator of his soul. This is very proper. Chesed LaAlafim, letter 2. See Moreh BeEtzba, letter 63, and what we wrote above in siman 1, letter 40. 2) Tur writes that after putting on tefillin one should arrange the blessings, etc. We already wrote above in siman 25, letter 26, that the Arizal would recite all the morning blessings through the passage of the Akeidah, and afterward wrap himself in a tallis, put on tefillin, and pray. 3) There, upon awakening he should say Elokai Neshamah. This blessing does not begin with Baruch because it is a blessing of thanksgiving, and blessings of thanksgiving do not begin with Baruch, as Maran writes in siman 6, seif 3. Nevertheless, it should be placed adjacent to Asher Yatzar, as we wrote above in siman 6, letters 3 and 5, in the name of the Arizal. See also below, letter 7. 4) There, he should say Elokai Neshamah. One should pause slightly between Elokai and neshamah, so that it not sound as though the soul is his god, Heaven forbid. Abudraham; Ateret Zekenim; Eliyah Rabbah, letter 1; Shaliach Tzibbur, page 45d; Beit Oved, letter 1; Chesed LaAlafim, letter 3; Yafeh LaLev, letter 2; Ben Ish Chai, Parashat Vayeshev, letter 2. 5) One should not say "tehorah hi," but simply "tehorah, Atah beratah," because in most cases a person has sinned and the soul is not literally pure. Kesher Gudal, siman 5, letter 8. Shaliach Tzibbur, page 46a, gives additional reasons. Beit Oved, letter 2; Chesed LaAlafim; Ben Ish Chai there. See Yafeh LaLev, letter 4, who discusses this at length. In Berachot 60b the word hi is also absent. Although Shaar HaKavanos, page 2a, has "tehorah hi," this need not be exact, and the custom is not to say hi. 6) There, he should say Elokai Neshamah. This blessing should contain 47 words, corresponding to the combined numerical values of Havayah and Ehyeh, which represent Abba and Imma that are never separated. See Nahar Shalom, page 11c in the newer edition, and what we wrote above in siman 6, letter 3. Shaliach Tzibbur and Chesed LaAlafim write likewise. The text is: "My God, the soul You placed within me is pure. You created it; You formed it; You breathed it into me; and You preserve it within me. You will ultimately take it from me and restore it to me in the future. As long as the soul is within me, I give thanks before You, Hashem my God and God of my fathers, Master of all works, Lord of all souls. Blessed are You, Hashem, Who restores souls to dead bodies." Chesed LaAlafim. This is unlike Ateret Zekenim in siman 6 and Beit Oved following him, who omit the word "bi" after "You breathed it." Shaliach Tzibbur rejects their view and writes that the universal custom, as well as the Gemara in Berachot 60b and Shaar HaKavanos, includes it. 8) There, he should say Elokai Neshamah. If he mistakenly recited Mechayeh HaMeitim instead of Elokai Neshamah, he has fulfilled his obligation. Peri Chadash; Yad Aharon; Kesher Gudal, siman 5, letter 9; Shaliach Tzibbur, page 46b; Beit Oved in the laws of Birchot HaShachar; Shitilei Zeitim, letter 1. Although Maamar Mordechai questions Peri Chadash, he ultimately writes that his view must be taken into account, and the rule of doubt regarding blessings supports leniency. 9) If one forgot Birchot HaShachar before prayer, he should recite them afterward, except for HaMachazir Neshamot, because Mechayeh HaMeitim has already exempted him, and except for Birchot HaTorah, because Ahavat Olam has already exempted him. This will be explained below in siman 47, seifim 7 and 8, and in Rama at the end of siman 52. If he failed to recite them even after prayer, see below, siman 71, letter 4, regarding how long their time continues. Initially, however, it is obvious that one should not postpone Birchot HaShachar until after prayer, because doing so reverses the order of the spiritual rectifications they effect. See Pri Etz Chaim, Shaar HaBerachot, chapter 3, and Nahar Shalom, page 11c in the newer edition.
