Before entering the synagogue, while he is still in its courtyard, he should say, “In the house of God we shall walk with the multitude.” He should feel awe and trembling upon entering, pause briefly, and say, “But I, through Your abundant kindness, shall enter Your house; I shall bow toward Your holy sanctuary in fear of You.” He should then enter. (1) At the time, etc. All these blessings were instituted because a person may not benefit from this world without a blessing. Whoever benefits without a blessing is considered to have misused sacred property. Two verses appear contradictory: “The earth and its fullness belong to Hashem,” and, “The earth He gave to mankind.” The Sages resolve them: before a blessing it belongs to Hashem and is forbidden like consecrated property; after a blessing it is permitted to mankind. Since before the blessing everything belongs to Hashem, it is sacred and subject to misuse. The Sages therefore instituted a blessing for each benefit a person derives from the world's regular order.
(2) From his sleep. Meaning specifically when he completes his period of sleep, not whenever he happens to awaken at any hour.
(3) He should say Elokai Neshamah. He should pause slightly between Elokai and neshamah, so that it not sound as though the soul itself is his god, Heaven forbid. The laws of Elokai Neshamah are explained above in siman 6, Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 12.
(4) Sechvi. In Scripture, sechvi means the heart, as in the verse, “Who gave understanding to the sechvi?” The heart understands, and through understanding a person distinguishes between day and night. The rooster also discerns this distinction. Hearing it benefits a person by informing him that day is approaching, and in Arabic a rooster is called sechvi. The blessing was therefore instituted upon hearing the rooster. See below, seif 8.
(5) Who clothes the naked. This does not specifically refer to one who slept unclothed. Rather, when he puts on his outer garment he recites this blessing.
(6) Upon his eyes. Meaning through his garment, because he may not place his hands upon his eyes before washing, as explained in siman 4.
(7) Who prepares. Agudah has the text, “Who prepared.”
(8) The belt. The avnet mentioned here means trousers, and the same applies to the avnet mentioned at the beginning of siman 91. Although in Biblical language avnet means a belt, as in “He shall gird himself with a linen belt,” the Sages sometimes use the word for trousers. Shulchan Aruch therefore says “when he puts it on,” rather than “when he girds it.” Mateh Yehudah.
(9) Who crowns Israel. This blessing mentions Israel, as does “Who girds Israel,” unlike the other blessings. The other blessings concern worldly benefits shared by everyone. These two are particular to Israel because of modesty. The belt prevents the heart from seeing nakedness, and the head covering expresses modesty and fear of Heaven, as stated in Shabbos: “Cover your head because of awe of your Master.”
(10) May it be Your will. The text is, “That You accustom us to Your Torah,” in the plural. Later authorities.
