(1) Elokai Neshamah. One should pause between the words Elokai and neshamah, and likewise between Elokai and netzor. The word tehorah should be followed by a pause, without pronouncing the final heh strongly, and one should not say “tehorah hi.” In “You created it,” the beis is soft, with a sheva, and the final heh is pronounced. The same applies to “You formed it,” with the yod bearing a sheva and the final heh pronounced. The words “You created,” “You formed,” “You breathed,” and all similar forms are pronounced with a kamatz. In the first “it,” the zayin has a kamatz. In “within me,” the second beis is soft. Maharash's siddur records the following vocalizations: in Mah Tovu and VeEchra, the reish has a kamatz. In Yigdal, metziuso has a shuruk under the alef, meaning the existence of Hashem; with a kamatz it would mean a found object. In ne'elam, the lamed has a kamatz. In ne'eman, the mem has a patach. One should not pause between daso and ra; the reish of ra has a kamatz, because ra modifies rasha. Bach writes in siman 38 that he saw the pious Rav Yeshayah read every morning, while wearing tefillin, the passages Kadesh and Vehayah Ki Yeviacha. Since all four passages are written in the tefillin, he maintained that it is proper to read all four each day while wearing them.
(2) Sechvi means the heart or the rooster. The beis of binah is soft. The tzadi of tzorki has a kamatz, and the kaf is strong. The lamed of yishlot has a patach. In Mah Chayeinu and Mah Chasdeinu, both mems have a segol. The tav of berisecha has a segol. In ahavcha, the heh has a patach and the beis a silent sheva. The reish of bechorecha has a segol. In ba'elyonim and batachtonim, the first beis has a kamatz and the second a patach.
(3) When he places, etc. Meaning, through a sheet or garment, since he has not yet washed his hands. We rule in siman 4 that a hand touching the eye deserves to be cut off. Me'adanei Melech and Shelah write likewise. Perishah writes: It appears that the reference is to a person whose eyes remain closed, who ordinarily passes his hand over them, or to one who has opened them but still feels sleepy and closes them, rubbing the skin over the eye in order to remove the bonds of sleep. This implies that direct use of the hand is permitted.
(4) Who opens the blind. Although the person is not actually blind but merely has closed eyes, the term opening is still appropriate, as in the verse, “Then your eyes will be opened.”
(5) Who spreads the earth, etc. The blessing praises Hashem for placing the earth upon the water, contrary to its natural tendency. We say “Who girds Israel” and “Who crowns Israel” because the girdle separates between the heart and nakedness, and the head covering expresses fear of Heaven. The other blessings concern universal benefits shared by all people. Abudraham explains this on page 17. For this reason, these two blessings require separate formulation even though they were already included in Malbish Arumim.
