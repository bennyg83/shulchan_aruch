(8) One hundred, etc. Some derive the number from the letters of the verse “And now, Israel,” reading mah as meah. Others use the Atbash substitution, by which mah becomes yod-tzadi, whose numerical value is one hundred. The word mimecha likewise has the numerical value one hundred. Rokeach writes that the one hundred blessings protect against the ninety-eight curses together with “every sickness and every plague,” totaling one hundred. The Beis HaMikdash was therefore one hundred amos high. Each blessing corresponds to ten gold pieces, yielding the verse, “The thousand are yours, Shlomo.” Shelah writes that a person should teach his household to recite all blessings aloud, both blessings over mitzvos and blessings over enjoyment. The voice promotes concentration, and listeners can answer Baruch Hu Uvaruch Shemo and amen. Rabbi David Abudraham asks what our blessings add to the Holy One, blessed is He. He answers that our blessings exist only so that we may merit His blessings, as the Sages say, “Whoever blesses is blessed.” Magen Avraham cites Rashba that the final letters of the words rochev basod form keruv. “Rochev” alludes to His supervision and salvation, because riding denotes dominion. “Bechor” indicates His greatness above all. “Keruv” alludes to His dwelling among the keruvim and to the verse, “Hashem is near to all who call Him.” Rabbi David Abudraham further explains that blessings were instituted partly in direct address and partly in third person because the Holy One, blessed is He, is both revealed and concealed with respect to His Divinity. The soul too is perceptible and hidden. The blessing is spoken with the mouth and contemplated in the heart, teaching a person to cleave constantly to his Creator by virtue of his soul. Abudraham writes that one who can add beyond one hundred blessings is praiseworthy, as David said, “And I shall add to all Your praise.” On Shabbos and Yom Tov, when the shorter Amidah reduces the number of blessings, see below, siman 250, that one completes them with fruit. On Yom Kippur some write that one should take myrtle or spices and smell them from time to time, reciting a blessing. Others disagree and permit fragrance only where there is a mitzvah-related reason, such as the myrtle used at a bris milah. They argue that one should not repeatedly derive pleasure through fragrance on a day of Torah-mandated affliction merely to complete one hundred blessings, since the total can be completed through answering amen to the chazzan's repetition and to the Torah and Haftarah blessings. Magen Avraham favors the first view in siman 265, but that is not a proof because the case there concerns a bris milah. See the end of siman 621.
