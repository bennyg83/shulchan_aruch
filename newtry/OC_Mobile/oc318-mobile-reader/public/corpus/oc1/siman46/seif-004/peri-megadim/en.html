Who did not. See Taz. Some versions read, “Who made me an Israelite,” and this is also the wording in Menachos 43b and Tur. Why do we multiply blessings? One may answer that they concern distinct matters. We bless and thank Hashem for the three separate degrees by which He increased His kindness toward us. The blessing “Who made me according to His will,” cited by Beit Yosef and Levush, means that a woman accepts the Divine decree upon herself. Taz explains instead that womanhood is necessary for the preservation of the world. This is compelling, for otherwise how could women recite a blessing not mentioned in the Gemara? It must be inferred from the blessing “Who did not make me a woman.” See Magen Avraham, letter 9. One should consider why a slave born into slavery does not recite “Who made me according to His will,” according to either Beit Yosef's or Taz's explanation. At first glance, a slave also should not recite “Who did not make me a woman,” because a slave is even more degraded, as the Gemara concludes in Menachos 43b. Perhaps a slave is considered superior in certain respects. A woman faces danger in childbirth, whereas a slave bears additional mitzvos and the covenantal mark of circumcision, concerning which thirteen covenants were made. Rambam also writes in Hilchos Avodah Zarah that a slave is obligated in certain laws concerning the beard. Thus, in some respects he is superior to a woman.
