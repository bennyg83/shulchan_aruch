Who did not make me a non-Jew. This is the correct text. The version “Who made me a Jew” is a printer's alteration. Bach cites a question: why do we not recite the positive formulation “Who made me an Israelite,” as in the other blessings such as Pokeach Ivrim? Some answer that since the schools of Shammai and Hillel concluded that it would have been better for a person not to have been created, we do not bless Hashem for creating us. We therefore say, in effect, “Although You created me, I thank You that You did not make me a non-Jew, slave, or woman.” Bach answers that if one said “Who made me an Israelite,” this would include being free and male, and he could not afterward recite separate blessings concerning a slave and a woman. His words imply that if one mistakenly recited “Who made me an Israelite,” he should not recite the other two blessings. It appears to me that even the term Israelite or Jew includes a woman, because the Torah generally speaks in masculine language while women are included, except where it says “sons of Israel,” which excludes daughters of Israel. Sanhedrin 84, Bava Kama 15, Menachos 93, and the beginning of Erchin should be examined. Beit Yosef writes that the blessing concerning a slave should precede the blessing concerning a woman, because the Gemara says a slave is more degraded. Bach implies that according to the rule in seif 5 concerning Matir Asurim, if one first recited “Who did not make me a woman,” he should no longer recite the blessings concerning a non-Jew and a slave.
And even a convert. Meaning that he should say, “Who made me a convert,” because conversion too is called making, as in the verse, “And the souls they made in Charan.” Darkei Moshe. Shelah and Bach write that this formulation is inappropriate, because becoming a convert resulted from his own choice to accept Judaism. He should therefore recite only the blessings concerning a slave and a woman. According to them, “Who did not make me a slave” refers to his original creation. Although at birth he was even lower than a slave, a slave could not convert unless his master freed him. According to the kabbalists, however, these blessings concern the soul's departure at night and its return without attachment to a non-Jewish or slave soul. According to this, a convert may also recite them.
