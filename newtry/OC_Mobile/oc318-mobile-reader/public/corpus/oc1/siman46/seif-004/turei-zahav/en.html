Who did not make me a non-Jew. Many ask why this blessing was instituted negatively rather than positively as “Who made me an Israelite,” and likewise why we say “Who did not make me a woman.” Some answer that since the schools of Shammai and Hillel concluded that it would have been better for a person not to have been created, the blessing does not praise the creation itself. We therefore thank Hashem at least for not creating us as a non-Jew, slave, or woman. My father-in-law explains that if one said “Who made me an Israelite,” that would imply free and male, leaving no basis for the two later blessings. This is difficult. Tosafos in Menachos and Yoma writes that wherever two matters can be covered by one blessing, one may not multiply blessings. Here too, one blessing should have included all three. It appears that the negative wording teaches that one should not attribute any deficiency, Heaven forbid, to Hashem's creation of non-Jews or women. They too are necessary to creation. Even non-Jews are necessary because converts emerge from among them. The Holy One, blessed is He, spared two nations for the sake of the two good descendants who would emerge from them, Rus and Naamah. This also explains Hashem's blessing to Avraham through Hagar that twelve princes would descend from her. The increase of such descendants is a blessing because converts may emerge from among them, as the Sages say that Israel was exiled among the nations so that converts would be added. All the more so, a woman is a beneficial creation and fulfills certain mitzvos. A man therefore thanks Hashem that He did not make him a non-Jew, while acknowledging that Hashem did make others as non-Jews for a necessary purpose. The same applies to a woman. He merited not to require that particular role, but its creation remains valuable. This would not be conveyed by “Who made me an Israelite,” which could imply that all other forms of creation lack purpose. This also explains why women recite “Who made me according to His will,” although that blessing is not stated in the Gemara. Since even the man's blessing recognizes the value of woman's creation, she may appropriately bless Hashem for her own role.
