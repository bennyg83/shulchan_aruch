In Magen Avraham, se'if katan 14: “Therefore, it appears to me that a blind person should not recite the blessing,” etc. Pri Megadim emends the text to read that a blind person should recite Poke'ach Ivrim, because he benefits from light through other people, as stated in siman 69. It is difficult, however, to say that Magen Avraham would rule contrary to Hagahot Maimoniyot in the name of Tosafot. There is a distinction: the blessing Yotzer Or was instituted over the creation of light, and a blind person also benefits from light through others; Poke'ach Ivrim, however, explicitly praises God for opening the eyes, and this person's eyes were not opened. If a blind person could bless because other people's eyes were opened, then a deaf person should certainly be able to recite Hanoten LaSechvi Binah, since the principal subject of that blessing is the discernment between light and darkness. A deaf person benefits from light, and others can inform him of the rooster's discernment, even when he is in a dark house, as the Rosh explains. Yet the wording “when he hears” indicates that a deaf person does not recite it, just as “when he opens his eyes” indicates that a blind person does not recite Poke'ach Ivrim. Even according to the variant reading “when he passes his hand over his eyes,” this applies only to one who has sight. Without emending the text, Magen Avraham's reasoning is that a blind person lacks the very phenomenon mentioned in Poke'ach Ivrim, and therefore does not recite it even though he benefits through others. A deaf person, by contrast, may recite Hanoten LaSechvi Binah because it is a blessing over a need of the world and he benefits through others, like the blessing Yotzer Or in siman 69. Moreover, according to the Rosh, the biblical meaning of sechvi is the heart. The blessing was fixed for the time when one hears the rooster because in Aramaic a rooster is called sechvi and because one then receives this benefit; a deaf person's heart nevertheless discerns the light. It is also possible that the word “deaf” in the cited text is a scribal error. Hagahot Maimoniyot holds that both a deaf person and a blind person recite the blessings because they benefit from them; only a traveler alone in the wilderness, who receives no such benefit, does not. The other view excludes a deaf person because he can never hear the rooster. Magen Avraham writes that authorities maintain these blessings were instituted for the needs of the world, but leaves the matter requiring study; in a case of uncertainty concerning a blessing, we rule leniently and do not recite it. Magen Avraham therefore holds that a blind person should not recite Poke'ach Ivrim but should recite Hanoten LaSechvi Binah. The first view might also have excluded Hanoten LaSechvi Binah because it concerns discerning the day, but Magen Avraham holds that a blind person does recite it: unlike a deaf person, he hears the rooster's discernment and also benefits through other people.
