(40) One may not carry. Although utensils that were in the middle house when Shabbos began may be carried into both courtyards, and utensils from houses in either courtyard may be carried into the middle house, utensils may not be carried from the houses of one courtyard to the other courtyard through the middle house, because the two courtyards did not make an eruv with each other. This applies where their two eruvin were placed in other houses in the courtyards. If both eruvin were placed in the middle house, everyone becomes joined through that house, and one may carry from one courtyard to the other, comparable to the case below in siman 378, seif 1. See Machatzis HaShekel.
(41) If there was a wall between two courtyards, etc. It makes no difference whether the wall is four handbreadths wide. Even a thin partition separates the courtyards when it is ten handbreadths high.
(42) Ten handbreadths high. A lower wall is not considered a separation. The area is treated as one courtyard, for which they make one eruv and not two separate eruvin.
(43) And he made upon it, etc. We rule that a partition combines with a mound to complete a height of ten handbreadths.
(44) They cannot, etc. The same applies where one courtyard is ten handbreadths higher than the other even without a partition.
(45) They may lower them into the courtyards. As ruled at the beginning of the siman, roofs and courtyards are one domain. The top of this wall is no more restrictive than a roof. This applies only to the courtyards, but not to the houses if the wall is four handbreadths wide.
(46) And lower them from it, etc. This applies whether each courtyard made its own eruv or neither made an eruv, because all courtyards are considered one domain, as stated above in seif 1.
(47) The wall is four handbreadths wide. Since it is four handbreadths wide, it is a significant place in its own right and is not subordinate to either courtyard. It is therefore forbidden to place objects upon it from the houses or to lower objects from it into the houses.
(48) If it is not four handbreadths wide, etc. An area narrower than four handbreadths is a makom petur. One may therefore place objects upon it from the houses and lower them from it into the houses.
(49) They should not transfer. One may not place an object from a house upon the wall and then lower it into the other courtyard, lest one come to transfer directly from the house to the other courtyard without placing it down in between. See above, siman 346, at the end of seif 1, where the Mechaber cites two opinions. The Rama's wording here indicates that he is stringent.
(50) It is permitted, even with utensils. The case is where that courtyard made an eruv for itself. If it did not, one may not carry from a house into the courtyard, and certainly not onto the partition.
(51) That were in the house when Shabbos began. For the residents of that courtyard, the top of the wall is considered like the ground of their courtyard, since it is not ten handbreadths high from their side and is convenient for their use.
(52) Even with utensils, etc. Since the residents of the first courtyard may carry from their houses onto the partition, because it is considered part of their courtyard, allowing the second courtyard to use the partition as well would place it under the control of both and would prohibit the first courtyard from carrying from its houses onto it, since each courtyard made its own eruv. Both sides therefore cannot use it. The right of use is given to the side for which its use is more convenient, namely the first courtyard, because the wall is lower on its side. Even HaOzer, in the name of Rashba, permits the second courtyard to use it for utensils that were in that courtyard when Shabbos began.
(53) Both may use it. For it is a makom petur.
