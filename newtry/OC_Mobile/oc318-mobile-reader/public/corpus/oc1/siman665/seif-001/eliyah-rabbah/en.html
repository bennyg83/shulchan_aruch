[1] And even if it became invalid, etc. This is the language of Beit Yosef: Orchot Chaim wrote that the Yerushalmi says an etrog that became invalid on the first day may be eaten. End quote. This is surprising: why is it not forbidden because it was set aside at twilight, etc.? End quote. Bach wrote that in order that the Yerushalmi not conflict with our Talmud, it appears that it agrees that if it became invalid after the mitzvah was performed, it is forbidden all seven days; rather, it speaks where it became invalid on the first day before he fulfilled the mitzvah, for then there is no muktzeh and it is mere designation. End quote. I wrote in Eliyahu Zuta that they did not examine the Yerushalmi itself. This is its language on the Mishnah that children eat their etrogim on the seventh day: "They said this only of a minor, but not of an adult. R. Avina said in the name of Rav: an etrog that became invalid on the first Yom Tov may be eaten. R. Yose said: there, he is not fit to fulfill with it, but others are fit to fulfill with it; here, neither he nor others can fulfill with it." End quote. It appears to me that its explanation is that it asks from an etrog that became invalid and is permitted, showing that we do not say it was set aside for the mitzvah for the whole day, only for its mitzvah; if so, why is an adult forbidden on the seventh day? The reason is that it was set aside for the entire day, as stated in Sukkah 47. It answers that on the seventh day, even though he has already fulfilled the mitzvah with it, nevertheless others who have not yet fulfilled it can still fulfill with it, so it is muktzeh. But when it became invalid, it is not fit at all for fulfilling the mitzvah, as it appears to me. The general rule is that the Yerushalmi itself asks Beit Yosef's question and answers it as I explained. Bach's explanation, that it became invalid before he fulfilled the mitzvah, is plainly untenable, for if so the Yerushalmi asks nothing. Recently the Taz was printed, and he wrote that the Yerushalmi holds like Reish Lakish, that an etrog on the seventh day is permitted because it was not set aside for the entire day, only for its mitzvah. This is very surprising, for the Yerushalmi explicitly forbids it on the seventh day; he too did not examine the source. As for the law, in Eliyahu Zuta I brought great proofs and demonstrations that the halachah is that it is kosher when it became invalid. I also found that Shibbolei HaLeket ruled so. There I wrote that one should not rely on me to be lenient against the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. But now I have seen that Sefer Amarkal, in the name of Sefer HaItim, also permits it when it became invalid, even though he forbids an etrog on the seventh day; he wrote the reason that it is comparable to oil in a lamp, which according to R. Shimon is forbidden only while the lamp is burning, and even according to the Rabbis, since it was not set aside because of the sanctity of the day but according to his view for its mitzvah. End quote. In any case, one may permit here when it became invalid even according to us, as the Taz wrote, and so it is implied in Hagahot Ashiri. Even though the Tur and Tzedah LaDerech imply that it is invalid, in any case on the eighth day one may be lenient; and even on the first Yom Tov it is proper to rely and be lenient in time of need. If it became invalid before the mitzvah was performed on the first Yom Tov, or even if it did not become invalid but he has another, Bach concludes that in practice it requires study; but Sheyarei Knesset HaGedolah ruled plainly that it is permitted. The Taz appears to hold that it is forbidden. It appears one should be lenient at least when it became invalid. See siman 638.
