(1) In which they have not bathed. Meaning, not even once. If they have bathed there, it thereafter has the full status of an old bathhouse, since there was both designation and an act.
(2) It is permitted to recite. Although regarding a beis hakisei we rule above, siman 83:2, that mere designation already prohibits recitation, a beis hakisei is different because it is more repulsive.
(3) And some are unclothed. Some authorities maintain that even if everyone is now clothed, or no one is present at all, nevertheless, since the room is designated for some people to be unclothed and others clothed, it has the status of a bathhouse. Others are lenient. According to them, in the room where the bath attendant lives, although people also stand there unclothed, it is permitted to speak words of Torah, recite Kiddush, and pray when no one is present. All this concerns the intermediate chamber. The inner chamber, where everyone stands unclothed and bathes, is more repulsive, and all agree that it has the status of a beis hakisei. It is forbidden even when no one is present. Even if that bathhouse is used only in summer and is closed during winter, it retains all the laws of a bathhouse then as well, because its designation has not yet been removed. If, however, a physical alteration was made to it, such as removing its oven or the like and converting it into a dwelling, one may place a mezuzah at its entrance and recite blessings and prayers there. The same rule applies to a former beis hakisei.
(4) But not, etc. Likewise, all blessings and words of Torah are forbidden there. The Taz implies that after the fact, one who recited a blessing there does not repeat it. Similarly, one who recited Krias Shema there does not repeat it, even if an unclothed person was then present in the intermediate chamber, provided that person was not directly opposite him during the recitation, or he turned his face and body away. Otherwise, he must repeat it because he recited opposite ervah, as stated by the Peri Megadim. Regarding a mikveh room, the Taz concludes that it has the same status as the intermediate chamber of a bathhouse. No blessing may be recited there except the blessing over immersion, which is necessarily recited there and is therefore treated like an after-the-fact situation. This applies only when the person reciting the blessing is not unclothed and does not stand facing unclothed people. Otherwise it is forbidden because of ervah, unless the person turns both face and body away. The same applies to a woman reciting the blessing in the mikveh room. The Peri Megadim writes that it is preferable for her to recite the blessing while in the water. See above, siman 74, seif katan 16, where it is written that she should preferably cloud the water with her feet so that her limbs are not visible through it. The reason is that the place within the water is more plausibly not considered like the intermediate chamber of a bathhouse. Plainly, even there she must turn her face and body away from unclothed women standing in the mikveh room while reciting the blessing, as is evident above in siman 79:2. According to the Taz, even when no unclothed person is present, no other blessing may be recited there according to those who are stringent regarding the intermediate chamber. The Magen Avraham above in siman 45, however, implies leniency because there is no filth there, and the Birkei Yosef rules likewise. In a mikveh into which hot water is poured, one might say that there is steam; nevertheless, it is possible that it lacks the degree of filth and steam found in a bathhouse. As for mikvaos located within an actual bathhouse, it is certainly forbidden to recite the blessing over immersion there. Even reciting it while in the water requires study, because the steam of a bathhouse is considerable and may prohibit recitation even in the water, as stated by the Peri Megadim. See also what he wrote on this matter in siman 45, Eshel Avraham.
(5) To think there. This is permitted even opposite unclothed people, because there is no prohibition against thinking Torah thoughts opposite ervah.
(6) Even the name Shalom is forbidden. Shalom is one of the names of the Holy One, blessed is He, as it is stated, “And he called it Hashem Shalom.” Likewise, one may not greet another person with “shalom” in filthy passageways, which have the full status of the inner chamber. If a person is named Shalom, some prohibit calling him by that name in a bathhouse except in a foreign-language form. Others permit it, because the speaker does not intend the concept of peace but merely to call that person by his name. In matters of Rabbinic law, one may follow the lenient opinion, and this is the common practice. A God-fearing person should nevertheless be stringent, since many later authorities prohibit it. He should swallow the final mem and say only “Shalo,” or pronounce it “Shalon” with a nun. In an ordinary letter as well, it is proper not to write shalom with the letter vav, but rather shalem, because letters are commonly discarded in refuse.
(7) To answer amen. This refers to responding to someone who recited a blessing outside or in another room. It appears that the prohibition applies specifically in the inner chamber, which is like a beis hakisei. In the intermediate chamber one may answer amen. If someone asks a halachic question in the inner chamber, one may not reply, “One may not answer in a bathhouse.” Similarly, if someone greets him there, he may not reply, “One may not respond in a bathhouse.” In either case, however, he may say, “This is a bathhouse,” because that statement does not even constitute thinking words of Torah. In the intermediate chamber, one may tell him, “One may not answer in a bathhouse.” Some are stringent even there, unless considerations of peaceful conduct require such a response. One should not drink in a bathhouse, since it is forbidden to recite a blessing there, and one may not drink without a blessing. The Peri Megadim concludes that one who wishes to drink should recite the blessing in the outer chamber, or in the bath attendant’s room when no unclothed people are present, or enter the water of the mikveh until it rises above his ervah. He should also cover his head, recite the blessing, and drink a small amount there with the intention of continuing to drink in the bathhouse. This is permitted only when the rooms are within one structure of the bathhouse. To recite the blessing entirely outside the bathhouse is forbidden. It is certainly forbidden to recite the blessing and drink nothing until entering the bathhouse, because one may not interrupt between the blessing and drinking. See above at the end of siman 62 in the Mishnah Berurah and Biur Halachah. The inner and intermediate chambers of a bathhouse are exempt from mezuzah. Likewise, if a bathhouse has a courtyard in front of it where some people stand unclothed, that courtyard is also exempt. The bath attendant’s dwelling requires a mezuzah because it is a proper room, but since unclothed people are commonly present there, the mezuzah must be covered. A malt house that is occasionally used as a bathhouse once or twice a year, or a drying house, requires a mezuzah, though some say the mezuzah should be covered. It is also permitted to pray there. A room containing bathtubs for bathing appears from the later authorities to have the status of the inner chamber because of the abundant steam and filth.
