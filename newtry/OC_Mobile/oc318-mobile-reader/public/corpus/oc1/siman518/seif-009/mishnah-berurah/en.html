(43) A house filled with fruit. Meaning, it was sealed on every side.
(44) Prepared. This excludes fruit that he placed there as long term storage, which would be muktzeh.
(45) And it was breached. This applies even if the breach occurred on Yom Tov. We do not say that since the house was sealed at bein hashmashos and the fruit could not then be taken because dismantling a structure was forbidden, the fruit became set aside for the entire day. This is not included in muktzeh, since the fruit was not set aside because of an intrinsic prohibition, but merely stood in a place from which it could not be retrieved. According to this reason, even if the house had previously been sound and it was not expected to become breached on its own, it is still permitted to take the fruit. The same applies to a person who has turnips buried in a pit and a non-Jew opened the pit on Yom Tov or Shabbos. The turnips are not muktzeh. However, if the non-Jew opened it for the sake of a Jew, even when the fruit belongs to the non-Jew, it is forbidden to eat or move it until after Shabbos or Yom Tov and the passage of b'chedi she'ya'asu. One must be careful about this during Pesach when a non-Jew brings turnips to a Jew. If the Jew knows that the non-Jew opened the pit that day, he may not buy from him. See Biur Halacha, which explains that one should rely on this leniency only in pressing circumstances.
(46) He may take from the place of the breach. He may not enlarge the breach in the house in order to take more fruit.
