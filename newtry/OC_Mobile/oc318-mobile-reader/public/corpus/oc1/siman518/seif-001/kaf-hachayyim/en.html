1) [Seif 1] "Since carrying was permitted for a need," etc. The same applies to kindling: since it was permitted for food preparation, it was also permitted for a non-food need, as stated above at the beginning of siman 511. Likewise the other melachot involved in food preparation, such as slaughtering, baking, and cooking. If one performs them for no need at all or for a weekday, some authorities hold that he nevertheless does not receive lashes, while others hold that these were permitted for a non-food purpose only when there is at least some need of that day, such as carrying a child or a lulav, or a mitzvah need. Where there is neither a mitzvah need nor any ordinary need that day, such as carrying stones, he receives lashes, as Maggid Mishneh writes in chapter 1, law 1. See also below, letter 12, and the beginning of siman 527. 2) There, "Since carrying was permitted for a need," etc. This applies even through a public domain, as explained in the Mishnah, Beitzah 12a. So write the Rosh there, Yam Shel Shlomo, first chapter of Beitzah, siman 34, and the other later authorities. 3) There, "such as a child," etc. Rabbeinu Chananel explains: a child to bring for brit milah; a lulav with which to fulfill the mitzvah; and a Torah scroll from which to read. Tosafos there writes that it is not specifically a child being brought for brit milah; the same applies when he is being carried for an outing. Rosh writes that if a person needs to go to the synagogue or take a walk for the rejoicing of Yom Tov and cannot leave the child alone at home, he may carry the child with him. Tur and Levush write likewise. Ran explains: either to bring him for brit milah or to take him outside for an outing, because this involves Yom Tov enjoyment. Yam Shel Shlomo likewise writes that the child mentioned may be carried even merely for an outing. Magen Avraham, Chemed Moshe, Shulchan Aruch HaRav, Chayei Adam, Mikraei Kodesh, and Mishnah Berurah write likewise. 4) There, "such as a child and a lulav," etc. This implies that carrying for another mitzvah that is not needed that day is forbidden. Bach; Knesset HaGedolah; Eliyah Rabbah; Mishbetzot Zahav. 5) There, "and a Torah scroll." The same applies to bringing sefarim to the synagogue and even returning them, because the Sages permitted the end of the matter for the sake of its beginning. Rosh, cited by Beit Yosef; Olat Shabbos; Eliyah Rabbah; Chemed Moshe; Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Mikraei Kodesh. This means that if one fears they will be stolen, he may return them. If there is no concern of theft, returning them is forbidden. Even when there is concern of theft, if they had already been left there for many days, one may not now return them. Yam Shel Shlomo; Olat Shabbos; Magen Avraham; Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Chayei Adam; Mikraei Kodesh. This applies when he does not intend to study from them again that day. If he wishes to recite songs and praises from machzorim on Yom Tov, however, he may always bring them. Chayei Adam; Mikraei Kodesh. The same applies to other sefarim: if he wishes to study them, it is permitted to carry them in every manner, as is implied by the halachic authorities. Mishbetzot Zahav explicitly writes that sefarim needed for one's fixed study are considered a need of that day. 5) Any carrying permitted for a Yom Tov need by analogy to food preparation is likewise permitted for jewelry because of the honor of Yom Tov. Rosh in the first chapter of Beitzah in the name of a responsum of the Geonim; Tur; Levush; Taz; Olat Shabbos; Chayei Adam; Mishnah Berurah. See above, siman 516, letter 21, and siman 303, letter 112. 6) There, "and utensils." {Rama: Which he needs somewhat.} This implies that according to the Shulchan Aruch it is permitted even when he does not need them at all. Although Beit Yosef appears uncertain whether some need is required, here the Shulchan Aruch writes without qualification in accordance with the implication of Rif and Rambam cited by Beit Yosef. Gra likewise explains that Rif, Rambam, and Shulchan Aruch permit even where there is no need at all, following the plain meaning of the Gemara's principle, "since carrying was permitted for a need," etc. Maamar Mordechai writes similarly, but afterward states that the Shulchan Aruch remained uncertain as in Beit Yosef and therefore did not decide the matter explicitly either way. Shulchan Gavoah writes that Rama's gloss merely explains the words of the Shulchan Aruch. Yad Aharon writes likewise. Thus the Shulchan Aruch can be read in either direction, but Rama definitively prohibits carrying unless there is at least some need. Levush, Yam Shel Shlomo, Taz, Chemed Moshe, Shulchan Aruch HaRav, Chayei Adam, Mikraei Kodesh, Ben Ish Chai, and Mishnah Berurah rule likewise. 7) There, "and utensils." One may carry utensils on Yom Tov from a city to a village within the techum for the needs of a mitzvah meal where the host did not prepare all the utensils he needs. Responsa Chasam Sofer, siman 147; Pischei Teshuvah. 8) There, Rama: "Which he needs somewhat." Regarding carrying knives, Yam Shel Shlomo writes that in his youth he saw people careful not to carry knives when going for a walk outside the city on Yom Tov, because after eating the knives were no longer needed that day. Within the city but outside the eruv they did carry them. Taz disagrees and writes that knives are considered a food need throughout the entire day, because if one happens upon a fruit he can eat it with the knife. Eliyah Rabbah agrees, as does Shulchan Aruch HaRav. Magen Avraham, however, cites Yam Shel Shlomo without disagreement, implying that he accepts that view, and Chayei Adam writes likewise. Nevertheless, even according to Taz, if one knows with certainty that he will not need the knife on this outing, such as when going to the synagogue or the like, he may not carry it. Shulchan Aruch HaRav and Mishnah Berurah write likewise. 9) A timepiece, however, may be carried according to all opinions because it is needed at all times. These laws apply to women as well as men. Ben Ish Chai, Parashat Bamidbar, letter 9.
