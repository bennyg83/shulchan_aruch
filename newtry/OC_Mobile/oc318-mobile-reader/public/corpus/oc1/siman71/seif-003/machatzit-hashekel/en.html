Seif katan 3: He is obligated, etc. The Bach rules, etc. The Beit Yosef raised a contradiction between the Tur's statements. Here in Orach Chaim the Tur writes that toward evening, since he can go beyond the boundary after nightfall, he is exempt, implying that he is exempt even if he does not actually go. In Yoreh Deah, siman 381, the Tur writes, 'if he goes beyond the boundary after nightfall,' implying that if he does not do so, he is obligated. The Bach resolves this and rules that the passage there concerns other mitzvos, which do not require concentration. Therefore, if he does not go beyond the boundary, although he is somewhat preoccupied because he could do so, he remains obligated. Here, however, the discussion concerns Krias Shema, which is recited with awe and requires additional concentration. The Tur therefore holds that even if he does not actually go beyond the boundary, since he could do so and is somewhat preoccupied, he is exempt.
This requires study, for the Beit Yosef wrote, etc. The Beit Yosef wrote the following to resolve his aforementioned question, and the Magen Avraham therefore adds a further difficulty. With respect to Krias Shema, it is entirely inapplicable to speak of exemption or obligation, whichever way one approaches it, as the Magen Avraham writes. Rather, the Tur's purpose is to teach that this rule should not be extended to all the mitzvos in which an onen is obligated throughout Shabbos and Yom Tov, for he is exempt from them while going beyond the boundary after nightfall. He therefore writes that the obligation extends only until evening. Since he did not write this because it was necessary for the laws of Krias Shema, he was not concerned to clarify his wording and relied upon what he wrote in the Tur, Yoreh Deah, which is the principal location of this law. These are the Beit Yosef's words. If so, one may say that this is also the Beit Yosef's intention here in the Shulchan Aruch. This is likewise written in Responsa Shevus Yaakov, part 1, siman 8; see there. Nevertheless, the Magen Avraham challenges the Bach, because the Bach's resolution cannot be accepted, namely, that this passage refers to Krias Shema. With respect to Krias Shema, there is no case, since it was not customary to recite it during the day. If the passage refers to the evening Krias Shema, whose time according to our practice begins while it is still daytime, he should nevertheless be exempt even if he does not go beyond the boundary after nightfall.
Nevertheless, on Motzaei Shabbos everyone, etc. Meaning, on a weekday permission to recite the evening Krias Shema before nightfall was granted only because of the burden upon the congregation, and they rely upon the Krias Shema recited before bed, as explained below in siman 236. An individual was therefore not permitted to recite it before nightfall. Certainly on Motzaei Shabbos, when even the congregation recites it at night, its time begins only after the emergence of the stars, and the Magen Avraham's question against the Bach returns. As a matter of practical law, the Taz writes that in such a case, if he will not go beyond the boundary, he should recite Krias Shema before nightfall as on a weekday. Responsa Shevus Yaakov, part 1, siman 8, cites a certain authority who ruled like the Taz and further permitted him to pray the Motzaei Shabbos prayer on Shabbos after plag haminchah, as stated below in siman 293, that in pressing circumstances one may pray the Motzaei Shabbos prayer after plag haminchah and also recite Havdalah. That responsum disagrees with this authority. Even if we say that he should recite Krias Shema, it is certainly improper for him to pray and recite Havdalah, because this combines two contradictory positions. You obligate him in prayer on the ground that it is still Shabbos and aninus has not taken effect, yet how can he then pray a weekday prayer? If it is a weekday, aninus applies and he may not pray. These are the responsum's words. In my humble opinion, I do not understand this, because as long as he may not attend to the deceased's needs, aninus does not apply, and the reason is not the sanctity of Shabbos. Even after he prays a weekday prayer and recites Havdalah, he remains forbidden to perform melachah until night, as stated below in siman 293. Thus, although he prays a weekday prayer, aninus still does not apply, since he is not yet able to attend to the deceased's needs. The Taz further writes that even on a weekday, if a son has someone else who will attend to the needs of his father or mother, he should go to the synagogue and recite Kaddish, because this honors his father or mother. Eliyah Rabbah challenges the Taz from his own words, for in Yoreh Deah the Taz writes specifically that he should recite Kaddish on Shabbos, implying that he should not do so on a weekday. In my humble opinion, this is no difficulty. There the Taz discusses the usual case, in which the son has no one else to attend to their needs, for who will be more concerned to attend to them than the son? He therefore writes specifically Shabbos and Yom Tov. Regarding the principal ruling, however, the Nekudas HaKesef and Avodas HaGershuni disagree with the Taz, because Kaddish was instituted to save the deceased from Gehinnom, and before burial there is not yet any judgment in Gehinnom. The aforementioned Shevus Yaakov agrees with the Taz, arguing that, on the contrary, the remedy should precede the affliction, just as prayer should precede affliction. Nevertheless, that responsum concludes that he had not seen people follow this practice, though if an authority ruled that the son should recite Kaddish, the ruling is not reversed. See there further.
