And he prays — in Avnei Nezer; and immediately when they throw [earth], etc. And it appears that in a place where there is a holy society and the deceased is handed to bearers — behold, the law of onenut ceases from him, as explained in Yoreh De'ah, siman 341, that he is permitted to eat meat and drink wine; and if so, it is obvious that he also blesses and prays, even though mourning has not yet begun until they return and say that he was buried, as explained in siman 376. Nevertheless, since in any case the law of onenut is not here, properly the obligation of Shema and prayer applies to him. And again I found that so in Be'er Heitev, and he brought proof from Maharikash, and wrote that so too Mahari Ayash; and there he also wrote that although mourning did not begin until the burial was finished, and for another city until they return, etc. — nevertheless onenut is lighter than mourning. However, if he goes with the deceased on a ship or in a wagon, he has the law of onenut; and see there why it suffices until they begin to throw [earth] (and this is when he was not handed to bearers) — onenut is lighter, see there. And see in Eliyah Rabbah that until half an hour after midday prayer is permitted. And see Yoreh De'ah, siman 341, that if the time passed one does not complete the prayer. And in Machatzis HaShekel in the name of Zera Emes he wrote that his view for halacha and practice is that when the mourner returns from the house of mourning after a third of the day, he does not say even the prayer of eighteen [blessings], only it is appropriate that he read Shema alone without blessings, to accept upon himself the yoke of the kingdom of heaven, etc. And see in responsa Devar Mitzvah, volume 3, siman 13, that he may pray after a third of the day when praying out of its time. And also his view that those three blessings — that He did not make me, etc., and the blessing on the Torah — are incumbent upon a person all day; and therefore after burial, at whatever time it may be, he is obligated to say them — so too the Shabbos Shel Pesach in his name.
