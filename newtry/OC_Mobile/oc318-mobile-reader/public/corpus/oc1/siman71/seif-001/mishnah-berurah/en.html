1. For whom he is obligated. These are the seven close relatives: his father, mother, son, daughter, brother, sister, and wife.
2. Does not rest upon him. For example, his married sister died, and her husband is obligated to attend to her burial.
3. Is exempt, etc. If he recited Krias Shema, he has not fulfilled his obligation and must recite it again after the burial. [Novellae of Rabbi Akiva Eiger.]
4. And from prayer. He is likewise exempt from all blessings, including blessings over enjoyment, and from all mitzvos stated in the Torah. If he wishes to eat bread, although he need not recite Hamotzi, he must nevertheless wash his hands without reciting the blessing over washing. [Birkei Yosef, Yoreh Deah 341.] If there is a burial society in the city whose designated bearers remove the deceased, and after the relatives have attended to the deceased's needs they entrust the deceased to them for burial, the relatives become obligated in all mitzvos immediately upon entrusting the deceased to them, even before the deceased is removed from the house, because the burden no longer rests upon them. Accordingly, onenim do not act properly when they wait to pray until after the burial. As soon as they have reached an agreement with the chevrah kadisha concerning the burial place and have paid all expenses, including the shrouds, they are obligated to pray. Sometimes, however, they have not yet agreed where the deceased should be buried, which pertains to the deceased's honor, or the eulogies are still being delivered. During that time they remain exempt, but immediately afterward prayer becomes permitted and obligatory. In a place without a burial society designated for this purpose, however, even if another person will attend to the burial on his behalf, aninus continues until the deceased is buried and earth is cast upon him. As soon as they begin casting earth upon the deceased, the mourner should leave for another area of the cemetery and recite Krias Shema and pray there. If aninus continues until close to the end of the time for Krias Shema, which extends until one quarter of the day according to the proportional hours of that day, and praying in the regular order would cause the time for Krias Shema to pass, he should recite Krias Shema without its blessings. He should then pray in the regular order and recite Krias Shema again with its blessings. If aninus continues beyond four hours, the Magen Avraham holds that he should not recite Bircas HaShachar, meaning the blessings before Baruch She'amar, even if he then has time, because he was exempt during the principal time of their obligation, which is the morning. Others disagree, and because of doubt concerning blessings, one should be lenient and not recite them, except for Bircas HaTorah and the blessings, 'Who has not made me a non-Jew,' 'Who has not made me a slave,' and 'Who has not made me a woman.' According to the Magen Avraham as well, he may recite these blessings. He should not put on tefillin; see above, siman 38, seif 5. He should pray only the eighteen blessings of the Amidah. Regarding the blessings of Krias Shema, the Shulchan Aruch above, siman 58, seif 6, implies that their time has already passed. According to what I wrote above at the end of siman 58 in the Mishnah Berurah, however, he may recite even the blessings of Krias Shema, since the time passed because of circumstances beyond his control. According to all opinions, it is possible that he may recite Pesukei DeZimrah; see above, siman 52, Novellae of Rabbi Akiva Eiger. If four hours, which constitute one third of the day, have not yet elapsed, but the end of that period is near, and praying in the regular order would cause the proper time for prayer to pass so that he would have to pray the Amidah after one third of the day, he should omit portions of Pesukei DeZimrah in the manner explained above in siman 52 regarding one who arrived late at the synagogue. He should not omit any part of Krias Shema or its blessings, even though this will force him to pray the Amidah after one third of the day. [Derech HaChaim; see there for his reason. This requires consideration in light of what I wrote initially.] Derech HaChaim further writes that the blessings, 'Who has not made me a non-Jew,' 'a slave,' or 'a woman,' and Bircas HaTorah may be recited throughout the day. Even if the burial occurs after midday, he should recite them. The eighteen blessings of the Amidah, however, may be prayed only until midday and no later. He also need not make up that prayer during the next prayer period. Thus, if he did not pray Shacharis, he does not make it up at Minchah. This differs from one who forgot or was prevented from praying Shacharis, who makes it up at Minchah. In that case, he was genuinely obligated but failed to pray because of some circumstance, whereas here he was exempt by law. The same applies to Minchah and Maariv. This applies only if the deceased lay before him from the beginning of the prayer's obligatory period until its time ended, such as where the deceased lay before him from dawn, when the entire eastern horizon became illuminated, until after midday. If the death occurred only after the obligation had already begun, such as after the entire eastern horizon became illuminated, then even if the burial did not occur until after midday, he makes up Shacharis at Minchah. Since the obligation had taken effect when the time for prayer began, even though there was not enough time to complete the prayer, he must later make it up at Minchah. It is possible that in this case he is also obligated to recite Bircas HaShachar if he did not recite them upon arising in the morning, since he was obligated when their time began. See above, siman 52, Biur Halachah.
5. To be stringent. Meaning, this concerns a case where he has no one else to attend to the deceased's needs. Alternatively, it concerns every case, because he was prohibited from reciting out of respect for the deceased, so that people should not say that this deceased person is insignificant in the onen's eyes and that he is not distressed by the death. This is the Mechaber's own principal ruling. His later statement, 'we do not prevent him,' means that nevertheless we cannot prevent one who acts otherwise, because he has an authority upon whom to rely where another person will attend to the burial needs on his behalf.
6. On his behalf. Meaning, even if the obligation to bury the deceased rests upon him, but he has other people who will attend to it for him, and he wishes to be stringent with himself, we do not prevent him. This is the view of the Raavyah. The Maharshal writes in responsum 70 that nowadays the practice does not follow the Raavyah.
7. Siman 341. There it is ruled that he may not be stringent under any circumstances, and this is the principal halachah. [Later authorities.] Nevertheless, a son who is an onen for his father, where others are attending to the deceased without him, may go to the synagogue to recite Kaddish for him, because this honors his father and is permitted according to all opinions. This is certainly so on Shabbos and Yom Tov. This applies specifically where there are no other mourners, but where other mourners are present, he should not do so. [Peri Megadim and Derech HaChaim.]
