Seif 1. One who had a relative die, etc. NB: By law, if he was an onen until after chatzos, it is proven from all poskim that after chatzos is not prayer time and prayer does not apply except as makeup — therefore since at the time of obligation he was exempt, makeup does not apply. Now a book was newly printed called Mishkenos Yaakov; I saw in its Orach Chayyim section that he thought to disagree with this from what the Torah obligated to bring Pesach Sheni — and per the view that the second is makeup of the first, how could the Torah say that those who were impure from contact with a corpse should do the second, behold they were engaged in a mitzvah and exempt from the mitzvah, yet they are obligated on the second? From this he proved that likewise an onen even after chatzos is obligated in makeup, etc. I wonder how it entered his mind to disagree with the Rosh, even if he had solid proofs, and especially to obligate prayer — there is concern of a blessing in vain; and especially the proof is not a proof. First, what proof is this? Behold there are views: one holds the bearers of Joseph's coffin were the impure ones; one said Mishael and Eltzafan were — and it is explicit in a baraisa that per these views they could already have purified themselves but did not; if so they erred and did not purify, and one may say permitted inadvertence — in any case exempt does not apply since they could purify, and it is proven explicitly in Shas Sukkah 25b that according to this view there is no proof at all that one engaged in a mitzvah is exempt from a mitzvah — only R' Yehuda holds there they were impure from a mitzvah. If so one may say R' Yehuda holds they were impure from a mitzvah — he holds like the view that the second festival is independent; and the view that it is makeup of the first holds like the view that Joseph's coffin bearers or Mishael and Eltzafan were the impure ones. And further, even if R' Yehuda holds they were impure from a mitzvah, it has no similarity to the Rosh's words at all — the Rosh discusses when at the time of the other's obligation he is engaged in this mitzvah and the mitzvah itself exempts him from the other; this is called real exemption and makeup does not apply. But there he was not at the time of Pesach obligation; he was exempted from the force of engaging with another corpse — for this reason he is not exempted, for it is in his power to appoint an agent to sacrifice for him. Also behold then they were not engaged with a corpse at all and were not exempted except from the force of becoming impure from a corpse, and an impure person may not eat Pesach — if so impurity is what pushes him off from Pesach. And what of that the impurity came to him initially through engaging in a mitzvah — in the end now the mitzvah does not exempt him, only impurity; and regarding impurity it is like compulsion. And what the sages learned from this that one engaged in a mitzvah is exempt from a mitzvah — that refers to then, at the hour they engaged with the corpse how they became impure through it; behold they knew they could not make Pesach and that one engaged in a mitzvah is exempt from a mitzvah — but in any case since then the time of Pesach obligation had not arrived, as the Gemara says there explicitly, and at the time of Pesach obligation he was not engaged in a mitzvah and is not exempted except from the force of impurity; and regarding impurity it was like compulsion, therefore he is obligated in Pesach Sheni. And proof from the verse itself that said "and they could not make the Pesach" — if they were completely exempt it would not be fitting to say "and they could not," but it should have said "and they did not make the Pesach"; and from that it says "and they could not" it implies they were compelled that they could not. And one may infer from "and they could not make the Pesach on that day" — meaning that initially they could certainly have done it, for it was in their hands not to become impure, only they were exempted because one engaged in a mitzvah is exempt from a mitzvah; only on that day when they were no longer engaged in the mitzvah, now on that day they were considered compelled. And in any case the Gemara's inference is fine that they could have done it immediately — otherwise it would suffice to say "and they could not make the Pesach today" or "now"; and from that it says at length "on that day" you learn two things. And in any case the Rosh's words are firm: wherever one is engaged in a mitzvah, such as an onen who must now engage in burying the deceased and the mitzvah itself exempts him, or one engaged in communal needs — this is called real exemption and makeup does not apply; so it is correct, understand carefully. When I examined that book I saw [in siman 88] in a gloss that he wanted to bring proof for daily prayer from what we hold that in a synagogue one is exempt from mezuzah, and also from that he does not become impure with negaim. His words are very astonishing: first, their main debate is whether prayer is a Torah obligation or not — but as optional conduct we certainly find prayer in Torah with the patriarchs and Moshe Rabbeinu, and in Scripture "and they shall pray to Him in their land," and much of this, and David's prayer. And further, behold on the contrary, if prayer is also not from the Torah, all the more so it was not a residence at all and there is no applicability of house to require mezuzah or impurity with negaim, since it is not a house at all — only there was an assumption that since for us prayer is an obligation, therefore it should be considered a house and require mezuzah and become impure with negaim; therefore it teaches us that nevertheless he is exempt, for prayer does not make a residence; so it is correct, understand carefully. I saw in the siddur of the gaon of Lissa, may his righteous memory be blessed, in the law of an onen, that he wrote if, heaven forbid, another died after day had already broken and prayer obligation applied to him, he is afterward obligated to complete with Minchah; his proof from a lame person who is exempt and need not make up — and if so, for a lame person it is explained in many places Chagigah chapter 2 that if obligation fell upon him he is afterward obligated in makeup. In my humble opinion, with forgiveness of the gaon's honor, there is no proof: granted regarding a lame person the change is from his body becoming lame — this is considered only compulsion and not exemption, only that he is compelled and cannot go, therefore he remains within obligation and when he can go afterward he must complete. But an onen who can recite and has no bodily change, only exemption for another reason — here once exempted, exempted, and there is no more makeup. And a similar distinction is explained in Taz [siman 108] regarding one engaged in communal needs; and although Magen Avraham [siman 93] disagrees with Taz — that is because whoever engages in communal needs also has no bodily change, therefore he too is called exempt — but the essence of the distinction is correct. And what the Rosh brought proof from a lame person — that is to the contrary to exempt him: a fortiori from a lame person who is only compulsion and exempt from makeup, all the more an onen; but to obligate him there is no proof from a lame person, for a lame person is compulsion. And further, granted there the entire festival is like one day and is considered one extended day and counted as one time; but regarding prayer, since the day itself is divided into several parts — evening, morning, and afternoon — each is its own time, and behold we hold there is makeup only in the adjacent prayer, but afterward makeup does not apply — it is proven that whenever another time interrupted in the middle, makeup no longer applies. And behold in Chagigah, if he did not complete on the first and second days he can complete all seven and it is not considered an interruption — if so here too on that day, since a time interrupted when he was truly exempt, afterward makeup does not apply; therefore it seems he is completely exempt, as in my humble opinion — correct and it fits well, understand carefully. If burial was on the second day, etc., whether exempt from tefillin — the view of Maharitatz brought in Be'er Heitev that he is obligated in tefillin, and the Ateres Zekeinim disagrees. Now it seems to me a clear proof like the Ateres Zekeinim from Yerushalmi Berachos chapter 3, which challenges there for the view that on the second day he is exempt: if on the second day he is exempt, the deceased is before him — why challenge? If we say like Maharitatz that on the burial day he is obligated, then necessarily what it says "on the second day exempt" means from the day of death — then why challenge? Behold one can say we find the law of one whose dead is before him, meaning like the third day from death, such as if he was not buried until the third day — and thus from the day of death he was obligated and from onan status he is exempt. And necessarily it is proven that even when it is not the day of death it is also considered a bitter day and we count from burial day; therefore it challenges well: even if it were the third day from death, nevertheless if he will be buried today he is exempt today, and tomorrow even though the deceased is not before him — then "before him" applies, and it fits well. This is a clear proof in my humble opinion, understand carefully. Regarding an onen who after the fact fulfilled others in some mitzvah whether they fulfilled or not — see my responsum to Yoreh De'ah siman 341 what I wrote on this. Also regarding if an onen ate or relieved himself and afterward was buried, whether he must bless Birkas HaMazon if food was not digested or Asher Yatzar — see the siddur of the gaon, may his glory be exalted, of Lissa, and what I challenged on him in that responsum there, understand carefully. Regarding one who had a relative die whom he is obligated to mourn, even if it is not incumbent upon him to bury him, he is exempt from Kriat Shema and prayer, etc. See Yoreh De'ah siman 341; see Taz who wrote that rabbinic doubt applies only after the fact and in another matter — his intent is that we hold one does not make rabbinic doubt ab initio, and so here too. But in truth there is no proof from this that the reason one does not make rabbinic doubt ab initio is because one does not nullify prohibition ab initio, as implied from the Taz's words; also here where he wrote "in another matter" — his intent is as written in Yoreh De'ah siman 399 and siman 334, that even without intent to nullify the prohibition one does not nullify except in another matter there — so too here. But according to what is written in Yoreh De'ah siman 410 and Shulchan Aruch Orach Chayyim siman 128, that although one does not make rabbinic doubt and safeik safeka ab initio, that is only in optional matters, as one does not nullify ab initio — so too one does not make doubt ab initio; but regarding a mitzvah matter, since it is permitted to nullify ab initio, as written in Shulchan Aruch Orach Chayyim siman 426, that like a mitzvah not given for benefit one may nullify ab initio — so too regarding a mitzvah one may make safeik safeka and rabbinic doubt ab initio, and so here. And in our case, what the Shulchan Aruch's words contradict each other — it seems one should distinguish: where he was already obligated in this before his relative died and afterward another relative died, since obligation already fell and there is doubt whether he is now exempt — although it is rabbinic doubt, he should be stringent and recite; but where his relative died before obligation fell upon him and there is doubt whether he became obligated — in this he may not be stringent from doubt. And we already find similar among poskim who distinguish regarding doubt of blessing: where he was certainly obligated and there is doubt whether he was exempted — he blesses too; but where the doubt is whether he became obligated — he acts and does not bless. See Peri Megadim siman 67 in Eshel Avraham — so too one may say in our case: in Yoreh De'ah it discusses if his relative died before fulfillment of the mitzvah fell upon him and similar, and doubt whether he became obligated — he may not be stringent; but here it discusses that he was already obligated before becoming an onen — here he may be stringent. And one may distinguish in what we hold regarding "exempt and performing" called a commoner: where he was exempt from the start and wants to be stringent he is a commoner; but where he was already obligated and obligation fell to do it, only afterward he came to exemption — then if he wants to be stringent upon himself he is permitted and is not called a commoner. As in my humble opinion — correct to decide the law. But the custom is not so, as the later authorities wrote; nevertheless to settle the Shulchan Aruch's words one may say so, understand carefully and it fits well. See Magen Avraham here who wrote regarding Birchas HaShachar he does not bless since at the time of its obligation he was exempt. I saw in responsa Zichron Yitzchak who wrote in the name of his father the gaon, may his righteous memory be blessed, that on Yom Tov, since at night onan status does not apply as the Moharam of Lublin wrote, and since it is explained above that before daybreak he can bless them — therefore he became obligated in them before daybreak; therefore even if he did not bless then, he blesses after the deceased was buried, for it is not fitting to say that at the time of his obligation he was exempt, etc., and this is correct and he aimed well. But one must add a condition: specifically if he rose from his bed that night before daybreak — then he became obligated immediately before daybreak; therefore even if he did not bless then he is obligated to bless after burial. But if he did not rise until day had broken — then since he did not awaken until daybreak he was not obligated then at all and obligation did not fall until he rose from his bed, and then it was already daybreak and he was immediately exempt and he does not bless afterward since at the time of his obligation he was exempt. There is a distinction on Yom Tov between rising before daybreak or not; so it is correct, understand carefully. It further seems to me a new law: even for those who hold in Shulchan Aruch here that an onen may not be stringent upon himself — that is only regarding a mitzvah that has an action recognizable to all that he does for the sake of mitzvah; there the Yerushalmi's reason of honor for the deceased applies. But in something without action — such as if the onen stands and hears the shaliach tzibbur blow shofar on Rosh Hashana or read the megillah — he may intend to fulfill and is exempt thereby, for here no prohibition applies: from the side of preoccupation with the deceased behold he has someone to carry his burden, and the prohibition is only from honor for the deceased. And where there is no action, only intent — in what is it recognizable that he intends for mitzvah or not, and in what is disgrace to the deceased applicable through intent alone? Therefore here he may be stringent upon himself. Do not challenge from the Rosh's language in Berachos chapter 3 who brings proof that on Yom Tov onan status does not apply, from the Rambam who was an onen on Rosh Hashana and commanded to blow shofar before him to fulfill for him, etc. — what proof? For hearing he may be stringent upon himself. But one may say: there, since he said explicitly to fulfill for him, therefore through that statement it was disgrace to the deceased and is like an actual action. But where he did not appoint an agent explicitly, only heard the shaliach tzibbur blowing who intends to fulfill for everyone — any person can intend to fulfill thereby, and he is not obligated to return and blow when the deceased is buried. And do not say: since he cannot appoint an agent explicitly, how can the shaliach tzibbur fulfill for him — since every fulfillment is only through agency, and since he is not in the category of explicit agency and cannot make an agent, this agent is not made automatically. But is he not in the category of agency? Behold he could actually appoint an agent, only from honor for the deceased it is forbidden — and this is only from the prohibition of maris ayin alone; therefore where there is no concern of maris ayin and disgrace, it is truly permitted. And further, behold regarding one under a vow of benefit we say in Nedarim 36b that to make an agent explicitly he cannot, but to say "whoever wishes to contribute may come and contribute" he can say — so too here, even though he cannot make an agent explicitly, nevertheless he can intend to fulfill through hearing from the shaliach tzibbur; but to say explicitly to a person to fulfill for him is forbidden; so it is correct in my view. But see Magen Avraham siman 496 seif kaf ches — it does not imply so. But one may distinguish: here he may be stringent, nevertheless after burial he is not exempted thereby since he became a real obligated party, and initially he heard when he was exempt by law — similar to what the Rambam said regarding eating matzah in his childhood because he ate when he was exempt; so too one may say here. And with this the Rosh's proofs fit better than from that Rambam cited, for there he sought to fulfill for him explicitly to exempt him — understand carefully. Regarding an onen who fulfills others' obligations — see what I wrote in responsum to Yoreh De'ah siman 341; see Peri Megadim on Orach Chayyim in the general introduction where he was uncertain about this there, understand carefully.
