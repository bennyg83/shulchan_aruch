(1) [Se'if 1] One whose relative died, for whom he is obligated to mourn, etc. See Yoreh De'ah 374:4 for those relatives over whom one is obligated to mourn.
(2) There: Even if the burial is not incumbent upon him, etc. For example, his married sister died and her husband attends to her burial, or his brother died and has sons obligated to bury him; he is nevertheless exempt [Tur, Beis Yosef, and Levush, unlike Rabbeinu Tam]. Even if the relative was in another city and he was notified before burial, he is exempt, as Maran rules in Beis Yosef and Yoreh De'ah 341:1. We follow Maran's rulings, unlike Bach, who permits one in another city. Maamar Mordechai rejects Bach and Pri Chadash; Yafeh LaLev and Shulchan Aruch HaRav rule likewise.
(2) There: Even if it is not incumbent upon him, etc. Rosh writes that before burial he is also forbidden meat and wine, even when the burial is not incumbent upon him [Olas Tamid].
(3) There: Even if it is not incumbent upon him, etc. The manuscript Chamudei Daniel writes: if one's relative died before he prayed and others can bury the deceased, he should not be told, so that he may pray. If he must attend to the burial, he must be told [Pischei Teshuvah, Yoreh De'ah 341:8].
(4) There: He is exempt from Keri'as Shema and prayer. As soon as they begin casting earth upon the deceased, he should recite Keri'as Shema and pray; Mahari Weil acted so. Magen Avraham holds that before midday he may still pray Shacharis, as in siman 89; several authorities agree. Zera Emes disagrees and rules in practice that if the mourner returns from the cemetery after the first third of the day, he should not even recite the Amidah, but should recite Keri'as Shema alone without its blessings to accept the yoke of Heaven; this was the custom in Modena. Machazik Berachah, Shaliach Tzibbur, Shaarei Teshuvah, Beis Oved, and Chesed LaAlafim cite or follow this view. He need not make up the missed prayer at the next service, since he was exempt when its obligation applied [Yoreh De'ah 341:2; Eliyah Rabbah; Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Shaarei Teshuvah; Chesed LaAlafim]. See above, siman 58, paragraph 25.
(4) Magen Avraham writes there that he should not recite the morning blessings afterward, since he was exempt when their obligation arose, like Havdalah in Yoreh De'ah 341:2. Eliyah Rabbah and Olas Tamid cite him. Shulchan Aruch HaRav likewise says that even if burial occurs before the time for Keri'as Shema and prayer passes, he does not make up the blessings, whose obligation arose when he got out of bed; reciting them would be a blessing in vain. Responsa Devar Moshe III 13 agrees generally but distinguishes the three blessings 'Who did not make me a gentile,' 'a slave,' or 'a woman,' and the Torah blessings, which are incumbent all day and must be recited after burial. Levush Serad and Machatzis HaShekel imply that Magen Avraham applies only to the view that one blesses these blessings solely upon personally experiencing their benefits. According to the view in siman 46:8 that they were instituted for the world's normal order and are recited even without personal benefit, they may be recited after midday. Pri Megadim, Nehar Shalom, Maamar Mordechai (though he leaves it requiring study) and Mayim Chaim agree; the latter records that this was practiced with Maharit Algazi's approval. Maaseh Rav of the Gra says one who omitted the morning blessings before prayer may recite them all day and even at night before sleep, except the Torah blessing if Ahavah Rabbah exempted it and Elokai Neshamah if Mechayeh HaMeisim exempted it. Maamar Mordechai is certain they may be said through the first three hours, the ordinary rising time even of kings, but questions later. Derech HaChaim rules that if aninus extended beyond four hours he omits the blessings preceding Keri'as Shema, since their Talmudic times relate to rising; even though they address the world's needs and are said after staying awake all night, that extends only through the first third of the day. The Torah blessing and the three identity blessings may be said all day, even after midday following burial. Shenos Chaim rules that one who did not bless by midday may no longer do so, apparently because the blessings were instituted while the day's strength is still rising, before the shadows of evening incline. Zera Emes permits them throughout the day, as at the end of siman 52. The plain-law authorities thus give three limits: the first third of the day, midday, or the entire day. We do not follow Magen Avraham's ruling that an onen omits them even when burial occurs before the times for Keri'as Shema and prayer, because above, siman 46, paragraph 49, we followed the Arizal and later authorities that they address the world's normal order. He may therefore say them if burial occurs while others may still say them. Yet Shaar HaKavanos teaches that HaNosen LaYaef Koach is one of the eighteen blessings instituted before prayer and that these blessings correspond to Zeir Anpin of Asiyah; Pri Etz Chaim and Nehar Shalom likewise imply they have no place after prayer, once the worlds of Beriah, Yetzirah, and Asiyah have been rectified and elevated to Atzilus and mochin drawn through prayer. Therefore, if he has delayed but not yet prayed, since prayer may be recited until midday he may also recite the morning blessings before it. The same applies to an onen whose relative was buried before midday. According to Zera Emes and those who allow prayer only through the first third of the day, the blessings likewise extend only that far. An ordinary person who forgot them before prayer may not be prevented from saying them afterward if still within the first third of the day, but not later, because of the dispute and the principle of doubt concerning blessings. The Arizal's rule that they address the world's normal order and must be said daily overrides doubt only when one stayed awake all night or did not remove his head covering and similar cases, not regarding saying them at any time, for their time is before prayer. The Torah blessing, however, may be recited at any time of day if omitted, because it contains two intentions, as explained above in siman 46, paragraphs 29 and 9.
(4) There: He is exempt from Keri'as Shema, etc. He is likewise exempt from every mitzvah stated in the Torah, as the Gemara says explicitly [Olas Tamid].
(5) There: He is exempt from Keri'as Shema and prayer. Where designated bearers remove the deceased, and after the relatives complete the burial preparations they hand the deceased over to those bearers for burial, the relatives may eat meat and drink wine from the moment of transfer, even before removal from the house, since the deceased is no longer incumbent upon them [Yoreh De'ah 341:3]. Birkei Yosef writes that they may likewise bless and pray. The Yerushalmi permits meat and wine once a person who died in captivity is handed to the bearers, for aninus has ended; Maggid Mishneh at the beginning of Mi SheMeiso and the Bavli imply the same. Maharikash extends the rule to all other laws of mourning, and Shevet Yehudah agrees. Shaliach Tzibbur and Shaarei Teshuvah cite them. Mishbetzos Zahav likewise permits prayer and Keri'as Shema, though without tefillin.
(6) There: Even if he wishes to be stringent, etc., he may not. If one began praying or reciting Birkas HaMazon and then became an onen, whether he must stop or may complete the prayer or blessing is discussed at length by Birkei Yosef, who cites a dispute and ultimately distinguishes cases without deciding. Shaliach Tzibbur likewise debates the issue without decision. Beis Oved rules like Maharad Pardo that he must stop. I say that one who wishes not to stop should not be discouraged, since he has authorities upon whom to rely, provided he directs his heart to Heaven and can compose himself to concentrate on his prayer or blessing. I later saw that Chesed LaAlafim rules likewise.
(7) There: If another person can attend to matters for him, etc., one should not prevent him. Maran rules without qualification in Yoreh De'ah 341:1; by then adding, 'Some say he may not,' he implies that under the primary ruling one should not prevent him. Olas Tamid, Eliyah Rabbah, and Birkei Yosef explain Maran this way and answer Lechem Chamudos's objection; Ateres Zekeinim agrees. By the accepted rule that when Maran states an unqualified ruling and then 'some say,' he follows the first, as above in siman 13, paragraph 7, Maran rules here that when someone else can attend to the deceased, one who wishes to be stringent should not be prevented. Since we have accepted Maran's rulings, he should not be stopped [Eliyah Rabbah; Beis Oved]. See also above, siman 55, paragraph 13.
