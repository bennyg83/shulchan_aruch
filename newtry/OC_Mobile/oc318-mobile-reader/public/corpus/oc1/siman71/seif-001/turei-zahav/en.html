Even if the obligation to bury does not rest upon him, etc. This excludes Rabbeinu Tam's case. His married sister, the mother of the Ri, died in another city, and Rabbeinu Tam ate meat and drank wine because she had a husband upon whom the obligation to bury her rested. The Rosh disagreed, maintaining that the Sages did not distinguish between cases; see there. It appears to me that this dispute depends upon the reasons given by Tosafos and the Rosh at the beginning of the chapter Mi Shemeiso, in the name of the Yerushalmi cited shortly below, for why an onen is exempt from Krias Shema and prayer.
And even if he wishes to be stringent. The Yerushalmi states that this is either because of the honor due to the deceased or because no one else will bear his burden. The practical difference is where someone else will bear his burden, for according to the first reason it remains forbidden even then. The Beit Yosef writes that one who wishes to be stringent and recite when someone else will bear his burden may do so, since the matter is a Rabbinic doubt. This is unrelated to the law of a groom in the preceding siman, where we rule that he is not obligated to recite, for there the matter depends upon concentration, unlike here. The Mordechai writes in the name of the Raavyah that on the second reason rely those onenim who go to the synagogue and pray when others are attending to and guarding the deceased. I found this wording in a corrected edition of the Mordechai. The Beit Yosef quotes the Raavyah as saying, 'They rely upon this and go,' but the word 'onenim' is missing there. The same wording appears in the Agudah. The Maharshal writes in responsum 70 that nowadays the practice does not follow the Raavyah. Nevertheless, it appears to me that a son who is an onen for his father, when others are attending to the burial, should go to the synagogue and recite Kaddish, for this is an honor to his father and is permitted according to all opinions.
See Yoreh Deah, siman 341. Meaning, there the Shulchan Aruch does not rule this way. On the contrary, he rules that the mourner may not be stringent at all. Although the Rama there also writes, 'See Orach Chaim, siman 71,' the primary ruling appears to follow the Shulchan Aruch's words in Yoreh Deah. We rule leniently in a Rabbinic doubt only after the fact or where there is no alternative. Here, however, why should we instruct him at the outset to act against the reason of honoring the deceased? Refraining from action is preferable.
