Even. See the Taz, who cites the Rosh that even when the deceased is in another city he is exempt and may not recite. See Yoreh Deah, siman 341, and the Shach there, seif katan 5, contrary to the Bach, who rules that when the deceased is in another city and her husband is in the city, he may eat meat and drink wine; see there. The Rosh and the Rambam in chapter 4 of Hilchos Krias Shema differ. According to the Rosh, meat and wine are forbidden because the Sages did not distinguish between cases. According to the Rambam, the reason is that his mind is not settled until he buries the deceased. This applies to Krias Shema, which requires concentration, but not to meat and wine. Even now, however, we generally do not concentrate properly in any event. The Taz writes that this depends upon the two reasons stated by the Yerushalmi in the next subsection. According to the reason that no one else will bear his burden, in Rabbeinu Tam's case meat and wine are permitted and he is obligated in mitzvos. According to the reason of honoring the deceased, meaning that people should not say that the deceased is insignificant in the eyes of the onen and that he is neither alarmed nor distressed by the death, as the Levush writes, even when the deceased is in another city and her husband is in the city, meat and wine are forbidden and he may not recite Krias Shema. The glosses to the Levush are not the Levush's own words. This accords with the Rosh's explanation that honoring the deceased means that he should remain free to consider the burial needs, and for that reason, when the deceased is in another city, the Rosh had to invoke the rule that the Sages did not distinguish.
