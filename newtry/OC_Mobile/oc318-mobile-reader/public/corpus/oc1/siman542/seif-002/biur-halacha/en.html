Through a worker, etc. See Mor U-Ketziah, who wrote that it appears to him that if one has a wife and minor children whom he is obligated to feed, he may also work for what they need for today, but not for those whom he is not obligated to feed; rather, they should go begging if they cannot work themselves. End quote. This is not at all clear, for the mitzvah of festival joy also applies on Chol HaMoed, as stated in Rambam, chapter 6 of Hilchot Yom Tov. What joy will he have if his children go begging? More than this is proven from Sheiltot, parashat Berachah, that even if one can obtain a money loan and buy, they permitted him to do labor, namely to harvest and grind his wheat, because since he must borrow, his mind is distressed and he cannot fulfill "you shall rejoice on your festival." All the more so in our case. However, regarding the law itself, whether he can obtain money by loan in our case, there is room for study, for Rambam ruled there that even if he is wealthy and has money and can find produce to buy in the market, he is also not obligated; so wrote R. Yitzchak ibn Giat. It must be that the reason is that a person's joy is in benefiting from his own and not from others' produce; therefore Rav permitted harvesting his wheat. In our case, however, in any event he will eat others' produce, so perhaps with a mere loan we do not say that his mind is distressed. In any case, when his children would go begging, his mind is certainly distressed and the joy of the festival will certainly be nullified for him, and it is surely permitted for him to work himself in order to earn for them.
What he will eat. The view of Magen Avraham is known, and also of some later authorities who copied his words, that this applies only where he does not even have bread and water. The view of Eliyah Rabbah is that even if he has something, but lacks what he needs for Yom Tov, it is also permitted. In my humble opinion, the Bavli and the Yerushalmi differ in their interpretations of the Mishnah on this point. In the Yerushalmi it is stated regarding that Mishnah, that one may not write debt documents on the moed, but if he does not trust him, or if he has nothing to eat, he may write. R. Yirmiyah said: if he does not trust the borrower, or if the scribe has nothing to eat; this is like the Bavli's explanation on page 12. R. Yose said to him: if you say so, you will end up permitting every occupation on the moed. Rather, it means: if he does not trust the previous lender, or if this lender has nothing to eat. Korban HaEdah explained: R. Yose said to him, if when the scribe has nothing to eat it is permitted for him to do his work, you permit all labors on the moed, for most workers do their labor for their livelihood. End quote. If he explained like Magen Avraham, that he has literally nothing to eat, not even bread and water, there is no difficulty at all, for we see plainly that most workers will not lack bread and water even if one day passes without work. Rather, he must explain that he lacks what he needs for Yom Tov, namely meat and wine as customary; therefore he asks: if so, all workers should be permitted to do their labor on Chol HaMoed, since most laborers are limited in means. Therefore he was compelled to explain the Mishnah otherwise. Our Gemara follows the view of R. Yirmiyah and is not concerned for R. Yose's question, because it explains "he has nothing to eat" according to its plain sense, and there is no difficulty, as above. It appears to me another reason why the Yerushalmi did not want to explain it so: in the Mishnah on page 12 it teaches there, "one may not buy houses..." unless he has nothing to eat, and there it necessarily means that he lacks what he needs, as the Rosh and Mordechai wrote in chapter 1, siman 23, from that Yerushalmi in chapter 2. Therefore the Yerushalmi holds that here too it means that he lacks what he needs, and so it properly raises its question, as above.
The result of all this is that if he lacks what he needs for Yom Tov, according to the Yerushalmi one should not permit a worker to do labor for this, for then all tradesmen would be permitted to do labor; and we have not found that the Bavli explicitly disagrees with him on this, as it appears to my humble opinion.
