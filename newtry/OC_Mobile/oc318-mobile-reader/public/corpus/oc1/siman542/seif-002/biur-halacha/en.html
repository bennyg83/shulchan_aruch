Through a laborer, etc. See Sefer Mor UKetzi'ah, who wrote that it appears to him that if he has a wife and young children whom he is obligated to support, he also does [work] for their sustenance for today; but not for the need of those he is not obligated to support; rather they should go door to door if they cannot do [work] themselves; end quote. And this is not reasonable at all, for the mitzvah of simchas ha-chag applies also on Chol HaMoed, as is stated in the Rambam, chapter 6 of Hilchos Yom Tov; and what joy would he have if his children go door to door begging? And greater than this is proven in the She'iltos, Parashas Beracha, that even if he can obtain [funds] by borrowing money and buying, they permitted him to do melacha, namely to harvest and to grind his wheat, because since he needs to borrow, his mind is distressed and he cannot fulfill "and you shall rejoice in your festival"; and all the more so in our matter. However, regarding the core ruling itself, whether he can obtain money by loan in our matter, there is room to examine, for the Rambam ruled there that even if he is a wealthy man who has money and finds [food] to buy from the market, he is also not obligated [to buy rather than work]; and so wrote Ritz Gi'at; and necessarily, the reason is that a person's joy is that he enjoys from what is his own and not from others' produce; and therefore Rav permitted harvesting his wheat; which is not so in our matter, where in any case he will eat others' produce; possibly we do not say that through a mere loan his mind is distressed. And in any case, when his children would go door to door begging, his mind is certainly distressed and the joy of the festival is nullified for him; it is certainly permitted for him to do [work] himself in order to earn for their sake.
What he will eat. The view of Magen Avraham is well known, and also some Acharonim who copied his words: that it is specifically when he does not have even bread and water; and the view of Eliyah Rabbah is that even if he has [food], but he does not have enough for his Yom Tov needs, it is also permitted. And behold, to my humble opinion the Bavli and the Yerushalmi disagree in the interpretation of the Mishnah on this: for in the Yerushalmi it is stated regarding that Mishnah that one does not write promissory notes on the Moed, and if he does not trust him or he has nothing to eat, he may write: Rabbi Yirmiyah said, if he does not trust the borrower, or the scribe has nothing to eat [and that accords with the Bavli's interpretation on folio 12]; Rabbi Yose said to him, if you say so, you end up permitting craftsmanship on the Moed; rather, if the lender of a past [loan] does not trust him, or this lender has nothing to eat. And Korban HaEdah explained, and these are his words: Rabbi Yose said to him, if so, that if the scribe has nothing to eat he is permitted to do his work, you permit all melachos on the Moed, for most craftsmen work for their livelihood; end quote. And if one interprets like Magen Avraham, that he has nothing to eat at all, even bread and water, there is no difficulty at all, for we see plainly that most craftsmen will not lack bread and water even if some day happens when he has no work; therefore he must be interpreting that he does not have enough for his Yom Tov needs, namely meat and wine as is customary, and therefore he challenges: if so, all laborers are permitted to do their work on Chol HaMoed, for most craftsmen are of limited means; and therefore he is forced to give another interpretation of the Mishnah. And our Gemara holds like the view of Rabbi Yirmiyah and was not concerned for Rabbi Yose's difficulty, because it interprets "he has nothing to eat" according to its plain sense, and there is no difficulty at all, as above. And it further appears to me that there is another reason the Yerushalmi did not want to interpret that way: because in the Mishnah on folio 12 it taught there, one does not buy houses, etc., or he has nothing to eat, etc., and there necessarily it means he does not have enough for his needs, as the Rosh and the Mordechai wrote in the first chapter, siman 23, from that Yerushalmi in chapter 2; and therefore the Yerushalmi's view is that here too it means he does not have enough for his needs, and therefore the challenge is apt, as above.
The upshot of all this is that when he does not have enough for his Yom Tov needs, according to the Yerushalmi one should not permit a laborer on that basis to do melacha, for if so all craftsmen would be permitted to do melacha; and we do not find that the Bavli explicitly disputes him on this, as it appears to my humble opinion.
