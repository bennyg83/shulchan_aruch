Rambam, chapter 8 of Hilchos Berachos, halachah 11, writes that if one had beer and concluded Borei peri ha’adamah, or fruit and concluded Borei peri ha’eitz, or a grain dish and concluded Hamotzi, he is not required to repeat the blessing. Lechem Mishneh there explains, based on Rambam’s responsum to the scholars of Lunel cited by Kesef Mishneh in chapter 1 of Hilchos Krias Shema, halachah 8, that regarding beer produced from grapes, though I do not know its precise process and its blessing is Shehakol, see siman 202, seif 11, the statement is not false because it derives from the vine. Likewise, Borei peri ha’adamah may refer to fruit that can grow on a tree whose trunk does not remain through the winter, and grain too may be called bread. Therefore, although he did not fulfill the ideal formula, since from the beginning of the blessing through “King of the universe” he intended to conclude properly and in the end did not say an outright falsehood, he fulfilled his obligation. This differs from one who says the evening blessing in the morning, where he stated a falsehood. See there. Kesef Mishneh there and Beit Yosef here, however, explain that these examples are not exclusive. The same applies even if he stated the exact opposite, provided that his original intent was correct. According to Rambam, the beginning of the blessing is decisive, since he did not record the Gemara’s law otherwise. See Kesef Mishneh, chapter 1 of Hilchos Krias Shema, unlike Lechem Mishneh in chapter 8 of Hilchos Berachos, who understands Rambam to be uncertain. Rather, according to Rambam, the initial intent is decisive both leniently and stringently. Therefore, the Mechaber added here the case of water, and so forth: even though he concluded falsely and did not correct himself within the time required for speech, he is not required to repeat the blessing. Conversely, if his original intent was mistaken, even if he concluded correctly he has not fulfilled his obligation according to Rambam. The “some say” view is the exact opposite: the Gemara’s question was unresolved, so we rule leniently. They explain that he concluded correctly but his initial intent was mistaken, and leniently he fulfills his obligation. Yet in the first case they rule stringently, because he stated a falsehood, as Raavad writes in his glosses. In seif 2 the case is one who knew the truth. If he did not know, according to Rambam he would not fulfill his obligation. The Mechaber teaches that in such a case even the “some say” view agrees that he fulfills his obligation. According to all opinions, if he intended water, mistakenly concluded Borei peri hagefen, and immediately added Shehakol, he has fulfilled his obligation. All the more so if he first said Shehakol and afterward added Borei peri hagefen, since he already fulfilled his obligation with Shehakol. Levush implies that seif 2 follows the “some say” view in seif 1, but he reverses the order and presents Rambam’s view last. See there. As practical halachah, the ruling follows the “some say” view, as Magen Avraham rules in seif katan 1. This apparently applies even to grape beer and the three distinctions cited by Rambam: whenever he uttered an improper conclusion, he has not fulfilled his obligation.
