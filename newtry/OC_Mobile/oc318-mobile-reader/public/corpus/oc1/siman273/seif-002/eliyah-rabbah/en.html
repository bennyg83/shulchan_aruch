And afterward he changed his mind, etc. This implies that if from the outset he intends to eat elsewhere, it is valid. One could say, however, that prior intent also does not help and "changed his mind" is mentioned only because the following clause permits moving from corner to corner even after a change of mind. Yet seif 5 says, "and he intended to eat, and we already said in seif 2 that in such a case it is permitted," implying that here only changing one's mind creates the prohibition, whereas with prior intent even another house is permitted. The beginning of siman 178 also implies this. This requires study because Shulchan Aruch and Rama permit prior intent only from room to room or from roof to ground, meaning two places within one house, but not from house to house. Tosafot, Rosh, Mordechai, and Ran in Arvei Pesachim prohibit another house, and one should not be lenient. The underlying sources about synagogue kiddush and guests can be explained because the original enactment remained even after guests ceased to be present, not because prior intent makes a second house the same place. Maharshal's proposed distinction that a synagogue is not a place fit for ordinary eating is difficult against Mordechai, Kol Bo, Tosafot, Rosh, and Ran, who do not distinguish. Maharshal's proof from Rabbah telling his students to taste something before going home also does not establish that intent to eat elsewhere suffices, because according to Rashi, Rashbam, and Tosafot the concern was that the lamp at home might go out and they then would not eat there at all. Mordechai understands the case differently and therefore can use it as proof. In practical law, given the authorities that prohibit moving to another house, one should not be lenient.
[Levush] For such is the normal manner, etc. This applies specifically where the walls of the house separate the places. If there is no additional wall and only the sukkah walls stand between them, Magen Avraham cites Mordechai and Tosafot that even without prior intent it is permitted, because sukkah walls are built for the mitzvah rather than ordinary household use and the move is like going from corner to corner without walls. This requires study. The Yerushalmi cited by Tosafot Pesachim 100 reconciles the views by saying the sukkah case concerns prior intent while Shmuel concerns no prior intent. If sukkah walls were not treated as true walls, it should instead have reconciled them both without prior intent. Mordechai's own use of this distinction is because he holds even moving from corner to corner requires prior intent. According to Tosafot and those who rule that corner-to-corner movement is permitted even after changing one's mind, we must conclude that sukkah walls are genuine walls. This also explains why Tosafot and the other poskim never mention Mordechai's novelty. Gedulat Mordechai further reports that old manuscripts lack this passage.
