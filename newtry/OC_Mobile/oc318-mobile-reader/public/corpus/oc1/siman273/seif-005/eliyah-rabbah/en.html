[Levush] Rather, in another place, etc. Olat Tamid asks why "changed his mind" is needed, since even if he ate elsewhere he fulfilled the obligation. He says it is strained to establish the case as two houses. According to what I wrote, the Levush allows even two houses with prior intent, making the question stronger. It therefore appears to me that the novelty is the opposite: he changed his mind and ate here, in a place he had not initially intended to eat, and nevertheless fulfills the obligation because he ate where he made kiddush. If he ate at the other place that he had intended initially, it would be even more obvious according to the Levush. If he ate elsewhere without having intended that place, it appears he must make kiddush again. Olat Tamid's explanation of Rama, that one initially intended not to eat immediately but changed his mind and ate immediately, cannot be correct, because Rama already implies that eating immediately suffices even if that was not his initial intention. Beit Yosef at the end of the siman also supports the Levush.
[Levush] Even the blessing of Hamotzi, etc. Bach and Nachalat Tzvi disagree. It is also difficult from the Levush himself at the end of siman 167, which implies otherwise.
[Levush] And he intended, etc. This requires study. With intent to eat elsewhere, even if he did not eat or drink at the kiddush place, he appears to fulfill the obligation; and if he did eat or drink there, he fulfills it even without such intent. Perhaps the meaning is that without prior intent the later eating is a new meal and he must first bless over a cup as in the daytime. Shiltei Giborim at the beginning of Arvei Pesachim writes something similar. Magen Avraham, however, implies no new cup is required, as with seudah shelishit in siman 291.
But fruits, etc. With pastry or other mezonot from the five grains one does fulfill kiddush in the place of a meal, because such foods are certainly more significant for Shabbos meals than wine, as in siman 291. Beer does not suffice even where it is chamar medinah, according to Magen Avraham.
