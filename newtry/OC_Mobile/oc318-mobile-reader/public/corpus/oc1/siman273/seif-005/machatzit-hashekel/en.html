Seif katan 5: He must eat, as stated in Darchei Moshe, etc. Rama’s wording requires explanation. He first writes that unless one intended to eat there immediately, even if one later ate at the place of Kiddush he did not fulfill his obligation, because his original intent was improper. Immediately afterward, however, Rama writes that if he intended otherwise and then changed his mind and ate there, he fulfilled his obligation. Levush explains that the first case is where he intended not to eat at all, neither there nor elsewhere. If he later changed his mind and ate there, some say he did not fulfill Kiddush. The latter case is where he intended to eat elsewhere but ultimately ate at the place of Kiddush; since he intended at the time of Kiddush to eat a meal, and ultimately ate where he recited Kiddush, it is Kiddush in the place of a meal. Magen Avraham cites Maharil, the source of Rama’s ruling, and explains differently. Mere improper intent does not invalidate Kiddush. Rather, in the first case he actually carried out his plan, interrupted with other matters, and only afterward ate where he had recited Kiddush. In the latter case, although he had intended to interrupt, he did not actually do so, but immediately ate there, and therefore fulfilled his obligation.
If he intended, etc., and an unavoidable circumstance occurred. This is a new ruling of Magen Avraham. Even if he interrupted, when the interruption was forced and he remained in the same place, it does not invalidate the Kiddush.
This is also implied by the Gemara, page 100. Rav Huna recited Kiddush, and before he ate the lamps went out. He entered the wedding house of his son, where lamps were still burning, recited Kiddush again, and ate. The Gemara inferred from this that he held there is no Kiddush except in the place of a meal.
Specifically because he left, etc. Since the Gemara relates that he left his place, it implies that this departure required a second Kiddush. Rama likewise speaks of one who initially intended to interrupt, implying that where he did not intend to interrupt but was compelled to do so, he has not necessarily lost the first Kiddush.
But if he left, etc. If he left the place, even under compulsion, he must recite Kiddush again, as in the incident of Rav Huna. See citations 4, 50, 81.
