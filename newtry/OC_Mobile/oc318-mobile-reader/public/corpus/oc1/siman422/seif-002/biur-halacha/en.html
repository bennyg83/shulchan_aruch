And we recite, etc. The Gemara in Megillah 20 states that Hallel may be recited throughout the entire day, except for the Hallel recited on Pesach night. Responsa Sheilas Yaavetz states that Hallel is connected to Shacharis. Therefore, one should not taste anything until after Hallel, unless he is physically weak, in which case it is permitted, and there is not even a measure of piety in refraining. Regarding reciting Hallel in a mourner’s house during shivah, see above, siman 131, Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 20.
Hallel. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that regarding Hallel, one who has already fulfilled his obligation may discharge the obligation of another, whether for an individual or for a congregation. This applies specifically where the listener is not proficient and does not know how to respond. If he knows how to respond, one who has already fulfilled his own obligation cannot discharge him unless the listener answers Halleluyah after him. Magen Avraham writes that the first part of his ruling is straightforward, and it is stated explicitly at the end of the third chapter of Rosh Hashanah that regarding Hallel and Megillah, one who has already fulfilled his obligation may discharge another. Nesiv Chayim wrote that the statement in the third chapter of Rosh Hashanah concerns only blessings and not Hallel, but he overlooked the end of the chapter. Rabbeinu Yerucham’s conclusion, that this applies when the listener is not proficient, is derived from Sukkah 38, where an adult recites for him and he answers Halleluyah. This indicates that he must at least answer Halleluyah, and Rabbeinu Yerucham holds that this is indispensable. Accordingly, when Rosh Hashanah states without qualification that one who has already fulfilled his obligation may discharge another and does not require a response, it must refer to one who is unable to respond. It is somewhat surprising how a person could be unable to answer the single word Halleluyah. Perhaps he does not know at which points he must answer it. Rabbeinu Yerucham’s reference to one who has already fulfilled his obligation is not exclusive. The same applies even where the reader has not yet fulfilled his own obligation. He cannot discharge a listener who is capable of responding unless that listener responds. Eliyah Rabbah rules accordingly. Peri Chadash, however, writes that the requirement to respond applies specifically where the reader has already fulfilled his obligation. If the reader has not yet fulfilled his own obligation and recites the blessing for himself and his fellow, the fellow fulfills his obligation even if he answers nothing. He supports this from the discussion at the beginning of the chapter Three Who Ate. See Peri Chadash there, who also disputes Rabbeinu Yerucham’s basic ruling and maintains that even a proficient listener may be discharged by another. I wrote similarly above in siman 273 in Biur Halacha. See also Ritva, Sukkah 38. It is further explicit in Sukkah 38 and in the poskim that women are exempt from Hallel because it is a time bound positive mitzvah, except for the Hallel of Pesach night, in which they are obligated because they too participated in that miracle, as Tosafos writes there. Therefore, women cannot discharge men unless the men repeat every word after them. The baraisa concludes that a curse should come upon a man who must have his wife recite for him. Magen Avraham appears to apply this rule even on Rosh Chodesh. In my humble opinion, this is not certain. The rule is readily understood on days when the complete Hallel is recited, because men are obligated by Rabbinic law, whereas women, even in a place where they have long practiced this mitzvah, are bound only by custom. Men are obligated, while women act only by custom. On days when the incomplete Hallel is recited, however, men are likewise obligated only by custom. In a place where women have also accepted this practice, what difference is there between them? Perhaps Magen Avraham refers to a place where women had not previously accepted this practice, so they have no obligation even by custom, and a woman now wishes to recite Hallel and discharge others. This requires study. Know that it is clear that a woman herself may recite Hallel and recite its blessing even though she is exempt, just as meticulous women accept other time bound positive mitzvos upon themselves and recite blessings over them. It also appears reasonable that according to our custom of reciting a blessing over Hallel on Rosh Chodesh, a woman may likewise recite the blessing just as a man does, similar to what Tosafos writes in Arachin 10. Their words are cited in Shaar HaTziyun, note 5. Siddur Yaavetz states that women should not recite a blessing over Hallel on Rosh Chodesh, but the accepted custom does not follow his view.
