Ibid., seif 2. “They recite Hallel with omissions,” etc. Note: The Gemara in Arachin 10b asks: Since Rosh Chodesh is called a moed, let Hallel be recited. It answers that Rosh Chodesh differs because melachah is not prohibited on it, as the verse states, “This song shall be for you as on the night when a festival is sanctified.” A night sanctified as a festival requires song, whereas one not sanctified as a festival does not require song. See there. A Torah scholar asked me from Pesachim 95a concerning Pesach Sheni, where the Gemara says that Hallel is not required while eating it, based on the same verse: a night sanctified as a festival requires song, whereas one not sanctified as a festival does not. The Gemara asks that, if so, Hallel should not be required while offering it either, and answers that the verse excludes the night but does not exclude the day. Accordingly, since Hallel on Rosh Chodesh is recited by day, why is Rosh Chodesh excluded by a verse concerning a festival night?
I answered that there is no difficulty. There are two grounds for reciting Hallel. One is that the day commemorates a miracle, even if it is not a festival, as on Chanukah. The other is that the day is a festival and moed, even if it does not commemorate the miracle itself. Rosh Chodesh does not commemorate a miracle. The initial thought that Hallel should be recited rests only on its being called a moed. It therefore cannot be derived from Pesach as a day of miracle; the proposed comparison is only to a festival because Rosh Chodesh is called a moed. The Gemara answers that no proof can be brought from a festival, since melachah is prohibited on a festival. To establish that Hallel depends upon the prohibition of melachah, the Gemara cites the verse concerning a night sanctified as a festival. Once there is no proof from the term moed, no verse is needed to exclude Rosh Chodesh, since reason alone gives no basis to require Hallel when it is neither a day of miracle nor a festival. Pesach Sheni, however, is connected to the miracle, for it serves as compensation for the first Pesach. Moreover, seeing the place of a miracle and recalling a miracle are comparable to the day of the miracle. It would therefore be reasonable to recite Hallel on Pesach Sheni even though it is not a festival, just as on Chanukah. A verse is consequently needed to exclude Hallel while eating it. Since the verse is required, the Gemara properly distinguishes that the night was excluded but the day was not. This is correct, with Hashem’s help. Examine carefully.
Ibid., in the gloss: Some say that an individual also recites a blessing over it, etc. Note: This is the view of Tosafos, that a blessing is recited over Hallel on Rosh Chodesh. Tosafos prove it from Megillah: Rav arrived on Rosh Chodesh and heard the congregation reciting Hallel. He wished to stop them, but when he heard that they were omitting passages, he said that they possessed the custom of their ancestors. Tosafos ask that he should already have known this from the fact that they did not recite a blessing. In my humble opinion, this can be resolved. The Gemara says that one who recites Hallel every day is a blasphemer. See Magen Avraham in the laws of Rosh Chodesh. If there is no obligation to recite Hallel on Rosh Chodesh, reciting it as praise rather than supplication would seemingly be prohibited. It appears, however, that the description “blasphemer” applies only when one recites the entire Hallel. When passages are omitted, this concern does not apply. We rule that a custom contrary to halachah is disregarded. Rav initially understood from their failure to recite a blessing that their practice was only a custom, but he nevertheless wished to stop them because he thought they would complete the entire Hallel, which would constitute blasphemy and would be contrary to halachah. Once he saw that they omitted passages, there was no such concern, and their custom could be followed because it did not conflict with halachah. This appears correct. A proof for this distinction is that we do not complete Hallel on the seventh day of Pesach because of the statement, “My handiwork is drowning,” yet we do recite an abridged Hallel. Understand and examine carefully.
