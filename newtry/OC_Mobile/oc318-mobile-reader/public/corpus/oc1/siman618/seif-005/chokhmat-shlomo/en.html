(There, se'if 5) If the sick person says he does not need to eat and the physician is uncertain, we feed him, etc. Note: This law requires analysis. What is different from what the Shulchan Aruch wrote above, that if the sick person says he does not need and a physician says he does need, we do not feed him? If so, were it not for the sick person's statement, the matter would be uncertain to us whether he needs to eat or not, since it is one against one, and it is an evenly balanced doubt whether he needs or not; the sick person's decision tips it to the side that he does not need. Why then, when the physician himself is uncertain, being equally balanced in his own mind whether the person needs or not, should the sick person's view not tip the matter to the side that he does not need? It seems that if the physician himself is uncertain this is worse than where one physician says clearly that he needs. One cannot say that the first case is different because there is one witness who clearly says he does not need, for opposite him there is one witness who clearly says he does need; if so, what difference is there between one against one, where the matter is balanced, and the physician himself being uncertain, where we have no clarity either to be lenient or stringent and it is like a balanced matter? Why in this case does the sick person's decision not help, unlike the first case? This is a great difficulty in my opinion. Especially because one who examines the Yerushalmi brought by the Beis Yosef, from which this law originates, sees that it concludes with the reason: a sick person says, 'I can fast,' and the physician says, 'I do not know'; Rabbi Abbahu in the name of Rabbi Yochanan says it becomes a doubt involving life, and in a doubt involving life we rule leniently. See there. If so, the main reason is that it becomes a doubt involving life and we are lenient. But where one physician says he needs and one says he does not need, it also becomes a doubt involving life if the sick person said nothing. Why there do we not follow leniency? Therefore my heart tells me that the Yerushalmi disagrees with our Talmud and holds that even where one physician says he needs and one says he does not need, we feed him. The Yerushalmi teaches that even if there is no definite statement to be lenient, only the physician himself is uncertain, we still feed him, and all the more so in one against one. Our Talmud, which holds that when one physician is against another the sick person's decision helps, teaches that even in such a case the sick person's decision helps to be stringent, all the more so where the physician himself is uncertain. Each source stated its own novelty. But the authorities who ruled like both require much analysis. In practice it would have seemed that since the halachah follows our Talmud against the Yerushalmi, in such a case we would not feed him. But what strength do I have to disagree with the authorities, especially to be stringent in saving life? Therefore the matter requires analysis in my eyes. Examine it carefully.
