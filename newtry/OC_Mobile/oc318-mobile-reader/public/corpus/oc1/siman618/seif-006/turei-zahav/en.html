And the same applies if the sick person and one physician, etc. So wrote the Tur. Many ask: why do we need this one who is with him, since we rule that even the sick person himself, when he says he needs, is effective even against one hundred? The Beis Yosef answered in the name of Mahari Abohav that one might have thought it is worse if another is with him, as the Rema wrote here, that we are not concerned that the sick person says he needs because he believes the physician. This is difficult for me from the Gemara's question. We learned: A sick person is fed on the basis of experts. On the basis of experts, yes, but on his own basis, no? On the basis of experts, yes, but on the basis of one expert, no? From this it challenges what was said there, that we feed on his own basis or on the basis of one physician. It answers: Here what are we dealing with? Where he says, 'I do not need.' Then let them feed him on the basis of one expert? He does not need, for there is another with him who says he does not need. It asks: This is obvious, for where there are experts who say to feed him, it is danger to life and in a doubt involving life we rule leniently. It answers: It is needed where there are two others with him who say he does not need. Although Rav Safra said that two are like one hundred and one hundred are like two, that applies to testimony; but regarding assessment we follow the majority of opinions. This applies to assessment in monetary matters; here, however, it is danger to life, and we do not follow the majority of opinions. Since there are two who say he needs, even if one hundred say he does not need, it is a doubt involving life. It then asks: But from the fact that the latter clause teaches, 'If there are no experts there, they feed him on his own basis,' the first clause must refer to where they say he needs; the text is deficient and should read: In what case is this said? Where he says, 'I do not need,' then we require two experts. But if he says, 'I need,' then if there are not two experts who say he does not need, but only one, they feed him on his own basis. This is difficult: why does the Talmud force itself to establish the Mishnah as deficient? Why not answer that we follow the majority of opinions, and both in the first clause and the latter clause the sick person says, 'I need'; but in the first clause, where there are experts with him, he is not counted at all because he believes the words of the two experts, and it is as though he is absent, and the permission is based on the experts. The practical difference would be that if the majority of opinions prohibit, we follow the majority and do not join the sick person's opinion with those who permit. Then the latter clause would properly conclude that if there are no experts, but he himself says he needs, he is believed. If there were room for this reasoning, that he believes the experts, the baraisa would be well resolved. Even if you say that this reasoning is not enough to establish it as certainly so, in any case we could say that regarding one other with him we do not apply this reasoning, but regarding two experts we certainly could say he believes two physicians and is drawn after them. It seems to me to answer the words of the Tur as follows: the sick person's statement 'I need' is effective only after he asks to eat, as the Tur wrote nearby, for then it is certainly clear to him regarding his illness, and he is believed even against one hundred. But if he asks nothing, and only when they ask him he says, 'I need,' this is not so effective; it is as though another physician says this, meaning he says it by his own assessment. Then he is not believed more than one physician and is not believed against two. Therefore here one person with him is required, so that it is considered as though there are two physicians here, and he is believed even against one hundred. So it seems to me in resolving the words of the Tur.
