The same applies if the patient and one physician, etc. So wrote the Tur. Many ask: why is this one physician together with him needed? We hold that even if the patient himself says he needs to eat, that is effective even against one hundred. Beit Yosef answered in the name of Mahari that one might have thought it is worse if another is with him, as Rama wrote here, that we are not concerned that the patient says he needs it because he believes the physician. This is difficult from what we ask in the Gemara: we learned, "a patient is fed according to experts"; according to experts, yes, but according to himself, no; according to experts, yes, but according to one expert, no. From this we challenge what was said there, that we feed according to himself or according to one physician. We answer: here we deal with a case where he said, "I do not need." Then let them feed him according to one expert. No, there is another with him who says, "he does not need." The Gemara asks: that is obvious; where there are experts who say to feed him, it is a doubt of danger to life, and doubt of danger to life is ruled leniently. It answers: this is needed where there are two others with him who say he does not need. Even though Rav Safra said that two are like one hundred and one hundred are like two, that applies to testimony; but in assessment we follow the majority of views. That applies to monetary assessment, but here it is a doubt of danger to life and we do not follow the majority of views; since there are two who say he needs it, even if one hundred say he does not need it, it is a doubt of danger to life. The Gemara asks: from the fact that the latter clause teaches, "if there are no experts there, they feed him according to himself," it follows that the first clause speaks where he says he needs it. The text is deficient and should read: When is this said? When he says, "I do not need"; then we require two experts. But if he says, "I need," if there are not two experts saying he does not need it, but only one, they feed him according to himself. The difficulty is: why did the Talmud press itself to establish the Mishnah as textually deficient? Why not answer that we follow the majority of views, and in both the first and latter clauses the patient says "I need"; but in the first clause, where experts are with him, he is not counted for anything because he believes the words of two experts, so he is as if absent, and the permission is according to the experts. The practical difference would be that if the majority of views were to prohibit, we would follow the majority and would not combine the patient's opinion with those permitting. On this the latter clause would conclude well: if there are no experts there but he himself says he needs it, then he is believed. If there were room for this reasoning, the baraita would be resolved. And even if you say this reasoning is not sufficient to establish it so, nevertheless we could say that with one other together with him we do not adopt this reasoning, but with two experts we do say that he believes two physicians and is drawn after them. It appears to me to answer the Tur as follows: the patient's statement "I need" helps only after he asks to eat, as the Tur writes nearby; then certainly his illness is clear to him, and he is believed even against one hundred. But if he asks nothing, and only when they ask him he says, "I need," this is not as effective; it is as if another physician says this, namely according to his assessment, and then he is no more believed than one physician and is not believed against two. Therefore here one physician is needed with him, so it is considered as if two physicians are here, and he is believed even against one hundred. This seems correct in resolving the Tur's words.
