Seif 1. "The lighting performs the mitzvah and not the placement, etc.; therefore, a lantern that was burning the whole day, etc." NB: The Rambam's wording is: "A lantern that was burning the whole day, at the conclusion of Shabbat he extinguishes it, recites the beracha, and lights it, for the lighting is the mitzvah and not the placement." I saw that the Pri Chadash challenged him, because his language implies that if placement performed the mitzvah, extinguishing would not be needed, which is against the Gemara. The Gemara asks: if you think placement performs the mitzvah, it should have said, he extinguishes it, lifts it, lights it, and places it. This implies that even according to the view that placement performs the mitzvah, extinguishing is needed. The two answers the Pri Chadash gave do not appear correct. His second answer, that the Rambam speaks where it was lit for Chanukah according to Tosafot there, certainly does not appear correct; if so, he should have explained, especially since the Gemara asks that it should have said "he extinguishes and lifts," implying that since it says simply "a lantern that was burning," it does not speak of Chanukah. Since the plain meaning is that it was for Shabbat, if the Rambam spoke of Chanukah he should have explained. Also, why did he leave the explicit law in the Gemara, which speaks of Shabbat, and take another law not explicit in the Gemara? Nor does the Pri Chadash's first answer appear correct, that the Rambam's intent is by implication, that placement is not required, and if placement performed the mitzvah one would also need placement. For such a matter the Rambam should have explained and not relied on an implication. Rather, it appears well resolved. Tosafot themselves asked there: if placement performs the mitzvah, why require extinguishing? It is no worse than if a deaf-mute, shoteh, or minor lit it. In my humble opinion it is resolved as follows. This is similar to shechitah, where we hold that intent is not required, and even if a knife fell without intent the shechitah is valid. Nevertheless, if it fell on its own it is invalid, because an act is at least required. If a deaf-mute, shoteh, or minor slaughtered it, it is valid, proving that their act is considered an act; only intent they lack. Their act is better than something falling on its own. So too with Chanukah, even according to the initial thought that placement performs the mitzvah, this means only that lighting for the sake of the mitzvah is not required. Therefore if a deaf-mute, shoteh, or minor lit it, it would help; although they lack intent, and even according to the view that mitzvot require intent, he agrees that rabbinic mitzvot do not require intent when the person is fit for intent, because anything fit for mixing does not require actual mixing. But since a deaf-mute, shoteh, and minor lack intent, their lighting would not help if lighting performs the mitzvah; also, since they are not obligated, they cannot discharge others. Still, if placement performs the mitzvah and not lighting, their lighting would help. However, if it lit by itself, it certainly would not help, for at least a human act is required in the lighting, and automatic lighting does not help. Therefore, likewise if it burned from erev Shabbat, the first act has already gone, like the Gemara says in Berachot regarding lighting from a non-Jew, that the first column of flame has gone, and similarly where one turned water through a wheel and slaughtered, the first force has gone, and afterward it is as if it happened on its own. So too here, the first force has gone and afterward it is like it burned by itself. Therefore it is worse than if a deaf-mute, shoteh, or minor lit it, because at least an act is required, and this is automatic. This resolves Tosafot's question. According to this the Rambam is well resolved. There is a dispute in the Gemara whether one's fire is because of his arrows or because of his property, and we hold it is because of his arrows. The Nimukei Yosef in Bava Kamma chapter 2 asked that if one's fire is because of his arrows, it should be forbidden to light a candle before Shabbat that continues burning on Shabbat. He answered that it is as though he did the whole act at the time of permissibility. See there. If so, at least it is considered his action, and everything is considered his act; it is a human act and would not need extinguishing, for it is no worse than a deaf-mute, shoteh, or minor lighting it. Thus the Gemara's question, that it should have said he extinguishes and lifts, is because otherwise it could not ask why extinguishing is needed, since one could say that Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi holds like the view that one's fire is because of his property, so there is no human act here and it is like it lit by itself, worse than a deaf-mute, shoteh, or minor. Therefore it asks: let that be so, at least lifting should also be required. But for us, who hold that one's fire is because of his arrows, every lighting is like his action, so it is no worse than a deaf-mute, shoteh, or minor. Therefore the Rambam wrote that only because lighting performs the mitzvah does he extinguish it. This resolves the matter. Examine very carefully. This appears correct in explaining the Rambam's words, with God's help.
There, in the same seif. "For if he lit it inside and took it outside, he has not fulfilled, etc." NB: See my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5596, where I wrote in resolution of the Tur's words, and see the Bach and Taz, who wondered why this reason is needed since we hold that lighting performs the mitzvah. See what I wrote in my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5609, in siman 673, to resolve this. It also appears that a practical difference emerges for the law where a courtyard has two entrances and one must light at both. The Ran wrote that since this is only because of suspicion, no beracha is required. In my humble opinion this does not appear correct, for the baraita teaches that if one lit it inside and took it outside, he has done nothing. From the phrase "he has done nothing," it is proven that he must recite a beracha. The Gemara concludes that the reason is because one who sees says that he lit it for his own need. How then can it say "he has done nothing"? It is proven that because of suspicion one must recite a beracha. According to this, it is well understood why the Tur and Shulchan Aruch stated this reason: to prove from here that if because of suspicion one must recite a beracha, then similarly with two entrances one must recite a beracha even though it is because of suspicion. However, one can distinguish: this suspicion, that he lit for his own need, means he made no publicizing of the miracle at all, so he must recite a beracha. But with two entrances, although people suspect him of not lighting a Chanukah lamp, he actually did publicize the miracle; therefore perhaps no beracha is needed. This can be said. But the Tur may hold that the cases are equal. Examine carefully. I was uncertain in a case where everyone uses only wax and tallow candles and not olive oil, and now someone lit with olive oil, or where he was among the mehadrin and held many lamps in his hand corresponding to the days: do we say that here it is not relevant to say that one who sees will think he took it for his own need, since this is not the usual lamp for his own need and it is not usual to use many lamps, for one would suffice, so perforce it is for Chanukah; or even in such a case we decree, because the Sages did not distinguish in their enactment. This requires study in practical law. Examine carefully.
