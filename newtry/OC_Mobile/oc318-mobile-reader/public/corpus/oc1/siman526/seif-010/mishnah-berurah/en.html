(48) The ruling does not appear to refer to a definite nefel, because such a child may not be buried on Yom Tov. In that case there would independently be no question of performing brit milah, because the custom is to perform the act at the grave, as the Mechaber writes in Yoreh De'ah, siman 263, seif 5. Rather, the discussion concerns the doubtful nefalim of seif 9, whom we treat for purposes of burial with the presumption of viable children and whose burial is therefore attended to on Yom Tov. Even so, one may not permit an additional act merely to fulfill the custom of removing the orlah, because that is not one of the needs of burial. (49) "And it is forbidden to bury him." According to the explanation of the Mechaber given above, Rama's wording is not entirely precise, because Rama does not dispute the case of seif 9. It may be that because the Mechaber wrote simply "nefalim," Rama wished to prevent a mistaken inference that only removal of the orlah is forbidden while burial remains permitted; Rama therefore teaches that one who has the definite status of a nefel may not be buried on Yom Tov either. One may alternatively say that even regarding a doubtful nefel such as the case in seif 9, Rama holds that it is preferable to postpone burial until after Yom Tov so that the orlah can be removed before burial. Under this approach, one buries a doubtful male nefel only after that act has been performed, while a female nefel may be buried without this issue. Some later authorities write accordingly. If, however, a child did not receive brit milah because of illness or a similar reason and then died after 30 days, he is certainly buried on Yom Tov, and the orlah may then also be removed as part of the burial procedure. Know that some later authorities disagree with the underlying rules concerning nefalim and hold that burying a nefel is itself a mitzvah. According to them, it is possible that one should at least permit burial on the second day of Yom Tov through a non-Jew. See Chochmat Adam, rule 170, seif 7.
