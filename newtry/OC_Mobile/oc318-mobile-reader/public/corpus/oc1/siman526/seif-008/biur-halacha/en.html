It is permitted to set out on the road, etc. In the gloss: "and it is permitted to bring a corpse, etc." See Magen Avraham s.k. 17, who cites the Rivash's dissenting view, because one may make a coffin only when the corpse is with him in the courtyard, and all the more here, where an observer will say he is going for his own needs. Magen Avraham objects that where the matter is publicized it is permitted, and Machatzit HaShekel explains that since they heard of it from other messengers, it is publicized. He also writes that we are not concerned that Jews will meet them on the road, because Jewish travelers are uncommon on Yom Tov; however, if the corpse is not lying in the field but in a house, one should be stringent. Magen Avraham is very brief and appears to contradict himself, for in s.k. 7 he wrote that walking outside the techum is like cutting cedars, which is forbidden even for a famous person. The explanation of Machatzit HaShekel is also unclear, for the Rivash himself cites the law of a famous person in that responsum and nevertheless does not apply it here. Magen Avraham himself writes in s.k. 7 that incidental publicity is nothing. Even if we explain Magen Avraham simply, that because in our sins we are few our case is like a famous person, the first difficulty remains: for cutting cedars, a famous corpse does not help. The Rivash intended this, as is explicit there and as Magen Avraham himself copies: all the more here, where the observer will say he is going for his own needs, which is also the reason for cutting cedars. His further claim that we are not concerned they will meet Jews is likewise difficult from s.k. 7. I later found that Rabbi Akiva Eiger also asked from this seif against Magen Avraham s.k. 7 and answered that since the corpse lies in disgrace in the field, they were more lenient. This is surprising, for one who studies the Beit Yosef sees that this seif stems from the Tashbetz, who challenged the Rivash's stringent responsum; in the Rivash's case the non-Jews had locked the corpse in a cave with his clothing, and still the Shulchan Aruch rules leniently. After examining Kenesset HaGedolah, the Rivash, and the Tashbetz, it appears that s.k. 7 derives from Responsa Rivash siman 116, while s.k. 17 follows the Tashbetz's dissenting reasoning. Though Magen Avraham did not see the responsum, he partly arrived at it. We will copy its substance.
1. We are few in our places, and our dead have the status of a famous corpse, as Tosafot write. 2. Publicity was forbidden only where the matter can be done privately or delayed one day without the corpse becoming so disgraced; but in a case like this, even public action is permitted, like Rav Yosef regarding beams, where "their privacy is by day," because it is impossible. Here too, where the hour presses and one cannot wait because of concern for stench, dogs, or burning, we do not worry about publicity. 3. There is no publicity, for they go outside the techum, where only the buriers are present, and leaving the city beyond the techum is not called public action. 4. Publicity is a concern only in making a coffin or grave, which are Torah melakhot. He also writes that since no prohibition is found here, from where should we be stringent by our own reasoning in a rabbinic prohibition, all the more where human dignity is involved, and especially where there is no one to attend to him; he is a met mitzvah, for whom even a Kohen Gadol becomes impure. On a weekday we would certainly be obligated to bury him, and the same applies on Yom Tov Sheni, which is like a weekday. Such a corpse is even more severe than one in the city. The argument that in any event there is not enough time to bury him is no argument, because any Yom Tov act that hastens burial matters, since every delay increases disgrace, all the more where there is concern for dogs or burning. These are the Tashbetz's words in substance. His words include the Magen Avraham's rulings in s.k. 17 and siman 547 s.k. 10 and 7. But Magen Avraham's conclusion, to be lenient only when the corpse lies in the field and not in a house, is imprecise. It seems he thought the Rivash's case was where the corpse lay in the field, and therefore the lenient authorities feared dogs; but the Rivash explicitly states that the non-Jews placed him in a cave with his clothes and sealed the cave. Since he was in their hands, they feared that because of the great stench they might burn him or leave him for dogs. Even in such a case the Mechaber copies the Tashbetz's lenient view. If one wished to distinguish, the distinction should be whether he is in non-Jewish hands or guarded by his family, even if there is no one to bury him or no cemetery there. Perhaps, after first citing the Yam Shel Shlomo's stringency in all cases, Magen Avraham did not want to be lenient like the Tashbetz except when the corpse lies in the field.
The summary is as follows: according to the Rivash, even in his own case with several grounds for leniency, he forbids; all the more where a corpse lies in a Jewish home and it is merely impossible to bury him there, it is certainly not proper to go beyond the techum to bring him, for he compares walking outside the techum to cutting cedars. According to the Tashbetz, even for a corpse lying in his house outside the techum, if it is nearby enough that one can bring him and bury him that day, there is room to permit, because walking beyond the techum is not like cutting cedars, which is a complete melakhah, and walking outside the techum is not called publicity. Accordingly, the same applies where the corpse lies here and one must go beyond the techum to bring shrouds, a coffin, and the like; it is permitted. The Mechaber appears to uphold the Tashbetz's view, and the Rama, who cites the Rashba's responsum in the gloss, also implies that one may go beyond the techum and bring a corpse. The Rashba himself is not proof, for perhaps he allowed going because the slain person might still be alive and could be saved; but the Rama did not cite that detail, implying that for any corpse it is permitted to go and bring him, unless the Rama is speaking only on the Mechaber's case, where he was in non-Jewish hands. I found that Kenesset HaGedolah noted this and wrote that the Mechaber and Rama follow the Tashbetz. He also cites Responsa Mahari Taitatzak, who permitted even riding horses in such a case, and he objects that riding horses is certainly forbidden. Where there is concern for dogs, however, Eliyah Rabbah writes that even melakhah may be done on Yom Tov Sheni to save the corpse. Kenesset HaGedolah debates at length whether walking is permitted and concludes stringently, and it seems Magen Avraham was concerned for him. In my humble opinion, where the corpse has already been delayed, one may be lenient to go beyond the techum and bring him; likewise where the corpse lies here and has been delayed, one may go beyond the techum to bring shrouds or other burial needs in order to bury him that day, following the Tashbetz, especially since the Mechaber and Rama incline to this view. Know that all this is on Yom Tov Sheni, not Yom Tov Rishon, for a met mitzvah does not override Shabbat or Yom Tov; through a non-Jew, however, one may permit, as Eliyah Rabbah writes.
