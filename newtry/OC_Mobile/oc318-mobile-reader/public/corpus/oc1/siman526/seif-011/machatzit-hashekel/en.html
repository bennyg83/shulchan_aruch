(Seif katan 11) A rope, etc. Although Rashi appears to imply, etc., nevertheless it seems to me that this is a scribal error, etc. The Rosh writes in the first chapter of Moed Katan 8a, on the Mishnah that burial chambers and graves may not be dug on Chol HaMoed, that Rashi explains this as referring to graves for people who have not yet died, to be used after the festival, and it is forbidden because of excessive exertion. The general rule that even on the second day of Yom Tov Jews may attend to a corpse applies only to moving it or another matter involving no significant exertion, such as washing it or cutting its hair, but not digging. Rashi's words are difficult: he initially explained that the graves are for people who will die later, so why cite the law that Jews may attend to a corpse on the second day of Yom Tov, which concerns burial that same day? Moreover, his statement “but not digging” implies that one may not dig a grave on the second day, which is incorrect, for the second day was treated as a weekday. The Rosh explains that Rashi was troubled by the following comparison. If a person dies late on the second day of Yom Tov and there is insufficient time to bury him that day, Jews may nevertheless attend to him that day for burial after Yom Tov. Why, then, should one not similarly dig graves on Chol HaMoed for burial after the festival? Rashi answers that only moving and acts without significant exertion were permitted in such a case, but not digging, since there is no time to bury him that day. Similarly, one may not cut his shroud, and the same applies on Chol HaMoed. We learn from this that if one dies on the second day and they do not intend to bury him that day, no prohibited melachah may be performed for him that day, apparently not even through a non-Jew. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 82, infers from the Rosh's mention only of moving that even carrying into a karmelis is forbidden. He then distinguishes between an actual melachah, namely carrying, and cutting hair, which he does not consider a melachah. Maharshal disagrees, maintaining that cutting hair is more of a melachah than carrying into a karmelis. Rather, the distinction is between laborious and non-laborious acts. A burdensome melachah is forbidden when burial will not take place that day, whereas cutting hair, although a Torah melachah, involves little exertion and is permitted. Certainly carrying into a karmelis would also be permitted according to the Rosh. Maharshal then writes that the text is nevertheless erroneous and disagrees with the Rosh. He argues that Rashi's question cannot have been based on the rule that the second day is like a weekday even when burial that day is impossible, because perhaps Jews may not attend to the corpse at all in such a case. Indeed, Rashi's own conclusion, allowing even the cutting of a shroud and myrtle, plainly refers only to a case in which they intend and have time to bury him that day. The opening statement that the second day was treated as a weekday must likewise refer only to burial that day. Therefore the text must be corrected. The Mishnah's opening may refer either to graves for those who will die later, or to graves for those who have already died. Rashi's question applies only to the second interpretation. According to the first interpretation, which is the accepted halachah, there is no question. Consequently, neither interpretation proves that any act, whether cutting hair or carrying, is permitted when burial is not intended that day. This is because he does not need the hair, etc. Eliyah Rabbah and Chemed Moshe understood Magen Avraham to mean that because he does not need the hair, it is a melachah not needed for its own purpose.
The Tur in siman 278 rules like Rabbi Shimon, that one is exempt for a melachah not needed for its own purpose. They therefore questioned Magen Avraham's conclusion that Rambam discusses one who needs the hair, meaning that Rambam rules like Rabbi Yehudah, who holds one liable for a melachah not needed for its own purpose, as Magen Avraham writes at the end of siman 334. In my humble opinion, this is not his meaning. If so, why did he mention a melachah not needed for its own purpose only regarding carrying and not regarding cutting hair? Moreover, he concludes that carrying still bears the name of a melachah, whereas here there is no melachah at all. He would also have had to say that Terumas HaDeshen disagrees with Rivash, cited by Magen Avraham in siman 303, seif katan 22, who holds that one is liable for cutting hair even when he does not need the hair, because that was how the melachah was performed in the Mishkan. Rather, Magen Avraham means that cutting hair from a corpse is not a melachah at all, even according to Rabbi Yehudah, who holds one liable for a melachah not needed for its own purpose. His reason is as he writes later: one who circumcises an eight-month infant on Shabbos has done nothing, etc. Although cutting skin or flesh from a living person incurs liability for detaching, as Taz writes at the end of siman 336, nevertheless an eight-month infant is considered dead, and cutting his flesh is like cutting ordinary flesh and involves no prohibition. What difference is there between circumcising and cutting the skin, or cutting hair? It follows that there is no prohibition of shearing or detaching from a corpse. The Yerushalmi and Rambam, who hold one liable even for cutting from a corpse because of shearing, refer to one who needs the hair, for then he assigns significance to the act as a melachah. A similar principle is cited by Magen Avraham in siman 318, seif katan 36, in the name of Maggid Mishneh: when one does not intend the act, it is inappropriate to call it an inevitable consequence and impose liability merely because, when intentional, it constitutes a melachah. When unintentional, there is no melachah at all, since he does not seek to fashion a utensil. This resembles cutting a twig, where one is exempt unless he cuts it to use as a toothpick. Whenever the liability is because of fashioning a utensil, one who does not fashion it is exempt, even according to Rabbi Yehudah, who generally holds one liable for an unintended act. See there.
