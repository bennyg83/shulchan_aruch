Seif 11, "a dangerously ill person." It is agreed that pikuach nefesh overrides Shabbos, and likewise other prohibitions. In my Torah novellae to Parashat Emor of the year 5616 I raised a question whether pikuach nefesh overrides several prohibitions simultaneously, or whether only 1 prohibition is displaced for pikuach nefesh but not 2 together. I discussed there the Gemara in Yoma concerning feeding the patient the lesser prohibition. This also bears upon the question whether, for a dangerously ill person, it is preferable to slaughter an animal on Shabbos or to feed him neveilah. At first glance, neveilah involves only the prohibition of eating neveilah, whereas slaughter on Shabbos involves a Shabbos prohibition. Yet if several prohibitions are combined on 1 side, the calculation may change. The wording cited by the authorities, that if there is "also" a minor available, requires explanation. Chasam Sofer, Orach Chayyim, siman 25, explained it with reference to having a minor perform the slaughter, but that explanation is difficult, because the formulation should then have been more direct. It appears to me that the point is this. We rule that if a minor slaughters while adults supervise him, the slaughter is valid. We also rule that when a minor eats prohibited food on his own, beit din is not commanded to stop him, although an adult may not directly feed him the prohibition. From Yevamos 114 it appears that the Torah prohibition is specifically direct feeding. Telling him verbally is at most rabbinically prohibited, and there is room to say that by Torah law the instruction itself is permitted. Accordingly, slaughter has at least a theoretical route by which it can sometimes be accomplished permissibly, namely through a minor under proper supervision. Neveilah, by contrast, has no such permissive route for the sick adult himself; feeding it to him is allowed only because pikuach nefesh displaces the prohibition. This distinction can make slaughter the preferable course. A similar principle appears in Beit Yosef on Tur, Orach Chayyim, siman 11. The rule that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment applies only when the 2 cannot be fulfilled together in any permitted manner. If there exists some permitted method in principle, even if it is unavailable at present, the positive commandment does not displace the prohibition. Here one may reason conversely: since slaughter can sometimes be performed through a permitted route, it is halachically lighter than an act for which no permitted route exists at all. This requires careful consideration.
