Everything that people normally do, etc. This is the Ramban's language in Torat HaAdam, and the Maggid Mishneh writes that from this it implies that even if withholding that particular matter does not endanger him, it is done. In my humble opinion this requires study, for it appears that many poskim disagree with this reasoning. Rashi defines a choleh she-yesh bo sakkanah as one who would not die if this treatment were not done, implying that "danger" means he is endangered with respect to this treatment; Beit Yosef already cites him. Rashi explains similarly in Yoma 84b, and the Ran in the Rif explains like Rashi in several places. There is also proof from the Mishnah, "a sick person is fed according to experts," and without assessment he is not fed, as Tosafot, Meiri, and Tosafot Yeshanim explain. Many Rishonim explain that the Mishnah concerns a dangerously ill person; yet an assessment is needed whether fasting endangers him, although on a weekday we would certainly give him foods beneficial to the sick. Thus on Yom Kippur we require assessment, and the same applies to chillul Shabbat. Moreover, Rabbi Yitzchak holds one may not feed him until there is doubt lest he die, and even Rabbenu Tam, who disagrees, writes that although the patient does not say he will die, we fear he may faint and become endangered, for often he does not know whether he is in danger. Thus even for Rabbenu Tam it is specifically because of possible danger, not because it is good for him. So too write Tosafot Yeshanim, Tosafot in Mefanin, and Meiri. Meiri writes that for a woman in childbirth, one desecrates not only for medical treatment but even for settling her mind, because through fear she may become endangered; this too shows the basis is danger. Rashba's responsum also implies that one desecrates only to remove danger. Even from Ramban himself there is not such strong proof: when he writes that one does for him everything normally done on weekdays, foods and medicines, it is reasonable that he does not mean even things unrelated to removing danger. Since he speaks where no expert is present and they do not know what to do, he says to do all weekday treatments that, according to their understanding, remove danger. But if they themselves know that a thing would not remove danger even on a weekday, they may not do it. His mention of foods and medicines refers to light foods customarily given so the sick person not weaken, for withholding them can sometimes strengthen the illness and lead to danger. I bring proof from Ramban himself in Torat HaAdam, where he says that settling the mind of an ordinary sick person permits chillul Shabbat only in a matter through which he may become endangered. It is thus proven that settling the mind of a woman in childbirth is only because Chazal knew she may be endangered by it. So too Meiri and Ran write that we do not require doctors to say with certainty that warming him will heal him; even if it is doubtful, we warm him. This excludes only doubtful effectiveness, but if it is certain that it merely benefits him and does not remove danger, one does not desecrate Shabbat. This is also the plain sense of the sugya in Ein Mefanin: one might have thought a fire is made for a woman in childbirth but not for another sick person, although it is certainly good for him, because he is not endangered by that until the Gemara concludes that for a sick person too there is danger. Especially according to the Rambam, even in the conclusion one does not make a fire for a sick person, only for a woman in childbirth because cold harms her greatly. The Maggid Mishneh, however, understood the sugya as speaking of a non-dangerously ill patient; this is difficult, for according to the many poskim who hold the conclusion permits making a fire for a sick person, it would yield the unheard-of law that one may make a fire through a Jew for a non-dangerously ill patient. The Rambam novellae cited in Shemen Rokeach, from Sefer HaBatim, state that the Rambam's view is that one does not make a fire for a sick person even when he is dangerously ill. This is also clear in Shiltei HaGibborim, Mefanin.
Besides the Maggid Mishneh, I found some poskim who seem to hold like him. Tashbetz part 1 siman 54 writes that even kindling and burning, a full melakhah, are done for a dangerously ill person, even if the essence of his treatment is not the lamp but it is needed for his medical needs; from the baraita in Mefanin, if she needed a lamp, it appears that even for settling the sick person's mind alone, though not needed for treatment, and similarly other melakhot by which the patient is settled, they are done on Shabbat. This seems to follow the Maggid Mishneh. I also found in Meiri at the end of Yoma, s.v. "whether to give him to drink or for his treatment," that although this washing for strengthening his limbs is not urgently needed and one might say to wait so Shabbat not be desecrated, nevertheless it hastens the strengthening of his limbs and we delay nothing that benefits the completion of the patient's healing. This too seems like the Maggid Mishneh. But Meiri's view requires study, for above we cited him among those who forbid, as is explicit in Ein Mefanin. Perhaps strengthening the limbs can also sometimes remove him from danger. I also found in Or Zarua, Hilkhot Yoledet, a dispute: Rabbenu Gershom permitted warming water on Pesach night for a woman who gave birth seven days before Pesach, while R. Eliezer forbade, saying that the reasoning of this law is not bathing, for Shabbat was permitted to be desecrated only for matters of pikuach nefesh. This might imply that Rabbenu Gershom follows the Maggid Mishneh, but it is not proof; perhaps he holds that bathing too involves pikuach nefesh, as several Rishonim hold below in siman 331.
Let us return to our matter. From the poskim cited above, Rashi, Rabbenu Tam, Rabbi Yitzchak, Tosafot Yeshanim in Yoma, Tosafot in Mefanin, Meiri there, Rashba in responsum, and Or Zarua in the name of R. Eliezer, it is clear that whenever the patient is not endangered with respect to this thing, one does not desecrate Shabbat. The Ran also implies this, as he copies Rashi in all places and from what he writes in chapter 8, Shemonah Sheratzim. Ramban too, as we explained, appears to hold this. Rabbenu Gershom is not explicit as disagreeing. Thus the only grounds for leniency are the Maggid Mishneh, the implication of Tashbetz, and the implication of Meiri in Yoma, though we must distinguish his own words so they not contradict. It would therefore certainly be proper to be stringent for Torah prohibitions, especially where there is no danger. Rekanti siman 104 writes that some say a dangerously ill patient overrides Shabbat only for things which, if not done, would lead to great deterioration, but not things of little need; others permit, and the words of the forbidders appear correct. Radvaz part 4 siman 130 was asked this and answered briefly that there are opinions and he is lenient like the permissive view, but he brings no proof, only reasoning. In my humble opinion the main law is as we wrote and as Rekanti decided. Know further that even according to the forbidding view, this is only where it is clear that withholding this matter will not worsen the illness. But where there is concern that withholding it will weaken him and worsen the illness, one desecrates Shabbat for him, like Rabbenu Tam's statement regarding Yom Kippur, and as ruled below at the beginning of siman 618. According to Meiri's words in Yoma, if this act of chillul strengthens his limbs, one should also not withhold it from him, since he is dangerously ill.
That one waits, etc. Where one groans from the heart and is dangerously ill, and doctors said his treatment is to drink milk every day, it is forbidden to desecrate Shabbat with a Torah prohibition for this, as below in seif 33. Although he is endangered if he never drinks, he is not endangered by missing one time, as Rashba in Ketubot 61, Ramban in Shabbat chapter Chavit, and Milchamot there write.
