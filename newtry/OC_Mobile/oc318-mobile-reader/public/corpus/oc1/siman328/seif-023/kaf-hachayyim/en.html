He cites Taz at the end of Yoreh De'ah, siman 84, in the name of Maharshal from a responsum of Rabbi Yitzchak, who writes that it is like an internal wound and is dangerous. Although Responsa Rosh, klal 42, appears to prove that it is not considered danger to life, we rule leniently in matters of danger to life. One may rely on Maharshal in the name of Rabbi Yitzchak to feed the patient forbidden food if the treatment is established; see there. Pischei Teshuvah there, paragraph 13, cites him. See Knesses HaGedolah there, Hagahos Beis Yosef, paragraph 113, who cites this responsum of Rabbi Yitzchak in the name of several authorities and states that at times the patient is endangered by falling into fire or water; see there. Zivchei Tzedek, siman 84, paragraph 132, cites him; see there. At first glance, the same should apply to desecrating Shabbos to perform an established treatment. According to the end of this seif, however, when it is known that the illness can wait and does not require desecration, Shabbos may not be desecrated. Epilepsy is known to be an illness that can wait. The practical difference may arise when it first appears and physicians say that if immediate treatment is not given, the illness may worsen and become established in the body; it is possible to be lenient.
