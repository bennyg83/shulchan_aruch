(s.k. 3) And is in pain, etc. It should read: that part, etc.; and extracting a tooth according to one opinion, etc. In this he gives the reason that it necessarily discusses where his whole body became ill from it. For if extracting a tooth were only a D'Rabbanan prohibition, one could say that it discusses even where his whole body did not become ill and it is only a partial illness, and even so it was permitted, because for a partial illness we permit a double shevus, meaning telling a non-Jew to perform a shevus, as the Magen Avraham wrote below in s.k. 12 and above in siman 307. Therefore he wrote that it is a melacha according to the Torah, and telling a non-Jew to perform a melacha according to the Torah was permitted only when his whole body became ill, as below in seif 17. The Taz in s.k. 1 disagrees and writes: Granted, Ameimar in tractate Beitzah, folio 22, permitted painting the eye, which is a shevus, through a non-Jew, and Rav Ashi said to him, does the master not assist by closing and opening the eye, and he answered that assisting has no substance. Nevertheless, we say at the end of Makkos that both the one whose hair is rounded and the one who rounds it are liable. The Gemara asks, but the one whose hair is rounded does nothing, and it is a prohibition without an act; Rava answers that it is where he assists by positioning the hairs. Thus assisting does have substance. Only in the case of Ameimar, where initially every person's eyes are open and he does not assist at the beginning but only afterward when the eye is being painted, in such a case assisting has no substance. But in the case of one whose hair is rounded, before the one rounding begins, the one being rounded must position the hairs, and it is as though the one being rounded begins the melacha; assisting in such a case has substance. If so, the same applies to extracting a tooth, for a person's mouth is normally closed, and when the non-Jew wants to extract the tooth the Jew must open his mouth, which is like the one whose hair is rounded, where the assistance has substance. He also brought the words of Maharshal that even to extract it because of danger is forbidden, for the extraction itself is also dangerous. These are his words. The Shach in Yoreh De'ah siman 138 rejected the words of the Taz, saying that there is no distinction between whether the Jew begins the assistance or not; in every case assisting has no substance. Rather, the case of the one whose hair is rounded is different, for the Torah explicitly included him for liability, as Rashi wrote there, since it is written, do not round, in the plural, to include also the one whose hair is rounded. The Gemara asks only that even so he should not receive lashes, since it is a prohibition without an act; to this it answers that it is because he assists, meaning that through this it is considered a prohibition that has an act. But in general, where the Torah did not include the assistant for liability, we do not make him liable, and we say that because of this slight assistance he is not fit to be made liable. See there, and see the responsum of Chacham Tzvi, siman 22, regarding this.
