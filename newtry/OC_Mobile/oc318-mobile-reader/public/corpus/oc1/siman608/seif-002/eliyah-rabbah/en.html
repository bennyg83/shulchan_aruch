[2] [Levush] On the ninth of the month he might, etc. The Rif in chapter Yom Kippur brings this baraisa and concludes: perhaps he should be liable for the additions? The verse says "on the day itself"; for the essence of the day you are liable, etc., until here. This is surprising, for the Gemara asks: and the tanna who expounds "on the day itself," what does he do with "on the ninth of the month"? Rashi explains that since the verse needed to exclude the addition from punishment, we automatically hear that one adds. It answers that he needs it for the teaching that whoever eats on the ninth is considered as if he fasted, etc. If so, how did the Rif bring the derivation of "and you shall afflict" and also the tanna of "on the day itself"? I saw the Lechem Mishneh in chapter 1 of the Laws of Resting on the Tenth, who wrote at great length but did not arrive at a correct path; see there. Therefore I looked to explain it by first setting out three difficulties that bothered him. First, what is the Talmud's question? Perhaps without the derivation from "on the ninth of the month" for the main prohibition of addition, we would not have learned from "on the day itself" to exclude the addition from punishment, but for another exposition. Second, perhaps we nevertheless need "on the ninth of the month" because of Shabbos and Yom Tov, in order to understand that "you shall rest your rest" refers to addition. Third, why did the tanna repeat "etzem, etzem"? Although one is for affliction and one is for labor, mentioning one would have sufficed. Therefore it seems to me unlike Rashi's explanation. I will explain what the Talmud said there just before this: from where is there a warning for affliction? Ravina said: this tanna learns "etzem, etzem"; it is free, for if it were not free, etc.; five verses, etc.; and one is to make it free, etc. The repetition of "free, etc." and "one is to make it free" is difficult. Also, at first glance it is astonishing: since one is to make it free, let us learn that just as he was warned regarding labor, so too he was warned regarding affliction, as it learned there in that manner. If so, why does it need to say the derivation of "etzem, etzem"? If we say it is only a sign for the tanna, and this is what it means: this tanna, who holds that "etzem, etzem" is learned as free, etc., meaning one verse of the five, this is forced. There are also several other precise difficulties here, which I leave to the understanding reader. Therefore it seems to me that this is what it says: this tanna learns "etzem," meaning he learns a gezeirah shavah: affliction, about which "etzem" is written, from labor, about which "etzem" is written, that one was warned regarding affliction as regarding labor. About this it says "free," so that you should not ask that "etzem" is needed to exclude addition from punishment. Therefore it says it is free, for the tanna of the latter clause does not hold like the tanna of the first clause. And what it says, "five verses are written," is a separate matter. If so, we can say that this is the question: and the tanna of "etzem, etzem," meaning the one who learns a gezeirah shavah and that it is free, and did not learn to exclude addition from punishment, must say this is because he has no other verse for the main law of the prohibition of addition. If so, what does he do with "on the ninth"? According to this, the first tanna, who learns from "etzem" to exclude addition from punishment, holds that the derivation of "on the ninth of the month" teaches addition, unlike Rashi's explanation. Thus the Rif, who brings both, is well settled and correct. Examine carefully.
