And He created. See Taz. The view of Beit Yosef is explained in the Shulchan Aruch. The word “with wisdom” follows Rashi on Berachos 60b, who explains that the wonder itself is the wisdom, or Tosafos there, who explain the phrase “Who fashioned.” The Tur omitted both explanations, and Beit Yosef explains that the creation of man is certainly an act of wisdom. It is difficult, however, why the Tur chose an independent interpretation and omitted both Rashi and Tosafos. Beit Yosef further explains that “if one of them were to close” refers to the one special opening, the mouth, at the moment of birth, for if it remained closed even briefly one could not survive. The phrase is in the future tense because even afterward, if it were to close excessively, one could not survive. “If one of them were to open” refers to the many organs, any one of which, were it to open even after birth, would make survival impossible even briefly. Taz asks why Beit Yosef says that one should not say “hollow spaces” because creation does not apply to them, while the same objection should apply to “openings.” Bach further asks that Scripture says, “Who creates darkness,” although darkness is merely the absence of light. It appears to me that creation can indeed apply to something without tangible substance, such as openings and darkness, because they nevertheless constitute a created function: through openings substances enter and leave, and the removal of light produces darkness. Hollow spaces differ, because the cavity comes into being automatically through the formation of the surrounding organ. This explains why Beit Yosef and the Shulchan Aruch mention “openings,” such as the mouth, and “hollow organs,” such as the internal organs. With regard to openings they do not mention organs, because the reference is to the openings themselves, to which creation applies. Accordingly, the wording “He created within him,” rather than “He fashioned,” is fitting, because fashioning applies only to something tangible, whereas creation may apply even to something tangible, as in “He created man.” Taz further asks that the Tur cites the Gemara in Niddah, which states that in the womb the navel is open and the mouth is closed. This implies that “if one were to open” refers to the navel at birth, just as “if one were to close” refers to the mouth remaining closed. If the reference were to internal cavities that might open later, why use the future tense? Taz therefore explains that the Tur does not follow Rashi’s division. Rather, “hollow spaces” refers to the mouth and navel themselves, while “openings” refers to the intestines. In the womb the navel and intestines must be open, while the cavity of the mouth must be closed; after birth the arrangement must be reversed. This is the wondrous wisdom of the Creator expressed in the words “Who fashioned with wisdom.” The doubled wording “openings, openings” teaches that the two are not open at the same time. At one stage one is open and the other closed, and at another stage the reverse is true. The same organs are thus described both as openings and as hollow spaces, depending on their condition. It is therefore accurate to say, “If one of them were to open or one of them were to close,” both in the womb and after birth. The author of the Shulchan Aruch, however, explains the Tur’s “hollow spaces” like Rashi and “openings” according to the Gemara in Niddah, referring only to the mouth. This yields the difficulty that “one of them” has one meaning in the clause concerning closing and another meaning in the clause concerning opening. See Rashi on Berachos there, who explains that opening refers to the hollow spaces and closing to the openings. His point is to exclude the reading that “hollow spaces” means the organs themselves. Just as “closing” refers to the openings, not to pierced organs, so “opening” refers to the cavities, not to the organs. I have elaborated according to my limited understanding, though to comprehend the intent of the holy authorities I still require clarification. See what I wrote in Mishbetzos Zahav.
