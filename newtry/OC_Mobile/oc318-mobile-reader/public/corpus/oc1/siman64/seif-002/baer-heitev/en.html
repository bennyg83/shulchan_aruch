One verse. This does not mean specifically one verse; even if he omitted a single word, he likewise returns to that point. So writes the Rosh.
The paragraph. Meaning, if he knows that he recited Shema with concentration, he returns to VeAhavta. But if he does not know whether he recited Shema with concentration, it is obvious that he returns to the beginning. Bach; Magen Avraham. The Taz writes: It appears obvious that if this occurred to him in the first paragraph, he does not return and begin with Shema Yisrael, since according to one explanation there is a prohibition in reciting the paragraph and repeating it; rather, he begins with VeAhavta. It appears to me that this too concerns a case where it is known that he read the first verse. For if he does not know whether he read even the first verse, then even assuming that he certainly read it, he certainly did not read it with concentration, and he must return from the first verse onward. Otherwise, he would be reading out of sequence. Why, then, specify that he does not know where he erred? Even if he knows, since he did not read the first verse with concentration, he must return from the beginning of Shema onward. Rather, it necessarily concerns one who concentrated during the first verse, but afterward read without concentration, erred, and does not know where he erred; he returns to VeAhavta. This is the intent of the Taz, as stated by the Bach and Magen Avraham. The Yad Aharon understood that the Taz disagrees here with his father-in-law, the Bach; see there. He was not precise.
