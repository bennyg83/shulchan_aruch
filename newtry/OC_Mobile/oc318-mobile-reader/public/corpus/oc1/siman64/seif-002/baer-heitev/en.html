In it — specifically when he erred; but if deliberately or intentionally [he skipped], he returns to the beginning. Pri Chadash.

One verse — it is not specifically [only] one verse, but even one word as well he returns there — thus wrote the Rosh.

The section — meaning: if he knows whether he said Shema with intention, he returns to v'ahavta; and if he does not know whether he said Shema with intention, it is obvious he returns to the beginning — Beit Chadash, Magen Avraham. And Taz wrote it appears simple that if this happened to him in the first section he does not return, for he begins Shema Yisrael, for according to one explanation it is forbidden to say the section and repeat it; rather he begins v'ahavta — end quote. It seems to me that it also deals when it is known he read the first verse: if he does not know [about kavanah] even on the first verse, then even if you say he read the first verse, he certainly read it without intention and must return from the first verse onward; for otherwise it would be reading out of order — and why specify that he does not know where he erred? Even if he knows, nevertheless since he did not read the first verse with intention he must return from the beginning of Shema onward. Rather, necessarily it deals where on the first verse he had intention and afterward read without intention and erred and does not know where he erred — he returns to v'ahavta; this is the meaning of Taz, and as Beit Chadash and Magen Avraham wrote; and the author Yedei Aharon understood that Taz disagrees with his father-in-law Beit Chadash on this — see there — and he was not precise.
