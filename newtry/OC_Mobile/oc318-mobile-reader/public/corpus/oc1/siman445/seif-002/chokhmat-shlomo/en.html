"And if he gave it to a non-Jew before the sixth hour, he need not destroy it, etc." NB: I saw that the Chok Yaakov and Be'er Heitev brought in the name of Responsa Bach that it is forbidden to give a large amount of chametz to a worker or to a non-Jewish servant whose food is upon him, because this is like paying one's debt with issurei hana'ah. His words are very astonishing. Paying a debt with issurei hana'ah applies when, at the time of payment, the object is already forbidden in benefit. But if at the time of payment it is permitted, what does it matter that afterward it will become forbidden in benefit? Would it be forbidden to pay a non-Jew a debt with chametz before Pesach, even where the debt is not yet due? So too here: since the non-Jew receives it and releases him from his obligation to feed him before the issur begins, and if he had sold it to the non-Jew and the non-Jew became indebted to him it would be permitted, here too it is as though he sold it to the non-Jew, the non-Jew became obligated in money, and with that monetary debt he released him from his food obligation. It is like giving him money to buy food, and even if he buys chametz it is permitted, as written in siman 450. Moreover, there are those in siman 450 who permit telling a storekeeper to give chametz to his workers and saying, "I will pay," and this is not called paying one's debt with issurei hana'ah, as the Magen Avraham wrote there, even though their food is upon him. Even those who are stringent there would agree here, for there he gives during Pesach itself, whereas giving before Pesach is certainly permitted. Therefore, whenever the non-Jew acquires it in a manner that is generally effective, it helps here too. One cannot say it remains under the Jew's liability because if it is lost he must give him other food; if so, even a small amount of chametz left into Pesach would be the same. Also, this is like accepting liability for a non-Jew's chametz in the non-Jew's domain, which is permitted. And if not, why may one give a worker a dinar to buy chametz, and according to the permissive view even tell a storekeeper to give him chametz? Once the chametz reaches the non-Jewish worker, the Jew would still be liable if it were lost, for he would need to pay another. Perforce this is like accepting liability for a non-Jew's chametz in the non-Jew's domain, which is permitted. The main point is as I wrote long ago in my novellae: liability for chametz, and likewise wanting its continued existence, is forbidden only where the liability itself begins from the chametz, such as accepting another's chametz under liability, or buying chametz for a non-Jew, or hiring oneself as a worker for chametz or yayin nesech. But where the obligation did not arise from the chametz, only from another source, and the chametz merely removes that prior obligation, this is not called liability or wanting its continued existence. The chametz is only removal of harm, like driving away a lion, which is not benefit, as Tosafot wrote in Bava Kamma regarding an animal that fell into a garden. Although paying another's debt is permitted only without his knowledge and forbidden with his knowledge, that is where he gives it directly after it has already become forbidden. But if he does not give it directly, only tells a storekeeper to give it, or gives it while it is still permitted, then at the time of giving it is heter; afterward, when it becomes forbidden, he is no longer giving it, but benefits automatically, no worse than paying another's debt without his knowledge. Therefore the Bach's words are astonishing. My heart tells me that the Bach speaks only where the non-Jew eats in the Jew's house, and these are the words of the Agudah brought by the Magen Avraham in siman 450, that he should not eat in his house because of suspicion. The suspicion is that people will say he is paying his debt with issurei hana'ah, thinking he gave it to him during Pesach. But if it is not in the Jew's house, it is certainly permitted. Why did he not give the reason that people will suspect the Jew of violating bal yera'eh? Since by Torah law bitul alone suffices, presumably he nullified it, for one does not abandon the permitted and eat the forbidden. Although it remains rabbinically forbidden, regarding rabbinic marit ayin it is permitted in private chambers, as the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 301. Therefore the Bach used the reason of paying a debt with issurei hana'ah, which is a Torah issur and applies even in private chambers. This appears correct to me in the Bach's intent; otherwise his words have no explanation. In any event, for practical law this is a complete heter, except that the non-Jew should not eat it in the Jew's house because of suspicion, as the Agudah wrote. Later Responsa Bach came to my hand, and it is not in siman 147 as the Chok Yaakov wrote, but in siman 143. In truth it all follows the stringent view in siman 450; according to those who permit there, all the more this is permitted. Thus the Chok Yaakov's words are astonishing for bringing this without qualification. The Bach's own proof is also astonishing, for he cites the Agudah's stricter case that it is forbidden to give a worker a dinar to buy chametz. In my opinion they are not comparable: there it is during the time of issur, while here it is still during the time of heter. Can a person not pay before the due date if the creditor agrees to accept and release him from the debt? To avoid leaving the matter without proof, I bring proof from Pesachim chapter 1: Rav Gidel said in the name of Rav that one who betroths with wheat of Kurdanya from the sixth hour and onward, we do not take the kiddushin into account. Why did he not teach a greater novelty: even if he betrothed before, during the time of heter, but stipulated that the kiddushin take effect only at the sixth hour, we do not take them into account, since when the kiddushin take effect the chametz is forbidden? Especially according to those who explain that after the sixth hour it is Torah-forbidden, why not teach this? Perforce, in such a case she is betrothed, for Rav follows his view in Kiddushin 59 that even if the money was consumed, she is betrothed because it was consumed in her domain. So too here, the chametz already became hers and only a monetary debt remained; he is not benefiting from issurei hana'ah but from the debt, which is heter, and she is betrothed through the debt, not through the chametz. Even if the chametz is physically in her hand, it is as though absent. All the more, giving chametz to a worker during the time of heter is not considered benefiting from issurei hana'ah. This is clear and correct, with God's help. Therefore the Bach's words in this matter do not stand. Examine carefully.

"But if he burned it from the sixth hour and onward, since it is forbidden in benefit, he may not heat an oven or stove with it, etc." NB: A case came before me: a woman threw challah into the fire on Shabbat HaGadol before Pesach and thought it had burned. Later they swept the coals from the oven, left them for Pesach, and used those coals to cook pots. They then found the challah among the coals. I ruled to permit the cooked foods, except for the pot that was adjacent to the challah, where it was likely that the challah touched the pot; that one I forbade. My reason was that if one charred it before its time, it is permitted in benefit after its time. If we knew the challah had been well charred before Pesach, there would be no concern at all. Since we do not know, we are concerned perhaps it was not thoroughly charred, as the Rosh wrote regarding muries. Nevertheless, the requirement that it be unfit for a dog applies only when it remains intact. Through mixture, it suffices if it became unfit for human consumption, as is proven in siman 442. Here too, although it may not have been thoroughly charred, it was certainly unfit for human consumption. Since zeh v'zeh gorem is like a mixture, and for that reason zeh v'zeh gorem is permitted in issurei hana'ah because of nullification, this is chametz through mixture, where being unfit for human consumption suffices. The Rosh's concern lest it was not thoroughly charred follows his view that Rabbi Shimon permits only where it became mixed on its own, not deliberate mixing and keeping; deliberate mixing is like the item remaining intact, and for an intact item it must be unfit for a dog. But where it mixed on its own, it is through mixture and being unfit for human consumption suffices. Moreover, the Tur permits benefit even with muries, proving that he is lenient and is not concerned lest it was not thoroughly charred. Especially since there are views that even with chametz, zeh v'zeh gorem is permitted, one may rely on this here as a double doubt: perhaps zeh v'zeh gorem is permitted, and perhaps it was well charred. But where the pot was close enough that the actual challah likely touched it, there is concern for imparted taste, and this does not fall under zeh v'zeh gorem. There one should be concerned lest it was not well charred, and some of the above reasons do not apply; the Magen Avraham also forbids such a case even in benefit. Therefore I did not have the heart to be lenient there, and I forbade that dish while permitting the rest. This is correct, with God's help. Since in this siman I discussed at length in my Sefer HaChayim the law of zeh v'zeh gorem and the law that where something is not nullified even in a thousand, zeh v'zeh gorem is also forbidden, I will copy here what arose in my mind while learning the Rambam in Hilchot Temurah. The Rambam writes in Hilchot Temurah 3:5: one who consecrates an animal with a temporary blemish, and it was redeemed, and afterward he made temurah with it after redemption, it is a temurah but is not offered. The Lechem Mishneh wondered: in Bekhorot chapter 2 we say that its offspring is forbidden only where it conceived before redemption and was born after redemption, but if it conceived after redemption it is chullin. The same should apply to temurah, and offspring of sanctified animals is more stringent than temurah, for the offspring of partners' korbanot is sanctified while partners do not make temurah. In my humble opinion this is not difficult. In Pesachim we say that although zeh v'zeh gorem is permitted, in hekdesh it is forbidden because even in a thousand it is not nullified. The reason offspring of a korban is permitted when it conceived after redemption is zeh v'zeh gorem. If it conceived before redemption, it is not considered zeh v'zeh gorem because it has a heter through redemption and is a davar sheyesh lo matirin. But if it conceived after redemption, since it has no further heter, it is zeh v'zeh gorem and therefore is not considered offspring of a korban. With temurah, however, since it is at least the temurah of a korban and not zeh v'zeh gorem, even after redemption it is forbidden. With partners' korbanot, since they have redemption, they are not zeh v'zeh gorem and are considered offspring of a korban; in that respect offspring is more stringent than temurah. But where it is zeh v'zeh gorem, it is not offspring of a korban at all, and temurah is more stringent. This resolves it. I was also uncertain in these Rambam cases, where he says it is temurah, whether one also receives lashes; this requires study. See the Magen Avraham here, who wrote that where even the smallest amount is forbidden, zeh v'zeh gorem is also forbidden. Regarding the difficulty why this differs from davar hama'amid, where even in a thousand it is not nullified and nevertheless zeh v'zeh gorem is permitted, see what I wrote in my work on Avodah Zarah, Avodat Avodah, fol. 68b. Examine carefully.
