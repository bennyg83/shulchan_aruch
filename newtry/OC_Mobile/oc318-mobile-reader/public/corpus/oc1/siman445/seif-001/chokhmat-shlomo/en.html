Se'if 1. How is chametz destroyed? He burns it or crumbles it, etc. Note: see Magen Avraham s.k. 1, who brought the language of the Rambam at the end of Hilchos Pesulei HaMukdashin, that all items that are buried should not be burned, for although you are stringent by burning them, you are lenient with their ashes, etc. This is difficult to me from Pesachim folio 27: why did the Rabbis say to Rabbi Yehuda, any inference whose beginning is stringent and whose end is lenient is not an inference, for if he did not find wood to burn it, etc.? Without this they should have said more strongly: granted, you are stringent by burning them, but you are lenient with their ashes, and the end is therefore lenient. Perhaps they said this to Rabbi Yehuda according to his own view, for he holds at the end of Temurah that if one wants to be stringent and burn it, he may do so, which proves that he holds it is not considered a leniency that one may come to benefit from its ashes; or he holds that even the ashes of items that are burned are forbidden. Therefore, according to him, they could not say that for this reason its end is lenient. One can say further that with this Rashi's difficulty is resolved, namely that at the time of its destruction, its destruction may be by any means. According to the above, one can say as follows: certainly at the time of its destruction, granted that its destruction may be by any means, nevertheless since according to Rabbi Yehuda it is among the items that are burned, its ashes are permitted, and that is a leniency; whereas according to the Rabbis it is among the items that are buried and its ashes are forbidden. If so, it is a leniency. Only there was room to say that Rabbi Yehuda's intent is before its time, for then even if it were among the items that are buried, their ashes are permitted, since it was charred before its time, etc. If so, burning is properly stringent, for even among buried items their ashes are then permitted. Therefore they said: if you judge regarding before its destruction, then there it is a leniency if he did not find wood to burn it, for he will sit idle. In any event, its end is lenient; examine this, and it works well. I was later told that in Sefer Or Chadash he noticed my above difficulty and answered as I wrote above, that Rabbi Yehuda follows his own view. What appears to my humble opinion is as I wrote, with the help of Hashem. Another difficulty occurs to me regarding what the Talmud says there: Rabbi Yehuda returned and derived another inference, nosar has bal tosiru, etc. This is difficult: how does he compare chametz to nosar? Granted, nosar too is a negative commandment rectified by a positive commandment, nevertheless with nosar the positive commandment applies only if he transgressed the negative commandment; but to leave it initially in order to fulfill the positive commandment is forbidden. With chametz, however, Tosafos wrote in Pesachim folio 29 that one who keeps chametz in order to destroy it does not transgress, and it is proven there that initially it is permitted to pass over the negative commandment in order to fulfill the positive commandment. If so, chametz is more lenient than nosar; what proof is there from one to the other? This requires examination. A learned person asked me: what proof is there from kodshim to chullin? Kodshim are more severe. At first glance he said well. But it appears that it works well, for we derive leniency from kodshim, as the Taz wrote in Yoreh Deah siman 3. If so, Rabbi Yehuda says in any event: if burning is stringent, then there is an a fortiori inference from kodshim, and we can properly derive chullin from kodshim by an a fortiori inference. If they will say that its end is lenient, then in any event, if burning is a leniency, we again derive it by a common feature, for we derive a leniency in chullin from kodshim, as above in the name of the Taz. Examine this well, for it is correct. There, in the same se'if 1. Note: see Magen Avraham s.k. 2, who wrote that one must say chametz is not called a positive commandment of the public, etc. See Hilchos Megillah, siman 696 se'if 7, where it is explained that meat and wine on Purim are called a positive commandment of the public; this is the opposite of the Magen Avraham's words here. See my chiddushim to Pesachim folio 92, in my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5612, folio 4a. What seemed to me to answer this is to pay attention to the language of the Shulchan Aruch in Hilchos Megillah, where he wrote that the positive commandment of rabbinic aninus does not come and override the positive commandment of words of Kabbalah, which is a positive commandment of the public. Why did he not write the reverse, that the positive commandment of the public, which is from words of Kabbalah, comes and overrides the rabbinic positive commandment of the individual, as we generally say that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment, or that a severe positive commandment overrides a lighter positive commandment? Rather, the matter is as follows. Where the mitzvah requires intent for the sake of the mitzvah, according to the halachic decisors who hold that mitzvos require intent, or even according to those who hold that intent is not required, that is only because an ordinary act is considered with intent; but if he intends for an optional matter, he certainly has no fulfillment of the mitzvah. In such a case, if he intends for the sake of the mitzvah, the positive commandment overrides the negative commandment or the lighter positive commandment, and the overriding is called by the name of the positive commandment that he comes to fulfill. But where the act is such that even if he does not intend for the sake of the mitzvah, but intends for an optional matter, it also helps and is considered a mitzvah, and you come only to forbid it because of another positive commandment or negative commandment that prevents him from doing it, then the overriding is considered to be on account of the positive commandment that prevents the act, which overrides the other positive commandment. Therefore it works well. On the contrary, from there there is a proof for the Magen Avraham's words: it is not called an actual positive commandment of the public, since the individual does not discharge the public's obligation. Therefore, for the severe positive commandment to override the lighter positive commandment, it must be extremely severe; and where there is only a slight severity, it has no power to override the other positive commandment. But where it is the reverse, that the positive commandment that prevents the act is called the one that overrides the other positive commandment that obligates the act, then whenever the positive commandment of the act is somewhat severe in any degree, the second positive commandment has no power to override the slightly more severe one. Set this one against that one, and it remains optional. Granted, this one has the advantage of being passive inaction, but the other has another advantage, that it has some slight stringency; therefore neither overrides the other, and it remains optional. And where it is optional, it is permitted to do this act as an optional act. But where, as an optional act, he does not fulfill his obligation, and he must do it for the sake of the mitzvah, there one cannot say: set this against that and it becomes optional. Rather, one must say that the positive commandment that obligates the act overrides the positive commandment of passive inaction; for this, it must be very severe, and without that it does not override. Therefore, regarding Purim, since it is not an actual mitzvah of the public, for the individual does not discharge the public's obligation, one cannot say that this positive commandment overrides the positive commandment of aninus, although it is somewhat a mitzvah of the public and words of Kabbalah are like Torah words. Rather, since on Purim one does not require doing the act for the sake of the mitzvah, and if he eats and drinks as an optional act he has also fulfilled it, it is enough if we say that rabbinic aninus has no power to override this positive commandment. Granted, aninus has the advantage of passive inaction, but Purim has the advantage that it is somewhat public and, together with the fact that words of Kabbalah are like Torah words, therefore neither overrides the other, and it remains optional. Therefore the Shulchan Aruch was precise in its language, that he is permitted in meat and wine, meaning that it is optional; to forbid it is impossible, for then the positive commandment of aninus would override the positive commandment of Purim, and that is impossible. But with chametz, where he must intend for the mitzvah of destroying the chametz, as an optional act it is certainly forbidden to destroy it on Shabbos; rather, we would say that the positive commandment of chametz overrides, in the reverse direction, the positive commandment of Shabbos. In this, as long as it is not an actual positive commandment of the public, it is not considered a positive commandment of the public; and since the positive commandment of Shabbos also has the advantage that it is passive inaction, they remain equal, and it is forbidden to destroy it for the sake of the mitzvah, for as an optional act this does not apply. This works well with the help of Hashem; examine it. In this way one may answer his difficulty in Pesachim folio 92, which is in my above-mentioned work, why the Talmud does not say that for this reason an onen eats the Pesach offering, because the positive commandment of the public in Pesach comes and overrides the positive commandment of aninus. According to the above it works well, for with Pesach, where it must be specifically for the sake of the mitzvah, the overriding is called by the name of the positive commandment of Pesach; and since it is not actually public, because the individual does not discharge the public's obligation, it is not strong enough to override the positive commandment of aninus, etc., and it works well. However, the sugya there is still not settled, for we say in that tractate, folio 59, that the positive commandment of Pesach, which has kares, should come and override the positive commandment of completion, which does not have kares. If so, there too on folio 92 it should have said the same. Perhaps there, regarding offering, where there is actual kares, it is considered severe, for if he does not bring the offering he is liable to kares. But here, where it speaks of eating, since it follows the view that eating does not prevent fulfillment, even if he does not eat he will not be liable to kares; therefore it is not considered severe. Nevertheless, regarding the rule that the Sages did not uphold their words, it properly says in a place of kares, meaning that since in any event the Pesach offering has a severity that includes an aspect of kares in its performance, therefore even regarding eating they did not uphold their words. But regarding aninus, which has no aspect of kares at all, they did not uphold their words at all. This works well with the help of Hashem; examine it well.
