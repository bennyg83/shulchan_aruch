[1] Whether behind him, etc. The Levush omits this. Nachalas Tzvi explains that he follows the Tur, who derives a numerical allusion of twelve amos and therefore permits sitting behind the person praying, for otherwise the total would be sixteen. He questions the Rama for adding this to the Shulchan Aruch’s words, which follow the Tur. This poses no difficulty, for the Bach already explains that “behind him” is included among all the sides. In Eliyah Zuta I resolved the question from the numerical value twelve by explaining that the two lateral sides are counted as one. Moreover, even according to the view that one must distance himself before the person praying as far as the eye can see, it is obvious that sitting behind him is forbidden, since the area before him is not included in the twelve. I likewise found this in the Perishah in the name of the Darchei Moshe. Further, according to the Levush’s explanation that the verse “with you in her domain” is alluded to here, the area behind him is also within his domain. Lechem Chamudos, Perishah, and Bach likewise conclude that sitting behind him is forbidden. Behag, in the chapter Tefillas HaShachar, writes: A baraisa teaches that if two are sitting and one is praying, it is an unfavorable sign for the person praying; if two are praying and one is sitting, it is an unfavorable sign for the person sitting. Rather, he should distance himself five amos and pray, as it says “with you,” the word being written with a hei, indicating five. This applies when he sits occupied with mundane matters, but if he sits reciting Krias Shema or matters of prayer, there is no unfavorable sign. These are his words. This is surprising, as neither the Gemara nor the poskim mention it, and it requires study.
[2] There are those who permit, etc. The Levush likewise rules this way, because the Beit Yosef writes that it is possible all authorities agree to it, and the Bach rules likewise. The Perishah, however, rules that one must stand whenever he is not occupied with material belonging to the order of prayer. When the Semag and Semak mention one engaged in Torah, they mean Torah passages included in the order of prayer. This also appears from Behag and most poskim. A person who fears Heaven should therefore act stringently and stand. If he does not articulate words with his lips but merely contemplates Torah, it is certainly forbidden to sit. If he is studying laws of proper conduct, the Bach writes that all agree it is forbidden.
[3] There is one who says, etc. The Beit Yosef cites this only in the name of Ohel Moed and therefore presents it as “there is one who says.” The Perishah, however, writes that this also follows from the Tur. The Tur writes at the end of the siman that one may not pass before people praying within four amos. If the prohibition stated here against sitting were limited to four amos, standing there would be permitted; why, then, would passing before him be forbidden? It is unreasonable that passing should be worse than standing. Moreover, the prohibition against passing follows a fortiori from standing, as the Beit Yosef writes below. It must therefore be that sitting before him is forbidden as far as the eye can see, whereas within four amos even standing and passing are forbidden. It appears that there is a slight omission in his words. His intent is that if sitting before him were forbidden only within four amos, standing within those four amos would be permitted, as implied by the specific mention of sitting and by the derivation from “I am the woman who stood with you,” where Eli stood. Passing would then certainly be permitted. It must therefore be that sitting is forbidden as far as the eye can see, while standing and passing within four amos before him are forbidden. Yet his proof is questionable. Standing may be permitted even directly before him and within four amos, whereas passing is forbidden because the movement disrupts his concentration. As for his citation of the Beit Yosef that passing is derived a fortiori from standing, in my humble opinion he did not fully grasp the Beit Yosef’s words. The Beit Yosef’s alternative argument, that if standing is permitted then passing is certainly permitted, refers to the lateral sides, where the concern is not disruption of concentration but the appearance that one does not accept the yoke of Heaven. If standing there is permitted, passing surely does not create that concern. Directly before him, however, where disruption of concentration applies, passing specifically is forbidden, while standing may be permitted, since merely standing does not disrupt him and does not appear to reject the yoke of Heaven because the person is not sitting. Nor can proof be brought from the Tur and Levush in seif 7, who write that standing at the sides is permitted, implying it is forbidden before him. Had that been a proof, the Perishah should have cited it. Plainly, it is not a proof, as explained below.
[4] Even if he is engaged, etc. The Levush explains that he appears to be bowing to the person praying. Above, siman 90, seif 24, however, even one who prays behind his teacher does not appear to be bowing to him when outside four amos. One must say that sitting, even before another person, is worse. In truth, the Levush introduced this reason on his own. It appears that the prohibition applies only directly before him, but not to the forward lateral sides. This is unlike the lateral sides of the kohanim in siman 128, seif 28. See above, siman 79.
