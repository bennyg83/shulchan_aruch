[5] And this person stood beside him, etc. Olas Tamid infers from this wording that it applies specifically at his side, whereas directly before him one must rise even if he had already been sitting. This is somewhat difficult, for the preceding seif likewise says that if the person sitting beside the one praying is weak, he is permitted to remain seated, implying that directly before him he may not. Moreover, Lechem Chamudos, page 39, explains that one person cannot render forbidden to another a place that was previously permitted to him, comparable to an excommunicated person whose four amos are forbidden, yet one already sitting there need not rise. This would imply that even directly before him one may remain seated if he was there first. It is possible, however, that this case is more stringent because sitting directly before him is forbidden as far as the eye can see and appears like bowing. The Tur asks that the law is derived from Channah, yet Scripture says “and Eli was sitting,” indicating that he was already sitting. The Beit Yosef answers that “sitting” there means remaining. The Bach answers that Eli was sitting in the sanctuary of Hashem, and because it was a place fixed for prayer, he was required to rise even though he had been sitting first, since Channah had not entered his private domain. The same applies in a synagogue designated for prayer. The ruling that one who was sitting first need not rise refers to a house not specially designated for prayer. On this basis I explained in Eliyah Rabbah, siman 334, the Rama’s statement in seif 2 concerning the custom to expel an excommunicated person from the synagogue so that he not pressure individuals who must distance themselves four amos from him. Since it is a synagogue, even one already sitting must rise. The Shach explains instead that this refers to those who newly entered his four amos. That interpretation is strained.
