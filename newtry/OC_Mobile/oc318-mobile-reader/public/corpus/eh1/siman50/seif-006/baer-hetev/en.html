(ד) Coercion. There is no distinction between a case where he obligated himself to give the groom the bride and dowry, and if he does not fulfill this he will pay a penalty, as people now write, and a case where he obligated himself in a certain sum without condition and the groom gave a document saying that if he gives him the bride and dowry the debt is waived. If the bride does not wish to proceed, the father is exempt from the penalty, for there is a clear assessment that he obligated himself only if his daughter would not refuse. Beit Shmuel; see there. However, Taz wrote that where he gives a plain debt document he is obligated to pay, and the Mechaber in Choshen Mishpat was uncertain about this; see there. If one sent his son to seek a match for his daughter and did not give him power of authorization, and the son went, found a match, and bound himself with a penalty, and the father refuses, both are exempt. She'erit Yosef, siman 5; Keneset HaGedolah, folio 85a. Wherever the parties are exempt from the penalty, the guarantors are also exempt. If the coercion was monetary, he is exempt from the penalty. But if he obligated himself by oath, he is liable because of the oath, for an oath cannot be exempted because of monetary coercion. The guarantors, however, are exempt, for regarding money he is exempt, and the guarantor guaranteed only the money. In Shulchan Aruch Yoreh Deah siman 232 seif 12, however, Taz and Shach wrote that if there is great monetary coercion he is exempt even from the oath, specifically where there is a clear assessment that he did not swear with the intent to give a large sum of money; see there. So wrote Rashba in responsum, siman 771; see there. Bach wrote there that one may not violate an oath because of great monetary loss, and he disagrees there with Rama, who wrote that if coercion occurred and it was possible to remove it only through a large sum of money, it is called coercion. Taz and Shach agree with Rama and disagree with Bach; see there. It follows from the law: if the coercion is monetary, he is exempt from the penalty if he did not obligate himself by oath. But if he swore, he is not exempt unless there is a clear assessment that he did not swear with that in mind.
(ה) One who pledged. The same applies if he is alive but became poor, as written in Yoreh Deah siman 232 seif 16. Beit Hillel wrote that there it speaks specifically without kinyan and without a document, but only by force of the oath; we say it is coercion, for he did not swear with that in mind. But if there was a kinyan, he is obligated to give everything he pledged. If he has only his residence, he must encumber his residence for a period of time in order to pay his son-in-law after the marriage; and if he does not pay by the time they fixed, they sell his residence; see there. See Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 104, which implies that even if there was a kinyan, see there. One must say there is a distinction between death and becoming poor. Mahari Weil speaks where the one who pledged died, while Beit Hillel speaks where he became poor; examine carefully. Beit Shmuel wrote that even if he became poor and there was a kinyan, he is nevertheless exempt; see there.
