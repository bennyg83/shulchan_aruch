(ג) That one who pledged is exempt from the penalty, for this is complete coercion. Then the guarantors are also exempt. Even if he obligated his body, nevertheless he is exempt from the penalty, since it is not in his power and will not come into his power. Responsa She'erit Yosef. Such coercion is complete coercion and is uncommon, as written in Responsa Rashba. Even if you say that he can appease her with money, even if he stipulated that if she refuses he must appease her, must he give heaps of dinars? And if you say that he should have stipulated that if she refuses he is exempt, this is uncommon coercion, for all daughters agree to whomever the father wishes. Even if it were somewhat common, he need not stipulate. See Shach, Yoreh Deah siman 232, Terumat HaDeshen siman 55, and this responsum, where his words are explicit. See Mordechai, chapter 1 of Bava Metzia, which seems not like Rashba, and Mordechai, chapter Mi Shemet. See siman 144: we hold in gittin that even common-uncommon coercion does not help, but in monetary law even common-uncommon coercion need not be stipulated, and he is exempt. Chelkat Mechokek wrote that at first glance here, where the obligation was to give the groom the dowry and bride, and if he does not fulfill this he must pay a penalty, he is exempt from the penalty because he is coerced. But if he obligated himself in a certain sum without condition, and the groom gave a writing that if he gives him the bride and dowry the debt is waived, the claim of coercion is not relevant. Nevertheless, one may say that there is a clear assessment that he obligated himself only if his daughter would not refuse. I wrote in siman 77 in the name of Hagahot Maimoniyot that when his daughter claims "he is repulsive to me," he need not pay the debt document he gave his son-in-law for the dowry; this is compared to where his daughter died, where we do not extract the dowry money from him even if he gave a debt document, as written in siman 55, for presumably his intent was specifically that his daughter benefit from the dowry money. Therefore here too, even if he gave a debt document and his daughter refuses, he is exempt from the penalty. There I wrote that even if his daughter did not say "he is repulsive to me," but said other claims, he is exempt from giving the dowry, for there is a clear assessment that his intent was to give the dowry in a way that his daughter would benefit. Although there the marriage had already taken place, nevertheless he is exempt from giving the dowry when his daughter will not benefit; all the more so here, before the marriage, where she refuses, he is exempt. However, Taz wrote there that where he gives a plain debt document like ours, he is obligated to give, because our debt document is like already having paid, since he can give it into another's hand; siman 55 does not speak of our kind of debt document. Where the coercion is that he died, one can distinguish as Chelkat Mechokek did. If the son made a match for his sister without authorization from his father, and the father does not wish to make the match, its law is like where his daughter refuses. See Responsa She'erit Yosef, simanim 2, 4, 5, and 6. There are several practical laws, and I will write them briefly: if the father sent his son to find a match for his daughter and to inform him before making the match, but the son changed and said that he had full power to act, and made the match, and the father refuses and does not want the match, both are exempt from the penalty. This is similar to what is written in Choshen Mishpat siman 182, that when an agent changes and the sender wishes to void it, the sale is void. At first glance it is difficult why the son is exempt, since he caused the embarrassment. One must say that he holds one is exempt for indirect causation of embarrassment. One may also say that this is only grama and not garmi, for although it was not in his power to act, he thought his father would agree. From this we learn that if the father says he has a written authorization from his daughter and this was false, and she refuses, both are exempt, for it is obvious that the father relied on his daughter not refusing. Wherever the father is exempt from the penalty, the guarantors are also exempt. If he was coerced by money, he is exempt from the penalty; but if he obligated himself by oath, he is liable because of the oath, for one cannot be exempted from an oath because of monetary coercion. The guarantors, however, are exempt, since he is exempt monetarily and the guarantor guaranteed only the money. See Responsa She'erit Yosef siman 78. Rashba, in responsum siman 775, exempts even from an oath because of coercion where his daughter refuses, as written nearby, even if he can appease her with much money, because there is a clear assessment that he did not swear with this in mind.

(ו) The heirs do not need to fulfill it and are exempt from the penalty. The betrothed man is also exempt from the penalty, even though it was made with a kinyan, for there was no actual right for the son or daughter, since the kinyan was not made to give them a right, as written nearby. Therefore the heirs do not need to fulfill it and are exempt from giving the son or daughter this sum, except for whatever reaches her as part of their inheritance. In the following siman it is explained that where the son or daughter acquired, namely matters acquired by verbal undertaking, they must fulfill it and we collect from them; and if there was a complete kinyan to grant them rights, we collect even from encumbered property. This speaks where the father left assets to pay what he pledged, for otherwise it is obvious that the heirs are exempt. In Nachalat Shivah he left requiring study the Rav's citation in siman 113 of Raavad's view that we assess intent, for here too one might say that we assess that he undertook only while alive. Nevertheless, the main rule is that where the children acquired by proper undertaking, the heirs must fulfill it.
