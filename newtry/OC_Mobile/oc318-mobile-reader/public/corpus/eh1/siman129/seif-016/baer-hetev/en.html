(א) Who is called. See Maharam Mintz, siman 36, regarding one whose name was Meshulam, whose nickname was Zalman, and afterward he was called Zamil because of his beauty. One writes Meshulam, for that is his principal name and he is called to the Torah by that name; and one writes "who is called Zalman" and "who is called Zamil." The nickname Zalman is placed first because it is the nickname of Meshulam. Beit Shmuel. He further wrote there that where a person has two names and two nicknames, one writes each secular nickname next to its sacred name, for example: Moshe who is called Lima, who is also called Yitzchak who is called Leblig. One who has three names in the sacred tongue, such as Moshe, Yaakov, and Shmuel, writes "who is called" twice, such as Moshe who is called Yaakov, who is called Shmuel. The same applies if he has one sacred name and two secular nicknames: he writes "who is called" twice, and writes the second "who is called" with a vav, such as Meshulam who is called Zalman and who is called Zamil; see there, and see Keneset HaGedolah, folio 152a. A nickname that has been entirely forgotten need not be written; for added precaution one should write two gittin: in one he writes the principal name, and in the second he writes the principal name "who is called so-and-so." Maharam Mintz, siman 64.
(ב) Who is called. At first glance this contradicts what was written above, that when one of the names is a foreign-language name, one writes "who is known as." Beit Shmuel wrote that the Rav Rama's intent here is not to teach whether to write "who is known as" or "who is called"; rather, his point here is that the Hebrew name is primary. So too wrote Taz and Beit Shmuel. Where there is doubt, one writes "who is called," for the language "who is called" can also apply to a nickname, since that is what people call him. But with a Hebrew name the term "nickname" does not apply. See Beit Shmuel and Keneset HaGedolah, folio 152b. For a male one writes "demitkarei" without an alef; Darkei Moshe, Seder HaGet 63.
(ג) Primary. Darkei Moshe wrote regarding a woman who was called Simchah at birth and was later called Vreidel, not because of illness: Beit Yosef wrote that we follow the majority of the world. If most people call her Simchah, we write Simchah who is called Vreidel; if most call her Vreidel, we write Vreidel who is called Simchah. So too wrote Levush. But Darkei Moshe's own view is that one always gives precedence to the Hebrew name, and writes "who is known as" for the foreign-language name; so he wrote here. The same applies to a woman: the Hebrew name is primary. One should not object from the Talmudic cases of Yosi and Yochanan, where we follow the name used by most people, and similarly Sarah and Miriam, and likewise in Rama's responsum regarding Malka and Esther. We must say that where both are sacred names, we follow the majority of people; but where one is a foreign-language name, he holds that even if most people call him by that name, nevertheless we give precedence to the sacred name. The rule is: a name changed because of illness, even for a woman who is not called to the Torah and does not sign by that name, is nevertheless primary, and we give that name precedence, even if it is a foreign-language name and the first name was sacred, and even if most people call him by the first name. But a name changed for another reason and not because of illness: according to Beit Yosef and Levush we follow the majority and give precedence to the name by which most people call him, even if it is foreign and the second name is sacred. According to the Rav Rama, we always give precedence to the sacred name. See Beit Shmuel.
(ד) Responsum. Regarding the son of an apostate who divorced, Maharshal wrote that one writes concerning his father that he is an apostate, "and every name," etc. Taz wrote that one should not write "and every name," because of the son's honor. Shevut Yaakov, part 1, question 121, wrote about this that one should distinguish: Maharshal and Taz disagreed only regarding a son who was already accustomed in his city to be called to the Torah by his father's name. But one who never in his life was called by his father's name, because he was a minor and they called him by the name of his father's father, and he also signs himself that way, requires study how to write it in the get. He concluded that one should write "and every name and appellation that he has" concerning his father; see there.
