(א) And in each of them he has another name. One writes the name of the place of giving, and writes the name of the place of writing as "who is called." This is Rambam's language. My teacher and father-in-law, z"l, asked: here it implies that one should not write "and every name," etc., ab initio, but must specify the names, like Rabbenu Tam's view mentioned above in seif 2. Yet in seif 1 the Shulchan Aruch wrote that one writes "and every name," which follows Halachot Gedolot and other poskim. It must be that the Shulchan Aruch retracted from what he wrote above. He further asked on seif 1, where only "and every name" is required, while in siman 128 seif 3 regarding a city's name, if it has two names, one writes "and every name," and besides this writes "the city called so-and-so." This implies that "and every name" alone is insufficient, which follows Rabbenu Tam. He left this difficult. In truth, these two difficulties are one matter: the Shulchan Aruch distinguishes between personal names and place names. With a person's name, "and every name" may suffice according to one view, but with a place name one must specify, because a change of place is more severe and more likely to create error.
(ב) But if he made the place of writing primary. I will copy here one responsum that I sent to the gaon R. Yehoshua, rabbi of the holy community of Krakow, z"l. The question was as follows. Leah had remained an agunah in the holy community of Krakow for many years, about ten years, because her husband left her and did not wish to return to her, although many letters were sent after him and he paid no attention. It happened that he came to a fair in one of the Lithuanian provinces, and some people from the holy community recognized him and brought him before beit din, and he agreed to give a get. His name had been Hirsh, and he was called to the Torah by the name Tzvi; there he changed his name to Naftali, and the names Hirsh and Tzvi had become entirely submerged from him. He stated there before the av beit din, under severe oaths, that his name had been changed during illness to Naftali, and he was very pleased with this, since some people already called him to the Torah by the name Naftali instead of his former name Hirsh. Therefore he entirely discarded the names Tzvi and Hirsh and was called wholly Naftali. The beit din there were not expert in gittin and acted improperly: they made Naftali, the name of the place of writing, primary, and made Tzvi and Hirsh secondary. They erred by thinking that since the name was changed because of illness, that name was primary. The burden was then placed on the agunah, for this lawless man departed, and who knows whether they will find him again after many days. The agunah remained in confusion, for Maharik, shoresh 98, wrote explicitly that there is no distinction between one who changed his name and one whose name was changed because of illness: the name of the place of giving is primary, and Beit Yosef brought his words in siman 129.
Responsum. In Perek HaSholeach the first Mishnah states that originally they would change his name and her name, etc., until Rabban Gamliel enacted that one should write, "So-and-so, and every name that he has." The Rosh wrote that the baraita saying that he would change his name does not mean that he wrote a different name that was not his, but as the Gemara explains, that he had two names, one in Yehudah and one in Galil, for people who leave their place because of monetary concerns often change their names lest pursuers follow them. If he divorces with only one of the names, this is called a changed name: if he divorces with the name he has in the place where he stands, it is a change for the people of the place where the woman stands, for they will say she is not divorced, not knowing that her husband has this name elsewhere; and if he divorces with the name he has where the woman stands, it is a change for the people of the place where he stands, and if she comes to marry there they will say she is not divorced. Therefore Rabban Gamliel enacted that one write the name of the place of giving, which is primary because it permits the woman where she stands, together with every other name he has. This is also implied by the Gemara's statement that he must divorce his wife in Yehudah with his name of Yehudah and the name of Galil with it: the name of the place of giving is primary and the name of the place of writing is secondary. The Rosh further explained Rav Ashi's statement that this applies where he is established with two names: the two names are known to exist, but the people of each place do not know the name used in the other place, and therefore both must be written to avoid later disqualification. But if he is not established with two names, meaning neither place knows that he has another name in the other place, it is unnecessary to write "and every name," and even if afterward we discover that he has another name elsewhere, the get is valid, for we have only the name by which he established himself. The Tur wrote likewise. The matter is not that a real known second name may be ignored where it will create public suspicion; rather, Rabban Gamliel's enactment was made only where the second name is established and public enough to create concern. Where no such reputation is established, the lack of knowledge is itself what prevents the enactment from applying. But where the name of the place of giving is known and established, it must be treated as primary, and the name of the place of writing as secondary. Thus in the case at hand, one must examine which names were actually established at the place of giving and which had become entirely submerged. A name changed because of illness is not automatically primary against the place of giving; the primary rule follows actual established usage and recognition, especially where the get must permit the woman in the place where she stands. If the older names Hirsh and Tzvi were still recognized at the place of giving, they cannot be displaced by making Naftali primary merely because it was the name used at the place of writing. But if a name was entirely forgotten and not established there, it is not treated as a living primary name. The writing must therefore reflect the established names in the place of giving, with other known names added only as secondary, so that no one in either place will say this is not the husband who divorced.
