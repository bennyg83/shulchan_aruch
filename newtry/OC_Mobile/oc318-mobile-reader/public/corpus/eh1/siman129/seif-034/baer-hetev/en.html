(א) With an alef. Tiberias is written with a heh at the end and not with an alef; Responsa Beit Yosef, laws of gittin. Wherever we are uncertain about a name whether one should write an alef at the end or a heh, one should write a heh. The proof is that every such name in Scripture ends with a heh: Yehudah, Chavah, Yiskah, etc. Mahari Mintz. Keneset HaGedolah wrote about this that there is no doubt that his words apply even to names that are not in the sacred tongue, for if they were names in the sacred tongue we could see how they are written in Scripture. Perhaps he meant specifically names in the sacred tongue that are not found in Scripture. But I am astonished, for we find names in Scripture that end with alef: Shevna, Amasa, Ezra. See Maharashach, part 1, siman 46. Ilfa ends with an alef; one need not write an alef at the end except for emphatic names such as Yuta and Bila. Seder HaGet of Mahari Mintz, siman 26. Regarding the name Shevna, whether to write it with an alef or a heh, Keneset HaGedolah ruled that the name Shevna written with a bet is written with an alef at the end, but the name Shechna written with a khaf is written with a heh at the end; see there, folio 155. I found that the same rule and reason apply to place names: any place name that is not Hebrew is written with an alef at the end, for so we find place names in the Talmud: Nehardea, Pumbedita, Aspamia, Cordova, all with an alef. When one is uncertain how to write a name in a get, whether full or defective, or whether it ends with alef or heh, one may rely on earlier writings of sages, even letters of greeting, and we establish them on their presumption that they investigated the truth carefully. Keneset HaGedolah, folio 195b.
