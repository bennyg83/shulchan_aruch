Seif 14 (Two names.) One man came to have a get paturin arranged for his wife. He said that at times, when he remembered that his circumcision name was Hirsch Leib, he signed himself that way, but he was called to the holy Torah only by the name Tzvi, and everyone called him only Hirsch; that was also his usual signature. He then said he did not remember ever signing himself that way at all. Only in the registers called "distaten" they write him as Hirsch Leib, because this is his byname by the government decree, as is known that everyone has a byname; and he called himself Leib because he knew that this was his circumcision name. It appears simple that only the name Tzvi should be written here, because the name Leib has already fallen into disuse. This is certainly so if, according to his later words, he does not sign himself by the name Leib. In the distaten, as everyone knows, even one who has two names, such as Hirsch Leib and the like, is not written there with both names as names; rather, they write only his first name Hirsch and the family byname, and without the family byname they do not write at all. If so, what they wrote for him as Hirsch Leib means that everyone knows his name is only Hirsch and the word Leib is the byname. There is no doubt in this. Even if we were concerned to rely on his first words and say he meant an actual signature, still, since he has the byname Leib, even a signature does not prove anything against the name by which he is always called and by which he is called to the holy Torah. One can say that what is written in his signature, Leib, is only as a byname, for many, indeed almost most people of the province, always sign themselves with the byname, as is known. However it may be, the names Hirsch and Tzvi alone are already in the category of a nickname by which everyone calls him, and according to everyone that is valid at least bedieved. Wherever there is some need, one may rely on a nickname by which everyone calls him. Nevertheless, I did not want to act in practice on this; I sent him to the holy community of Buchach, since he was traveling there anyway to his family, and I wrote to my honored father-in-law all that he had told me. He will investigate there in the best way possible. Regarding the name Yeshayah: now in all these countries the custom is to call to the holy Torah with a vav, like the words of Beit Shmuel of blessed memory, who concludes so also regarding the name Gedalyah ben Achikam of blessed memory. But everyone calls him only Shayeh, and no one calls him Yeshayah. The signatures are written in abbreviated form, like Yesha', and there is no proof from this whether it is with a vav or not. Regarding Chananyah, Beit Shmuel wrote that when one is called by one name and people call him by another, and we find both names with a vav and without a vav, one should write Chananyah d'mitkarei Chananyahu, or the reverse. He also hinted similarly regarding Chizkiyah. If so, one might have said that since people do not call him with a vav, one should write Yeshayahu d'mitkarei Yeshayah. But Beit Shmuel, in letter samech, writes in the name of Maharshal that when he is called Shayeh one should write d'mitkarei Shayeh, and Beit Shmuel considered whether to write "hamchuneh" and whether to write a hei at the end. There would also have been room to write d'mitkarei Yedayah, since Shayeh is a shortening of Yeshayah, like Chiel from Yechiel. Regarding Sarah and Sharka, he wrote in practice that since the vowels are alike there is no concern, and so too here: whether a letter and vowel are lacking or added, as long as it resembles that word, this is not considered a change, as between Sarah and Sharka. Nevertheless, despite all this, it is proper to write only Yeshayahu, since there are doubts about the word Shayeh lechatchilah. When he is called to the holy Torah with a vav, everyone agrees there is no concern at all. So too it was testified before me in the name of my honored father-in-law. All names Yeshayah in our country are practiced alike, and especially since this is only lechatchilah, while bedieved it is certainly valid with a vav in such a case. There is even a double doubt for our lechatchilah: perhaps every name Yeshayah is only like a byname to Yeshayahu and subordinate to it; and even if they are clearly two names, when people call him Shayeh one can say it is as though they say Yeshayah or Yeshayahu, since in any event it is certainly only a shortened expression. The signature proves nothing. The unclear should be learned from the explicit, and only the version with a vav should be written; this works well according to everyone. Beit Shmuel wrote to write Shaul with the vav after the alef, and the simple custom is that he is called that way to the holy Torah. But people pronounce the shin with cholam and the alef with malei-pum, so at first glance there would be room to write d'mitkarei Sho'el. I wrote about this to my honored father-in-law, and as I recall he answered orally through a messenger to write as he is called to the holy Torah. I also remember that the author of Kedushat Levi and his son, the rabbi of Pikuv, together with the famed chassid R. Yisrael, wrote to invalidate a get in which Sho'el was written. So it seems to me now. At first glance there would still have been room to write d'mitkarei Sho'el, because it is like another name; and according to everyone the name Shaul alone is valid, since the Torah name is primary, and all the more where he signs himself that way. Nevertheless, lechatchilah it is proper to write also the name by which he is called, as Rama wrote in Responsa, siman 84. Yet in this case there is no concern to write the name Shu'el mentioned by Beit Shmuel under letter shin, because there the shin is pronounced with malei-pum and by that he has the byname Fox, unlike here where the shin is pronounced with cholam. Although there is some doubt that it means "one who asks," and the term "hamchuneh" is not appropriate since everything is Hebrew and the meaning is the same, and we have not found a personal name in which the descriptive noun sho'el is used as a proper name, still it would not have been a defect if d'mitkarei Sho'el were written. However, since even the common pronunciation of the shin with cholam does not prove that one must write shin followed by vav, for there are cholam vowels without vav, such as Moshe and Shlomo, and since the pronunciation of the alef by people does not indicate a change from the name Shaul, and since in rapid speech it sounds somewhat like an alef with patach, tzeirei, or segol, the pronunciation is not opposed to seeing it as the regular pronunciation of an alef with malei-pum, shuruk, or chirik. Since so, there is no further concern. We learn the unclear from the explicit, and we do not presume changes whenever it is possible to avoid them, especially here, where at least the name Shaul alone is completely valid. To add an explanation of the common name, if we write it with any change from Shaul, there is a concern that we are establishing him with a name he does not have at all. Today we are arranging such a get where the father of the divorcing man is named Shaul, which is even more lenient than the divorcing man’s own name. A name by which gentiles call him should apparently not be mentioned at all. Only with city names are we concerned to mention also what gentiles call the city, since that name is common also in the mouths of Jews when speaking with gentiles in their languages. Here, however, the byname used by gentiles is not common at all in this man’s own speech. Usually even when he or other Jews speak with gentiles, they mention him by the Jewish-language name. There is care regarding the proper personal name of Jews not to change it to gentile bynames, as Chazal said that they did not change their names. Even if in speech with gentiles they used only the gentile byname, most speech about this man is certainly in Jewish language, and even villagers speak mostly with their households. This differs from city names, where the names used by gentiles are the majority of common speech, since Jews are the minority. But for a Jewish personal name, most speech is according to the Jewish pronunciation. The decisors wrote that, Heaven forbid, one should mention a gentile name in a get that is written according to the law of Moshe and Israel. Also, most gentile bynames are close in pronunciation and are in the category of shortened names, even where letters change, like Saye from Sarah. Perhaps patach is also related to kamatz, since grammarians wrote that patach is a derivative of the great kamatz. So too some call Fishel "Feshka" and we are not concerned. In any event, bedieved there is no concern according to everyone, and the holy name alone is certainly sufficient. There are many women called Mindia. Beit Shmuel mentioned only Mindil. Here there is room to say it comes from mende or mendeh with a pasik, especially where there is no recollection that it is connected to the name Mindil. Nevertheless, it seems one should write Mindia or Mandia, as I wrote elsewhere in the name of my honored father-in-law regarding Rizia or Razia, which depends on pronunciation, since the yud at the end is emphasized. There would be reason to write Mandia, since it is not pronounced with a long chirik but like a short chirik. But since there is a male name Mendel, and the lack of a yud after the mem indicates a patach, and they do not write Mandl, therefore there is inclination to write Mindia so that the mem not be read with a patach. Still, all this requires further study, and I have not written decisively on it at all.
