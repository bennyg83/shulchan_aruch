One who appoints his wife as a shopkeeper. The Mechaber’s words are at first glance difficult, for in this seif he writes simply that he may make her swear even on doubtful claims, while in the next seif he brings a dispute whether he can make her swear, implying that his main view follows the first opinion that he cannot make her swear, since he brings the dissenting view as "some say." In this sugya there is a dispute among the decisors. According to Rabbenu Chananel, Rabbenu Tam, the Rosh, and the Rashba, the halacha follows Rabbi Shimon: the husband cannot make his wife swear on doubtful claims, even about her acting as a shopkeeper, before she claims the ketubah. Even if money is in her hand and she says it belongs to so-and-so, he cannot make her swear, as written by Tosafot and the Rosh. When the Mishnah says that if she is not claiming the ketubah the heirs do not make her swear, it does not mean only heirs, but even the husband also cannot make her swear. The Mishnah says heirs because the first clause says that when she claims the ketubah, the heirs make her swear, and there it is specifically heirs; therefore the latter clause also uses heirs. So wrote Tosafot and the Rosh. The Bach and Chelkat Mechokek understood Tosafot differently, that "heirs specifically" means that only against heirs, where she must swear that her husband did not give her bundles, do we roll onto her an oath that she did not take for herself; but where she need not swear that he gave her bundles, she need not swear that she took for herself, except by way of gilgul. This is not correct. Tosafot mean simply that in the first clause it is specifically heirs because the Mishnah speaks where he gave her exemptions, which help against the husband but not against the heirs according to the conclusion. They did not address whether the oath that she did not take for herself exists only by gilgul. On that question the decisors dispute. If she claims the ketubah, she must swear. Some say everything depends on her claim: when she wants to collect the ketubah, she must swear even if she has no oath that her husband gave her bundles; she must still swear that she did not take for herself. Others say she need not swear that she did not take for herself except by gilgul, when she already must swear that her husband did not give her bundles. A practical difference is where he died suddenly, where there is no concern that he gave her bundles, as I wrote above; and also with a divorcee, who need not swear about bundles unless he makes that claim. According to the view that the oath about taking for herself comes only by gilgul, she need not swear that she took nothing for herself. But according to everyone, if she does not claim the ketubah and he makes a definite claim that he gave her bundles, he can roll onto her an oath that she did not take for herself, because we hold one may roll an oath where there are grounds. From the Rosh and Tur it appears that when she claims the ketubah she needs two oaths: that her husband did not give her bundles, and that she did not take from him, and these two oaths are equal, not one by gilgul of the other. So wrote the Chelkat Mechokek against the Bach, and Responsa Ralbach also follows this approach. What the Tur wrote at the beginning of the next siman, that the husband who makes his wife swear about the ketubah can also make her swear about all she handled, implies at first glance that it is only by gilgul. One may answer that the Tur there refers to her spindle and dough, where according to everyone he can make her swear only by gilgul. But regarding a shopkeeper, he can make her swear without gilgul. This is precise in Tosafot and the Rosh: the first Tanna does not make her swear about her spindle except by gilgul, and Rabbi Shimon does not disagree about the spindle but speaks of a shopkeeper. Although one could explain that Rabbi Shimon applies even to her spindle, the Tur did not understand so, so as not to create three disputes about the spindle. The rest of the seif continues according to this distinction.
