Gloss: and even seclusion is forbidden. The Mordechai learned this from Rabbi Yehudah, who said that even in the house of erusin they would recite the blessing in Yehudah, because they would seclude themselves. He explained that the first tanna disagrees only because he speaks of a place where they do not seclude themselves. He afterward also wrote that Yehudah is different, because he secludes himself with her. R. David Yechiel inferred that a man is forbidden to seclude himself with his arusah without a blessing. This implies that with a blessing it is permitted. Yet he also wrote the blessing text, who forbade to us the arusot, and explained that just as the sages decreed against seclusion with an unmarried woman, they also decreed against seclusion with arusot. This is difficult: if an arusah is forbidden even in seclusion, does a blessing make her a nesu'ah? Necessarily the expression is not literal; it means before the blessing. The Maggid Mishneh wrote the opposite on the Rambam's language cited in the Shulchan Aruch, that an arusah, etc., is forbidden. This is explained in many places, and it is obvious that it is rabbinic, the proof being the custom of Yehudah, where they secluded themselves; if it were Torah law, seclusion would not have been permitted. He apparently brings proof from Rabbi Yehudah because it was obvious to him that even after the blessing she remains an arusah. If so, what is the status of this blessing? One must say that specifically a kallah, meaning an arusah, without this blessing is by rabbinic law like a niddah; and a niddah, as long as he has not had intercourse with her, is forbidden in seclusion. But with the blessing she is only under the ordinary prohibition and not like a niddah, and therefore she is permitted in seclusion. This also appears to be the view of R. Yechiel above, and even the gloss may be explained this way: when it says even seclusion is forbidden, it means an ordinary arusah who is without the blessing. After the blessing, although she is still an arusah and remains forbidden to him, seclusion is permitted. The Chelkat Mechokek, however, wrote that this seclusion is different, and that this is what they did in Yehudah. These are the words of Hagahot Maimoniyot in Kiddushin and Responsa Maimoniyot, Hilkhot Ishut siman 18, which wrote further that it is reasonable that a kallah without a blessing means without chuppah, but the text says without a blessing because the blessing is made at the time of nisu'in. What the first chapter of Ketubot says, that Rabbi Yehudah said they would bless even in the house of erusin, was in order that her entry into chuppah be considered from the time of erusin; this was taught in Yehudah. The essential matter depends on chuppah, for any intercourse of erusin is like zenut so long as she has not entered chuppah. Once she has entered chuppah, she is his wife for every matter, even without a blessing; nowhere do we find that blessings prevent the marriage from taking effect. Responsa Maimoniyot further wrote that what Rabbi Yehudah said in the first chapter, that they blessed even in the house of erusin because they secluded her before her entry into chuppah, means that this itself was her entry into chuppah. Shev Yaakov siman 24 wrote about this that it sounds as though he explains that that seclusion was her entry into chuppah. According to this explanation, it is obvious that one cannot prove from Rabbi Yehudah that an arusah without a blessing is forbidden in seclusion, for according to this she was a complete nesu'ah and his wife, permitted to him, and the blessing was made as should be done ab initio to permit intercourse, as explained in Shev Yaakov. All the more, this is no proof for forbidding residence lest they seclude themselves without a blessing, which is the Mordechai's law cited above. Therefore, in my humble opinion, the Rama's words cannot be explained according to the Chelkat Mechokek. Further, it is difficult to me: how can the seclusion in Yehudah have been entry into chuppah, when on Ketubot 12 it is stated explicitly that it was one hour before the chuppah and only so that his heart would be familiar with her? It follows that this seclusion was not at all with her father's intent for marriage, but only so that he be familiar, etc. It is clear in my humble opinion that if she died after this seclusion, her husband would not inherit her when she was a minor or a na'arah, for she requires chuppah with the father's consent, as stated in siman 90. We also rule in Kiddushin 46 that if she was betrothed with consent and married without consent, she may not eat terumah. The same, in my view, applies that this does not permit intercourse. How then could this seclusion be chuppah to permit intercourse? All the more difficult is the Chelkat Mechokek's explanation of the Rama, where there is no consent of her father at all even to the seclusion, only that they live together. How can a minor or na'arah become married through such seclusion? As for the Chelkat Mechokek's second answer, that he can have intercourse with her for the sake of marriage, which the Beit Shmuel also adopts and adds that the Mordechai held so, it seems that he explains even the Mordechai this way. In my humble opinion this is not so. If so, from where did the Mordechai infer that it is forbidden to seclude oneself without a blessing? Perhaps the blessing was only to permit intercourse that he would perform for the sake of marriage. Also, with a minor or na'arah, where without doubt even intercourse for the sake of marriage without the father's consent does not help, what does the blessing accomplish that permits them to live together? It implies even regarding a minor. Therefore, in my humble opinion, the explanation of the Mordechai and Rama is as above, and like the words of the Maggid Mishneh. Still, it appears from the Rambam's plain language that an arusah is forbidden rabbinically, and he does not distinguish in the prohibition of seclusion between one without a blessing and one with a blessing. It seems he does not hold like the Mordechai that the first tanna does not disagree with Rabbi Yehudah; rather, the first tanna does disagree, and even in Yehudah he was not concerned for seclusion without a blessing and did not hold that she is truly like a niddah. The Rambam rules like the first tanna. It is also possible that he holds the first tanna disputes Rabbi Yehudah about the meaning of kallah without a blessing, whether it refers to an actual blessing or to chuppah. If so, perhaps the Maggid Mishneh's proof that an arusah is only rabbinically forbidden from the fact that she is permitted in seclusion means even without the blessing, from the first tanna and the custom of Yehudah. It is also possible that even the Mordechai does not disagree with the Rambam, that is, with Shulchan Aruch seif 3, from which the Beit Shmuel infers that he does not hold like the Mordechai that blessings are indispensable. One may say that the Mordechai agrees that after complete nisu'in through chuppah and kiddushin the blessings do not prevent the marriage; only with an arusah, who is otherwise forbidden, when she is also without blessing her prohibition is more severe, even in seclusion. This is somewhat precise in the phrase a kallah without a blessing, implying that his wife without a blessing is permitted. His inference is from Rabbi Yehudah's need for a blessing to permit seclusion. Therefore the Tur and Rama, although they wrote generally like the Rambam, did not refrain from being concerned for the Mordechai, requiring a blessing for an arusah who secludes herself in order to permit seclusion. This is also proven by Tosafot's explanation: Rabbi Yehudah says, etc., and we say a kallah without a blessing, etc.; because sometimes he has intercourse with her not for the sake of chuppah, they make the blessing beforehand so that she will be a kallah with a blessing. This implies that if we knew he would have intercourse with her for the sake of chuppah, such as with an adult woman, there would be no concern, because the blessing would no longer prevent the marriage. So too it is explicit in Darkei Moshe, siman 62, end of s.k. 106, where he writes that Tosafot in the first chapter of Ketubot say that if she entered chuppah the blessing does not prevent; see there, folio 7. The concern is only that he may have intercourse not for the sake of chuppah; they held that then her prohibition without blessing is severe, like intercourse with a niddah, and they made the blessing so that at least she would be a kallah with a blessing. R. Yechiel z"l, however, was not satisfied with this, and inferred that the blessing itself permits seclusion while she is still an arusah. Shev Yaakov wrote that the explanation is this: since in Yehudah they seclude themselves, we are concerned lest he have intercourse with her not for the sake of chuppah and certainly would not bless, thereby transgressing kallah without a blessing. Therefore they instituted the blessing. But he says that the expression not for the sake of chuppah was not meant to say that if it were for the sake of chuppah it would be proper even without a blessing; the concern is only that he may come not for the sake of chuppah. This is difficult: what did they accomplish with the enactment of a blessing, if there remains concern that he may have intercourse not for the sake of chuppah? He further wrote there that according to Responsa Hagahot, which explains kallah without a blessing as chuppah, it is forced that this seclusion was chuppah and they blessed because in any event ab initio one should bless. He asked: if so, why does Rabbi Yehudah say even in the house of erusin, and why was this blessing made twice? He concludes differently regarding Rashi and Tosafot, that there had not yet been chuppah at all. His implication is that according to Tosafot, chuppah without blessing does not permit intercourse, while blessing without chuppah does permit. This is astonishing; no posek says that blessing without chuppah makes her a nesu'ah. Certainly she was still an arusah, and we explicitly bless, who forbade us the arusot. Further, according to him too, if from the time of the first blessing she was permitted to intercourse, what is the second blessing at the time of chuppah? Is it for the acquisition rights that they bless? Also, Rashi explicitly wrote, s.v. who forbade to us, that even an arusah they did not permit until she enters chuppah and with blessing. If one means that according to Rashi and Tosafot she truly was not permitted by the blessing alone, but they nevertheless made it because the prohibition depends on both, as explained in Rashi, and they did what they could to repair the matter, this is necessarily the truth in my humble opinion regarding Rashi. But in Tosafot, the explanation is as I understood from Darkei Moshe: with chuppah, where she is a nesu'ah, the blessings do not prevent; but with erusin, the prohibition is severe without blessing, and they did what they could to repair it. According to R. Yechiel z"l, however, it is more straightforward: the blessing functions only to permit seclusion. It appears to me reasonable that according to Rashi z"l both chuppah and blessing are indispensable, unlike seif 3; but the proof of Penei Yehoshua from the Mordechai is not compelling as above. He should rather have brought Rashi's words. From Rashi's first words on Rabbi Yehudah there is no proof, for they can be explained like the Mordechai, namely to permit seclusion. Shev Yaakov, however, wishes to say the opposite: that the Rambam agrees that even after nisu'in the blessing prevents intercourse, and what he wrote that she is a complete nesu'ah means only with respect to acquisition, just as even when nisu'in are made, intercourse remains forbidden according to the Maggid Mishneh. In my humble opinion this is not so, as is proven from the plain language of the Rambam. He began: an arusah is forbidden to have intercourse with until he brings her into his house, and this is chuppah. On this he proceeds: if he betrothed and brought her into chuppah, she is a complete nesu'ah. To say that his intent is that nevertheless she remains forbidden to him, Heaven forbid to attribute such a meaning to his clear language, especially since until this point he has not taught anywhere that a kallah without a blessing is forbidden. So too the intent of the Maggid Mishneh is clear when he wrote, this is obvious. The Beit Shmuel z"l wished to prove even more strongly that if the Rambam held that a kallah without blessing is literally forbidden like a niddah, it would be chuppat niddah. Shev Yaakov objects that there is no proof from niddah, for she is not fit for intercourse at all, unlike one without a blessing, where it is in his power to bless. See Ran in the first chapter of Pesachim, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 62, and see Beit Shmuel there. Regarding the phrase and he shall seclude himself with her, etc., and the opinion that chuppah is not seclusion, the Chelkat Mechokek wrote that it appears everyone agrees to this. In my humble opinion he means that although the Ran, who challenged the Rambam, understood it as actual seclusion, the Chelkat Mechokek holds that even the Rambam requires only seclusion in a private place, like the Itur's view with the attendants, brought in Beit Yosef siman 61. So too it is implied from the Rosh in Perek Af Al Pi, who wrote regarding the Rambam's statement that they may not marry until she becomes pure: his proof appears, etc. It is difficult: why not say simply that she is forbidden to marry, meaning seclusion, because she is a niddah? According to the above it is settled. Although one can press according to the Ran at the beginning of Ketubot, it is forced. So too it is proven from the Maggid Mishneh, who wrote on that language, they may not marry, that chuppah is for the sake of intercourse, and since he is forbidden to have intercourse with her and even to seclude himself with her, it is not proper that she enter chuppah. If the Ran's understanding were correct, why is it not proper? It would be impossible, as the Ran himself wrote at the beginning of Ketubot. Necessarily it is as stated, and he needs his reasoning. Alternatively, as Beit Yosef wrote in Yoreh De'ah siman 192, although she sleeps among them, nevertheless one is concerned that on one of the days the guards may not be careful and he will have intercourse with her. This too proves as above. Therefore he further wrote that for this purpose the permission of a Torah scholar when she was claimed for marriage is that he is permitted to marry her, etc. The Beit Shmuel, because he decided that the Rambam follows the Ran, wrote in siman 61 on these words of Beit Yosef that at first glance it is astonishing: if seclusion is forbidden, what marriage did they permit, since chuppah is seclusion? This is not difficult at all, as above; it is not actual seclusion, but a private place. Penei Yehoshua, siman 3, already explained even the Shulchan Aruch here this way, because it brought in siman 64 seif 5 the law of a widow. See Beit Shmuel there, s.k. 6, who wrote that in siman 61 he ruled like Rav Pappa that chuppah is seclusion fit for intercourse, and therefore here one must say that we require seclusion of intercourse, meaning actual intercourse, but the language does not imply this. Penei Yehoshua was not satisfied with this and explained as above. Although in Orach Chaim siman 339 it is clear that actual intercourse is required, nevertheless in siman 64 the plain law does not require actual intercourse, for Beit Yosef did not mention that opinion there; only in Orach Chaim did he mention and cite it. Perhaps here in Even HaEzer the main view is like the Mishnah Berurah in chapter 10. Nor can one prove from the Rambam's ruling that chuppat niddah does not acquire that he must require actual seclusion fit for intercourse, as the Beit Shmuel in siman 80 seems to imply. One can say niddah is different, for she is not fit at all, unlike this chuppah where he can remove everyone from before him, which is considered fit, in the manner stated in Kiddushin that whatever is in his power is not treated as lacking an act, as above in the name of Shev Yaakov. Aside from the Chelkat Mechokek's proofs from the Ran's difficulty, there is this difficulty: if the intent is actual seclusion, and according to the Beit Shmuel's understanding that it must be fit for intercourse, then presumably he must remain secluded for the time required for intercourse; otherwise what difference is there between not fit because of place and not fit because of time? How then does the Gemara ask in Ketubot 48, perhaps the verse excludes one who entered chuppah but did not have intercourse? If she entered such a chuppah, she is presumed to have had intercourse, as explained in siman 7 seif 12. Although one can press that it excludes where she obligates herself by admitting that she did not have intercourse, it remains difficult from Yevamot 58, where Rabbi Shimon concludes that he acquired her when she was an arusah, she then became secluded with another, and afterward entered chuppah but did not have intercourse. How can one find such a chuppah, fit to make her drink sotah waters and yet not presumed to have had intercourse? According to the Chelkat Mechokek and Penei Yehoshua everything is settled. In this, in my humble opinion, the words of Beit Shmuel s.k. 4 are not compelling. If you insist that the phrase he shall seclude himself means actual seclusion, nevertheless it appears to me that the Rambam agrees with the Itur that there is also another kind of chuppah. Such chuppah depends on local custom, each place according to its custom, like the principle that situmta acquires; he merely mentioned the essential chuppah by law, similar to the basic acquisitions. This somewhat explains his language, that this is what is called nisu'in everywhere, while he agrees that there can be chuppah beyond this where each place fixed something to call chuppah.
