They recite the seven blessings. At first glance it is difficult: what do the seven blessings accomplish, since he writes nearby that seven blessings without chuppah are ineffective? One can answer according to the Ran at the beginning of Kiddushin: according to the Rif and Rambam, after kiddushin chuppah is required, and then he may have relations with her even if he did not intend for nisuin; but before chuppah it is forbidden to have relations with her not for the sake of nisuin. If his intent is for nisuin, however, chuppah is unnecessary. Thus even according to the Rambam he may have relations with her in her father’s house, provided his intent is for nisuin. The Mordechai holds that without blessings she is forbidden, and therefore we recite seven blessings; then we are no longer concerned that wicked people may not intend for nisuin, since permission stands before him. However, the Mordechai’s view that a bride without blessing is forbidden like a niddah does not appear to be the Rambam’s view, for he wrote that if one betrothed and brought her into chuppah without blessing, she is a full wife, whereas when she is a niddah he holds it is ineffective, as written in siman 60. One must say that according to the Rambam the statement in Masechet Kallah, "a bride without blessing is forbidden like a niddah," refers to chuppah, which is called by the name of blessing, as explained in Piskei Mahari and in Responsa Rosh. But the blessings do not prevent the marriage. Rashi and Tosafot, however, understand like the Mordechai that it refers to actual blessings. According to them, if the blessings were not recited, she may have the status of a niddah and not receive additional ketubah. Nevertheless, one can say that even though she is called forbidden like a niddah, it is not literally like niddah, and bedieved the chuppah works without blessings. See siman 61.
And he separates her for himself. But if he brought her temporarily to his house and had relations with her, this is not nisuin. What he wrote, "and he is secluded with her," means seclusion fit for relations. Therefore the Rambam holds that if she is a niddah, her law is like an arusah. According to this, since with a virgin we require seclusion fit for relations, the Yerushalmi’s statement that those who marry widows on Friday afternoon must bring them in before day must mean actual relations. However, one can say that the Rambam did not rule like that Yerushalmi. See siman 61.
And one who has relations with his arusah. The Ran at the beginning of Kiddushin asks: if there was chuppah, he acquired her without relations; and if there was no chuppah, these relations were prohibited, so how can he acquire her through prohibited relations? He answers that since he had relations with her for the sake of nisuin, chuppah is not required and there is no prohibition, since she is designated for relations. Therefore relations for the sake of nisuin acquire without chuppah.
And some say that chuppah is not seclusion, etc. So wrote the Ran at the beginning of Ketubot and brought proof from the Gemara. The Rambam disagrees and writes: until he brings her into his house and is secluded with her. Even according to the Ran, this applies specifically where she was a virgin with the first husband; then even without seclusion it is chuppah as soon as he brings her to his house. But with a widow, bringing her to his house is ineffective, for according to the above Yerushalmi chuppah does not acquire a widow, and bringing her to his house is chuppah. Thus this helps only for a virgin, not for a widow, unlike the Chelkat Mechokek, who wrote that bringing her to his house helps even for a widow. Responsa Mishpat Binyamin also holds that bringing her to his house does not help for a widow, and at least seclusion fit for relations is required. Therefore, if a widow came for marriage to the groom’s house on Thursday night while she was a niddah, she may not immerse on Shabbat, because bringing her to his house did not acquire her before Shabbat. This is not like the Chelkat Mechokek.
And some say that the chuppah of a virgin, etc. See Bach, siman 60, who wrote that our custom is to satisfy all opinions: on Friday morning they cover the bride’s head with a scarf, and in the evening at the time of kiddushin and birkat chatanim they spread a canopy over poles, stand the groom and bride there, and recite the blessings of erusin and nisuin. But the primary chuppah is that the groom and bride eat together after the chuppah; therefore one should prevent many people from entering there at that time. According to this one must be careful that this eating take place before Shabbat, since it is the main chuppah. However, what he wrote, to prevent many people from entering, implies that a few may enter, and there is no reason for this, since we require seclusion fit for relations. If so, no one should enter, so there should be complete seclusion.
And a widow, from when they are secluded. This is based on the above Yerushalmi. See siman 63.
