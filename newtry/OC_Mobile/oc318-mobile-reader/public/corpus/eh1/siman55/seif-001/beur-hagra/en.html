And some say that the chuppah, etc. In the Yerushalmi at the end of Sotah: what are "canopies"? Sheets decorated with designs and golden ornaments hanging from them. It teaches that one may make frames and hang on them whatever he wishes. So too in the Bavli there: "it was taught also, etc.; what is chuppah, etc." The Yerushalmi calls the crowns of bridegrooms gilded ornaments, but it is one matter. See Rashi there, s.v. "zehorit," and Tosafot Gittin 7a, s.v. "crowns"; this is our chuppah. The Beit Yosef in section 61 cites the Ittur, who rejected this opinion from the expression "until he brings her into the chuppah," implying entry into a designated place. The text should refer to the Yerushalmi in Sotah, not Sukkah. Rather, chuppah is the father's handing her over and bringing her into a house containing a distinctive feature, such as decorated sheets. This is the practice with a virgin; with a widow, once he brings her in for the sake of marriage and there is a designated place and a prepared bed, it is like a house containing a chuppah. See the Rosh, Sukkah chapter 2, and the Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri in Ketubot. The main point is that the chuppah which effects acquisition is her departure from her father's house and her going with the groom to his house for the sake of marriage, showing that she is in her husband's domain, as the Ran holds. It is not necessary that he seclude himself with her. Nevertheless, the making of the chuppah is with sheets like a canopy, as in the Yerushalmi, and this is our chuppah too. Since among us, even after marriage they may live in her father's house, the father removes her from his domain to the synagogue courtyard, and there they establish the chuppah with sheets; that is the essential chuppah and effects acquisition for all matters. The synagogue courtyard is the place of acquisition, no worse than her own courtyard. The canopy itself is not indispensable, but since the custom is to make it, their intent for marriage centers under it. The canopy is not an independent domain that acquires; the courtyard is.

And he betroths her there. The reason for this custom is so that she not become forbidden to him through an open entrance claim. The Ran wrote this, and so appears in the Yerushalmi at the beginning of Ketubot: if he found the entrance open, he is forbidden to keep her, etc.; if he betrothed her at the chuppah, why is this necessary? In those places of penalty, they said that Rabbi Matanya did so for his daughter. With this we do not need the forced explanation of Tosafot in Ketubot 13a, s.v. "if," nor the Rosh there, regarding the custom to marry on Friday. Although he could still cause her to lose her ketubah, we are not concerned for this, as stated on 9b. Therefore one should not distance intercourse from the chuppah. The current custom was established so that he rejoice with her for three days, since the seven days of feasting have lapsed, as stated regarding a widow, "the Sages were diligent, etc." From the Yerushalmi it appears that they made the chuppah at night, but from the Gemara's statement of diligence it appears, like our custom, during the day.
