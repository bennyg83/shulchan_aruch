Some say that all sivlonot that he sent immediately after the shiddukh, a day or two, etc. The Beit Shmuel wrote regarding Maharik: at first glance it is difficult how he resolves, etc. This is the language of Maharik, folio 200b: he further wrote and expanded at length, meaning the disputant, to cite my words, that there is proof that I did not receive them for kiddushin, since I received them only through an intermediary, etc. What did he gain by his long discussion from the Mishnah of one who sent? It is obvious that when he is not in the city, the way is to betroth by an agent, and explicitly, etc. But when they are in one setting, why should he not give it into her hand, and why should she not receive it from his hand? Who would err and compare the cases to each other? Thus he establishes the sugya specifically where he sends from one place to another. Similar to his words we find in Gittin in the sugya of a third party: say it was for divorce; when they are in one place, if she had given it for divorce she would have given it to him herself. All the more so one may say this regarding kiddushin, because it is a greater mitzvah for him to do it himself. Any concern for sivlonot is only to require her to receive a get initially. The Chelkat Mechokek wrote that Maharik wrote this only where the majority send sivlonot first, which is similar to water that has no end. If so, according to the reason of the Ran cited in the Beit Shmuel, that the majority is not considered a true majority because it is only by force of custom and not by nature, one may say that therefore even where the majority send sivlonot first, if she married she must leave. So too it appears that Rav Pappa spoke uniformly and said, they betroth, etc., we are concerned, all in one category; just as where the majority betroth first, if she married she must leave, so too where the majority send sivlonot first. According to Tosafot's explanation, that this is learned from the law of water that has no end, in truth in my humble opinion one should learn that if she married, she never leaves. Rather, in my humble opinion the Chelkat Mechokek's intent is that where the majority betroth first, one must be concerned for Rashi's version and rule that if she married she must leave; for according to Rashi's version, Rav Pappa spoke only about a place where the majority betroth first, and about that he said we are concerned even if she married, that she must leave. However, the Beit Shmuel relied on Terumat HaDeshen, who wrote that even according to the explanation of the geonim it is only a concern, and all the more so according to Rashi's explanation, where the Rosh wrote according to his view that it is only a concern. Therefore, in every case, if she married she does not leave. If so, there is no contradiction from the Ran. Apart from this, the Ran's words are brief, for at the beginning of HaManiach it is proven that such a majority is also considered a majority. Rather, it is as stated in Milchamot, which concludes with the very language of the Ran: therefore, in a place of the prohibition of a married woman they were stringent to be concerned. Thus even according to the Ran's reason, it is only an ordinary stringency initially. See Nedarim 53, where the Gemara answers: no, it is needed where the majority obtain supply from one source; one might have said we follow the majority, so it teaches that a doubt in prohibition is stringent. This is difficult: how is this different from the entire Torah? According to the Ran's words it is understandable, for this too is only a majority of custom. But if so, that in all prohibitions they were stringent with such a majority, it is difficult how HaManiach concludes that in monetary law we do not follow the majority; why not say that with such a majority we do not follow it even in prohibitions? One may answer that this is only rabbinic, that they were stringent with this kind of majority, as explained in Milchamot. If so, were it not for Shmuel's view that by Torah law in monetary matters we do not follow majority, he would hold that everywhere, even here, we would follow it; for in monetary matters rabbinic stringency does not apply, since stringency for this one is leniency for that one. This is also Rav's reason. However, my sharp son-in-law Rabbi Yehudah, may his light shine, from Lissa, when he was then with me, answered that the reason in Nedarim is because it is a matter that has a permitted alternative. According to him, the Rambam's language is precise, for he specifically says: every doubt in vows is stringent. See Mishneh LaMelekh, chapter 7 of Hilkhot Me'ilah. Even according to his conclusion, that even with a matter that has a permitted alternative we say that anything that separates is presumed to have separated from the majority, because no prohibition was established at the place of doubt, nevertheless here, where the minority is established, although one cannot give it the status of a living creature to make it rabbinically fixed, since the importance is not in the body of the prohibition, perhaps because of the stringency of a matter that has a permitted alternative it is considered established and has no rabbinic nullification. See also Lechem Mishneh in Hilkhot Nedarim. In my humble opinion this is explicitly clear according to Rashi z"l, who wrote: we are concerned for sivlonot, etc.; and if she became betrothed to another, she needs a get from the first. Thus he comes to teach us a post-facto law. Otherwise he should have written only, we are concerned and she needs a get, which is literally initial guidance if she wishes to marry another. If so, why did he not teach a greater novelty, that if she married she must leave? Necessarily he teaches that specifically after-the-fact kiddushin, where it can be repaired with a get, requires a get; but if she married, where requiring a get would forbid her to both, she does not even need a get. One should not infer stringently that specifically if she became betrothed, but if she married she must leave both; if so, he would not have been silent, and would have used the shorter phrase, if she married without a get she must leave both. Still, according to Rashi z"l this requires study if she became betrothed and a get is impossible, such as if the first went overseas, whether she must leave. It is also heard from Rashi z"l that if she became betrothed, specifically the first gives a get and not the second, lest people say the first divorced her and then remarried his divorcee after she had been betrothed to another. This requires study; see the beginning of siman 44. It must be that he found it reasonable to be stringent in this because this rabbinic decree is due to concern for a possible Torah doubt. Further, at first glance there is proof that even according to Tosafot's explanation, if she married she does not leave, even where the majority betroth first. For most poskim agree that we are concerned even if both say that they did not intend kiddushin, unlike one opinion in the Mordekhai that speaks where he claims, I betrothed you. If so, Maharik's question on folio 172 is sound: how is this different from what we rule like Rabban Gamliel, that even where the majority are invalid for her, she is believed because of certain claim versus uncertain claim? Granted, if the case is not about requiring her to leave if she married, but only requiring a get where possible, there is not so much difficulty from what we rule like Rabban Gamliel, that she is believed even initially where the majority are fit, while here we are concerned for the minority according to Rabbenu Chananel's version. One may distinguish initial cases: there, at least for her it is after the fact, for if we do not believe her she becomes disqualified to a kohen; whereas here it is possible to repair with a get or chalitzah. But if she married, it is certainly difficult, for that is a complete after-the-fact case: if we do not believe her, she becomes a zonah and must leave both. However, in our poverty, one cannot build a foundation on such proofs, for we do not derive one rabbinic enactment from another, and we have only what Chazal said. So too is the implication of Maharik. From the words of the responsum of the Rashba cited in the Beit Yosef there is some implication to be concerned even if she married, since he sought other reasons; nevertheless he concludes, and all the more so this woman who married. Examine carefully. Some say that if he sent with witnesses, even though there were no witnesses at the giving, etc. The Beit Yosef already disagreed with this, and Mahari Levi z"l in responsum brings it with the agreement of the Rivash. So it appears to me as well. It is not at all similar to witnesses of intercourse. What does it matter that there is a presumption that an agent, etc., returns to give them to her? Who can testify that she received them? This is clear.
