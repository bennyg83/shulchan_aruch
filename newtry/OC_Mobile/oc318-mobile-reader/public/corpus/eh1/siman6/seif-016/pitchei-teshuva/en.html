They saw her pregnant. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah Tinyana siman 28 regarding a virgin who had relations and became pregnant, and said that the fetus was from a particular kohen. He too admitted this, and she said she had been with him. The question was whether she may marry him and whether the child is fit for kehunah, given possible concerns that she may have had relations with disqualified men as well. The Noda BiYehudah discusses whether their admission is effective. He notes that this is not like ordinary testimony for yibbum, and that even where the Rosh is lenient in some cases of admission, here there is reason to say that the kohen is not believed, because perhaps his eyes are set on her, like the law of a captive woman where the redeemer who wishes to marry her is not believed to testify that she is pure. Also, where he admits to forbidden intercourse, particularly with an unmarried woman who ordinarily has the status of niddah, one must consider the principle that a person does not make himself wicked. If so, perhaps his admission should not be accepted. On the other hand, there are distinctions: in some contexts the concern of eyes set on her applies only where without his testimony she would be absolutely forbidden, whereas here there may be majorities and other grounds for fitness. The responsum analyzes the views of the Rambam and the Rosh, the Beit Shmuel in siman 6 s.k. 31, and the broader question whether when it is known that she had relations with a fit man we still suspect she also had relations with others. If the relations with the fit man are established by witnesses, or by other reliable knowledge, then according to the Beit Shmuel one is not concerned for another man. But where all we know is from their own mouths, and he wishes to marry her, perhaps his statement is not enough. The matter also depends on the structure of the majority. If her residence or neighborhood is considered a Jewish neighborhood by itself, there may be a majority of fit men. The Beit Shmuel wrote that where Jews have a distinct neighborhood, it is like a separate city without gentiles living there. Later authorities discuss how far this can be relied upon. Beit Meir challenges the breadth of the Beit Shmuel's rule, whereas Chatam Sofer in simanim 9 and 18 supports it regarding concern for a gentile; but regarding a disqualified Jew, such as a natin or mamzer, further analysis is needed. Where there are relatives forbidden to her by arayot in the household, there may be a compounded doubt toward stringency, for perhaps the fetus is from a relative and is a mamzer, and if not from a relative then perhaps from a gentile or another disqualified man, which would affect her fitness to kehunah. Some responsa distinguish whether the father or another relative was actually present and whether there is reliable testimony excluding that suspicion. The responsum further considers whether a maidservant or other witness who knows of the intercourse with the kohen can establish it. Since one witness is believed in matters of issur where there is no established prohibition, and since the issue is to identify that this fit man had relations with her, there may be room to treat it as known that this man was with her and not suspect another, especially where the surrounding majority is favorable. The Noda BiYehudah also discusses a similar case of an arus and arusah, where the woman attributed the pregnancy to the arus and he admitted it. There the question is whether one must suspect that she had relations with others. Some distinguish between a woman specially attached to one man, where most relations are assumed to be with him, and a case of occasional hidden relations, where the presumption is weaker. The Pitchei Teshuva cites later responsa that debate this, including Brit Avraham, Chatam Sofer, and Knesset Yechezkel. The principle emerging from several authorities is that where she certainly had relations with a fit man and there is a majority of fit men, or at least no clear majority of disqualified men, one may be lenient, especially after the fact. But where her own statement and his admission are the sole basis and there are strong concerns of disqualified men, some are stringent. The responsum also examines the rule that a person does not make himself wicked. Although one might say the kohen is not believed to admit forbidden relations with a niddah, this may not invalidate the statement when both parties state a definite claim and bet din need not investigate further, as Rashba wrote in Kiddushin and as Penei Yehoshua cites in Ketubot. Likewise, where before the doubt arose she had a presumption of permissibility, and the new concern is only perhaps she is pregnant from another man, their definite claim may prevent applying the presumption that one does not make himself wicked to create a prohibition. Another approach is that the young man says he does not suspect her at all and his heart is not troubled, because it is clear to him that she had relations with no one else; perhaps this is considered a definite claim. Moreover, if the remaining concern is only rabbinic, such as where a Jewish neighborhood gives one majority and the requirement of two majorities is rabbinic, then the case resembles the law of a woman pregnant from another man where the parties admit the truth and we do not intensify the matter because of the principle that a person does not make himself wicked. The responsum then turns to the suggestion of using conditional kiddushin. In a stringent case, the Noda BiYehudah advised that the kohen marry her with an explicit condition, if she had not had relations with someone disqualified to her. If the condition is not fulfilled, the kiddushin does not take effect, and there would be no Torah prohibition of a kohen marrying a woman disqualified to him. The Pitchei Teshuva notes that the Noda BiYehudah did not necessarily rely on this as the core permission, but used it as a corrective measure where possible. Later discussion raises several difficulties with such a condition. If the husband later dies, she may still not be able to marry another kohen except through a similar condition, for in this case she was never truly permitted in the ordinary way. There is also a question about daughters later born from this marriage: if there remains a Torah concern of chalalah for a kohen, conditional kiddushin may not help them unless they too marry kohanim conditionally, which is remote. By contrast, the present fetus may already be considered fit after the fact for purposes of marrying into the community, and many authorities are lenient about a child of a gentile or slave with a Jewish woman regarding kehunah at least after the fact. The responsum further questions whether conditional kiddushin is effective in a marriage that, without the condition, would be a prohibited kiddushin. The Noda BiYehudah elsewhere wrote that in kiddushin involving a transgression, agency may not help because there is no agency for transgression. Since a condition must be something capable of being performed through an agent, perhaps a condition is ineffective here. On the other hand, the Mishneh LaMelekh discusses that the formal laws of conditions may apply only to future conditions, whereas here the condition concerns a past fact, whether she had relations with a disqualified man; perhaps such a condition need not satisfy every rule of conditions. After all these discussions, the responsum cited concludes that because this condition has many complications, in the actual case it would be proper to permit without relying on the condition where the facts are stronger: the woman lived in a Jewish neighborhood, no forbidden relative was found there, and the doubts lean toward leniency. Still, because public impropriety should be rebuked and because the Noda BiYehudah had suggested such a measure, he advised that the kiddushin be performed publicly under the chuppah with an explicit condition stated at length: if she had not had relations with one disqualified to her. If the condition helps, good; if it does not, it does no harm. The community leaders should also impose whatever corrective public measures they deem proper. He adds that all this was said to permit the sinners to marry each other, but other issues remain. Regarding chalitzah, see below siman 156. Regarding the fetus: if it is male, for purposes of birkat kohanim see Beit Shmuel siman 4 s.k. 40 and the relevant responsa; one should be cautious in Torah-level matters. If she gives birth to a daughter, whether she is impaired for kehunah depends on the dispute whether the child of a gentile or slave with a Jewish woman is impaired for kehunah; after the fact she certainly would not leave, and therefore the parents may be believed for this, though the child may retain some lesser impairment comparable to a child conceived from a niddah. If the fetus is male, regarding pidyon haben the father need not redeem him, because he claims with certainty that the child is his; but when the son grows up he should redeem himself without a blessing, and he may give the money to his father whichever way the truth lies. Regarding ritual impurity if one of them dies, the father becoming tamei for the son is a Torah prohibition, and the author did not wish to rely on his own opinion to permit it; but the son may become tamei for the father whichever way one looks at it. Responsa Radbaz, part 3 siman 526, contains further distinctions in a place where yibbum is practiced, and from his words one can learn to the present case. See also Responsa Chemdat Shlomo siman 78.
