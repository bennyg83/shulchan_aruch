Because he writes to them by way of admission. See Sefer Yeshuot Yaakov, who was asked about a shtar chatzi zachar that was not written in this form, but merely stated that the son-in-law should take like a half portion of a male child. Even at the end of the document it said, "All that is above I accepted upon myself as a complete obligation, to affirm and uphold." What is the law of this document? Should we say that since it was not written as an admission he takes only from the property the father had at the time of writing, or should we say that since it says he accepted it as a complete obligation, we rule in Choshen Mishpat siman 60 that although a person cannot transfer something not yet in existence, he can nevertheless obligate himself regarding something not yet in existence? He discusses this somewhat at length, and his view inclines that here it does not help, even though it says that he accepted it as a complete obligation; he takes only from the property the father had at the time of writing. The end of that responsum was not found there. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, Choshen Mishpat, simanim 25 through 28, on a similar matter, where he debates this with his son-in-law, the gaon Rabbi Yosef z"l. It is clear there that they regarded it as obvious that regarding property acquired after the writing of the document, the son-in-law certainly has no share or remembrance. Even the money that existed at the time of the kinyan he did not acquire, for currency is not acquired by chalipin. Even the language of obligation written in the document, that he accepted upon himself as a complete debt to affirm and uphold, does not help at all for this, because he did not obligate himself to give it personally, such that the obligation would attach to his own body. Rather, he wrote that he undertook to affirm and uphold all the above, meaning that he obligated himself that his sons should give it to the son-in-law; how can he obligate himself on behalf of his sons? And even if the document says, "I accepted upon myself with a severe ban and an oath by the Torah," that too does not help, because the heirs are not obligated to fulfill their father's oath. The whole analysis there concerns only the property that existed at the time of the kinyan: whether he can extract it from the orphans, since the kinyan was made on everything together, and a kinyan made upon something effective together with something that has no substance is the subject of a dispute among the poskim. Some rule like Rav Hamnuna in Bava Batra 143, that he acquires nothing at all, and the dispute is explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 203 seif 10 in the gloss. If so, how can the holder of the document extract from the orphans with a document that contains a doubt of law, when the property remains in the possession of the heirs? They discuss this at length with deep reasoning; see there. Some of their words will be brought in Pitchei Teshuva to Choshen Mishpat siman 203, there, s.v. exchanged coins.
