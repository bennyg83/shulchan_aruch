If he wrote "on the fourth," etc. The reason is explained in the responsum of the Rosh, because "on the fourth" has no other meaning except the day of the week, since afterwards it says "twenty days of the month." In Perishah he further wrote a proof, that if it referred to the month it should have written "on four" and not "on the fourth." See there. See Get Pashut, who writes that the Perishah did not intend to rely on this proof alone, such that even if he also skipped the words "twenty days" and wrote "on the fourth of such-and-such month," it should be validated. See above, siman 127, seif 7. This is certainly not so, for in such a case one should not validate even in a place of igun. Rather, the Perishah's intent is that this reason is an additional support to the reason of the Rosh. He further writes that it appears to him that even if he wrote "on four" instead of "on the fourth," skipped the word "of the week," and then wrote "twenty days of the month," the get is valid, based on the reason of the Rosh alone, because "four" has no other meaning except the week, since afterwards it says "twenty days," etc. Also, it cannot be explained as a postdated get, see above, siman 127, seif 9, such that people might say the get was written on the twenty-fourth of the month, because if so he should have written "and twenty in it." Since he wrote the word "twenty" without a vav, it implies that four is the date of the week and twenty is the date of the month. Also, if he skipped the word "year" and wrote "five thousand," etc., it is valid, because the plain wording explains itself that it is discussing the count of years. See Raanach, part 1, siman 106. A get that says "on the third day of the week, days of such-and-such month," and skipped the number of days, is valid. Maharam Alshich, siman 102. If the get was written in the month of Elul and the get says "on the fourth year five thousand," etc., and skipped the word "of the week," the number of the month, and the name of the month, there is room to say the get is invalid because it is antedated, since people may explain "on the fourth" as the name of the month Tammuz. However, my view inclines that it is valid, because the custom is not to write the months except by the names that came up from Bavel, and "on the fourth" has no meaning except the fourth day of the week. These are his words. Regarding what Get Pashut wrote, "also if he skipped the word year," see the responsa in Gevurat Anashim, where two great authorities of the generation disagreed. The view of the Gaon Morenu HaRav Yehoshua, of blessed memory, was that it is obvious that if he entirely skipped the word "year" and wrote only "five thousand," etc., "to the creation of the world," it is valid, and no worse than if he skipped the word "of the week," where the Rosh, of blessed memory, wrote that it is valid because it has no other meaning. The same applies here: it is obvious that one cannot explain "five thousand," etc., as days or months. First, because that would be treating them as subordinate and making days into months and months into years, as stated in Perek Get Pashut, page 166, in the case of a shtar in which it was written "six hundred and a zuz," where we do not say six hundred perutot, because one treats them as subordinate to zuz, etc. Second, since the way of the world is to count years from creation, there is no concern, as Tosafot and the Rosh wrote in the first chapter of Avodah Zarah. In Shulchan Aruch below, siman 127, seif 10, it is validated where he skipped "to the creation of the world," etc., and all the more so in our case, where no fool would err and count this as months, since it would then be before the giving of the Torah by several thousand years, etc. My grandfather, the Gaon Maharam Katz, of blessed memory, responded that he does not agree, and it appears to him that if he skipped the word "year," it is like a get that has no date, and one may say perhaps it refers to months and is antedated, etc. From the words of the Rosh, of blessed memory, regarding where he skipped the word "of the week," there is proof to the contrary, for he wrote the reason that "on the fourth" has no other meaning except the week, since afterwards it says "twenty days of the month." If so, in our case, where it has another meaning, it is invalid. Furthermore, here one can say more strongly that "five thousand" refers forward to what is written in the get, "twenty-one days of such-and-such month, five thousand," meaning that the month is the five thousandth from creation. Also from the Gemara in the above Perek Get Pashut there is proof to the contrary, for Tosafot wrote there: It is difficult, for zuz too may be treated as subordinate to istira, etc. They answer that people commonly write shtarot in zuz, etc. If so, in our case, people commonly write months in shtarot, for the verse says "the twelfth month," so perhaps "five thousand" refers to months. I do not understand this, for in any case we do not find a count of months greater than twelve. Also, what he wrote that no fool would err in this, from the words of Tosafot in the first chapter of Avodah Zarah it is clear that we do not rely on this. They wrote there that if he skipped the thousands there is no concern because it says nearby "he left the first thousand," etc. This implies that without that reason it would be invalid, even though he skipped five thousand, etc. There is another proof from what the Mordechai wrote, brought in the Beit Yosef, see Shulchan Aruch, siman 127, seif 12, and what I wrote there, that regarding a ketubah where the scribe erred and wrote "thousands" and skipped "four," Ri of Vienna validated it, because one cannot say a minority of two thousand years since he did not write "two thousand." This implies that without this reason we would invalidate it and say it was written two or three thousand years earlier. If so, in monetary law, and all the more so in gittin, etc. He discusses this at length. Nevertheless, it is explained at the end of the responsum there that in a great case of igun, one can possibly validate. See there. See there further regarding a case where in the word shenat, the nun looked like a gimel, and a child who was neither wise nor foolish read it as nun. The Gaon Mahari wrote that this is simpler to validate, for even if we say that the word shenat is essential, it is valid in our case since we recognize the form of the matter, as the Ba'al HaItur wrote regarding "on the fourth" and "on the sixth," that if the reish of revi'i or the shin of shishi is missing, there is no concern, etc. The Gaon Maharam Katz, of blessed memory, wrote that the opposite is true: this is worse, because even if we say that if he did not write the word shenat at all it is not invalid, nevertheless a change invalidates it, as proven from Tosafot in the first chapter of Avodah Zarah, etc. Furthermore, this is worse than an ordinary change, for it resembles what the Talmud says, not to write lemechak because it implies laughter. Here too it is like shigeg, lacking a root-letter gimel, as in the verse in Iyov, "if I have erred, my error remains with me." If so, here it implies that the five thousand is by error and not truthfully, and is like laughter. Therefore, even if a child who is neither wise nor foolish reads it as shenat, it does not help, as Maharik wrote that where it is obvious that the letter does not have its proper form, what does the child's reading help? Nevertheless, in a great place of igun, it appears to me to lean toward validating even in this case, etc. See there.
