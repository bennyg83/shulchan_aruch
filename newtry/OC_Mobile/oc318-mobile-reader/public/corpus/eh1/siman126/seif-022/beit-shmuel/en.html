If he did not lengthen the vavs. This means he did not write them in the form of vavs but in the form of yuds; therefore it is invalid. But if he wrote vavs and merely did not make them longer than other vavs, it is valid, as written by Tosafot and Responsa Rivash, siman 314. It also implies that the Rosh holds so, for in his rulings he did not write that they must be longer than other vavs. The Beit Yosef and Bach explain the Rosh’s responsum cited by the Tur as holding that if he did not lengthen them more than other vavs, it should not be given unless because of igun; if already given and the husband is far away, it is valid. This explanation does not appear correct. The Tur himself wrote earlier that the vavs need not be longer than other vavs, so he cannot later cite the Rosh as invalidating on that basis. Rather, the Rosh speaks where the vavs are like yuds, and even then he validated it. So ruled the Taz: even without pressing circumstances it is valid if he merely did not lengthen them. The three views brought here are these: according to the Rambam, if he did not lengthen the vavs of "teruchin," "shevukin," and "piturin," or wrote the yud of "lemehach" or "piturin," or did not write the three yuds of "deyitehevyin" or "deyitzviyin," it is invalid even if the husband did not protest and even if he did not write it himself. If she remarried, she need not leave. The Itur, cited by the Rosh, Tur, and Maggid Mishneh, holds that it is invalid only if the husband protests that he wrote it that way intentionally. According to that view, if the husband wrote it or the scribe wrote from his mouth and the husband protests, she must leave her new husband; if the scribe did not write from his mouth and the husband does not protest, the get is valid and she may marry; and intermediate cases are invalid lechatchilah but if she married she need not leave. If the get came from elsewhere and we do not know who wrote it, lechatchilah it should not be given except in a case of igun, or if already given. If the foot of the hei in "lemehach" was not distanced, the law is the same. The "some say" here is not necessarily the Ba'al HaItur, as some small Shulchan Aruchs indicate. According to Ra'avad and Itur, the husband’s protest helps even if he did not write it; Rivash writes otherwise. The Rashba’s responsum explicitly states in the name of Rav Hai and the Geonim that when the husband wrote it, even if he does not protest, or the scribe wrote generally and the husband protests, the get is invalid. See the Rama in siman 155 regarding feet of heis touching. Regarding "monin" with a vav, the matter may depend on this dispute if the husband wrote it and protests, but the main view is that the dispute applies only to what is explicit in the Talmud; with "monin" everyone agrees she need not leave. In many places they write "monin" with a vav. If they want to correct before delivery, it is like the touching heis in siman 125; in a case of igun the scribe may correct even after the witnesses signed, because it is not a substantive correction.
