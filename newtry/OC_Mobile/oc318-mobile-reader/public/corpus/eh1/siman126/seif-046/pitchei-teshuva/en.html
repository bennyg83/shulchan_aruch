Who was my betrothed. In Get Pashut, s.k. 102, he writes: A married woman was divorced before laymen, and there was a doubt about the get whether it was valid or not. The divorcing husband then betrothed her again, then later changed his mind and wished to divorce her. Now, since the first get is doubtful whether it was valid, if so the kiddushin took effect and one should now write arusati lechatchilah. But if it was not a get, the kiddushin did not take effect, and one should write antati. Therefore, to avoid doubt, one must write two gittin, one in the language antati and one in the language arusati. So the Rosh acted, as written in his responsum, end of siman 10. See there. This was also brought in Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 4 of Hilchot Gerushin, law 12. To me this requires study, for from the words of the responsum of the Rosh brought in Shulchan Aruch below, siman 143, seif 16, that he has no remedy unless he betroths her and then divorces her again plainly, and he did not write that he must give two gittin, it implies that one get is enough. As to how they should write it, my view inclines that they should write arusati. I do not know the reason, for at first glance it is more correct to write antati, since even if she is truly only betrothed, it is valid according to all views, unlike if he writes arusati and perhaps she is truly his wife, regarding which there are those who are stringent, as the Beit Shmuel wrote, s.k. 39. Also, I do not recognize the proof he brought from siman 143, for there is no doubt there regarding the first get, and certainly the obligation there is to write arusati, and this requires study. He further writes that if he wrote at the beginning of the get "you are my wife so-and-so," and at the end of the get wrote "who was my betrothed," or the reverse, where he wrote at the beginning "you are my betrothed," and at the end wrote "who was my wife," it requires study whether it is valid bedieved.
