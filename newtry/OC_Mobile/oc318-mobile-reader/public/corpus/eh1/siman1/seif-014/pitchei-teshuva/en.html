See Beit Shmuel below siman 158 sub-section 1, who wrote: it seems that nowadays when the cherem of Rabbenu Gershom is in effect prohibiting him from marrying another woman upon his wife, one witness is not believed to testify that his wife died — for this is a matter that was established as forbidden, and one witness is not believed, as stated in YD siman 127 — end quote. He wrote this there with some uncertainty, but in siman 15 sub-section 20 he wrote it definitively — see there. And see in Noda b'Yehuda EH part 1 siman 33, passage beginning "the conclusion," who wrote that Beit Shmuel's words on this are not compelling — see there. Also in Sefer Korban Netanel ch. 10 of Yevamot note 100, he disagrees with Beit Shmuel on this — see there. Also in the responsum of Beit Ephraim EH siman 1 in the passage beginning "and furthermore," he wrote that Beit Shmuel's ruling requires examination, for one can say that one witness is believed here since it is a matter that would become known ("milta de'avida le'igluyei"); and it seems that nowadays people do not scrutinize this at all, for whoever receives a letter from a friend in a distant land that his wife died marries [another] on his word, and no one scrutinizes to protest — end quote. Also in Sefer Yeshuot Yaakov siman 158 sub-section 1 he disagrees with Beit Shmuel: since it is only a rabbinic matter, one witness is believed even when the prohibition was established, and especially in a matter that would become known — see there.
And there he further raised the question: regarding one witness who testifies that his wife died, and he comes to court to ask whether he is permitted to marry her sister — which is a karet prohibition — and also whether he is permitted to marry another due to the prohibition of the cherem; for even if we believe him regarding the permission to marry another, he is nevertheless forbidden to marry her sister (see Beit Shmuel siman 15 there). Now, since he comes to ask about two things — biblical and rabbinic — together, perhaps we cannot be lenient even in the rabbinic matter, for that would be showing contempt for the words of the Sages; as the Radbaz wrote, cited in Magen Avraham Laws of Keri'at Shema — in a case of doubt whether one recited Shema, he repeats it because Shema is biblical; even though only the first verse is biblical, nonetheless he must recite all three portions, since if we are uncertain about both biblical and rabbinic matters together and we are stringent about biblical while lenient about rabbinic it would be like showing contempt for the rabbinic matter (I did not find this in Magen Avraham Laws of Keri'at Shema; see siman 67 sub-section 1 which gives a different reason; but in siman 184 sub-section 7 Magen Avraham wrote this in the name of the Rav Shimon Chiyon and Mahariv HaLevi regarding doubt whether he said Grace after Meals — that he must say even the fourth blessing for this reason — see there). But actually the analogy does not hold, for here if he marries another he will in any case not marry her sister — therefore there is no basis to be stringent — see there.
And see Noda b'Yehuda part 2 siman 6 regarding a man whose wife and son and daughter left him during a year of famine due to the difficulty of their sustenance, and nothing was known of them for more than eight years — whether the husband should be permitted to marry another woman. He responded that one cannot find a basis to help him, since his wife committed no wrong — she left due to famine, and as understood she left with her husband's permission, and who knows the reason for her delay — perhaps it was due to compulsion. And there is no place of mitzvah here, for we also apply a presumption of life to his son and daughter — in which case he has fulfilled the mitzvah of peru u-revu. And even though Kenesset HaGedolah in the Gloss on Beit Yosef note 78 cites a responsum of Rashakh that if she was taken captive the husband may take another woman — perhaps that refers to where he has not fulfilled peru u-revu. And he concluded: although there is reasoning to rule and permit him so that he not stumble in sinful thoughts every day — nonetheless, you must set limits on your words, and if a woman disappears from her husband for some time for some reason, or is ill for some time — would we permit him to marry another upon his wife? Therefore my inclination is not to permit this — but nor do I say it is forbidden, since he has already sat eight years without a wife; and if he finds one hundred rabbis to permit him I will not protest — end quote.
And there in siman 7 he was asked about precisely such a case, except that one witness testified that he heard from someone that the woman died — and he returned a ruling to permit on the basis of the witness's testimony, and wrote: even though in Noda b'Yehuda part 1 siman 33 I did not decide definitively to refute Beit Shmuel's words entirely, nevertheless in siman 54 in the passage beginning "and truly" I cited the words of Tosafot that what is not a matter of personal status ("davar she'bervah"), even if the prohibition was established, it is not clear that one witness is not believed — we are only uncertain; and if so, in a rabbinic prohibition one should be lenient; and especially since for many years she has been missing, it is probable that she died in the year of famine — therefore one should permit.
And he wrote further there in siman 8, in a case where the witness testified that while he was in a certain place a certain woman died, but the witness did not know whose wife she was, and the man upon hearing these words said "that is my wife" — there is room to argue that the husband himself is not believed here since there is cause to suspect he has set his eyes on another woman (see below in the name of the responsum of Beit Ephraim what he cites in the name of Tzemach Tzedek and the gloss on Gittin). Nevertheless, since nowadays the time of the cherem has already passed there is no concern about a biblical prohibition; and further, since from the outset the prohibition was established only from his own words — that he said he has a wife — one should be lenient — see there.
And he further wrote at the end of siman 136, where he was asked by the Gaon of London regarding men who come from distant countries to his community and sent gittin to their wives, and letters came from the rabbinical court that the messenger delivered the get to the woman properly, but the rabbinical court's signatures are not recognizable there — whether the divorcers may marry women there. And he wrote: I have already decided in Noda b'Yehuda part 2 that one witness is believed to testify to a man that his wife died; and since one witness is believed, certainly a document with witnesses — even if not validated — since according to Rashbatz (cited below at the end of siman 142 in the Gloss) this is superior to one witness — therefore my inclination is to permit — see there.
And see further there in siman 147, in a responsum from the author's son regarding a woman who went insane and seven years ago fled her husband in a state of madness, and her whereabouts became unknown and it was not known what happened to her — whether the husband is permitted to marry another woman. He cited his father's words in responsum siman 6 above, where he did not wish to permit regarding a woman whose whereabouts are unknown except on the testimony of one witness. And he wrote that even though there is reason to discuss this — for against the presumption of life there is another presumption that if she were alive she would have returned to her husband since there was no quarrel between them — nevertheless, far be it from him to disagree with his father of blessed memory. Nevertheless, he concluded to be lenient for this husband, since his wife left his house in a state of madness and according to his words she had been presumed insane for several years; and in the case of one who went insane the Rama ruled that one should be lenient to permit him to marry another; and even if one could say that this applies specifically when she is insane before us, but here there is cause to fear she may have recovered — nonetheless we apply to her the presumption from the time she left her husband's house; and there is proof for this, for otherwise she would have returned to her husband's house, etc.; and even though Bach wrote that permission of one hundred rabbis is required, one could say that Bach only wrote this when she is certainly alive, but when there is also doubt whether she may have died, Bach too is lenient. And he concluded: since the matter is not so clear, etc., one should not rely on me alone until two more rabbis also agree; and regarding the obligations the husband has toward the wife and also the deposit of a get three times — since there is cause to fear lest the wife appear — they should follow what his father ruled in Noda b'Yehuda part 1 siman 3 (see below sub-section 16) — see there. (And in the responsum of Chatam Sofer siman 2 regarding a man whose wife went insane several years ago and he brought her to another city to doctors, and now says he was informed she died, and he also brought one witness who heard that this man's wife died in that city — whether he is permitted to marry another. He elaborated there on Beit Shmuel's words in simanim 15 and 158 above — that one witness is not believed, etc. — and his inclination is to be stringent like Beit Shmuel, and wrote: even if we force ourselves into a narrow opening to be lenient because of the husband's agunah situation — that applies when a healthy woman disappeared, analogously to how we are lenient in the agunah of a woman; but here she went insane — he can obtain permission through one hundred rabbis with deposit of get and ketubah, and there is no agunah situation here, etc.; and furthermore the woman is in a known location, not across the sea or in wilderness, and the matter can be clarified — therefore I do not wish to be among those who permit — see there.)
And see the responsum of Beit Ephraim siman 1 regarding one who resided in a community for about twelve years and told several people that he had a wife and one daughter in another place, and maintained this for a long time; and now he came before the rabbi and declared that he had lied and never had a wife — he said so only out of embarrassment, since he was already thirty years old and still unmarried. Whether he is believed with this justification (amtala) and permitted to marry, or not. The matters to discuss regarding this justification are two concerns: first, to remove the cloak of embarrassment it would have sufficed to say he was a widower — so why did he add that he had a daughter from her? Second, due to the rule that anyone who maintained a status for thirty days — a justification is no longer effective, as Beit Shmuel wrote in siman 10, note 9, and one is flogged and burned on the basis of the established status. He elaborated greatly to explain that this justification is not inferior; and also there is reason to argue that even a justification is not needed at all in our case, because whenever one established a prohibition upon himself — which we enforce and which cannot be retracted — it is not in the category of certainty but only of doubt (perhaps his original words were the truth and he is now lying by retracting); and if so in our case, since after the fifth millennium it is only a custom, and in all cases of doubt and dispute we are lenient, as Beit Shmuel wrote sub-section 21 — perhaps retraction works even without a justification. And Beit Shmuel's ruling about the thirty days is not absolute, for in the responsum of Rama siman 2 it is explained that even after thirty days a justification is effective where the status was established only from his own words and through speech alone (see what was written in siman 19 there). And furthermore here he has a miggo (an argument from a stronger claim he could have made): if he had wanted he could have said his wife died and he would have been believed, as explained in the responsum of Tzemach Tzedek and the gloss on Gittin — he himself is believed on this matter; now too when he retracts he is believed by miggo; and one could say Beit Shmuel also agrees with this. And the conclusion of the paragraph was that one should not be stringent in this matter; nevertheless the court should warn him sternly and inform him of the seriousness of the cherem of Rabbenu Gershom — everything according to the nature of the person; and if he says "truthfully my words are true and I am unmarried" — Israel is not suspected of this, especially since this too is a matter that would become known — see there at length. And what he wrote that "whenever one established a prohibition upon himself — which we enforce and which cannot be retracted — it is not in the category of certainty," etc. — requires examination in light of what is written in Shulchan Arukh below siman 3 seif 1: "and if he married her or became impure he is flogged" — implying even if he retracts (see what was written in siman 3 there, and see what was written below in siman 115 seif 7 sub-section 37; and what was written at the beginning of his words to debate the law of "one who establishes a prohibition upon himself" — whether it is from the principle of admission of a litigant or because of a vow — see Pitchei Teshuva to YD siman 1 sub-section 15 and below siman 37 sub-section 11 what was written about this.
