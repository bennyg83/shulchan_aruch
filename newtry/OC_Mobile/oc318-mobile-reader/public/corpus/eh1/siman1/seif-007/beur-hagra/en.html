And he and they converted. Otherwise, what does the Gemara mean when it says, "and both statements were necessary, etc." A non-Jew inherits his father according to Torah law. See Rashi and Tosafos there, who were forced to explain the discussion regarding the firstborn right. According to the Rambam, however, it is clear. The Gemara means as follows: Reish Lakish said that he has not fulfilled procreation in the case of a convert who converted, etc., meaning where the children also converted, for after conversion they are not legally his children. His own conversion is not the reason, since while he was a non-Jew, etc., as Tosafos writes there, s.v. R. Yochanan, and even Reish Lakish agrees to that. He disagrees because of the children's conversion, which is as though the children died, since a convert is regarded as a newborn child. Concerning this the Gemara says that both teachings were necessary, for originally, etc., meaning before the children converted, even after the father converted. This differs from inheritance, because a non-Jew does not inherit a convert, as stated there in Kiddushin. This is why the text says, "they converted," in the plural. The passage in Bechoros does not prove the Rambam's view. Bechoros 47a challenges from the Mishnah, and if the Rambam were correct, how do we know that the Mishnah speaks where the children also converted? Likewise, in Choshen Mishpat, siman 277, seif 9, it does not state that the children converted. The Gemara there and in Yevamos explains why both teachings are necessary, yet the children's conversion is not mentioned at all, only the father's conversion. Even if one says that the children did not convert, it is certainly true concerning them that a convert is regarded as a newborn child, as stated there. The Gemara states plainly that in such a case he certainly has no paternal lineage, and this is obvious without proof. This is the view of all the poskim, unlike the Rambam.
