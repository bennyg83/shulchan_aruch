Second gloss: “All of this,” and so on, “but dowry and other nichsei tzon barzel have the law of other debts.” The Beit Shmuel writes that it sounds that he holds that an ordinary creditor takes from property that is only fit to come. In my humble opinion, this is not necessary, for perhaps if he had waited to gloss until the end of the se'if, we would always say that the main distinction is because of inferior land. The Shach has already written that the Rama himself did not decide this matter in his responsum, and so it appears to the reader there. However, one must examine what practical difference there is in this dispute whether a creditor collects from what is only fit to come. The Shach cites from Hagahot HaRosh a practical case: where property fell to him after death from his mother’s father, which his paternal brothers can inherit only through him, a creditor collects from it if a creditor collects from what is only fit to come. I have already wondered greatly at this, for we hold that a son does not inherit his mother in the grave in order to pass the inheritance to his paternal brothers. The Bach there apparently therefore wrote the case regarding a son who inherited his father, who died during the lifetime of the son’s maternal grandfather, and the father was the borrower. Even this is astonishing, for here too the verse “in place of your fathers shall be your sons” applies. Although one could force an answer and say that the verse was stated only regarding the father’s family, this requires study, for from where do we know such a reasoning from the Talmud? The language of Hagahot HaRosh also does not tolerate his explanation and emendation. Furthermore, since the Tur in Choshen Mishpat, siman 211, regarding cancellation of a sale, does not distinguish where “in place of your fathers” does not apply, such as where the property fell from his mother’s father, that he cannot cancel the sale, this implies that there is no distinction. If the practical difference is regarding wages for work, I already wrote in this se'if that according to us, since we hold that Rabbi Natan’s law applies even to workers’ wages, a creditor collects even from workers’ wages. It is specifically regarding ketubah, because of two leniencies, that it is considered fit to come. It appears to me that there is a major practical difference. It is stated in siman 90, se'if 1, in the gloss, that a chatzi zachar document gives the husband inheritance rights in the power of that document through his offspring. See what I wrote there. If this son was himself indebted, in my humble opinion even during his lifetime, after he already inherited this document from his mother, there is no aspect of Rabbi Natan’s lien. First, the maternal grandfather is not obligated to pay this debt until one moment before his death, and even this he can nullify by not leaving property to be inherited by his sons. See the words of the Ritva cited by the Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 86, se'if katan 11. Here one can say that everyone agrees Rabbi Natan’s law does not apply, since he can consume and destroy everything and be exempt from his obligation. In my poverty, I am even uncertain whether this document can be collected during his lifetime in the manner explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 101, and it seems close to me that it cannot. All the more so where this son died and his father inherits him with property that is only fit to come, namely what his mother’s brothers give him from their father’s inheritance, and the debt is automatically discharged, it is called fit to come. One can then discuss whether, if an ordinary creditor collects from fit-to-come property, his father is obligated to pay his debts from it. In this case, certainly the phrase “through,” and so on, does not apply. If a creditor does not collect from fit-to-come property, he is exempt. Perhaps, in order not to make Hagahot HaRosh and those who copied it into people who erred in an explicit Mishnah, one can force an explanation and say that they intended this kind of case, where if his father died, the law would concern the paternal brothers. Recently, the book Urim VeTumim came into my hands, and I found that he cited my difficulty in the name of the responsa of Rabbi Avraham ben Chaim. Thank God, I was very pleased. What he wrote in that book to resolve it based on the words of Maharam here in this se'if, in the first gloss in the name of “some say,” that if Yaakov died during the lifetime of his son Reuven, and so on, Reuven’s widow does not collect her ketubah from this, and he turned the answer of Maharil to a different intent, namely that even regarding a creditor this fit-to-come status applies, does not appear in Maharil. The words of Maharil are because it is extracting money, and in Nachalat Shivah too Maharil’s answer is mentioned only regarding ketubah. One who studies Maharil, siman 74, will see even more explicitly that his words were said only regarding the ketubah of the son’s wife, for he gives another reason, that it was not given for collection during the lifetime. From this he initially made a practical distinction. According to his view, it is certainly understandable, because it is obvious to me that where the son lived after his father, the grandson can no longer say, “I come through my father’s father,” for how could he be considered the heir of his grandfather when the grandfather left an heir? Only afterwards, in order to sharpenly explain the gloss, his spirit carried him elsewhere, that the heir can say, “I do not want this benefit.” See there, for these are words that the mind does not accept. Even with all his words, the language of the gloss, “property fell to him from his mother’s father,” cannot bear his explanation at all. According to his explanation, the inheritance fell to him during his lifetime, only after his death the wife of his mother’s father died and her lien was removed after his death. Heaven forbid to say that such a sharp reading ever entered the mind of the gloss. See there and examine. “As other debts.” It is clear that in any case she is not better than an ordinary creditor, and from orphans she certainly collects only from inferior land. This is also in the Maggid Mishneh. In this respect, the Rashba in Gittin 50, s.v. amar Ravina, requires study. He writes that at first glance it would imply, meaning that dowry would not be limited to inferior land, because perhaps she would refrain and not marry. This is learned from our sugya, because it asks, “Let it be because the guarantor of a ketubah is not obligated.” If the Mishnah also speaks about nichsei tzon barzel, what is the question? Certainly a guarantor for dowry is obligated, because he caused her loss. This is difficult. Although according to the conclusion, that the Mishnah speaks about collecting from him personally, it is not speaking about nichsei tzon barzel, because of the concern of closing the door, meaning that she would refrain from marrying, nevertheless from orphans it certainly should not be collected better than an ordinary creditor, as above. If so, according to the explanation that the case concerns a guarantor and because every place where he cannot collect from him, from whom does he collect? From the orphans. He too, when he collects, collects only from inferior land. The difficulty still remains, for the Gemara could have answered that it speaks of a guarantor for dowry, and nevertheless the law is inferior land for the above reason. See the beginning of siman 93. It must be that the Rashba held that according to this approach, where the concern of refraining from marriage applies to dowry, even from orphans its law is medium-quality land, and it is stronger than other creditors. The reason can be explained: An ordinary creditor does not consider that the borrower may die, because the debt is collectable during life. But a ketubah is not given for collection during life, so she certainly considers this possibility, and there is concern that she will refrain even regarding orphans. However, the plain implication of the decisors is not this. Rather, it appears that they hold that although it is not given for collection during life, nevertheless she does not assume that he will die before her, or else they did not differentiate. The whole sugya also implies that from orphans it is obvious that she collects only from inferior land. One could force an explanation that this is truly the reason the Gemara concludes, “Actually, from orphans, and ketubah of a woman was necessary to state.” One might have thought that because of grace the Sages were lenient for her even regarding orphans, because she does consider the possibility. Therefore it teaches that “more than,” and so on, and the concern of grace does not apply. Rabbi Meir, who holds that grace does apply, indeed holds that even from orphans she collects from medium-quality land. However, what the Gemara says, “Come and hear, from the very beginning of the enactment,” proves in my humble opinion like the view of most decisors. Otherwise, what is the question, “If you would think it speaks about orphans, that is because they are orphans”? What difficulty is that? Regarding a woman, where she considers that he may die, even regarding orphans we need the reason of “more than,” and so on. Without that, even by orphans there would be concern that she would refrain, and one could always say that specifically from orphans she collects inferior land, but from him personally she collects medium-quality land. Rather, it must be that regarding orphans there is no logic that she should be better than an ordinary creditor, and there is no need for the reason of “more than,” and so on. Although even according to all decisors, according to Rashi’s explanation that “because of grace” means so that men will find favor in women’s eyes and women will marry them, this refutation itself requires study. The Gemara already answered that the reason the Mishnah taught that a woman’s ketubah from orphans is collected from inferior land is so that we should not say like Rabbi Meir, that even from orphans she collects from medium-quality land, because for the sake of grace they were lenient even regarding orphans. It teaches that we do not say this. It then needed a reason why we do not say this regarding orphans, because “more than,” and so on. So perhaps it is specifically from orphans. This works well according to Rabbenu Chananel’s explanation, s.v. mishum china, for even Rabbi Meir agrees to the reasoning of “more than,” which is strong enough to cancel this reason of grace. Only Rabbi Meir is concerned for grace so that men will hurry to marry them, and the first Tanna is not concerned for that at all. If he were concerned for that, the reasoning of “more than” would not cancel it at all. The reason of “more than” only cancels Rashi’s type of grace, as explained by Maharshal at the beginning of the chapter in Tosafot, s.v. uketubat ishah. See Sefer Kikayon DeYonah. If so, the objection is proper: if the case is from orphans, why speak of the reason “more than”? Even if Rashi’s type of grace applied, it would be because they are orphans, and Rashi’s grace is no stronger than closing the door for the borrower, which nevertheless does not apply to orphans according to all. But according to Rashi, of blessed memory, where we see that Rabbi Meir disagrees for this very reason of grace even regarding orphans, this requires study. However, according to Rashi one can say that even aside from this, one should examine why the Gemara does not object from Rabbi Shimon, who also said, “Why is a woman’s ketubah collected from inferior land? Because more than,” and so on. Rather, from Rabbi Shimon there is no question, as Tosafot explains in the Mishnah, in the above passage, and as I asked, that the reason “more than” is needed to exclude Rabbi Meir. This is not so in this baraita, which I explained as speaking from the beginning of the enactment. The meaning is that Rabbi Shimon says that even from him personally she collects inferior land because “more than,” and the Sages were concerned that if it were collected from medium-quality land, people would separate from them if they had to sell medium-quality land, and they were concerned so that men would hurry to marry them. So too Tosafot writes in Ketubot 10, s.v. ho'il, giving this reason regarding best-quality land and movable property according to Rabbi Meir. It emerges that this is the main reason for the enactment. But if the case were from orphans, that would be because they are orphans, which is the main enactment, and the reason of “more than” would be needed only to answer the reason of grace, so that she should not be stronger through grace than closing the door for the borrower, which Rabbi Meir is concerned for even regarding orphans more than closing the door for the borrower. If so, even according to the Rashba, of blessed memory, one could force an answer in this way. But from the beginning of his words, where he writes, “Therefore, whether from orphans or from him personally, it is inferior land, for there is no concern of closing the door before marriage, because more than,” and so on, it implies that he does not explain the phrase “from the beginning of their enactment” this way. One should also not say that nevertheless there is no difficulty against him, for I already forced an explanation of the sugya according to him: “Actually from orphans, and ketubah of a woman was necessary,” and it teaches that the reason of grace does not apply to her because of “more than,” and so on. This is difficult, for if so, according to Mar Zutra, according to the first Tanna, why is the law of orphans truly different from the law of collection from him personally? It must be that Mar Zutra means minor orphans, and because the Sages made an enactment for orphans. The Gemara therefore properly asks: “That is because they are orphans,” meaning, how can it say “from the beginning of their enactment because of ‘more than,’” when this is not sufficient, and the main reason is that because it is from orphans the Sages made an enactment for them? Rather, learn from it that it is from him personally, and that “more than,” and so on, does not apply to women as closing the door does, and truly there is no distinction. Even from him personally, it is inferior land. But this is not so, because then the original difficulty returns: this does not work for dowry, where the concern that she will refrain applies and it is like closing the door. Nevertheless, the Gemara could still have answered according to Mar Zutra that it speaks of a guarantor for dowry, and since it is from orphans, the Sages made an enactment for them even where closing the door applies. This requires study.
