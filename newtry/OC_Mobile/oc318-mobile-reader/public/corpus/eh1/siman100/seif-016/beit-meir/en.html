Gloss: “And some say that even in a place,” and so on, “but even the additional amount.” The Beit Shmuel writes: However, the additional third is not considered an actual additional ketubah; rather, it is as if she brought in this third herself, and so on. If so, if she has in her possession a document or tena'im stating how much she brought in, she collects that amount and the additional third. End quote. This requires study according to our custom, as explained in siman 66, Beit Shmuel, se'if katan 23, that we write a document of additional ketubah according to the sum of the dowry with an added third, and within this third is included the biblical ketubah, namely the two hundred zekukim. If this additional ketubah document is not in her possession, why should she collect through the tena'im even the amount of the dowry? Everyone agrees that a dowry is not a condition of the court, and he can claim, “I paid,” when she is not holding her main document, namely the tosefet ketubah document. This is not comparable to the additional ketubah practiced equally by everyone, where that document is given the law of the basic ketubah and has the force of a court condition, as explained in the Beit Yosef. If the body of the dowry itself cannot be collected, from where will help come to collect the third? And if the reason is that she has in her possession the original document or tena'im written at the time of the shidduchin, this is no proof. It is possible that although the father of the bride obligated himself in this amount, who knows whether it was fulfilled? Not only can one claim “I paid” against such a document, but perhaps it never came into the husband’s hand at all, because of the weakness created by the fact that she does not have the tosefet ketubah document as is customary. Perhaps this is why it was not written in the first place. Even if this refers to the final tena'im according to the custom of Ashkenaz, as stated in Sefer Nachalat Shivah, where it is explained how much was brought in as dowry, nevertheless the matter is difficult whichever way one looks at it. If in Ashkenaz they also practice the above custom of writing a tosefet ketubah document, then necessarily the final tena'im document was never written in order to prevent him from claiming “I paid.” For if so, it would be difficult: she would have two documents for one debt, the final tena'im document and the tosefet ketubah document, and she could collect and then collect again. It is very forced to say that their custom was established on the assumption that the husband or heirs would be forced to pay by means of a receipt, or by preserving this document so that she returns it when she first makes her claim with it. Why would they establish such a poor custom? In truth, because of this, in my humble opinion the custom of Poland is correct, where they do not mention in the final tena'im what she brought in. If there is a practical difference because of absence, Heaven forbid, then presumably we judge the tosefet ketubah document as the dowry minus one-third. If he adds more, he should change the text and specify how much is the dowry with the third and how much he added willingly, and everything should be in one tosefet ketubah document. I am uncertain regarding the custom of Ashkenaz, whether they practice with the tosefet ketubah document as we do. Except in this country I see that they practice both together, and in my humble opinion this is not proper. Rather, it must be that even according to the custom of Ashkenaz, the tosefet ketubah document is her main document, and against it he cannot claim “I paid.” But against the final tena'im document alone, because of the weakness that she does not have her main document, namely the tosefet ketubah document, he can claim “I paid,” and we claim this on behalf of the heirs. It is written only for the practical consequence of nichsei shevi'it. If so, I do not know what the Beit Shmuel is hinting to with “document or tena'im.” I do not know what kind of document this is, and the tena'im likewise require study.
