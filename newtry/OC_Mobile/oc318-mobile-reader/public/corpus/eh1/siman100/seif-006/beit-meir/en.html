“And they are not collected from enhancement.” The Beit Shmuel writes that this is not like the commentary on the Mishnah, which writes that after the enactment a woman collects from enhancement and from property that is only fit to come. See Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh. “But not from what is only fit to come.” The Ba'er Heitev writes that “what is only fit to come” applies even from the husband himself, citing Mahari Weil, siman 8. See there. The Levush wrote well on this, for although that is indeed explained there, nevertheless in my humble opinion the Rama, of blessed memory, deliberately omitted these words, because they are astonishing. He wrote that a man who divorced his wife and did not have the means to pay the ketubah, and afterwards money came into his hands in any manner, is exempt from paying. The proof that he holds this way is from what he wrote at the end of his words, where he raises a difficulty against himself from the Rambam, chapter 12 of Hilchot Ishut, who writes that if the husband was extremely poor and unable to give her bread, they compel him to divorce, and the ketubah remains a debt upon him until he has the means to pay. Mahari Weil writes that even though Maimonides disagrees, nevertheless since he holds that one does not collect from what is only fit to come, we do not extract money. It escaped him that the Rosh himself, in a responsum cited in Beit Yosef at the end of siman 117, rules this way as well, and it is explained in Nedarim 65b. His words were taken from the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. Since the Rosh wrote that the Mishnah in Bechorot, “a woman does not take from enhancement,” means even during the husband’s lifetime, Mahari Weil inferred that the clause “and she does not take from what is only fit to come” also applies during the husband’s lifetime. This is nothing. Does he explain that what the Mishnah says, “she does not take from enhancement,” means even from the husband himself? That is certainly impossible, for from him personally she collects even from the cloak on his shoulder. Rather, the Rosh only means that the Mishnah speaks in every applicable case: where she comes to collect from orphans, she does not seize the enhancement that the orphans enhanced; and where she comes to collect during his lifetime from purchasers, she does not seize what the purchasers enhanced. On this the Gemara’s question from an ordinary creditor applies, and it answers that this is among the leniencies of ketubah. But the clause “and not from what is only fit to come” can obviously be explained only after his death. Know this, for according to Mahari Weil’s view, the Mishnah’s clause “and not from what is only fit to come” must also be referring to land, for movable property would in any case not be subject to collection. Now consider: If he divorced and did not have the means to pay the ketubah, and then later inherited land, according to his view he would also be exempt. Heaven forbid to mention such a thing, for we explicitly rule in Choshen Mishpat, siman 112, that from the debtor himself a creditor collects from whatever he finds with him, even if he did not write da'aknei. It is already explained in Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 104, that there is a dispute among early and later authorities whether a creditor collects from what is only fit to come. But regarding this case no one was ever uncertain. The reason is that from him personally, as long as he is alive, the lien does not move from his body, and when what was only fit to come becomes actually held by him, the lien falls upon it from then, and she collects from him. So it is stated in Bava Batra 157. It is obvious that the law of “fit to come” applies only after one dies, when the personal lien is canceled and the lien had not taken effect on the property during his lifetime. This is clear without any doubt, and proof is unnecessary. The main law that he derived from chapter HaIsha Shenaflu, that we do not find a Tanna, and so on, concluding that since we see that according to Rabbi Meir she does not collect from movable property that is only fit to come even during his lifetime, the same should apply to us, who hold that she does collect from movable property, namely that she does not collect from what is only fit to come, is very astonishing. Even according to his own approach, the source disproves him. The Rosh there writes that according to our view that movable property is subject to collection, it is not at all relevant to attach collection from movable property to the leniency of ketubah, since there is no dispute about it. There is also proof from there that as long as he is alive, there is no place for “fit to come,” only for the existence of the law of Rabbi Natan. The practical result is that if he wishes to pay and does not wish to claim, “You are not my litigant,” then during his lifetime it becomes actually held and he is obligated to pay it. Were it not for Rabbi Natan’s law, the litigant would have room to say, “You are not my litigant.” Therefore, even before the Gemara descends to Rabbi Natan’s law, it does not say that the Mishnah is teaching that a loan is considered fit to come, since a yavam is like the husband during his lifetime. From the husband himself, it is obvious that “fit to come” does not apply at all. In my humble opinion, all his words in this matter appear astonishing.
