“One who was married.” This is the language of the Rambam, and the Tur copied the language of the Mishnah: One who was married to two women and sold his field, and the first wife wrote to the buyer, “I have no claim or dealings with you,” in a manner that is effective. This means, as the Gemara answers, where they made an acquisition from her, and also where she wrote to the first buyer but did not sign for him. As for why the Gemara did not answer that the buyer first bought from the woman, Tosafot answer that the language of the Mishnah implies that after the field was sold, she wrote to the buyer. One should not ask why the Tanna did not teach the case where he bought from the woman first, because he wished to teach incidentally the law of two buyers, where the concern of “I acted only to give satisfaction” does not apply. The law of one who bought from the woman first had already been taught in its place. In Sefer Torat Yekutiel, in the introduction, he writes that one must examine the Gemara’s question: “When she wrote, what of it? But the condition,” and so on. That case is not comparable, for there it discusses a field owned by partners, and it is correct that he has said nothing, as Rashi writes at the beginning of HaKotev, because there is no language of gift there. There, if he did not speak in the language of sale, it is nothing. But here, if he removed himself from the buyer, why should we not say that it is like waiver? If a creditor waives to the buyer that he has no claim against him, would it not help? One must simply say that certainly if he waived to the buyer so that he would have no claim or dealings against him, it would help like any waiver. Why should waiving to the buyer be worse than waiving to the borrower? Rather, here one must necessarily establish the case as one where he removed himself from the body of the field. Otherwise, why would the collection cycle return repeatedly? See there. I am astonished that he did not let his ears hear what his mouth had uttered in this very discussion, for he cited the language of the Rambam in chapter 19 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh: One who borrowed from one person and afterwards transferred his property to two people, and the creditor wrote to the second buyer, “I have no claim or dealings with you,” and they made an acquisition from him, he cannot seize from the first buyer. These are the words of the baraita in HaKotev, page 95, except that the Rambam added “and they made an acquisition from him” in order to resolve the difficulty: when he wrote to him, what of it? There, no repeated collection cycle is mentioned. The Mordechai at the beginning of HaKotev sensed the basic difficulty and wrote: If you ask, even with a married woman too, why can she not remove herself, since waiver does not require an acquisition? One can answer that since the produce has not yet come into the world, she cannot remove herself. End quote. In this answer, he means that it is like waiving something that has not yet come into the world. Here too, according to that answer, since he cannot seize from the buyer as long as he finds free assets, it is called a waiver before the obligation has arisen, and is like waiving something that has not yet come into the world. Nevertheless, since the land is already in the world, and he has a lien upon it if the creditor does not find other property, it is considered as though he has acquired a right in it, and removal does not help except through an acquisition. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 209, end of se'if 4 in the gloss, and the Sema, and examine carefully. Although presumably if the lender waives the guarantor’s obligation, it helps, even though he too cannot come against him before going to the borrower, one can say that a guarantor is different. His own person is a guarantor for whatever may be found, so it is considered more as though he is already obligated to him and the obligation is already in the world. This is unlike a buyer, upon whom there is no lien at all except on this land. Alternatively, one may forcefully say that the concept of waiver does not apply at all to a specific object where he has no lien on the person himself. Our Sages, of blessed memory, were more expert than we are in knowing how and when the language of waiver, removal, gift, or sale applies. From the second answer of the Mordechai it sounds that he objected because of the weakness of the phrase “claim and dealings,” and one may infer from this that if he used explicit language of waiver, it would help there. One may say the same here. This requires study, for in my humble opinion it appears as I have written.
