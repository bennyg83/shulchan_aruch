“And some say, specifically where he gave it to others,” and so on. Maharam, of blessed memory, in Mordechai, derived this from chapter Nachalot Shivah, page 49. He holds that when we rule that the law does not follow the first Tanna, this refers to the enactment that he and his wife are maintained from them. But regarding the woman, even during his lifetime, or at least after his death, where there is no person who will trouble himself for her, certainly because of her loss, such a recipient of a gift, who was fit to inherit, is not treated like an ordinary purchaser. The idea that “had he not received benefit from him, he would not have given it to him” does not apply. The Sages treated him only like an heir, and therefore she may seize from him for her maintenance, just as she is maintained from her husband’s property in the hands of the heir. From this he learned that the same applies to us: movable property of orphans is subject to collection, but purchasers and ordinary recipients of gifts are not. However, from such a recipient of a gift, she is maintained. The same applies to the ketubah, that she may seize from them. His main support is from what we see regarding the husband: in a place where there is loss to another, namely the widow’s maintenance, they did not treat the husband as a purchaser, because he did not spend money in acquiring it, even though he married her relying on it. All the more so with this kind of recipient of a gift, regarding whom the reasoning “had he not received benefit” does not apply. It appears in my humble opinion that they hold this only regarding maintenance and collection of a ketubah, which is a debt recorded in a document. But regarding an oral loan, even though his reasoning would also apply, since we hold that an oral loan is collected from heirs but not from purchasers, and one might say that this kind of gift recipient should have the law of an heir and an oral loan should be collected from him, nevertheless in my humble opinion he certainly follows the view of the Rif in siman 91. According to that view, even regarding the husband himself the question was not resolved, and we rule that he does not collect, because he caused his own loss by lending in that way, as I explained there from the responsum of Maharam Alshich. The same applies here. For an oral loan, or even a written document without agav, or even with agav according to us, because we hold of the market enactment, one does not collect from such a recipient. Know this, for he himself there at the end of chapter Nachalot Shivah rules that if there are witnesses that he transferred Shabbat and festival clothing to his wife and children, the creditor does not collect from them. All of this is only according to what we hold, that an oral loan, or a document without agav, is not collected from purchasers, as explained in Tur, Choshen Mishpat, siman 97, in the name of the Ramban. But if the children, regarding their gifts, had the law of heirs, then according to us, since movable property of orphans is subject to collection, he should collect even the weekday clothing of his children. Rather, it is clear that regarding an oral loan, where the reasoning of “he caused his own loss” applies, he agrees. His words were said only regarding ketubah and maintenance, where “he caused his own loss” does not apply. The Beit Shmuel writes in the name of the responsa of the Shach that an oral loan is not collected from heirs. He means where the father gave them a healthy person’s gift. Perhaps this is his intent according to his own view. See siman 112, se'if 7.
