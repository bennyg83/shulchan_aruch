Gloss: “The father who instructed.” It is stated in Mahari Weil, siman 40, from which this gloss emerges, concerning a case where one obligated himself to give his son a dowry, to be paid two years after the wedding. In the meantime, the son became an apostate, and the apostate’s wife came to collect her ketubah from this money. The father prevailed for two reasons. One reason was that the money was only fit to come, even though the son was alive. I have already elaborated on the contradictions in this reason. But he also ruled in favor of the father because the father immediately acquired the ownerless property. He wrote that although the woman’s lien was earlier, this is comparable to that which Rabbi Yochanan says: If one says, “Pull this cow, but do not acquire it until after thirty days,” he does not acquire it if it is standing in the public domain, even if he said “from now,” because once he said “until after thirty days,” the acquisition is not completed until after thirty days. So too here, since he specified that payment should be after two years, the obligation was not completed until after two years. Since this is so, the woman’s lien took effect only after two years, and the money had already become ownerless before then. End quote. In my humble opinion, this reason also requires study, for it is not at all similar to that case, where he explicitly said, “and do not acquire,” and so on. That is not so here. Consider according to his view: If the father died within the two years, how would the lien be completed after his death? If it was not completed during his lifetime, from where would the assets become subject to lien, since they had already fallen before the orphans? Rather, it must be that the lien takes effect immediately, only the time of payment is set for after two years. It is exactly like one who truly borrows and sets the repayment date for a certain time. Although in siman 66 I cited the Rosh, who wrote similarly regarding ketubah, that is different, because he wrote “when you marry another,” and one can say that the lien is completed one moment before the divorce or death. Even there, were it not for the proof from the Talmud, see there, there would be no room for his words. See siman 93, se'if 22. But here, where it is tied to a period of two years, how is it possible that the lien would be completed if he dies within the time? In my humble opinion, there is another basis for his ruling, namely clear estimation of intent, that he did not obligate himself with this in mind. This is worse than the dowry case of the Mishnah according to Rabbenu Tam. I later saw that perhaps this is included in his words, where he writes, “Greater than this Maharam wrote,” and so on, concerning one who pledged a dowry for his daughter. See there. It is also possible that this is a very unusual matter, that he would become an apostate, and it is worse. This requires study. What is implied simply from his words is that if the lien had taken effect immediately, she would collect, and the father could not acquire it from ownerless property because her lien was earlier. This is indeed explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 275, that wherever collection is made from heirs, collection is also made from one who acquired ownerless property. However, this requires some study, because there the Beit Yosef brings a responsum of the Rashba comparing ownerless property to monetary consecration, and therefore it is obvious to him that just as monetary consecration does not remove a lien from movable property, the same is true of ownerless property. If so, one could say according to the view of all the decisors cited in Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 255, that monetary consecration has the law of encumbered property, and an oral loan is not collected from it. Likewise, the Tur, Choshen Mishpat, siman 115, holds that monetary consecration removes a lien from movable property. See siman 93, se'if 20. If so, perhaps ownerless property too has the law of encumbered property, and debts and ketubah are not collected from encumbered movable property. Alternatively, one can say that ownerless property is worse than monetary consecration, and is like one who accepts a condition, no better than an heir. This requires study in the responsum of the Ramban, which the Beit Yosef hints to here and also there in Choshen Mishpat. Later, when I merited to see that responsum, I found it in siman 69, and thank God my understanding matched his words. See there, where he concludes as halacha that movable property deposited with another person cannot be used for ketubah collection if the deposit holder acquires it from ownerless property. It sounds there that in this case the enactment of the Geonim does not help, because it is treated like movable property of orphans. But from what they took from the domain of the convert, an ordinary creditor and ketubah creditor collect properly. See there and examine. The Chelkat Mechokek writes: Since he compares it to a worker’s wages, why does it matter that a time was specified? The Beit Shmuel answers that the Shulchan Aruch is speaking where he instructed this as a deathbed gift. Therefore, if he had not written a time, it would not be considered fit to come. This distinction is also necessary in Mahari Weil, for he wrote regarding the ruling of Maharil, which is the second law written by the Rama, “or where he instructed,” and so on, and his reason is that since the money was deposited in the hands of others for three years, it was considered fit to come. This implies that if it had been an inheritance deposited simply in the hands of others, and he had not instructed them not to give it to him, even though it had not yet come into his hands, it would not be considered fit to come. This is because immediately upon death, wherever it is, it stands in the possession of the heir. The same applies to a deathbed gift, which is considered a rabbinic inheritance. In any case, we learn from this that a biblical inheritance is certainly considered actually held even before it reaches the heir’s hand, except where he instructed not to give it until a later time. See siman 90. The Chelkat Mechokek writes that one who is precise in Mahari Weil’s language will see that he compares it to a worker’s wages, and that it is unlike a loan, where the money had been in his hands and his right did not disappear even when he lent it to others. This is the language of Mahari Weil. One may infer from this language that if he lent the money before he married, then married and died, she would not collect from this loan, because at the time he married her the money was no longer with him for the lien to take effect upon it. The Ba'er Heitev brings this in the name of Maharam Trani, though I do not have it, and in the name of the Rama. I examined the matter, and it is only that he forced himself regarding the core reason why the woman collects from a loan, even though for other matters it is considered fit to come. Perhaps they did not accept the Rosh’s reason based on the law of Rabbi Natan, because a ketubah is not given for collection during his lifetime. But according to us, who rely entirely on Rabbi Natan’s law, as explained in the Beit Yosef and Chelkat Mechokek, se'if katan 19, one who examines Choshen Mishpat, siman 86, in the Beit Yosef, will see explicitly that in this manner the opposite is true, and everyone agrees that Rabbi Natan’s law applies. Since when he borrowed from Reuven, Levi was already obligated to him, when he entered into obligation to Reuven, he brought Levi’s obligation with him. This is unlike a loan made after he was already obligated to Reuven, which is similar to our case, where he lends after marriage. In that case there is a dispute whether Rabbi Natan’s law applies. If so, from the plain language of the decisors regarding loan in this se'if, it is certainly clear that in every case she collects. The reason of the Mordechai regarding workers’ wages appears to me to be as explained in Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh, that he follows the “some say” view in the Ba'al HaTerumot. The Mishneh LaMelech writes: The author of Panim Meirot also wrote regarding that Mordechai about workers’ wages that perhaps the Rambam disagrees, because both a creditor and a woman collect from workers’ wages even though they had not come into his hand, since from the time the employer became obligated, he also became obligated to his creditor through Rabbi Natan’s law. See there. This law is not agreed upon, for there is an opinion that Rabbi Natan’s law does not apply to wages, as the Ba'al HaTerumot brings in sha'ar 51. End quote. This view is also mentioned in Beit Yosef, Choshen Mishpat, siman 86, and in Shulchan Aruch the ruling is not like it. According to this, the Rama requires study, for here he rules plainly like the Mordechai, while there he rules plainly that even regarding wages Rabbi Natan’s law applies. Perhaps regarding ketubah this is considered two leniencies, and we do not apply them: first, applying Rabbi Natan’s law, and second, saying that Rabbi Natan’s law applies to wages. According to the wording copied there in the Beit Yosef, namely: “There is an opinion that we apply Rabbi Natan’s law only to a debt that he lent or became obligated through commerce, but regarding a debt of wages or a grant, we do not rule like Rabbi Natan, for the verse expounds ‘to him,’ and not to his creditor. This shows that Rabbi Natan’s law applies only to a debt through a loan or commerce. According to us, his view is logical regarding a grant but not regarding wages.” End quote. According to this, we do not need the distinction of Mahari Weil and the Chelkat Mechokek at all. Rather, whenever the debt resembles wages, meaning it is not through commerce but a gift that the obligating party undertook, there is no difference whether he became obligated before marriage or afterwards. In every case, Rabbi Natan’s law does not apply according to this view. Regarding ketubah we follow this view. But an actual loan or commerce is different, and again there is no distinction. It is never considered fit to come because of Rabbi Natan’s law. However, in Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh, in his long responsum, toward the end of the passage beginning “Furthermore, I say,” he also writes: The Mordechai at the beginning of Yesh Nochalin writes that workers’ wages that never came into his hand and were not collected appear to be fit to come. The Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 104, reads “for the husband.” One should examine this: since he rules that a loan is not considered fit to come, why are workers’ wages considered fit to come? It appears that he holds like the “some say” view in Ba'al HaTerumot, that Rabbi Natan’s law applies only to a debt that he lent, or that became obligated to him because something came from his hand into the other’s hand, so that when his money reached the other, he already acquired a right in it. But with a debt of wages or a grant, the second borrower does not enter under the lien of the first borrower. According to this language, it sounds that this “some say” opinion distinguishes between a loan because of the same reasoning as the dispute of Mahari Weil and the Chelkat Mechokek. If so, perhaps the view of the Mordechai is specifically that a debt of wages earned after he became obligated to his lender, similar to a grant, is where Rabbi Natan’s law does not apply, because two deficiencies exist: this money never came into his hand for a lien to take effect on it, and when he borrowed, the one who later owed him these wages was not yet obligated to him. See the Mordechai in chapter HaManiach. But if he earned wages before he married, then at the time of marriage he brought this lien with him, and Rabbi Natan’s law properly applies, and he agrees that the ketubah is collected from it. Therefore, regarding a loan, the ruling is stated plainly that the ketubah is collected from it, even from what he lent before marrying her. In this case, although no lien took effect on the money itself, nevertheless at the time he married he brought this obligation with him. This is unlike wages earned after marriage, where there are two drawbacks. If so, Mahari Weil has no proof for his ruling, for one can say that the debt of his father, which was owed to him before he married, is such that at the time of marriage he brought this lien with him, and Rabbi Natan’s law properly applies. But it is more reasonable that the Mordechai follows the “some say” view in the language copied by the Beit Yosef, and therefore regarding wages he rules plainly that one never collects. The actual text of Ba'al HaTerumot requires study, because it is practically relevant in the case of Mahari Weil, where the husband died and the woman came to collect her ketubah from the father’s dowry debt that he had obligated himself to before the wedding. See Maharil, siman 74. Although our ketubot have the law of ordinary debts, and it is already agreed in Choshen Mishpat that Rabbi Natan’s law applies to a loan in every case, the practical difference would be where he added on his own more than the one-third addition to the dowry, which certainly has the law of ketubah. Regarding ketubah, it appears proper to rule like the Mordechai and the Rama, of blessed memory, because we do not apply two leniencies in ketubah. Perhaps even regarding the one-third addition itself there are two leniencies, since the Beit Yosef holds that it has the law of ketubah, and he wrote regarding Maharik, “his words do not appear to me.” This requires study.
