“And if the heirs sold them.” See Chelkat Mechokek, and see Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 252, se'if katan 6. “And nowadays the custom is to write in every ketubah,” and so on, “and what I will acquire in the future,” nevertheless, and so on. From this it appears that only regarding movable property, because of the market enactment, this writing does not help. Were it not for that, it certainly would help. But regarding land, the writing of da'aknei, property that I will acquire in the future, does help even for ketubah, so that she may seize from what he acquired and then sold. This is also proven, for surely the Rama did not establish this text for no reason in chapter 4 of Hilchot Yibbum. The Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 115, se'if katan 29, writes: According to this, it emerges that da'aknei does not help for ketubah and additional ketubah. He forced himself in explaining the language of the Tur and the author of Shulchan Aruch in siman 102, se'if 3, and concludes: According to this, what we write in ketubot, “and what I am destined to acquire,” does not help against purchasers. In my humble opinion, this is not so, as is implied here. This is also proven from the beginning of chapter Af Al Pi, where the Gemara does not state as a practical difference between ketubah and other debts the law of da'aknei, but mentions only enhancement. Specifically enhancement is different, because it is not truly da'aknei, but the Sages agreed regarding a creditor that it is considered like da'aknei through the seller’s writing, “I will stand it up for you,” as is well explained in Nimukei Yosef, cited by the Sema there. This is one of the leniencies of ketubah. But with true da'aknei, why should we distinguish? The main reason is that the Shach wrote this according to his interpretation of the sugya in Bechorot. He wrote that all the great authorities struggled needlessly, and he proposed that when Rava needs the reason that a creditor collects enhancement, that applies only to expenses. But for enhancement beyond the expenses, he does not need that reason, for through da'aknei he seizes it by law. Therefore, it was obvious to him that enhancement is actual da'aknei, and he learned that just as a ketubah does not seize enhancement, so too writing da'aknei does not help for it. All of this led him to reject the words of Nimukei Yosef in se'if katan 12 and 27 with both hands. But I have already elaborated and concluded that the words of Nimukei Yosef are necessary and clear in their reasoning. Specifically with a purchaser, through the above writing, the enhancement is considered da'aknei. But with a recipient of a gift, who has no one against whom to claim reimbursement, and all the more so with orphans, where both factors exist, namely they have no one from whom to seek reimbursement and when the enhancement came into existence the one who created the lien, namely the father, had already died, see there, se'if katan 16, it is not considered da'aknei at all. The early authorities did not struggle for no reason, for it is impossible to consider enhancement that comes through expenses in the hands of orphans as da'aknei. In my humble opinion, with due respect to the other early authorities, the clearest explanation is that of Milchamot, which he cites there. Since this is not its place, I will not copy the lengthy discussion of his words against most of the words of the Shach there. In any case, this is clear in my humble opinion: writing da'aknei helps for ketubah and additional ketubah. So it is stated explicitly in Rashbam, Bava Batra 133, s.v. amar lei Rav Kahana, that if she finds a place from which to seize, such as property, she seizes from it, as he wrote, “and what I am destined to acquire,” that future assets are placed under lien to her. The expression “she seizes” is certainly said regarding purchasers. The Beit Shmuel writes that he wrote this based on the responsum of the Rosh. I already wrote nearby that one should examine the Rashba on this. He means the end of se'if katan 5, where he wrote: “It is heard from these responsa,” and so on. Therefore he writes here that even without this the Rashba does not agree, and it appears according to all, and so on. I am astonished. Does the Rashba really have a different legal view from the Rosh? The Shach already cites in Choshen Mishpat, siman 60, se'if katan 4, that several decisors, including the Rosh, wrote this custom. Nevertheless, there in se'if katan 6 he decides that even if the property came after the sale into the hands of this creditor, who has agav, he must return it, since the purchaser already acquired it according to his acquisition. See his words at the end of siman 126, where he explains his view and challenges the responsum of Maharam Alshich. If this is the Shach’s view regarding an ordinary creditor, where in se'if katan 4 he expresses doubt because the custom is not to write this in every document, then all the more so regarding ketubah, where the custom is to write it in all ketubot, there is certainly no basis to question the custom not to seize based on the market enactment. Otherwise, no person would buy an object from someone who has a wife. Rather, the main purpose of writing this agav clause is only for the specific matters mentioned in siman 60 in the Sema. The clear conclusion is as the Shach writes, that even through an acquisition alone, even if the property came into her hands, it is taken out of her hands. This requires study.
