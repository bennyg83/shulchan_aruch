(Seif 6:) If he said to her, "on condition that you give me such-and-such vessel or such-and-such garment," etc. NB: The ruling that it is not a get requires study regarding the Mechaber and the later authorities, who wrote simply in a way that implies that it is not a get at all. In truth, one who examines Rashi on Gittin 74b, s.v. de-letza'urei ka-mekhaven, sees that Rashi writes that because of doubt we say he intended to distress her, and we do not assume leniently that he intended only to profit. It is therefore clear that Rashi holds this is only a doubt, not a certainty. Accordingly, if another man betrothed her, she at least requires a get from him, for perhaps the husband intended only profit and the condition has already been fulfilled. I later saw that Beit Shmuel above, s.k. 6, cited this in the name of the Bach, though he pressed Rashi's intent into another explanation, and Rashi's language does not bear that at all. It further appears to me to prove that this is Rashi's intent, for one may ask why Rashi waited until the end of the sugya, after the dispute of Rav Yosef and Rabbah, to explain the intent of "he means to distress her," and did not explain it earlier regarding the maneh, where the Gemara answers the Mishnah in Nedarim. It therefore appears that Rashi had a specific intent. Rashi was troubled by the Gemara's initial thought that Rabbah follows Rashbi, since the Gemara had already distinguished between vows and this case on the ground that here he intends to distress her, so it is not comparable to there. One must say that Rashi holds that the case of an aris is weaker than vows. With vows, there is no reason to attribute any intent except benefiting the son, and there is no proof or assessment that he intended some other reason; it is simply reasonable that he intended profit. But with an aris there are two possible intentions: either he meant to give him the third only if he himself irrigates, but if he does not irrigate he will not give it to him, since he did not trouble himself, and what difference is there whether the aris irrigated or the rain came; or he meant that in whatever manner the field is irrigated as he wishes, he will give him a third, and this is the Gemara's meaning that he intended profit. From the standpoint of reason, however, it certainly leans more to saying that he intended only if the aris would need to exert himself, and if he did not exert himself he would not give. It is therefore truly similar to gittin, for here too an ordinary divorce is given from animosity, and therefore we say that he means to distress her; likewise, an ordinary promise of a third is in payment for labor and not where no labor was done. Thus even after the Gemara distinguished between vows and gittin, it challenges Rabbah from the aris case, because he holds that an aris resembles gittin. According to this, one must understand the Gemara's answer there: when it says that there he means to distress her but here he means profit, the questioner also knew this possibility, but thought that here too it resembles gittin. Rashi therefore explains that the novelty of the answer is not that we know certainly that in gittin he means to distress her, such that this should also apply to the aris, but that in gittin the assumption that he means to distress her is only a matter of doubt; since gittin is a Torah prohibition, we rule stringently and attribute it to distress. But with an aris, which is monetary law, and monetary doubt is treated leniently, we attribute it to profit. Even if the aris is in possession, Rashi holds that seizure does not help for a claim of perhaps, and the produce remains in the possession of its original owner, since the aris has no right in it until the work is completed. Rashi's statement that a man generally divorces his wife from animosity is meant to distinguish vows, where even though that too may involve a Torah prohibition, we do not rule stringently, because there we say he certainly intended profit; here, where divorce is from animosity, there is some proof that he intended to distress her. Therefore we do not assume leniently. The aris, by contrast, even though there is also an assessment that the owner intended only if the aris troubled himself, is monetary, so we assume leniently. This is Rashi's explanation of the Gemara: when the Gemara says that there the Sages said only that he means to distress her, it means that we attribute such intent only by way of stringency, whereas here we attribute profit and rule leniently. Therefore Rashi did not explain this earlier, for at that stage we would have explained that in get we have certain testimony that he means to distress her, and in vows he certainly means profit. But since here it would then certainly be distress, there would be no distinction between money and prohibition, and the Gemara could challenge from aris to get. The Gemara therefore answers that it is only doubtful, and Rashi was compelled to explain it so. According to this, Rashi indeed holds that the matter is doubtful. However, from the Gemara there, which says regarding "they are waived to you" that she is not divorced, it implies that she is entirely not divorced and that there is no get at all. Although one could press that it says "not divorced" only stringently, it remains difficult: if he waives it, why should he not be believed that he did not intend to distress her but only to profit, especially according to those above in seif 5 who hold that if he says "let it be in your hand without condition," it helps. If so, even when he uses language of waiver and admits that he did not intend to distress her, why should he not be believed with a migo, since had he wished he could have said it in the language of no condition at all. Perhaps the Gemara speaks where he does not say that he did not intend to distress her, but if he says so, perhaps according to Rashi he is believed. This requires study.
