And some say that if she is appeased by receiving the money, it is a get. NB: See Beit Shmuel, who cites the Drishah and Maharshal as raising a difficulty against the view of the Rosh, that one should prove from the case that giving against one's will is not considered giving. In my humble opinion, it is very astonishing to say that this was Maharshal's difficulty, for there is no proof here that it is not considered giving. The Gemara explicitly concludes that according to Rabbah, whose ruling we accept, the reason of the Sages is that he intends to distress her. If so, that applies well when she does not give him the object that was stipulated; but if they give him the very object stipulated, then she has truly distressed him, and the concern of distress does not apply at all. It is possible that this is considered giving, and this is obvious even to a young child who studies the Gemara. How could the great Maharshal have raised such a question and left it difficult? The correct explanation is that a scribal error fell into the Drishah before them, and the correct text is as found in the newer Berlin editions of the Turim, where it says that one may prove from here that giving against one's will is considered giving, as the Beit Shmuel himself later asks. In that form Maharshal's question is sound. Also, what Beit Shmuel wrote, asking from the Gemara's conclusion that he intends to distress her, despite having already written that giving to beit din not in his presence does not help if we do not know whether he is pleased, requires explanation. It appears that his intent is to question Rabbah's own rule there: since there the reason of distress does not apply, and the condition is properly fulfilled, even though the litigant now claims that he specifically wanted the aris to irrigate and not the rain, and the other party does not wish to give him what was stipulated, nevertheless he must give it to him, even though it is giving against his will, since the owner does not want this. Since distress does not apply, it is certainly valid giving. So too with a condition in a get: if she gives him that very object, it is proper giving, for the Gemara compares Rabbah's rule to get and only distinguishes that there it implies distress. But where there is no distress, it resembles Rabbah's rule. Even if the husband does not want this, if he wanted to accept the giving we would have no issue; rather, he claims that he does not want to accept except where the aris actually drew the water, not where rain came. Nevertheless, it is considered giving; so too by get. This seems to be how Beit Shmuel's words must be explained, for otherwise they have no explanation at all; examine carefully. What the Mechaber wrote above in this seif, "if he said to her on condition that you give me such-and-such vessel," has its source in Gittin 74. The difficulty raised by the Chiddushei HaRim, cited in his commentary and novellae, should be seen there; I resolved it with God's help in a responsum to the holy community of Konstantin, placed in siman 135 in my fourth edition, siman 1; see there. While engaged in this matter, I saw that one should examine slightly from where the poskim know that even when he stipulated concerning a particular object that was never his, we still say that he intended to distress her. This is difficult, for the Mishnah that is the source of the rule speaks of giving him his cloak, implying that it had been his originally. If so, one could say that since we generally hold that a person prefers his own kav, in that case Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel holds that even with his own object we testify that he intended only profit and money helps, while the Sages hold that since one can say he prefers his own, and one can say he intended distress, these two considerations together make return of the money ineffective. But where it was never his, one could say that the argument that he meant to distress her is not strong enough by itself to void the get. Nor can this be derived from "on condition that you serve my father" or "on condition that you nurse my son," for if so Tosafot would not have needed to say in the sugya that they had a tradition; necessarily there is room to distinguish between them. See Penei Yehoshua in his novellae, who understood this. If so, it is difficult: perhaps the tradition applies only where he conditioned about his own object, where there is an additional assessment that he desires what is his, but in general from where do we know it? This requires study. In truth, the Tur mentions only the language of "my cloak," and one may say that he spoke specifically. But regarding Rambam and the Mechaber the difficulty remains as stated above; examine this carefully.
