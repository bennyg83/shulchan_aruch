(ב) However, regarding something that requires burning. So wrote the Ran according to the view that anything standing to be burned is considered already burned; then it is as though the letters are flying in the air. R. Shimon and the Sages dispute this principle in several places. Rashi wrote in Menachot 102 that we hold like R. Shimon, who says that anything standing to be burned is considered burned. Chelkat Mechokek brought proof that Rambam holds the halakhah does not follow R. Shimon, for Rambam wrote in chapter 4 of Hilkhot Pesulei HaMukdashin that if it became known after the blood of a conditional guilt-offering was sprinkled that he had not sinned, the meat may be eaten. This implies that if it became known before sprinkling, it may not be eaten, which follows the Sages, who hold that what stands to be sprinkled is not considered sprinkled. One should wonder at him: how did he overlook the Gemara in Keritot 24, which establishes that case even according to R. Shimon? R. Shimon said his rule only regarding something standing for sprinkling, but this offering, if he did not sin, is not standing for sprinkling, and R. Shimon agrees. See Kesef Mishneh, chapter 3 of Pesulei HaMukdashin, law 19, where Rambam rules that a service vessel sanctifies disqualified blood to be offered, and he writes that Rambam rules like R. Yose there in Keritot, who holds that if it became known that he did not sin while the blood was in the cup, he should sprinkle it, because the service vessel sanctifies the disqualified item to be offered. This is difficult: if so, why did Rambam rule in chapter 4 that only if it became known after sprinkling may it be eaten, but not when the blood is in the cup? Rather, it appears that regarding a conditional guilt-offering Rambam does not hold like R. Yose for the above reason, because once it is known that he did not sin, it is chullin retroactively. But generally a service vessel sanctifies disqualified items where a disqualification occurred in the body of the offering, as Tosafot wrote explicitly in Keritot. According to this, Rambam holds that what stands to be sprinkled is considered sprinkled, and what stands to be burned is considered burned; a conditional guilt-offering is different, and a service vessel does not sanctify it. Regarding other disqualifications where the vessel sanctifies, one may say he learned this from the sugya in Zevachim 87, where R. Yochanan resolved that a service vessel sanctifies. Perhaps Rambam's distinction in chapter 3, that a service vessel sanctifies blood but not other items, is also learned from that sugya: in Zevachim R. Yochanan resolved that it sanctifies, while in Menachot 6 he resolved that it does not; therefore Rambam distinguishes between blood and other items. This resolves the difficulty of R. Peter in Tosafot there. Nor can one bring proof from Rambam at the beginning of chapter 3 of Me'ilah, where he writes that permission to the kohanim is called so only once the blood is sprinkled, while the sugya in Menachot implies that according to R. Shimon even before sprinkling it is called permitted to the kohanim because it stands for sprinkling. Tosafot in Me'ilah 5 wrote that the sugya holds that even according to R. Shimon, sanctity with respect to me'ilah is removed only after sprinkling. See Rambam, chapter 13 of Terumot, where it implies he rules like R. Shimon, and see the Rosh at the end of Perek Gid HaNasheh and Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim, folio 19.
