The Beit Shmuel further writes, and the Taz writes, that this applies only to a minor who has reached the age of pe'utot, the developmental stage at which a child's transactions have some force, but below that age he is not believed even when speaking innocently. I quote the stringency alone. His language is: "It appears to me that in truth there is no practical dispute between the Tur and Rambam. The minor who is effective according to the Tur means one who has reached that stage. A minor is disqualified not because he is outside the category of testimony, for a slave is valid here, but because he lacks legal understanding and is comparable to a shoteh. Perhaps in such a case he is not validated even through innocent speech, for the Mishnah did not speak of this." In my humble opinion, however, the Gemara's question, "perhaps it was a locust," certainly implies that the Mishnah speaks at least even of one who has not reached that stage; once a child has reached the stage at which his purchases are valid, it is no longer their way to jest in that manner. He continues that according to Rambam the Mishnah certainly speaks of every minor, and it is effective only when the child says, "we were coming," etc., even if he has not reached that stage. But where he has reached that stage Rambam agrees that he is effective if he says explicitly, "he died"; at any rate, our master the Tur is worthy of reliance once the child has reached that stage. End quote. Although in my humble opinion Rambam's language does not imply that he agrees once the child has reached that stage, I am nevertheless uncertain whether Rambam requires an actual adult with pubic hairs, or whether for testimony permitting a woman to remarry the rule is like the gloss in Orach Chayim 55: once he has reached the years of adulthood we presume him to be an adult and say ordinarily that he has brought hairs. The source of that custom appears to me to be Massekhet Soferim 16, which says that a minor does not count for Barekhu, Torah reading, or Kaddish until he is thirteen, and that we do not examine them for these matters. One may raise an even greater doubt: perhaps Rambam at least agrees that when he has reached the years of majority he does not need hairs at all, since Rambam makes his disqualification depend on lack of understanding. In Hilkhot Edut 9:7 Rambam writes that even a minor who is perceptive and wise remains invalid until he brings two hairs, which implies that here the matter does not depend on hairs but on understanding. See the responsum of the Noda BiYehudah, responsum 41, who decides that actual hairs and examination are specifically required. According to his approach, he writes that the minor is believed about himself by an a fortiori argument: if he is believed as an adult to testify that a bride went out with a hinuma because most women marry as virgins, all the more should he be believed that he brought two hairs, since the majority supports him; and certainly if he is examined now and hairs are found. It appears that at first he argued by a fortiori even without any examination at all. In my humble opinion this a fortiori argument can be refuted. Evidence that we rely on a majority when he testifies as an adult about others is no proof that he may testify about himself, especially where perhaps at the very time of testimony he is still a minor, and if he is a minor his words have no force. We have not found testimony about oneself anywhere in the Torah, except for a woman regarding the death of her husband. Apart from this, the law itself requires examination in my opinion, and it seems more likely to me that hairs are not required at all. See Yoreh De'ah 65 in the gloss, which writes that he is called a minor until he is thirteen years and one day old, excluding the view brought in Darkhei Moshe that permits a child entering his thirteenth year. This is difficult, for we rule that "within the time" is as before the time. Rather, it must be that all agree that hairs are not required for testimony in which one witness suffices. The Kereti u-Peleti presses the language of the Shulchan Aruch, and I do not know how he would answer the permissive view cited in the Darkhei Moshe.
