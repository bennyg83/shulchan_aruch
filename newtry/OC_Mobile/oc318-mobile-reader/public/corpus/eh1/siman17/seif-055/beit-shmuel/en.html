And some say that she is not believed. This is the Ran's version in the Rambam, as written in the responsum brought by Beit Yosef. It follows from this that there is a distinction between a teiku and two versions, for here there are two versions in the Gemara whether plague is comparable to war, and according to the Rambam she is not believed, which implies that she must leave; whereas with a teiku he holds that she need not leave. From this one can also bring proof that in a dispute among poskim one should be stringent. However, one may say that here, regarding plague, by Torah law there is no permission when she says my husband died; the Sages were merely lenient for an agunah. Therefore, with two versions or a dispute among poskim, one should be stringent. But when he drowned in water that has no end, she is permitted by Torah law and the Sages were stringent, so one may say that in a dispute one is lenient. If a person was burned and his wife cried out and said, see, my husband has been burned, she is believed, specifically where there is nothing indicating that another person was there. But if a hand remains and we recognize that it is not from the man who was burned, even though she said my husband was burned, she is not believed. End quote of Ri Vav, Netiv 23 part 3, in the name of the Ramah. This is learned from the Gemara in Yevamot at the beginning of folio 115. See seif 32, where it is explained that even though this person fell into the water and they brought up a leg, if there is no distinctive sign on the leg, we say that this man who fell into the water came up and went on his way, and this leg belongs to another man. Here one must say that the reason we are not concerned that perhaps another person was here and was burned is because she cried, my husband was burned; therefore we are not concerned except where it is proven that another man was there.
