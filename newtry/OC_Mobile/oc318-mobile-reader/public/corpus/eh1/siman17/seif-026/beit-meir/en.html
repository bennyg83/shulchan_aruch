And they are bound in a collar. The Chelkat Mechokek writes that Rashi explains this as a group, implying that the number ten alone is a sign, etc. The same is implied by the Masat Binyamin, responsum 44, s.v. "and it further appears," as brought by the Beit Shmuel. The Beit Shmuel also brings the Taz, who disagrees and holds that number alone is not an especially distinctive sign sufficient for testimony permitting a woman to remarry. In my humble opinion this is proved from the Gemara's difficulty in Bava Metzia 28: it asks from the mishnah of one who found a bundle of documents, although the Gemara already concludes on 20b that the announcement is "a document," and Rashi explains that the claimant then says how many there were. If number alone were an especially distinctive sign, and on a Torah level, there would be no difficulty. Rather, even if one is precise in Rashi's explanation, number means number together with the place to which they were traveling, that they were going to the cities of Beiter or to Antioch. This is what the Beit Shmuel hints at in subsection 55 when, after the words of the Chelkat Mechokek, he writes, "see further below concerning this," meaning subsection 72, where he cites Mahari ben Lev. The Beit Shmuel further writes at the end of that subsection that Masat Binyamin rules that if a gentile recognizes one of them and testifies that the entire group was killed, we say presumptively that it was that group. This is surprising to me, for Masat Binyamin learned this from the responsum of Rosh on the incident of Abba Yudan: the court did not need to investigate the gentile as to whether the person who had joined him was the same one, lest perhaps the Jew went on his way and another later joined him. From there he learned that the same applies where he recognizes one member of the group: we are not concerned that the first group left and others joined him. If so, it is difficult that in the end of subsection 53 the Beit Shmuel writes that Masat Binyamin ruled like Rosh; this requires examination. The Taz discusses this at length and rules not like Rabbi Shmuel, and Masat Binyamin likewise copies the matter plainly.
