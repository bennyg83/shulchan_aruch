Even if it is not validated. The Beit Shmuel writes this, and see also the responsum of Mishneh LaMelekh, who writes similarly; this requires examination, for it is not clearly explicit in his words. He further writes that validation by one witness is effective because generally, etc., it is like testimony heard from another witness. I am astonished: generally the reason is simple, that two witnesses are required as in all testimony, and a document is validated by one witness only for the purpose of an oath. What connection is there to testimony from the mouth of another witness, which would be invalid even with two witnesses? By contrast, validation for testimony permitting a woman to remarry is no better than the body of that testimony itself, and therefore one witness is sufficient. This is the plain intention of the Maggid Mishneh. In Knesset HaGedolah of Maharach Shash he pressed himself to distinguish between witnesses who validate by recognizing the signature, where two witnesses are required, and one witness who says that the document was signed before him, where one suffices. This is not correct; the matter is as understood by the Mishneh LaMelekh and by our later great authorities. Regarding Rambam's requirement, the Beit Shmuel writes that this means the writing was composed in the manner of a person speaking innocently, and that the Maggid Mishneh wrote the same, making this a novelty. The Beit Yosef objects that this has no reason: if they wrote it in the manner of innocent speech, why should it be inferior? In my humble opinion, the Maggid Mishneh follows his own view in law 13, where he maintains the position of Rivash, which is the first opinion in section 14 of the gloss. He therefore interpreted Rambam to mean that even without introductory casual words, any statement made without being asked is called innocent speech, and was forced to explain this rule as a novelty. But according to the second opinion, derived from Rambam's language, that specifically requires introductory casual words, one may explain simply that Rambam wrote this to explain the Tosefta's case, which he copied in its own wording: "they found written in a document, So-and-so died." That sounds like nothing more than a notification without a question. Rambam therefore teaches that such writing is not innocent speech, and one must insist that it be the writing of a Jew. Yet he would agree that where there were introductory casual words, even a gentile's writing is valid. It follows that if a woman has a gentile's written statement made innocently with introductory casual words, and also a gentile who testifies orally without being asked and without introductory casual words, she is permitted either way, without hesitation.
