When she said in his name that he died. See Baer Heitev in the name of the Chelkat Mechokek and Shevut Yaakov. At first glance, from what is before us in Knesset Yechezkel, siman 305, where the language of the Shevut Yaakov is copied, it is not at all explicit that a gentile may not contradict the woman; it says only that a gentile may not contradict a valid witness. If so, only the Chelkat Mechokek's second doubt is resolved, not his first. Still, since the Beit Shmuel and Machatzit HaShekel also write in his name that a gentile cannot contradict her, presumably it is written that way in the responsum itself, and it should be examined there. See Machatzit HaShekel, se'if katan 31, who writes about the Shevut Yaakov's words: "I do not know from where he derived this," and that by law it appears that the maidservant is believed to contradict her; see there. The Kehillat Yaakov agrees, as was cited above in se'if katan 44. See also the responsa of my ancestor, Panim Me'irot, part 2, siman 116. He writes that the Chelkat Mechokek's doubt, where she said something in the name of a slave or maidservant and they then contradict her, may have been raised specifically for testimony permitting a woman to remarry, because in such testimony the Sages said that the witness is treated like two witnesses and relied on the woman's careful inquiry. But regarding other prohibitions one may say that he had no doubt: certainly she is established as having lied even by disqualified witnesses. Even if one says that his doubt applies everywhere, that is only where the person comes to testify and the Torah has believed him; where the obligation rests on him to clarify the matter, however, see there. See also Responsa R. Akiva Eiger, in the addenda to siman 113. He first writes that the Chelkat Mechokek's wording, "in the name of a slave or maidservant," is not necessarily precise, and the same would apply to one who testifies in the name of a woman, for we do not find a distinction among disqualified witnesses between a woman and a slave or maidservant. He then writes that perhaps the wording is precise. The first doubt of the Chelkat Mechokek is difficult to understand: when she says it in the name of a slave or maidservant, both the woman and the maidservant are disqualified, so surely the first one should be believed. One should not entertain a doubt that perhaps the Ramban's rule applies only to a disqualified person testifying from the mouth of a valid witness, for from his phrase "and so is the law in every case of one witness from the mouth of another" it certainly follows that the rule applies even when both are equal. If so, one must also analyze the Chelkat Mechokek's second doubt, where a valid witness testifies in the name of a slave or maidservant. Since the Ramban learned his rule from the passage in Ketubot about one who said, "So-and-so the sage purified this stain for me," and there it is a disqualified person reporting from a valid one, from where did the Ramban know that the first is believed even when both are equal? Necessarily he does not distinguish in this matter, because the reason is that the later testimony comes through the force of the first; therefore the first is believed, whether the reporting witness is valid or disqualified. If so, the same should apply to a valid witness reporting from a disqualified one. One must therefore say that the Chelkat Mechokek mentioned specifically a slave or maidservant because ordinary slaves and maidservants are morally loose and somewhat suspected of lying; about that he was unsure whether the first speaker is believed. His second doubt is also built on this: even if with a woman, who is also disqualified, she is believed to contradict the report, nevertheless with an actually valid witness one may say that a slave or maidservant is not believed. But where one testifies in the name of an ordinary woman, where there is no suspicion that she is lying and she is disqualified only by the Torah's decree, she is believed. This requires study; these are his words. What Baer Heitev writes further, that if two witnesses say in the name of one person, etc., is also written by the Chelkat Mechokek and the Beit Shmuel. Below, se'if katan 123, he cites this in the name of the Ra'anach; see there further. R. Akiva Eiger writes there that this seems to apply to two valid witnesses. Although with respect to the root of the permission their testimony is not full testimony, for they testify only from the mouth of one person, and even if they testified from the mouth of two it would still be testimony from the mouth of testimony, nevertheless as to this point, namely that so-and-so said this to them, they are full witnesses that he said it to them. He is not believed to contradict them and say that he did not say it to them, for on this point it is one against two valid witnesses. Once their testimony that he said it to them has been established, we return to the rabbinic enactment that a woman may remarry on the basis of one witness from another witness. But if, on the essential foundation, they are disqualified and it is not full testimony that he said it to them, he is believed to contradict them, since they spoke only from his mouth; these are his words. What Baer Heitev writes further, that if the first witness came and said that he told him falsely, etc., see the Chelkat Mechokek, whose words imply that even if they had already permitted her to remarry the law is so, for one cannot distinguish in this matter since, in the end, the first witness never testified in court. See Responsa R. Akiva Eiger there, who writes that at first glance the Chelkat Mechokek's words are unclear, for we see that the Shiltei Gibborim in chapter 15 of Yevamot cites the Riaz; however, after analysis it appears that the Chelkat Mechokek's words are correct; see there. Regarding R. Akiva Eiger's further difficulty with Maran the Beit Yosef in responsum siman 4, see what I wrote in Nachalat Tzvi. See also Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 75. His view is unlike that of the Chelkat Mechokek, who took it as obvious that the first witness may say, "I fabricated it," even after the second witness has already testified in his name before the court. The Chatam Sofer writes that his own heart does not think so. Just as it is obvious that even in testimony about women, where the basic testimony is valid only rabbinically, the first statement in court is decisive and the witness may not retract and testify again, because otherwise the Torah's ways would not be pleasant, for after she had already been permitted and married the witness could say, "I fabricated it," and give some excuse, as the questioner wrote in Responsa Ran, siman 34. The very same reason applies to a witness who says something in the name of another witness. Once the second witness's words have been accepted and the woman has been permitted and married, a stumbling block would occur if the first could say, "I fabricated it." This too is therefore included in the Sages' enactment to believe one witness from another witness. The Sages have the power in this matter, so that once the second witness testified in court, even though the first one's statement was outside court, the first may not retract and say, "I fabricated it." Even if she was permitted and married, it is like the general rule in the Torah that one may not retract and testify again. For this reason, the Chatam Sofer also addresses the Chelkat Mechokek's first doubt, where she said it in the name of a maidservant and the maidservant contradicts her. It appears that the maidservant is certainly not believed, in accordance with the Shevut Yaakov above. True, where she says it in the name of a trustworthy witness, the concern that the Torah's ways would not be pleasant does not arise, lest the first witness come and deny having said anything and be believed by the Ramban's tradition; since neither of them is suspected of lying, there is no need to fear that. But where one testifies in the name of a maidservant or a gentile speaking incidentally, whose mouths are full of falsehood, although they are believed for permitting a woman through the combination of the woman's careful inquiry and the fact that the matter is likely to become known, and this reporter is also believed that he heard it from them, nevertheless we are not immediately secure that they will not later come and deny their first words. If we believed them by the Ramban's tradition, we would thereby remove a woman from her husband whom we had initially permitted, and how could we permit her at first? That would not be the Torah's pleasant way. Necessarily the enactment was originally made that such people are not believed to contradict one who reports in their name. See there further regarding an agunah whose husband had been taken into the army thirty years earlier. A woman testified that she heard from a poor traveler that this woman's husband had died, and the woman was permitted on the basis of responsa from rabbis. When she came to receive chalitzah, it was heard that the female witness had retracted her testimony. The court summoned her and intimidated her, and she said that because she had pity on the woman she had added something the poor man had not told her at all about her husband's death, and that the same poor man had later come to her again and denied having said anything about this woman's husband; she accepted that she deserved repentance. The question was whether she is believed to retract. The Chatam Sofer answered that had he been there he would not have summoned the female witness at all to hear her words, since she had already testified. Even in testimony about women, which is valid only rabbinically and not by Torah law, the first testimony is decisive, as explained in Responsa Ran, simanim 34 and 47. In that case the testimony had already been examined by a court. Even if one says that being permitted through responsa is not called permission by a court until three convene together and say to her, "You are permitted to marry," as the Beit Shmuel states in se'if katan 124, here, if when she came for chalitzah a court had assembled to arrange chalitzah for her, that was certainly like permission by a court. But if there was not yet a court there and she had only asked the rabbi to arrange chalitzah, perhaps she had not yet been permitted, and if one witness came contradicting the first witness she would leave her permitted status. Nevertheless here no outside witness came to contradict the first testimony, and from the wording of the question it sounds that the testimony had been accepted by a court of three. If so, she can no longer say, "I fabricated it," neither the second witness nor the first. Even if the first witness were before us and contradicted her by saying, "You testified falsely in my name," the witness would be believed under the Ramban's tradition; but the second witness is not believed to say that the first witness contradicts his own first words. The court should not have sent for her at all or listened to her retraction, as is written in Shittah Mekubbetzet to Bava Batra 48. Still, one must examine the woman's statement that because she had pity she added to the story, and that when the traveler came again she questioned him. Her words imply that the first time she erred in her imagination about the traveler's words, and from conjecture understood it as if he had explicitly said that the husband died; the second time she investigated him carefully and he said he had never intended this. In such a case, had she immediately come to court when the traveler returned and cried out, "I erred in my imagination," there might have been some reason to be concerned for her words. But now that she remained silent until after the traveler had slipped away, and further waited until the woman came for chalitzah, and only after the court sent for her, in such circumstances she is not believed, as is proved from Rashi to Ketubot 18b. However, since in every testimony it is proper and obligatory at the outset to investigate whether she spoke from conjecture and imagination, and only here we are lenient because we do not judge what is possible from what is impossible, therefore, if investigation is possible, we must do whatever is possible. If this woman's husband died in the army and did not desert, it may be easy to investigate with the officer under whom he was listed, for their practice is to issue a document called a Totenschein. See what I wrote below, se'if katan 53. But if this is impossible, we do not judge the possible from the impossible; and if a Torah scholar qualified to rule agrees with me, I will join in permitting this woman. These are his words; see there.
