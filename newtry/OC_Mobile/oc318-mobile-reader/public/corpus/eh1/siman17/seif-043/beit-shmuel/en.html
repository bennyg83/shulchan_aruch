And they give her the basic ketubah. So the Rambam writes in chapter 16 of Hilkhot Ishut regarding a woman who says to her husband, "You divorced me." I already wrote at the beginning of siman 7 that the reason she collects the principal is the derashah of the ketubah, for it is written in the ketubah: "When you marry another, take what is written for you." This derashah is not applied to the additional ketubah payment. So writes the Maggid Mishneh there, and so writes the Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Ritva in Ha-Ishah Shalom, on the Mishnah that Beit Shammai say she may marry and collect her ketubah. All the more so, she does not collect nichsei tzon barzel. Likewise, if they permit her on the basis of a gentile speaking in the course of conversation or on the basis of one witness, she does not collect the additional ketubah payment. See Responsa Maharshaldam, siman 253. In Responsa R. Yosef of the house of Levi, siman 17, he writes that this law depends on the reasons given there for why Beit Hillel retracted to rule like Beit Shammai that she may marry and collect the ketubah. One reason is the ketubah derashah, and a second reason is an a fortiori argument: if we permit the severe matter of a forbidden married woman, should we not permit the monetary matter? According to the ketubah-derashah reason she does not collect the additional payment, but according to the a fortiori reason she collects even the additional payment. This is also implied in the Tur in this siman and in siman 100. Nevertheless, money is not extracted unless, when they permit her and his name has disappeared, the heirs may descend to the estate as stated in Choshen Mishpat siman 284; then she too collects the additional ketubah and nichsei tzon barzel. As long as she is not permitted she does not collect the basic ketubah or the addition; so hold the Rambam and Ra'avad. Although the ketubah derashah does not apply to the additional payment but only to the principal, so the addition should be like the rest of the estate and the heirs descend to the inheritance when he drowned in water that has no end, nevertheless she does not collect it. This is unlike the Hagahot Maimoniyot cited in se'if 35.

And her intention is not to marry. So wrote the Rambam, chapter 17 halachah 1. At first glance, according to his approach, the sugya of the Gemara is difficult to reconcile. It appears that he holds that we are not concerned that she will marry in prohibition, as the Gemara says, because of the stringency imposed at the end; rather, the concern is that her intention is only to extract the money. All the give-and-take in the Gemara concerns the ketubah and not her permission to marry. Therefore he holds that if she said, give me my ketubah and permit me, the question was not resolved regarding whether we give her the ketubah; but if she wishes to marry, this was not in question, and it is obvious that she is permitted. Therefore he ruled that she is permitted to marry but we do not give her the ketubah. If she married and afterward claims the ketubah, one may say that we give her the ketubah; what he wrote, that we do not give her the ketubah, speaks only as long as she has not married. Here, however, we do not permit her because she herself did not say, permit me. But what Beit Yosef and Bach wrote, that the Rambam holds she is permitted because it is a rabbinic doubt, since the question ended in teiku and is only rabbinic, is surprising. If so, why did he rule in the teiku cases of one witness in war and one witness in a quarrel that she may not marry? There it is even more reasonable to be lenient, for there is one witness testifying that he died.
