Or it is known that they testified without deceit or distortion. This language implies that even without incidental speech they are valid if the testimony is known to be honest. Machatzit HaShekel s.k. 30 questions this, since Maharai himself was unsure whether one who has not fully repented remains biblically disqualified unless he speaks incidentally, like a gentile; if so, what does absence of deceit help, and why should this differ from a gentile? Responsa Amudey Esh also challenges Rema on this point. Noda BiYehudah, at the end of siman 36, interprets Rema in Yoreh De'ah 69:10 and develops a possible principle: testimony that is not incidental but is supported by strong circumstantial indications may be better than incidental speech without such indications. If a gentile speaking incidentally is believed in testimony concerning a woman even without circumstantial support, then certainly testimony with strong indications might be believed even without incidental speech. He explicitly warns, however, not to rely on this even as a mere additional factor. Pitchei Teshuvah notes that because Noda BiYehudah printed the idea, it seems he meant that a careful reader who finds it persuasive may use it as a supporting factor; indeed, in Noda BiYehudah Tinyana siman 47 he himself joins it as a supporting consideration. This may sustain Rema's wording: "or it is known" refers to a case with strong circumstantial indications, which can help even without incidental speech. Rema did not say this earlier regarding a gentile because he would not rely on that consideration alone; here there is an additional factor, namely Maharai's doubt that perhaps the disqualification is only rabbinic and therefore valid for testimony concerning a woman. Chatam Sofer siman 46 later rejects relying on Noda BiYehudah's proposed leniency at all.
