For we are concerned about borrowing. The Chelkat Mechokek wrote plainly, and wrote, etc.: it is not specifically borrowing; here we are likewise concerned for sale or loss. He means that only according to the sugya's answer, on the view that signs are valid by Torah law, do we say that with utensils that people do not lend we are not concerned for borrowing, and all the more so for sale or theft, because people do not commonly sell their utensils except those made to be sold, and theft too is uncommon. But if signs are rabbinic, then with the prohibition of a married woman we require the highest level; even through remote concerns it is considered only an intermediate sign, which does not help. This is the reasoning of our teacher the Beit Yosef z"l in responsum, as the Beit Shmuel and Sefer Even HaEzer reported in his name. However, what he also wrote, that this is also the view of the Ritva cited in the Beit Yosef, in my poverty I do not understand. The Beit Shmuel asked from what is ruled in siman 132, that these utensils have a presumption that he does not lend them. He answered: perhaps with a get, where the litigant is before us and does not say that he lent it, we are not concerned for borrowing; one may say this even where he did not specify that he knows he did not lend it. But here we are concerned that perhaps he lent it, even with these utensils. That means, either with difficulty according to his own words, or following the path of the Chelkat Mechokek, perhaps he sold it or it was stolen from him. He wrote, however, that the sugya still is not settled. In my humble opinion he means that if there is such a distinction, that where the litigant is before us we are not concerned ordinarily, why did the sugya need to come to a pouch and money-bag, which are not lent? It could have said that we are never concerned for borrowing, even with ordinary utensils, except where the litigant is not before us. The gaon Noda BiYehudah, may his light shine, siman 32, s.v. now, also asked against the ruling of the Beit Yosef z"l: if so, when the Gemara rejects the proof that signs are Torah-valid from the fact that we return a donkey by signs of the saddle, it says that the case may be with witnesses about the saddle. But the difficulty remains: if signs are Torah-valid, we are still concerned for borrowing even with utensils that are not lent. At first glance this was a powerful question, except that the gaon attributes it to the Beit Shmuel. In my humble opinion, the Beit Shmuel's intent is as I wrote, and this question can be rejected: that give-and-take did not enter at all into the reasoning of borrowing, and its answer is proper. Rather, after we see that if signs are Torah-valid one is forced to be concerned for borrowing and to distinguish between different types of utensils, we say that perhaps even utensils that are not lent are not a superior sign because of the concern for borrowing, although that concern is uncommon. Still, his proof requires study. Perhaps it is because he understood that in truth, when the Mishnah taught, and by his utensils, it means plainly with all utensils. As I pressed to settle the Beit Yosef, so too one must press in this way to settle the view of the Beit Shmuel in his disagreement with the gaon Rabbi Hilman z"l, to answer what the gaon, may his light shine, asked against him there within s.v. and I say; see there. What the gaon author, may his light shine, brings as a proof from the Rambam for the view of the gaon Rabbi Hilman, because in chapter 4 of Hilkhot Gezelah he asks, why did Scripture specify a donkey? To return it by signs of the saddle; and a saddle is not lent to one's donkey. Necessarily, this is because he ruled that signs are Torah-valid, and then we are not concerned for borrowing. In my humble opinion there is no proof from this. Granted, according to that answer the baraita specifically chose a saddle, which is an item not lent, because the tanna explains the verse with it, and by Torah law, with utensils that are lent, even in monetary law we are concerned for borrowing. Nevertheless, as law, regarding money we certainly rule that we are not concerned, as explained in Tosafot Yevamot. Therefore he properly chose a saddle for this reason, to teach that regarding money we are not concerned for borrowing, and he attached it to the verse. However, the Chacham Tzvi z"l also permits based on an intermediate sign on the body together with a superior sign or visual recognition of his utensils, because even those who hold that we are concerned for borrowing mean borrowing by the public, that is, that this missing person lent it to someone. But borrowing by a specific individual, meaning that the person who has the intermediate sign lent it to one particular person who also had this sign, for this we are not concerned. This is as stated in Perek Get Pashut, folio 172, that although Abaye is concerned for falling, he is not concerned for one individual, etc. Although one cannot learn prohibition from monetary law, Tosafot and Rashi in HaIshah Shalom 116 wrote that we properly learn an unestablished prohibition from established monetary law. But he asked against himself at the end of s.v. and if you say, a second answer, and wrote: by this, meaning with the words of the Rosh on the Rif's ruling distinguishing between get and death, it is settled, so that you should not say: what about what we say in Perek Elu Metziot 27b, if you think we are concerned, when he found it tied in a pouch, why is it valid? Let us be concerned for borrowing. According to our path, for borrowing like this, where the names of lender and borrower would be the same, we are not concerned. Rather, a get is different, because it is possible with another. End quote. See seif 18, where I wrote that this distinction between get and death cannot be made as Talmudic law because of the question of the Baal HaMaor; rather, the Rosh wrote it only to explain the practical ruling of the Rif. In truth, if his reasoning that borrowing and falling are one category were compelled, I would have been more comfortable answering his question by saying that the question in Perek Elu Metziot follows Rabbah and the first version of Rabbi Zeira in Gittin 27, who establish that Mishnah of finding it in a container, etc., where the names were established, and it is a case of an established prohibition. The sugya assumes simply that we cannot learn from established monetary law. But from the outset of the law, one must examine his reasoning, for we see in Yevamot 115 above that the sugya answers: according to Abaye, why should we be concerned? If for falling, he is careful with it. According to Tosafot's explanation, this necessarily means that we are not concerned that perhaps another person with the same name found it, that is, falling to one individual; but falling to the public we are concerned. This is difficult: how does the Gemara say that regarding falling he is careful, when Abaye nevertheless is concerned? Necessarily both are needed. Since concern for falling itself is uncommon, because he is careful with it, it is enough that we are concerned for falling to the public and not to one individual. If so, who says that borrowing itself is uncommon like falling, where he is careful with it? If the thing itself is common, perhaps we are concerned even for an uncommon individual case, namely that he lent it to someone who has the same intermediate sign. However, if we see that borrowing itself is uncommon, like the borrowing of many utensils and the like, he certainly spoke well. Apart from this, in my humble opinion, one who inclines in a pressing situation to be lenient about concern for borrowing has not lost. See Shach, Choshen Mishpat siman 65 s.k. 25. Also the Kuntres Iggun of Maharachash cried out strongly against our teacher the Beit Yosef: from where did he know that the Rif and Rambam are concerned for borrowing? The Yam Shel Shlomo also explained simply like Maharal of Prague, for the plain reason that intermediate signs on the body together with signs of his utensils are considered a strong sign. The Chacham Tzvi z"l wrote further that there is room to permit by an intermediate sign on the body where he was found in a place where caravans are not common, and we know that this woman's husband had been in that place. He compares this intermediate sign to his name and his father's name where no one else with that name combination had been established, and there are two factors: no established duplicate and caravans are not common. Certainly in such a case we are not concerned; see there.
