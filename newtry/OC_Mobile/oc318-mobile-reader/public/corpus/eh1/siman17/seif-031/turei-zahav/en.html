They saw him hanged, and a bird was eating from him, etc. This means that he had not yet died and the bird was eating from his flesh. The Tur states, “or a bird was eating from him,” but both descriptions concern the same type of case, since a bird could not eat from him unless he was restrained or bound in some other place. The reason one may not testify merely because he was seen hanged is that we are concerned that after the witness left, someone may have come and cut him down. The Mordechai cites a Yerushalmi concerning a noblewoman who passed by, saw a man hanging, ordered that he be cut down, and he remained alive. I write this because I saw that my father-in-law, of blessed memory, ruled that the hanging discussed here means that they suspended him by his hands, as in the chapter Nigmar HaDin and as the verse states, “Princes were hanged by their hands.” By contrast, he maintained that where a government hangs a person by placing a rope around his neck, so that he is strangled immediately, one may testify to his death even if the witness left and did not remain until his soul departed, because one may testify whenever it is impossible for the person to survive and he will necessarily die shortly. These are his words. One must not rely on this ruling at all. See the responsa of the Bach, siman 327, for the practical law. I remember that in my youth such a case occurred. A witness testified that he saw a man being hanged and immediately left. My father-in-law sought to permit the wife because the hanging mentioned by the Gemara was not by the neck. He derived this from Sanhedrin, where the Sages teach: Had the verse stated only, “You shall hang him,” I might have thought that they first hang him and afterward put him to death, in the manner of governments. Therefore the verse states, “He shall be put to death and you shall hang him,” teaching that they first put him to death and afterward hang him. My father-in-law inferred from the phrase “and afterward put him to death” that governmental hanging did not itself kill; rather, they suspended the person by his hands and afterward killed him with arrows and spears. This distinction does not appear correct to me. A proof may be brought from the Targum to Ruth, which lists the four judicial deaths as stoning with stones, burning with fire, hanging on a beam, and killing by the sword. It places hanging on a beam in the position of strangulation. This shows that one liable to strangulation was hanged by the neck, not by the hands. Nor is there proof from the verse, “Princes were hanged by their hands.” On the contrary, had ordinary hanging been by the hands, the verse would have needed to say only that they were hanged. Rather, the verse emphasizes the extraordinary suffering: they were not hanged by the neck, which would have brought death quickly, but by their hands, prolonging their pain before death. The teaching in Nigmar HaDin, “and afterward put him to death,” concerns judicial hanging, where the condemned person was first stoned, since all who were hanged by beit din had already been put to death. Governmental hanging, however, was performed while the person was alive, and he was then left suspended until he died of strangulation. The phrase therefore excludes putting him to death before the hanging. Although Naomi told Ruth that we have hanging on a beam in place of strangulation, whereas the judicial procedure for strangulation is to sink him in refuse up to his knees, as stated in Sanhedrin 52, Naomi intended only to explain the nature of the death, that the neck is constricted, just as governments strangle through hanging on a beam. She was not explaining the precise procedure of our beit din. Furthermore, if the Mishnah’s phrase “he was hanged” meant only that he was suspended by his hands, it would be obvious that one may not testify to his death on that basis alone. This is especially so according to the Tur, who treats a bird eating from him as a separate case. If “hanged” meant only by the hands, there would be no basis at all to infer death. It is therefore clear that the Mishnah speaks of hanging by the neck. Even so, one may not testify, because perhaps he was immediately cut down and survived. I recall that several rabbis did not accept my father-in-law’s distinction. Accordingly, one must not rely on it leniently. It therefore appears that if a witness comes to testify that a man was hanged, he must be asked whether he remained until the man’s soul departed. However, if he heard from another person simply that a named man was hanged, that report is sufficient, just as we explained regarding the statement “I buried him” in siman 18. See the responsa of the Bach, siman 127, for the practical law.
Or they shot arrows at him. The Tur writes: “or he was covered with wounds, even in a place that renders him a treifah,” and this is difficult. Why should this differ from a case where the majority of both the windpipe and esophagus were cut, where witnesses may testify because the injury necessarily causes death? To resolve the Tur, we must first explain the Rambam’s rulings in seifim 30 and 31. The Mishnah states that one may not testify until the man’s soul departs, even if he was seen cut open, hanged, or being eaten by a wild creature. The Gemara asks whether a person who was cut open can live, since another source states that a person does not contract corpse impurity until his soul departs, even if he is cut open, hanged, or in his death throes. This implies that although he is not yet impure, he cannot continue living. Abaye answers that one source follows Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar and the other follows the Sages. A baraita teaches that one may testify concerning one who was cut open, but not concerning one who was hanged. Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar says that one may not testify even concerning one who was cut open, because the wound can be cauterized and he may survive. Rava answers that the Mishnah concerns a cut made with a heated knife. According to Rava, even the first Tanna agrees that one may not testify when the wound was made with a heated knife, since the heat cauterizes and heals the wound. Their dispute concerns an ordinary case. The first Tanna does not suspect that the knife may have been heated, while Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar does. The Tur cites the Ramah as ruling that the restriction applies specifically when the witness saw that the person was cut with a heated knife, and likewise when he was cut with an ordinary knife but was cauterized afterward. If he was not cauterized afterward, one may testify, in accordance with the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar. This language is difficult, because if the witness certainly saw that the wound was not cauterized afterward, even Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar would agree that one may testify. His stringency rests only on the possibility that the wound was cauterized. We must therefore understand the Ramah’s phrase “if he was not cauterized” to mean that the witness did not see him being cauterized and the matter remains uncertain. He rules like the first Tanna, who does not suspect cauterization without evidence. The Tur writes that this does not appear to be the view of his father, the Rosh, since the Rif and the Rosh cite only the Mishnah and omit Rava’s explanation that it concerns a heated knife. This indicates that even in an ordinary case they suspect that the knife may have been heated. Yet this too is difficult. According to Rava, the Mishnah concerns a heated knife according to all opinions, while the baraita’s dispute concerns an ordinary knife. Why, then, should the Rif and Rosh rule like Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar rather than the first Tanna? The Maggid Mishneh also cites the Rashba’s stringent view in an ordinary case. He wonders that if the Rif intended Rava’s explanation, he should have stated explicitly that there is concern for a heated knife. In my opinion, those who are stringent in an ordinary case follow Abaye’s explanation. The Mishnah then addresses an ordinary case and accords with Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar, so the halacha follows that view. Although the halacha generally follows Rava against Abaye, this passage is different because the subsequent discussion proceeds according to Abaye, as is evident from the question whether water aggravates a wound. We can now explain the Rambam and the Tur. Both understand that the Mishnah’s “one who was cut open” refers to a wound in a place that renders him a treifah, so he will certainly die. The cases of one who was hanged and one whom a wild creature was eating are different, because neither necessarily establishes certain death. The Rambam does not expressly mention the Mishnah’s case of one cut open, a point on which the Maggid Mishneh expresses surprise. Beit Yosef writes that the Rambam’s phrase, “even though he was stabbed with spears or shot with arrows,” is the Mishnah’s case. I do not agree, for then the Rambam would not have changed the language. Rather, the Rambam refers to the teaching at the beginning of HaIshah Shalom that a woman in wartime is not believed to say that her husband died. Sometimes she sees him struck by an arrow or spear and assumes he certainly died, while medicine may enable him to survive. That passage does not mean that she saw the arrow strike a place that necessarily renders him a treifah, for medicine cannot restore life where death is certain. It speaks of an arrow entering the body cavity, so she assumes that he will die, although the wound may in fact be treatable. Thus the Rambam’s ruling that one may not testify where he was stabbed or shot concerns a wound that may not have been fatal. By contrast, the Mishnah’s cut-open person suffered a certainly fatal wound. The Mishnah nevertheless withholds testimony because the wound might have been cauterized, as Rava explains in the case of a heated knife. The Rambam did not discuss that uncommon circumstance. He listed only situations in which death itself was uncertain. Where death is certain, as when the majority of both passages were cut, it is obvious that one may testify. This is included in the Rambam’s general rule that one may testify whenever it is impossible for the person to survive and he must die shortly. The Tur, however, appears to follow Tosafos. Rabbeinu Yitzchak explains that one who was cut open in a place that makes him a treifah will certainly die eventually, but it is not certain that he will die before the court permits his wife to marry. The Gemara proves that he dies immediately because the Mishnah lists him together with a person in his death throes, who is destined to die shortly. On this basis, the Tur’s language is understandable. Even where the wound renders him a treifah and he will certainly die before twelve months pass, one may not permit the wife to marry until his death is certain. The Tur could not simply say that one waits until the end of twelve months, because that might imply that even if he actually died earlier she would still have to wait twelve months. He therefore says that one waits until his soul departs. Since a treifah cannot live twelve months, it is certain that his soul will depart within that period. It is also clear that the Rambam would permit the wife after twelve months. Thus, after twelve months all authorities agree that she may marry. On this basis I permitted a woman during a period of persecution. A witness had fled from a place where people were being beaten and entered a room. He saw a man lying there with a wound that had penetrated the brain, then fled elsewhere and did not see him again. Approximately a year had passed since the assault. I ruled that the wife could marry according to all opinions. My father-in-law likewise wrote that according to the Tur she is permitted after twelve months. Where, however, the witness saw an open wound in the brain that had widened, it appears obvious that even according to the Tur she may marry shortly afterward, for this is comparable to cutting the majority of both passages. I saw that the Perishah similarly distinguishes a case where the leg was torn away above the knee. The editor notes that Chelkas Mechokek, seif katan 59, objects that according to Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar one who was cut open might survive through cauterization, and therefore perhaps no testimony is accepted even after twelve months. This objection applies also to the Taz’s explanation of the Tur. It is not difficult. The Taz understands that a wound in a place that renders the person a treifah causes death, and cauterization can preserve life only until twelve months, not beyond. The Rambam and Tur differ only as to whether we suspect the uncommon possibility of cauterization. The Rambam does not suspect it and permits testimony immediately where the fatal wound is established. The Tur suspects cauterization and therefore does not permit testimony until twelve months have passed. After twelve months, even Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar agrees that no treatment can preserve the life of a treifah, for it is a fundamental rule that a treifah cannot live more than twelve months.
