Two witnesses, etc., and she says, "I am certain." Rashi of blessed memory explained "I am certain" to mean: my heart does not trouble me, for I am certain that if he were alive he would have come. Ran of blessed memory objected that since she does not know with certainty, she stands liable to a guilt-offering. Rashi had already guarded against this by explaining that a guilt-offering comes only for one whose heart troubles him. Yet there remains a difficulty: granted, this explains him and her, but what shall we do with the children born from the second marriage? They would be doubtful mamzerim. Although a doubtful mamzer is permitted by Torah law, it is nevertheless astonishing to multiply cases of doubtful mamzer status from two against two among Israel. By contrast, according to Ran's explanation this is at first glance understandable, for her testimony that she is certain also helps legitimate the offspring, as we rule in siman 4. At first glance it therefore seemed to me that if the husband of this woman, who was one of her witnesses, died, even if afterward it became known that her first husband too had certainly died, but the doubt remained whether he had been dead at the time of the marriage, then not only according to Rashi's "certainty" would she be forbidden to a kohen because of the doubt of two against two, since according to the group that says "he did not die" she became a zonah; but even according to Ran's "certainty," where she claims with certainty, "I had relations with a fit man," nevertheless ab initio we rule that she may not marry, even where most men are fit, and certainly in a doubt of two against two. However, see siman 152, se'if 3, where I proved that one cannot approach this at all from the side of her being believed through her own certainty. Even so, where she claims certainty, the children are valid; see there. It must therefore rest on another reason, and if so perhaps she too is always fit for the priesthood. This requires study.
