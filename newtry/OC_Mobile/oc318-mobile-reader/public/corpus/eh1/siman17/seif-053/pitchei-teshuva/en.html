See Machatzit HaShekel, se'if katan 40, who writes that the Levush gave the Maggid Mishneh's reason: this would be two leniencies, a gentile speaking incidentally and testimony by writing. According to his words, if one witness testifies in writing from the mouth of a gentile who spoke orally in the course of conversation, it too should not help, for there would be three disadvantages: one witness from another witness, from writing, and from an incidental gentile statement. The Machatzit HaShekel discusses this at length and concludes that we do not rule this way, and that we should not invent stringencies on our own in the case of an agunah that are not mentioned in the Talmud. Even with a gentile's writing in the manner of incidental speech, there is room to be lenient wherever he wrote it in the course of another matter and in connection with other details, since we rule that a gentile speaking incidentally from the mouth of another gentile speaking incidentally is effective, as stated in se'if 16. In any event, he writes, the rule appears to apply only where the gentile speaks incidentally in writing; but where one witness writes from the mouth of a gentile who spoke orally in the course of conversation, that certainly helps. These are his words; see there. See Beit Meir, who writes that the Chelkat Mechokek had to introduce this rule according to his own view in halakhah 13, where he upholds the Rivash's view, which is the first opinion in se'if 14 in the gloss, that whenever there was no question, even without contextual connection, it is considered incidental speech. Therefore he had a difficulty here with the Rambam: why did the Rambam write that it was known to be Jewish handwriting? He therefore had to introduce this rule that incidental speech is ineffective through writing. But according to the second view, derived from the Rambam's language, that contextual connection is specifically required, there is no necessity for this rule. Here the case involved no contextual connection; it was merely written that so-and-so died and nothing more. Therefore the Rambam had to insist that it be Jewish handwriting. He would agree that if it had been written with contextual connection, it would be effective. From this it follows that if a woman has a gentile's written incidental statement with contextual connection, and also a gentile who testifies orally without a question but without contextual connection, she is certainly permitted whichever way one looks at it; see there. See Responsa Pnei Aryeh, siman 20, whose opinion is also that a gentile speaking incidentally in writing is ineffective. He brings that this is also the view of the Terumat HaDeshen, siman 240, and Maharam Alshikh, siman 118, and he rules that this is the practical law; see there. Responsa Maharam Alashkar, siman 67, writes similarly. However, in Ezrat Nashim he writes that the requirement that they know it is Jewish handwriting applies where the gentile writes to a Jew, for there is concern that perhaps he writes this in response to a Jew's question, or since he writes to a Jew one may say that he certainly intends to give testimony, for otherwise what business does he have with the Jew. But where he writes to another gentile in incidental fashion, for example where he comes to collect a debt from the estate of so-and-so who died, why should it not be effective? See there. The Kehillat Yaakov writes about him that he reasoned well; see there. See Yeshuot Yaakov, se'if katan 15. A case came before him concerning a man taken into the imperial army who was killed there, and a document came from a high office, the General Command, stating that this man had been killed and mentioning his name and his father's name. The matter was permitted on the basis of the reliability of the General Command, together with further estimates and evidentiary reasons showing that he was killed; see there. See also Responsa Chatam Sofer, simanim 43, 44, and 94. His opinion is to permit even without joining additional estimates. He writes that, needless to say, if a Jew himself saw in the military register that the man was listed among the dead, and the circumstances leave no concern for two people with the same name and father, nor for two towns of the same name, nor for mistaken inference, the woman is certainly permitted. That writing, on the day it is written, is no less than incidental speech. Although the Maggid Mishneh wrote that a gentile speaking incidentally in writing is ineffective, the reason seems to be that one should not add further to the leniency: two leniencies in one place, believing a gentile and from their writing, and also because one cannot discern from writing whether it was said incidentally. But here, in military records, their writing is better than their speech: speech is mere chance and they may lie, but they would not damage themselves so much by signing falsely. If, however, the register did not write explicitly that he died, and his name was only erased by crossing it out, one must doubt whether it was erased because he deserted, unless it is clarified that it was erased because of death. Even if the Jew did not see that register himself, but obtained an answer from the chancery in response to an inquiry, and then from another place obtained a death certificate written in the same terms and signed with the seal of the civil authorities, although both are like testimony from the mouth of testimony, since the writer himself did not bury him, nevertheless one may be lenient. Even according to the Rosh, who did not wish to permit where the testimony did not begin and end in a state of validity, as noted above in se'if katan 38, the reason appears to be that we should not add beyond what the Sages said, since all of this is only for the enactment of agunot; perhaps that enactment sufficed to believe a gentile speaking incidentally, which is common. Nevertheless, in this last generation, when, because of our many sins, cases involving soldiers are very common every day and no testimony can be obtained except by inquiry to the authorities, there is no greater enactment for agunot than this. At the end of siman 94 the Chatam Sofer concludes that he had already permitted this three times in cases that came before him, where Jews asked the commandant officers, they wrote to the General Command, and from there a death certificate was sent, copied from the military register, signed by their officers and sealed with the official governmental seal. Since the matter goes from one civil office to another and is also sealed with the official seal, whose forgery is punishable by death to the king, there is a strong presumption that both offices would not lie. Also, since they wrote by post and received the reply by post, it is like authentication: no one receives an answer by post from the commandant officers except the one who sent to them, and this is better than Persian documents discussed in Gittin 19. These are his words; see there.
