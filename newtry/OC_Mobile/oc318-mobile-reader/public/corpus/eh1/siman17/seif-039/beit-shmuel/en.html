Some say that she need not leave. This is the view of the Ran, meaning where she married before the prohibiting witness came; if she married afterward, it is explained above that she must leave. The reason the Shulchan Arukh writes "some say" is that from the responsa of the Ramban and Rivash one may infer that only permission granted by a court is effective, not her own marriage. See the responsum of Maharshaldam, who brings proof that if she married without court permission it is ineffective. From the Tosafot cited above, who hold that even if the court permitted her it helps only with respect to the rule that if she married she need not leave, one cannot infer that they hold that if she married without permission she must leave. They may mean as follows: if the court permitted her, then even if a witness later comes and says he did not die and she afterward married, she need not leave. But if they did not permit her and the second witness came before she married, she must leave. If, however, she married before the second witness came, she need not leave even without permission. See Mordekhai at the end of Ha-Ishah Shalom and see siman 7. At first glance, according to the Ran's approach that her marriage without court permission is effective and she need not leave, the Gemara's question in Ha-Ishah Shalom is difficult. On the Mishnah, "one witness says he died and she married," it infers: the reason is that she married, but if she did not marry she may not marry; yet Ulla says that wherever the Torah believed one witness, he is like two. The Gemara answers that "married" means that she was permitted to marry. Thus it is proved that Ulla too speaks where she was permitted; why not answer that the Mishnah means actual marriage without permission? One should not bring proof from the Rambam that he holds she need not leave, for he writes that if the second witness came before they permitted her, she must leave unless she married the witness who testified that he died. This shows only that if she married that witness, even without permission she need not leave, because he says with certainty that the husband died. If she married someone else, who does not know whether he died, perhaps only court permission helps; without court permission she must leave.
And the bet din must be three and valid, etc. Chelkat Mechokek discusses this at length. The general point of his words is that no bet din is needed here; it is like other rulings of prohibition and permission, and one judge is enough. Also, no bet din is needed to receive the testimony, for testimony from the mouth of another witness is valid even if the first witness did not state his testimony before bet din. The reason a bet din is generally needed to receive testimony is so that it not be testimony from the mouth of another witness; here, where testimony from the mouth of another witness is valid, a bet din is not needed to receive the testimony. We have not found explicitly the source of this law written by the Rama, that they must be three and valid. What is cited as responsum Rashba siman 790 is not there, and it can properly be rejected as Chelkat Mechokek wrote. But one can bring proof for this law from the Gemara at the end of Yevamot, where there is a dispute about testimony for women, whether it requires derishah and chakirah. Rabbi Akiva holds that it requires derishah and chakirah, and they dispute whether permitting women resembles monetary law or capital law, for there are arguments both ways: permitting her to another man resembles capital law, while her collection of the ketubah because of the permission resembles monetary law. According to all opinions, we hear that this is actual adjudication and is not like other rulings of prohibition and permission, where there is no legal judgment; here there is judgment. Although sometimes she has no ketubah, nevertheless they did not distinguish, as Nimukei Yosef wrote. Although testimony from the mouth of another witness is valid for testimony concerning a woman, we do not derive the possible from the impossible. Know this, for Rabbi Akiva holds that derishah and chakirah are required, and he also holds that testimony from the mouth of another witness is valid; but the first witness spoke without derishah and chakirah. Rather, one must say that the first witness is not comparable to the witness who comes before us: this one requires derishah and chakirah, and likewise must give his testimony specifically before bet din. So too it implies from Nimukei Yosef, who holds that a valid bet din is required, for he gives the reason that testimony concerning a woman is valid before lay courts because it is common and severe because of monetary loss, and therefore agency applies to this testimony. If you say no bet din is needed at all, it is obvious that a lay court is valid. Rather, certainly a valid bet din is required. What Chelkat Mechokek brought as proof from the rulings of Mahari, from the permission issued by Maharar Tevel, is no proof at all, for there a bet din was with Maharar Tevel, only the permission was called by his name. Also, if she already married, that is different.
