The Beit Shmuel wrote that even if he wrote to the bet din that he had heard from another, it is effective; only where he writes as a memorandum and not for practical action do we not rule leniently with testimony from the mouth of another witness, as written in Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 2 of Yevamot. His words are brief, so I will copy the language of Hagahot Maimoniyot. It says there: many times I wondered how we permit a woman to marry on the basis of writing, and I found in the Yerushalmi, etc., as in the Rif. Now one must wonder according to Rabbi Bun: how do we permit them on the basis of writing? Even according to Rabbi Yirmiyah the matter requires study, for perhaps the Yerushalmi speaks of witnesses who signed based on their own sight, like a loan document, which it cites there; but these witnesses wrote based on testimony heard from another, or from a gentile, or from a woman. I would distinguish in order to uphold the custom: the Yerushalmi speaks where they wrote on their own, not for a need and not for a practical case, but to stand as evidence; a document of that kind does not have the law of a shtar. He further wrote at the beginning of chapter 23, joining this distinction, that the writings we send in order to determine the truth of the testimony are different: if they had testified so before bet din, the bet din would have permitted her, and we too act as their agents and permit her. In a similar way Riva answered in Ketubot, chapter 2, regarding the law that a person may write his testimony in a document and testify from it if he remembers it on his own, but if he does not remember it, he may not. Riva asked: why should he not send his testimony to bet din, so that it be like a document and they validate it, as is said regarding an erased debt document? He answered that there it was made at the command of the borrower and lender and has the status of a shtar; but a document that a witness makes on his own has no status of a shtar, and is considered from their mouths and not from their writing. He brought the Midrashim that I cited, and he inferred from the phrase: now we allow marriage based on testimony from the mouth of another witness, that likewise based on writings we allow marriage. This implies that if not for the rule allowing testimony from the mouth of another witness, we would not allow marriage based on writings. Why should they not validate the signatures? Rather, in such a case it is not a shtar. End quote. From this the Beit Shmuel derived that even if he wrote to the bet din that he heard from another, it is effective, for that does not belong to the dispute in the Yerushalmi: it is a shtar, and there is no leniency of from their writing. But when he writes as a memorandum and not for practical action, then it belongs to the Yerushalmi's dispute, as the Ran also explained. Even though we rule like the one who permits, the Beit Shmuel accepts definitively what Hagahot Maimoniyot left requiring study, namely that perhaps the Yerushalmi speaks only of witnesses who signed based on their own sight, and that we do not rule leniently for testimony from the mouth of another witness because that would be two leniencies, testimony from another witness and testimony from writing. In my humble opinion this truly requires study, for the one who permits brought support from the baraita that now we allow marriage on the basis of one witness, and the same should apply from writings. With one witness we do not distinguish, and even two additional leniencies, such as from another witness and from a slave, are accepted. The same should be true of from writing; one should not distinguish between one leniency and two. As for Hagahot Maimoniyot's answer that when he writes testimony to bet din it is not considered from their writing because it is a shtar, although we rule in Choshen Mishpat siman 28 that one may not send testimony in writing because it is considered from their writing, one must say that this is only when testifying for the benefit of one litigant and it was not made at the command of both litigants, or with one witness, where it is not considered a shtar. See what I brought in siman 142 in the name of Rivash. Here, however, the consent of litigants is not relevant, for who is the opposing party? And one witness in this testimony is considered like two. Whenever he writes his testimony to bet din, it is considered a shtar and not from their writing. One must also press the phrase, we too act as their agents, to mean that we accept this writing as a shtar; otherwise the words are not understandable. It is known that the reason a shtar is not considered from their writing, even if the witnesses later died, is that at the time of writing it is considered as though their testimony had been investigated in bet din, and then they were able to testify. But a mute witness plainly cannot testify even if he signed his testimony in a shtar; what does it help that it is considered investigated, when even if he had then been investigated in bet din, the Torah disqualified him? He is one of the disqualified witnesses, as explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 35 seif 11. It is therefore obvious that even if he signed a shtar it is nothing. I therefore do not know what difficulty the Beit Shmuel saw when he wrote: at first glance it is difficult how this law is derived from there, since the Ran explains the Yerushalmi's dispute as speaking of writing for remembrance, and with this the Tur is resolved when he wrote that the Rosh did not decide. What difference does it make that he is mute? If it is written for remembrance, it is still from their writing, and the whole defect of writing for remembrance is that it is considered from their writing. This is unlike writing in order to testify, which can be considered a shtar as explained above. Therefore, since the Rif and the Rosh ruled that a mute person may testify by writing, it follows that even writing for remembrance also helps for testimony about a woman. In my humble opinion it is more plausible that the Tur wrote only that from its own place it would appear to rule this way, and then the Rambam wrote otherwise, and he concedes to him. Know also that aside from the Ran's precision that the language they found written indicates writing for remembrance, in my humble opinion the matter is proven from its place. If it were a full shtar signed by proper witnesses in order to testify, I wonder what room there would be to disqualify it for testimony about an agunah. Could one have thought to be stricter in testimony about an agunah than in monetary law? Even witnesses disqualified for monetary testimony are valid for testimony about an agunah, all the more a full shtar, which in monetary law is not considered from their writing. What we are stricter about regarding identifying signs than in monetary law is, as explained in Bekhorot, because signs are the beginning of testimony; but at the end of testimony we are lenient. Necessarily the Yerushalmi speaks, as they explained, of writing for remembrance or of one witness. See Choshen Mishpat siman 46 seif 10 in the gloss. Unless we say the opposite of the reasoning of Hagahot Maimoniyot, namely that because this shtar was not made in the ordinary way between two litigants who customarily write such documents, it is considered from their writing. However it may be, it is at least resolved from the case of the mute person that from their writing is valid here, and the same applies in all forms. Know that according to their interpretation, that the Yerushalmi is not speaking of a full shtar, one must press the language of the supporting baraita, for in its first clause it says that the power of witnesses is greater regarding testimony about an agunah, and that speaks of a full shtar; in the latter clause it says that the power of a shtar is greater regarding gittin and encumbered property, and that too must speak of a full shtar. Still, this is not so difficult, for the witnesses whose power is greater for testimony about an agunah also refer to inferior witnesses, and this is clear. The Beit Shmuel further wrote: there it teaches that just as they examine, and according to what we rule, etc., it appears, etc. This is also the question of Knesset HaGedolah, by Maharach Shash, against Rashba himself. He writes that Rashba inferred from the Rif's conclusion, that in other testimony the rule is from their mouths and not from their writing, that in testimony about an agunah from their writing is effective. According to this inference, one must say that the Rif holds the reverse of Rashba's own view regarding gittin, for according to this the Rif holds that writing is effective for gittin; yet Rashba proved that writing is not effective for a get, even by a sound person. In my humble opinion this is not difficult. Although in a get nodding the head is better than writing according to the Yerushalmi, on the contrary, the difficulty is stronger: why did the baraita exclude a mute person from liability for an offering because he cannot testify? He can testify by nodding. Although Tosafot in Gittin 71, s.v. Amar Rabbi Zeira, pressed to answer the difficulty only according to Rav Kahana, nevertheless according to Abaye's answer, that testimony is different because the Torah said from their mouths, this is certainly also the reason why he cannot testify by nodding, even though nodding is better than writing: the Scriptural decree requires from their mouths specifically. This is what the Rif concludes: but elsewhere not, for the Torah said from their mouths, meaning that just as writing is excluded by the decree of from their mouths, so too his nodding is excluded. Once we see that regarding testimony about an agunah nodding is effective and we do not insist on from their mouths, it follows that the same is true of writing. But with a get, where the insistence does not emerge from the decree of from their mouths, one can distinguish as the Yerushalmi does: nodding the head, which is an action of the body, is more like his voice than handwriting is. The phrase just as they examine for gittin was not stated to equate the law of testimony about an agunah with gittin, but only to equate the law of examination for agunah testimony to the examination used for gittin. This is clear. According to this, even without the above, the difficulty raised against the Ran is not difficult: in Perek Mi She'achazo the case does not concern writing for remembrance. What of it? The main proof is that since nodding is effective for testimony about an agunah, we see that in this testimony we are not precise about from their mouths, and automatically from their writing is valid, namely writing for remembrance.
