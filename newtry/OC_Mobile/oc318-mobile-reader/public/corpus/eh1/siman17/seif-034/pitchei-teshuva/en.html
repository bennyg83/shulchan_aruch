Or the woman. Chelkas Mechokek, s.k. 97, rules that the same applies when the woman herself says, 'My husband died': the court must ask her the cause of death, as explained here. Beit Meir challenges this and concludes: Were I not afraid to say so, it appears to me from the precise language of the Rambam and the arrangement of the Shulchan Aruch that this rule applies only to a witness, whether a man, woman, or slave, but does not discuss the woman's own testimony. One can therefore say that in peacetime she need not be asked the cause of death. This also explains somewhat why I have never seen or heard that when a woman traveled overseas with her husband and later returned alone, presenting herself as a widow because her husband died, the beis din required her to testify formally before them, saying, 'My husband died, permit me.' According to this distinction, since in peacetime no questions about the cause of death are required, her formal declaration adds nothing, and it is enough that she presents herself publicly as a widow. However, where there is reason to suspect that she is speaking on the basis of an assumption, she must certainly testify before a beis din. See there, and what I wrote below, seif 39, s.k. 152.
