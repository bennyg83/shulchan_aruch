Gloss: who testify, etc. The Beit Shmuel wrote, and the Taz is lenient; see Knesset HaGedolah siman 81. Empty and reckless apostates who returned in repentance, but not in complete repentance, should not be relied upon for their testimony. I saw in a manuscript responsum of the late gaon Rabbi Chaim Rapoport z"l, av beit din of Lemberg, which is under my hand, concerning an agunah where testimony was collected by the bet din of London from a man who had been an apostate and afterward returned to the Jewish faith. He wrote in this language: there is no reason to question it because he had been an apostate. In seif 6 the Rama wrote as above. Now, in Shulchan Aruch Choshen Mishpat siman 37 seif 22, the Rama brings that an apostate who retracted and accepted repentance upon himself is valid immediately, even though he has not yet completed it. The Sema wrote as follows: in Terumat HaDeshen he explains the reason, because the apostate had been mixed among the gentiles, who have all desires, and this person comes to separate himself from all of them and accept repentance; therefore they were not stringent with him, and we establish that he certainly made complete repentance. At first glance these two laws contradict one another. It appears to me that here in Even HaEzer the case is where it is known that he did not return in complete repentance, such as where they saw that he still treated prohibitions lightly and returned only from idolatry. In this case one may not rely on his testimony. In Choshen Mishpat, however, it speaks of an ordinary case, as implied by the phrase, we establish that he certainly made complete repentance. Since he wrote, we establish, this implies that ordinarily we establish him with a presumption of full fitness. In Even HaEzer, however, the language implies that we know he did not return in complete repentance. According to this, an ordinary apostate who returned to the Jewish faith is believed as one witness so long as it is not known that he did not return in complete repentance. The Taz too was stringent only regarding testimony about something he saw at the time when he was wicked; in that case it does not help even if he afterward made complete repentance. But in the case before us, where the beginning of the testimony was after he had already returned, he is believed. End quote. After I studied the source of the laws, I saw that in truth the Sema z"l, with due respect to his great honor, placed a slight stumbling block before readers at first glance by copying the words of Terumat HaDeshen for the law there. The words of Terumat HaDeshen in siman 198 were not mentioned at all as a reason; that is a separate matter, namely that returning apostates to the gentiles are not burdened with mortifications in proportion to the transgressions they committed. This is copied in Yoreh De'ah siman 158, and it is also explained in the responsum attributed to the Ramban, siman 180. What the Sema added, writing that we establish him, etc., is not the language of Terumat HaDeshen and is not needed for the law there. For the law there, which derives from Maharik, root 185, was written concerning the wife of an apostate who returned, where one person wanted to permit his wife to the public without chalitzah. About this Maharik wrote: all the more so, all the more so, where he returned in repentance. The long Mordekhai wrote as follows: an apostate who retracted, etc.; since he was permitted back among the community and accepted upon himself to fast, even though he had not completed it, when he retracted and accepted repentance upon himself he immediately returns to his fitness. For we learned: a kohen who marries women in transgression is disqualified until he vows benefit from them; and it was taught about this, he vows and serves, then descends and divorces. Thus, since he retracted, he is valid. End quote. You see that from the moment he accepted upon himself to repent and began it, he returned to his fitness, and he is like a full Israelite; it is obvious that his wife is betrothed. End quote of Maharik. This was needed for the Rama's citation in Choshen Mishpat. What he wrote, that he accepted repentance upon himself, means some mortifications and fasts arranged for him by bet din; he is valid immediately even though he has not yet performed them. These are precisely the words of the Mordekhai. What is written here, that empty and reckless apostates who returned in repentance, means that they returned to the Jewish faith, but not in complete repentance, meaning that he did not come before a bet din or teacher to arrange for him repentance appropriate to him through mortifications. Therefore they call such people one who immerses while holding a creeping thing in his hand, as is explained in the discussion in Mahari Weil siman 164 and Terumat HaDeshen siman 220. Terumat HaDeshen apparently wished to prove from a responsum of Maharam that this is only a rabbinic disqualification, which would be valid for testimony of one witness concerning a woman. Mahari Weil answered that since he has not yet returned to fitness, he remains disqualified by Torah law. The Rama was concerned to be stringent like Mahari Weil. Regarding other testimony there is no one who disagrees in the case we are discussing that he is at least rabbinically disqualified, as explained there. It follows that the matters in Choshen Mishpat and here have no connection at all. In this, it appears clear to me that apostates who go from country to country and present themselves there as valid Israelites, while we know they had been apostates, are presumed disqualified even for agunah testimony until we know that he came before a rabbi or teacher, confessed, and accepted repentance with mortifications. This is precise from the language of the Mordekhai cited by Maharik, who wrote, since he was permitted back among the community and accepted upon himself to fast. Before this, it is forbidden to accept their testimony plainly because of the bad presumption that had been established about them, until we know that he left it. However, in the case of the above gaon, who ruled regarding testimony collected by the bet din of London, where it was mentioned that he had been an apostate, he did not need this reasoning. Since one bet din does not scrutinize after another bet din, presumably they knew he was fit; otherwise they would not have been permitted to collect the testimony and give a signed document into the woman's hand. Nevertheless, one should know that the above gaon wrote this in the community of Piltz at the time of the assembly, when books were not available to him except the Shulchan Aruch. He also concluded accordingly that he wrote only as halakhah and not for practical ruling, and for this he is not to be faulted at all.
