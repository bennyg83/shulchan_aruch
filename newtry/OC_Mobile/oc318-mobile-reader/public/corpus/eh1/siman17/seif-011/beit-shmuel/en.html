They found it written in a document. Even if it was written in a gentile script, it is effective if it is known that a Jew wrote it, as Terumat HaDeshen, siman 247, writes in the name of Or Zarua. Even if he wrote to the court that he heard it from another, it is effective. But where he writes only as a memorandum and not for practical action, we do not rule leniently on the basis of testimony from the mouth of another witness, as stated in Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 2 of Yevamot. If, however, he writes as a record of what he himself saw, there is room to be lenient. The Yerushalmi distinguishes whether one may rely on writing, and the Ran explains that the dispute concerns a writing made for memory. The Rif, Rashba, and Ran infer from the fact that the Rif in chapter Mi She'achazo cites the baraita, "Just as they examine for gittin, so they examine for testimony," and establishes it with regard to testimony that a woman's husband died but not other testimony, because of "from their mouths and not from their writing," that he holds one may rely on writing in this matter. At first glance it is difficult to derive this from there, because the Ran explains that the Yerushalmi's dispute concerns a memorandum, while the case in Mi She'achazo is not one who writes merely for memory. The Chelkat Mechokek also asks how one can derive the law from there, since there the writer is known, whereas here he is unknown. The Tur writes in this siman that the Rosh did not decide the Yerushalmi's dispute whether writing may be relied on, although in Mi She'achazo the Rosh rules like the Rif. Therefore one must explain as above: the Yerushalmi speaks of a writing for memory, and for that reason the Rosh did not decide it; what he rules in Mi She'achazo is where it was not written merely as a memorandum. From the Rambam, however, it seems that he learns these laws from one another, for he writes both in one clause: they found written that so-and-so died, and likewise if a mute person was examined and wrote that so-and-so died. There the baraita teaches that just as they examine for gittin, they examine for testimony that a woman's husband died. Since regarding gittin we rule that one does not rely on writing, as in siman 120, it would seem that the same should apply to testimony about a woman. The Rambam, who rules that one may rely on writing here, follows his own view that writing also helps by a get when the man became mute. Regarding the Mechaber, it is at first difficult how we know to be lenient here for a mute person, when below he seems to hold principally like the authorities that writing does not help for a mute person. Even if it is not authenticated. The poskim wrote the reason: we hold that witnesses signed on a document are as though their testimony was examined in court, and authentication of documents is rabbinic. Here we establish the matter on Torah law. Nevertheless, there must be witnesses signed on the writing, as written in the Yerushalmi and as Ri"ו also wrote. If one witness signed it, it helps, as written in the Ran in the name of the Ramban. Although regarding one witness we do not say that it is as though his testimony was examined in court, as written in responsum Rivash and in the gloss to Shulchan Aruch, Choshen HaMishpat siman 46, nevertheless for testimony concerning a woman it helps.
It is not authenticated. So wrote Ramban and Rashbam, and according to the Maggid Mishneh the Rambam also holds so. However, the other poskim disagree and hold that authentication is required. Rashba wrote that the Rambam also holds so. See responsum Maharam Lublin, who also ruled that authentication is required. However, authentication by one witness helps, because generally authentication by one witness does not help since it is like testimony heard from another witness, whereas here, in testimony concerning a woman, testimony heard from another witness helps. So too the Maggid Mishneh wrote that authentication by one witness helps, and Maharam and the author of Chelkat Mechokek agreed that authentication is required. However, if a writing came from afar and is not authenticated, Chelkat Mechokek is lenient in a case of agunah and permits. A writing found written in a Jewish hand is attributed to the majority of Jews who are valid for testimony. It must also be written: so-and-so son of so-and-so from such-and-such place. See Tosafot, Yevamot 122, s.v. de-chazu lei, where Tosafot brought the Yerushalmi: if it was found written, so-and-so died. Before this they wrote about whether we are concerned that perhaps a demon was the one who cried out, so-and-so died, and their words have no connection to one another. One must say that they brought proof from this law: found written, so-and-so died, helps even if one does not know whether the writer was valid; so too we are not concerned that perhaps it was a demon. If one can say that he wrote so. It is not specifically that he wrote; the same applies if he stated it orally: we say perhaps he heard that he drowned in water that has no end, as written in Terumat HaDeshen siman 240 and in the rulings siman 141. In responsum Beit Yosef, in the name of one rabbi, it appears that the same applies if a rumor emerged that so-and-so was killed and someone said that he was killed: we say perhaps he heard the rumor that he was killed and did not see that he was killed. The Beit Yosef disagrees with this, for one can say that specifically when he drowned in water that has no end, where according to the majority he is dead, we are concerned that this person relied on the majority and therefore said he died; but if a rumor emerged that so-and-so was killed and one person said he died, we do not say that he relied on the rumor that emerged. See responsum Maharshdam siman 47. One may say that in that responsum, the rumor did not emerge that he was killed, only that he did not return home for a long time, and one person says that he died. In such a case, one may say that we rely on the one who said he died, for if he did not know that he died, how did he say he died? After all, no rumor had emerged that he died, only that he did not return home, so from where does he say that he died? But if a rumor emerged that he was killed or died, and one person says he died, one may say that he says he died because of the rumor that emerged, while he himself does not know that he died. So too is the view of Mishnat Binyamin siman 105. The Taz infers from Terumat HaDeshen that if a rumor emerged about someone that he died, and afterward one person wrote that he died, even though that rumor had no clarification, she is permitted.
