Except for the five women. See Ba'er Heitev, who writes that even a statement made incidentally in conversation is ineffective. Machatzit HaShekel s.k. 15 discusses this at length and likewise concludes that these women are not believed even when speaking incidentally; Birkei Yosef records that this is the consensus of most later authorities, and Yad HaMelekh infers from Shevut Yaakov that even a fully incidental statement, preceded by connected conversation, is not accepted. The reason is comparable to the rule concerning gentile courts below, seif 10: since they are personally connected to the matter, and these five women are presumed to lie because of their hostility, even incidental speech is not a sufficient ground for reliance. Their hatred is so strong that the Sages suspected a willingness to harm themselves while harming the other woman. On this basis, Pitchei Teshuvah considers whether a yevamah and the related doubtful categories, who are believed to forbid her, might be believed to permit her when speaking incidentally. Machatzit HaShekel's wording, however, implies that even those women who are believed to forbid are not believed to permit by means of incidental speech, unless the phrase "to testify about her" means specifically to permit her. Beit Shmuel's proof that incidental speech is ineffective was already given in Shevut Yaakov and earlier in Responsa Ra'anach; Mishneh LaMelekh notes that this proof is decisive only with respect to a rival wife, leaving the other four categories still in question. Ba'er Heitev's further discussion, drawn from Beit Shmuel, concerns a rival wife whose own later husband has died. Beit Shmuel suggests that she still may not testify about her former husband if her motive is to make the other rival forbidden to him as well. Pitchei Teshuvah explains that this applies where the witness remarried and the other woman remained in her place; but if the woman about whom she testifies had already left that household through divorce or death and married another man, earlier hostility is not suspected. Maharash disagrees and invalidates even after both women separated and remarried, while Machatzit HaShekel supports Beit Shmuel's distinction. Maharchash limits the concern for former hatred to rival wives; after separation, a mother-in-law and the other listed women are fit to testify. Chelkat Mechokek cites Mahar'a that all five women are also not believed to forbid her. Machatzit HaShekel writes that a yevamah and similar unresolved cases are certainly believed to forbid, but that a sister who is also a yevamah is not believed even to forbid. Noda BiYehudah Tinyana siman 64 is also cited. Mishneh LaMelekh is unsure whether, when one of the five testified, the woman remains a definite married woman, so that one who betroths her would be forbidden to her relatives and a child born from adultery would be a definite mamzer, or whether the testimony creates doubt because perhaps it is true. He connects this to the dispute of Rambam and Ra'avad in the laws of terumah. The fact that they are not believed even to forbid her is not proof, because one who is not believed to permit is likewise not empowered to forbid. Mishneh LaMelekh adds that where valid testimony has already removed the biblical prohibition, as when a qualified witness testified that the husband fell into endless waters and remained long enough that he would have died, and one of these five women later gives testimony sufficient for permission ab initio, perhaps she may be relied upon because women are suspected of corrupting matters before human courts but not before Heaven. Since, if she remarried, she would not be forced to leave, the hostile witness cannot fully ruin her; and although incidental speech by such women is generally ineffective, in a case already removed from biblical prohibition there may be room for leniency. The same may apply to the doubtful or disputed categories, but the matter still requires study.
