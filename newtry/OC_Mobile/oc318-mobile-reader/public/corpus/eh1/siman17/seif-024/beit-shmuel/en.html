And they recognized him by them. Here, according to all opinions, visual recognition without signs is sufficient. See siman 132, where it is explained that we do not rely on visual recognition unless he has a migo, and here no migo applies. One may say that there the concern is perhaps he lies because of his wages, or so that the other not resent him; but here that concern does not apply. According to this, if another man who is not the agent says that he has visual recognition of the get, it helps even according to the Rambam's approach there, that visual recognition does not help at all, namely according to the authorities brought there. It appears that only there are we stringent because another get is possible, but here he agrees. We find this distinction in the Rosh according to the Rif. One may also say that visual recognition of a person's body is stronger. But one cannot say that here it speaks where two witnesses recognized him, whereas one witness is not believed through visual recognition; for if so, how would one have thought that a gentile would be more believed than one witness? Also below, seif 28, it is explicit that even one witness is believed through visual recognition if he did not see the drowning.
For we are concerned that the item may have been borrowed. In the Talmud, according to the first answer, identifying marks are biblical proof, and the Mishnah's ruling that marks found in his clothing are ineffective is because we are concerned that the clothes may have been borrowed. The return of a get when the husband has marks in the container in which the get was found, without concern for borrowing, as written in siman 132, refers to objects that people do not ordinarily lend, namely a purse or rings. According to the second answer, the Talmud establishes that identifying marks are rabbinic, and therefore an exceptionally distinctive mark is required. On that approach one need not say that we are concerned for borrowing; rather, the Mishnah can refer to marks that are not exceptionally distinctive. This is also implied by Tosafot: when the Talmud does not prove that we are concerned for borrowing, we do not assume such a concern. Tosafot write, according to the latter answer, that the Mishnah's statement about marks in his clothing refers to weak marks, such as white or red color. They add that on this approach we no longer say that we are concerned for borrowing. Thus, since borrowing is not proven, they do not assume it. If so, according to the answer that marks are rabbinic, we also should not say that we are concerned for borrowing; so write the Ran and Nimukei Yosef in accordance with Tosafot. Although we rule that marks are rabbinic and nevertheless are concerned for borrowing, one may say that we are stringent for both talmudic explanations: according to the first, we are concerned for borrowing; according to the second, marks are rabbinic. Still, there may be a practical difference. If intermediate marks are found on the body and exceptionally distinctive marks are found on the utensils, they may suffice either way: if marks are biblical, as in the first answer, and we are concerned for borrowing, the bodily marks that are not exceptionally distinctive suffice; and if marks are rabbinic and such marks do not suffice, then we do not suspect borrowing. I heard this in the name of the Gaon Maharal of Prague. Yet this is not compelled, for one may say that when the Talmud establishes marks as rabbinic and requires exceptionally distinctive marks, the concern for borrowing follows automatically. Since an exceptionally distinctive mark is required, any possibility that the item was borrowed prevents the mark from being exceptionally distinctive. Many authorities indeed hold that we are concerned for borrowing. At first glance it is difficult: whence do we know to suspect borrowing? According to the answer that marks are rabbinic, this concern is not necessary according to the Maharal; and according to the answer that marks are biblical, there are also two possible explanations, for one may say that the Mishnah refers to white or red clothing, and then there is no proof that borrowing is a concern. According to what I have written, this is resolved. The Talmud says that purses and rings are not lent. The Beit Yosef, however, ruled in a responsum that we are concerned for borrowing even there, while the Chelkat Mechokek remains uncertain. This too must be explained as above: according to the answer that marks are rabbinic and exceptionally distinctive marks are required, even these utensils are not sufficiently distinctive, for although it is uncommon to lend them, people sometimes do lend them. The talmudic statement that we are not concerned for borrowing in these utensils applies specifically according to the view that marks are biblical; then no exceptionally distinctive mark is required, and we need not suspect borrowing in such utensils. But according to the view that marks are rabbinic, we suspect borrowing even in these objects. This is the Ritva's view cited by the Beit Yosef. Still, this is difficult, for the sugya challenges the Mishnah, which does not rely on marks in clothing, from a get that is returned by marks in its container. This implies that the law of a get and the law of permitting the woman are the same. Yet below, in siman 132, the ruling is that we do not suspect borrowing in the case of a purse. Perhaps by a get the principal party is before us, and since he does not say that he lent the object, we do not suspect borrowing; here, however, we fear that the husband may have lent it. Even so, the sugya remains unsettled. In my view the main ruling is that we do not suspect borrowing with purses and rings, in accordance with the plain sense of the sugya. Also according to Tosafot, the Ran, and Nimukei Yosef, we do not suspect borrowing at all. Although generally one should be stringent, as I wrote, with these utensils one may be lenient. I later saw in Chut HaShani, responsum 98, in the name of R. Naftali, several points like those I wrote. This also answers the Maharal's question: if, under the answer that marks are rabbinic, one says that we suspect borrowing, then visual recognition of the utensil would not help. Why then did the Mishnah teach that marks in utensils do not help? Even visual recognition, which is biblical, would not help; why mention marks, which are rabbinic? Rather, under that answer one must say that borrowing is not suspected. According to what I wrote, this is no proof: the Mishnah teaches that non-exceptional marks do not help, and the law of marks on the body and marks on utensils is the same. It refers to utensils that are not lent, where visual recognition would help, but intermediate marks do not help. All this concerns a purse found in the garments he was wearing or rings found on his hand. But if a purse was found near him, it is ineffective, for one can say that the purse had been lying there from earlier. These laws should not be compared to the case in Bava Metzia, chapter Elu Metziot, of fruit found near a vessel, as explained in Terumat HaDeshen 262; this is clear to me.
And even a mole. This is the Rambam's language. The general rule is that signs such as being tall or short and the like are weak signs and do not help, even when several signs are combined; if she married on the basis of such signs she must leave. Intermediate signs are called "distinctive signs," and the matter depends on whether signs are biblical or rabbinic: if signs are biblical, such signs help, and if signs are rabbinic, such signs do not help. In the conclusion, Rava holds that signs are rabbinic, and the Rif and Rosh cite only this conclusion. If she married on the basis of intermediate signs she need not leave, as R. Yerucham writes in the name of the Ramah, and so too the Darkei Moshe writes. The most distinctive signs are biblical, and therefore visual recognition is biblical. Regarding a mole, according to the Gemara's first answer Rava holds that signs are biblical, and R. Eliezer and the Sages dispute whether a mole is liable to change. It follows that according to the Sages a mole is not even an intermediate sign, for if signs are biblical, an intermediate sign would help, yet a mole does not help. Thus it is not considered a distinctive sign at all. According to the later answer, however, signs are rabbinic, and the dispute is whether a mole is an especially distinctive sign; if so, one may say that it is an intermediate sign. The Rambam clearly implies that a mole is an intermediate sign, and therefore he writes "even a mole." So too Responsa Mas'at Binyamin, siman 63, and the Bach in Kuntres Acharon. The Tur writes that a mole is not an especially distinctive sign because it is liable to change. This implies that it is not even an intermediate sign, for according to the first answer the dispute is whether a mole is an intermediate sign, and the Sages hold that it is not, because it may change. Still, one may say that the Tur holds it is an intermediate sign, following the later answer: they dispute whether a mole is an especially distinctive sign, and the Sages hold it is not especially distinctive because at times it changes, but it remains an intermediate sign. This is also implied by the Tur, for he is speaking about especially distinctive signs and writes that a mole is not such a sign; hence it is not especially distinctive, but it is an intermediate sign.
One may not testify about him. Radakh ruled that if the witness specified and precisely identified the place, saying that on such-and-such a limb in such-and-such a place there was a mark, it is an especially distinctive sign. Responsa Maharshaldam, siman 52, and Responsa Mas'at Binyamin, siman 63, disagree. The Beit Yosef writes in the name of Mahardakh that if he had a mark on his finger from some illness and his finger remained bent, the woman is permitted, for a bent finger together with a mark beside it or on it is an especially distinctive sign. The Taz writes that one need not state which finger; even if he merely says "on a finger," it is an especially distinctive sign. As to a scar, Mas'at Binyamin writes that it is not an especially distinctive sign. The Taz writes that if he says merely that the man had a scar, it is not a sign; but if he knows its exact location, namely on that limb near the top, end, or middle, it is a sign, like a hole beside a particular letter. If a witness testifies to the precise location of a scar or mark but does not know beneath which eye it was, it is ineffective. Although Mas'at Binyamin, siman 63, cites Mahari Bruna regarding someone who had a perforating hole in one tooth and the witness did not know which tooth, and treats it as an especially distinctive sign, there the hole itself is an extraordinary feature and the location need not be stated. Mas'at Binyamin's comparison of that case to this case of a tooth is incorrect; this is the Taz's conclusion.
Distinctive signs. See responsum Mishnat Binyamin there, who wrote that the rule is: a distinctive sign is something found only in one out of a thousand, and it is an unusual and exceptional matter. A through-hole in a tooth is a distinctive sign, and likewise a crooked neck there. In responsum Maharam Lublin siman 128, the number where one said that there were such-and-such persons is a distinctive sign, specifically where he said that they traveled on this road. So wrote responsum Mahari ben Lev, responsum Maharam Alashkar siman 76, and responsum Mishnat Binyamin. Above in seif katan 17 I wrote that according to the Rambam this is not a distinctive sign.
And even a hundred signs that are not distinctive. In responsum Mishnat Binyamin he wrote in the name of responsum Maharam Alashkar that only poor signs are not combined, but intermediate signs do combine, for through their combination they become a very distinctive sign. In Taz he cites the words of the Mishnat Binyamin as obvious, while Chelkat Mechokek disagrees with him. See Kuntres Agunot.
The same applies to a mark on his body. The Levush wrote that a mark is a distinctive sign. In Mishnat Binyamin there he wrote that a mark is not a distinctive sign. The Taz wrote that both explanations are true, and this is what it means: likewise, a mark is not a distinctive sign; but if he identifies the exact place, it is a distinctive sign. And if we say that likewise a mark is not a distinctive sign, the meaning is that if he does not identify the exact place, it is not a distinctive sign. It is still stronger than a mole, because a mark and scar are not likely to change and are not common in someone of the same age, and it is proper to rely on this. End quote. The same applies to a gentile speaking innocently. In Terumat HaDeshen siman 239 he brought the words of Or Zarua in the name of Rabbeinu Simchah and Rabbi Natan, who hold that with a gentile, recognition through visual identification does not help, because visual identification requires careful attention, and one who speaks innocently, whose intention is not to permit the woman, does not pay attention to this. Even through a distinctive sign it is possible that it does not help if he knew him in life, because perhaps he does not examine carefully, and when he sees signs similar to those signs he assumes that this is certainly the man whose signs he knew. In responsum Maharam Padua he ruled that even if he testifies through visual identification it helps, and so ruled responsum Maharam Mintz siman 39, writing that this is the accepted ruling in Israel. So too responsum Rashakh part 1 siman 180 ruled, and wrote that responsum Mahari ben Lev ruled likewise. If witnesses said, we heard from witnesses who said that they had signs for this killed person, one must be concerned that perhaps they were inferior signs, for in common speech even inferior signs are also called signs, so it appears to me.
