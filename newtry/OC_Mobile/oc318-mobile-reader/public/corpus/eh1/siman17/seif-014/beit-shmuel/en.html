How was he speaking innocently? He is not believed by the law of testimony, for a gentile has no standing in testimony at all; rather, it is because we are certain that he is telling the truth, and because of agunah the sages were lenient. We need not be concerned that she will hire witnesses or a gentile to say this, for because of the severity they imposed upon her at the end, they were lenient at the beginning. Nevertheless, one must examine carefully whether the words appear fraudulent. See Nimukei Yosef. Whenever they come to court to testify, even if he is speaking innocently, it does not help, and we say that his intent is to testify. See responsum Rivash.

And likewise if he said, etc. The Rivash brought proof for this from the fact that the Gemara asks perhaps it was a rival wife, perhaps it was a demon, but does not ask perhaps it was a gentile. This implies that even if it was a gentile she is permitted, because it is treated as speech in the course of conversation, even without his saying additional contextual words. The Taz writes that we do not fear lest it was a gentile, because a gentile does not gratuitously cause a Jew to stumble, as is written in Yoreh De'ah; therefore we are concerned only that it was a rival wife or a demon. This is not difficult. Even though a gentile does not cause a Jew to stumble for nothing, who says that without connected contextual speech it is considered speech in the course of conversation? If it is not in that manner, it is ineffective; thus the question would properly remain, perhaps it was a gentile. Rather, it must be that even without contextual words it is still considered speech in the course of conversation. This also answers the Bach's difficulty against the Rivash. In Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim this question is brought in the name of my teacher, the gaon R. Heschel, asking how the Rivash explains the parallel sugya in Gittin, where the Gemara also does not ask perhaps it was a gentile who said, "Whoever hears my voice should write a get for my wife"; there the Rivash's answer does not apply. This is not difficult, for there it is reasonable to say that we do not suspect a gentile said such a formula merely to cause Jews to stumble. Here, however, perhaps the gentile simply says that so-and-so died because he knows it; if such a statement is ineffective without contextual speech, the question should stand. See the Beit Yosef, who writes in the name of his teachers that all the courts of Salonika ruled permissively, and so ruled Mahar'am, Maharshal, Rashach, R. Yosef of the house of Levi, and Mas'at Binyamin. If a gentile relates the matter to another gentile, all agree that connected contextual speech is not required. The Bach writes that in a case of agunah one may rely on the lenient authorities, and this appears correct. Still, the Bach's proof that in pressing circumstances we are not concerned about a rival wife, because every remote doubt is disregarded, may be rejected: the concern that it was a rival wife is different, for it is an especially remote concern.
A pretext. If he said, it is proper to pay me well for this merchandise, because I had responsibility for the road, for so-and-so was killed on this road, this is considered innocent speech, and we do not say that his intent was to improve his sale. Responsum Maharam Lublin siman 123. If an innkeeper told someone not to go out at night lest he be killed the way so-and-so was killed, this is considered innocent speech, and we do not say that his intent was to remove suspicion from himself. Responsum Roshakh questions this. What the gloss wrote, in a place where there is concern for falsehood, must mean that there are grounds for the matter, for otherwise we are not concerned for any suspicion, as I wrote in the name of Hagahot Maimoniyot.
But other gentiles speaking incidentally. So writes Terumat HaDeshen. The Chelkat Mechokek discusses this at length and objects from Terumat HaDeshen's ruling about one who drowned in waters without an end: when a gentile, speaking incidentally, says that so-and-so died, we say that his incidental statement means only that he assumes the man died, not that he knows it. Here too, perhaps the gentile heard from the courts that the man was executed by judgment, assumed he had died because he trusted them, and therefore said that he died. In truth, these concerns are not alike. Terumat HaDeshen rules that a gentile who says a man was sentenced in the courts is believed. He also rules that a gentile speaking incidentally from the mouth of another gentile is believed, and we do not suspect that the first gentile was not speaking incidentally. He further rules that when one gentile asks another gentile outside the presence of a Jew, this is considered incidental speech. These three rulings all follow one approach. Since he holds that a gentile speaking incidentally from another gentile is believed, we do not suspect that he asked him, because he holds that even if one gentile asked another outside a Jew's presence, it is incidental speech. The general rule is that we do not entertain any concern where there is no basis for it, as written in the name of Hagahot Maimoniyot. Therefore we do not suspect that a Jew was present when this gentile asked the other, or that he asked in the name of a Jew; such concerns are not entertained, just as we do not suspect that he said it merely to frighten him. The concern that the second gentile asked the first, however, is not inherently remote, because that is the normal way people converse: sometimes one tells the whole matter on his own, and sometimes one asks his fellow. It would therefore be a concern, except that Terumat HaDeshen holds that even if he asked him, it remains incidental speech when the question was asked outside the Jew's presence. We do not suspect that a Jew was present, for the reason stated above. For the same reason we also do not suspect that he heard it from the courts; that is like other unsupported concerns. But if a report had circulated that so-and-so drowned in waters without an end, and a gentile said that he died, this is not a mere concern. Rather, we say that presumably the gentile knows no more than everyone else; he knows only the report that the man drowned in waters without an end, and on that basis he says that he died. Likewise, if a report had circulated that the man was sentenced in court and the gentile said he died, we say that he spoke because of the report. But if no report had circulated and he said the man died, we do not suspect that he heard it from the courts, and we do not suspect that he drowned in waters without an end. Such a possibility is only a general concern, and we do not entertain it unless there is a necessity for that concern. The Ran, who disagrees with Terumat HaDeshen and holds that a gentile reporting from another gentile is not incidental speech, also agrees that we do not suspect anything without a basis. Otherwise there would be concern that he asked in a Jew's name or that a Jew was present. Rather, the Ran must hold that when one gentile asks another, even if no Jew was present and he did not ask in a Jew's name, it is not incidental speech. Therefore a gentile from the mouth of a gentile is not accepted, because we suspect that he asked him; and this kind of case is a real concern, as I have written. Thus the Beit Yosef's inference from the Ran's responsum, that when one gentile asks another it is not incidental speech, is correct. This is how these disputes appear to me.
