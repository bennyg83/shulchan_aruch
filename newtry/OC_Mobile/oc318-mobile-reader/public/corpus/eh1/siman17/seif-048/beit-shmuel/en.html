Or that he apostatized. So wrote Shiltei Gibborim in the name of some who say, but he disagrees with them, writing as follows: it appears to me that they said she is not believed only in a quarrel, etc.; but if he apostatized and rebelled against her, or her husband was a denier of the fundamental principle, she is believed and we do not presume that she lies. The Rama ruled like the some-say view. One may say that because this is stringent he was concerned for that opinion. One may also say that the Rama holds that these two views in Shiltei Gibborim depend on the dispute between the Rambam and the other poskim whether, when he initiates the quarrel, we say she lies, as written nearby. When he apostatized, it is like he initiated the quarrel. Shiltei Gibborim follows Tosafot's approach that when he initiates the quarrel we do not say she lies, while the some-say view holds that she lies. But according to the Mechaber's approach, who rules like the Rambam that when he initiates the quarrel we say she lies, then when he apostatized we also say she lies. The Taz wrote that he wrote in a responsum that if there is one witness, she need not leave in such a case. What Chelkat Mechokek brought as proof from responsum Ran, who wrote that one witness testifying that an apostate died in a plague does not help because one witness in a plague does not help, and did not write the reason that one witness does not help in a quarrel, may be answered: the Ran holds that one witness in a quarrel is believed, and he holds that the question of one witness in a quarrel was resolved like one witness in war, as Rashba and Ritva asked against the Rif: why was this question not resolved, while the question of one witness in war was resolved, as written below.

And even if one witness came. The Talmud asks: what is the law of one witness in wartime, and likewise of one witness in a quarrel? Rashi explains that the question is whether we fear that one witness speaks by assumption. Ordinarily he is believed because she investigates carefully before remarrying; here she does not investigate carefully and may also speak by assumption, so he is not believed. On this reading, the sugya of the two Torah scholars who drowned is well explained: they were permitted on the basis of one witness in water, which is like wartime, and the Talmud establishes that the permission was by identifying marks, where speaking by assumption is not relevant. Tosafot, however, explain that with one witness there is no concern that he speaks by assumption. The question is instead whether one witness is not believed even when he says "I buried him," because he is believed only where she investigates carefully, and in wartime and similar cases she does not. According to them it is difficult to explain the sugya of the two scholars: how were they permitted on one witness who gave marks? There remains a concern that he is lying and that the man is not dead. The Maggid Mishneh raises a similar difficulty. If there were an exceptionally distinctive mark, one could say it was in a hidden place, impossible to know except at death when the witness saw him; so write Nimukei Yosef and the Bayit Chadash. But Tosafot write that it was a non-exceptional mark and that the witness had visual recognition. If so, he must have seen the mark while the man was alive and through it recognized him visually. Since he himself says that he saw him alive, why is he believed? Perhaps he saw the mark and the man is actually not dead. This requires study. The Rif writes that although this question was not resolved here, it was resolved from the sugya at the end of Yevamot concerning the man who drowned in the Tigris. Nimukei Yosef explains that, according to the Rif, the question concerns one who said "I buried him," as Tosafot explain, and the Talmud initially tries to prove from the story of the two scholars, but rejects the proof by saying they were permitted through marks. These must be exceptionally distinctive marks, in a hidden place, as stated above. The phrase "and they saw them immediately" must then be stated as an added point: even though they saw them immediately, marks were still required. Accordingly, the Rif's statement that the question is resolved from the end of Yevamot is well understood. There it cannot be rejected by saying that the witness gave exceptionally distinctive marks, for if so why would the Talmud say that this applies only when he saw him at once? If he gave such marks, immediate viewing would not be needed. In our sugya one could say the phrase is an added point, but there, since the text explains that he saw him at once, it must be that he did not give exceptionally distinctive marks. Thus the question is resolved: one witness does not lie even where there is concern that she will not investigate carefully. Nevertheless, all this concerns a witness who says "I buried him," for the whole sugya deals with that case. If he did not say "I buried him," then although it has been resolved that he does not lie, there is still concern that he speaks by assumption. In this the Rif does not agree with Tosafot, who hold that we do not fear such assumption. Rather, according to the Rif the ruling applies specifically where he said "I buried him"; without that, we fear that he speaks by assumption. The Rambam may also hold this way, for he rules like the Rif. Therefore he writes that if one witness says "I buried him," he is believed, because the question was resolved; but if he did not say "I buried him," she should not marry ab initio, because we fear that he speaks by assumption. If she did marry, she need not leave, because it is only a concern, not because the question was unresolved. The question itself concerns one who said "I buried him." This also answers the Maggid Mishneh's objection to the Rambam, who explains the trustworthiness of one witness by saying that the matter is likely to become known, rather than by saying that she investigates carefully. Since the question was resolved that, when he says "I buried him," the witness is believed even in wartime where she does not investigate carefully, it follows that one witness is believed even where she does not investigate; it must be because the matter is likely to become known. On this basis, the Rambam's ruling about one witness in a quarrel is also understandable. We fear that she may have hired him, although this reason is not stated in the Talmud's question. It is nevertheless implied: according to the Rif, the wartime question was resolved, while the question of one witness in a quarrel was not. The difference must be that in a quarrel there is concern that she hired him. The Maggid Mishneh, however, explains the Rambam as holding that the question concerns a witness who did not say "I buried him," and whether we fear that he speaks by assumption; it was not resolved, and therefore the Rambam rules that she need not leave if she married. He writes that the Rif also holds this way. This is astonishing to me, for the Rif writes that the question was resolved and also writes that the witness is not believed if he did not say "I buried him." If one witness in a quarrel says "I buried him," the law also depends on these explanations. According to the Rambam's approach, where the concern is that he lies because she hired him, he is not believed even if he says "I buried him." But according to the other authorities, he is believed when he says "I buried him." See Kesef Mishneh, who cites a responsum that even according to the Rambam we do not suspect that she hired him unless she brought him. Although this reasoning is not fully compelled, one may be lenient, since according to the other authorities we do not suspect that she hired him.
And if she married, she need not leave. In these approaches there are four unresolved questions: one witness in war; one witness in a quarrel; give me my ketubah and permit me; and where she herself established that there was war. Regarding one matter there are two versions whether she is believed. The Rambam ruled that with one witness in a quarrel and one witness in war, she need not leave. According to the Maggid Mishneh, he holds that the question was not resolved, and nevertheless she need not leave; and the same applies where she herself established that there was war. But with give me my ketubah and permit me, he ruled that she may marry ab initio, and according to what I wrote above this is proper, because the question does not concern the permission to marry. Regarding the other matter, according to the Ran's version in the Rambam, he ruled that she is not believed, which implies that she must leave; therefore one must say that with two versions we follow the stringent one. The Rosh holds that the question of one witness in war was resolved. Regarding one witness in a quarrel he wrote that the question was not resolved. Regarding where she herself established that there was war he wrote that it was not resolved and we act stringently. It appears that he holds that with one witness in a quarrel, in any case there is a witness, and therefore she need not leave; but if she herself established the war and there is no witness, she must leave. Regarding give me my ketubah and permit me he wrote simply teiku, and the Tur likewise did not write in the name of the Rosh that she must leave. It appears to me that he holds that here there is room for leniency, that she need not leave, for the reason I wrote above. But regarding the other matter, the Tur wrote that she is not believed, and there she indeed must leave.
And she came and said: "My husband died in war." Thus writes the Rambam. In Rashi it is explicit that even if she says simply, "My husband died," when war has been established it is as though she said that he died in the war, unless she states explicitly that he died on his bed. The Bach wishes to infer that the Rambam does not agree with Rashi. It is possible that Rashi learned this law from the difficulty he raises against the questioner who asks whether, when she herself established that there was war, she is believed by a migo that she could have refrained from mentioning the war. Rashi asks that this should be resolved from the Mishnah: when there is peace in the world she is believed; necessarily, then, it speaks where she says he died in war, for if she says he died on his bed she is believed even when there is war in the world. Rather, it must be that she said he died in war, and this is called "she established war." Rashi answers that the Mishnah speaks where she says merely that he died; if there is peace in the world she is believed, but if there is war in the world she is not believed, for it is as though she said he died in war. According to the Rambam one may say that the Mishnah speaks where she says he died in war and "I buried him"; then she is believed by a migo, since she could have said there was peace in the world. For when she says "I buried him" the concern is not that she is speaking by conjecture, but only that she may be lying, as the Ran and Beit Yosef write. Therefore, where she herself established the war she is believed by a migo. The question arises only where she did not say "I buried him"; then one may say that she speaks by conjecture, and perhaps even a migo does not help. But when she says "I buried him" and she herself established the war, she is believed. Where war is otherwise established she is not believed, for she has no migo. If she merely says he died, however, one may say it is as though she said he died on his bed. There remains a slight difficulty: how can the sugya seek to resolve the matter from the baraita, "robbers fell upon us," etc.; perhaps there too she says "I buried him." This can be answered, though it is not compelled by the Rambam's language that he disagrees with Rashi. Although his phrase "my husband died in war" suggests disagreement with Rashi, his continuation suggests the opposite, for he writes that if she says he died on his bed she is believed, implying that if she says simply that he died she is not believed.
Even if she says, "I buried him." The reason is explained in the Ran's responsum cited by the Beit Yosef and in the responsum of R. Eliyahu Mizrachi cited by the Kesef Mishneh. Although when she says "I buried him" the concern that she is speaking by conjecture does not apply, we nevertheless suspect that she is lying, because it is not normal to wait during war and bury him; therefore we are more concerned that she is lying. Thus when she says he died on his bed, meaning in his house and not in war, she is believed, for then she is neither lying nor speaking by conjecture. This differs from famine. In famine, when she says "I buried him," she is believed; but if she says he died on his bed, we suspect that she speaks by conjecture, thinking that since he had no food he certainly died on his bed. There is also another reason: since it became fixed in her mind that he died, she may lie and say "I buried him." A practical difference between these reasons would be a collapse, or where a snake or scorpion bit him and he died. According to the first reason she is believed; according to the second she is not. Likewise, if she says he drowned in water that has no end, was cast onto dry land, and she buried him, she is not believed according to the second reason; so writes the Bach. What the Bach writes, however, that even if she married she must leave, seems not to accord with the Rambam; according to him she would not leave, for she has a migo that she could have said he died on his bed, as in the case where she herself established war. Still, if it was known that he drowned and she says that the water cast him up and she buried him, then according to the other authorities she is believed, while according to the Rambam, for the reason stated above, she is not believed. According to this, however, it is not resolved why "I buried him" helps when she says he died in famine. See Responsa R. Eliyahu Mizrachi. It follows from there that if during a famine she says he did not die from famine, she is believed, whereas according to the Maggid Mishneh she is not.

And if she said, he died on his bed. Meaning, he died not in war, but in his house on his bed; Maharshal. But if she says he died in war and was not killed, she is not believed. So too it implies in the gloss nearby. See Tosafot there, s.v. de'i, who asked why we say that she established there was war, since she has a migo that if she wished she would not have said there was war; why not say she has a migo that she could have said he died not in war? They answered that we do not say a migo from he was killed to he died. This is still difficult, for she still has a migo: he died and was not killed in war, through the migo that he died in his house. One must say that when she says he died in war, we do not say a migo, because one can say that she says he died in war so that she can excuse herself and say, I thought he died or was killed in the war; meaning that a spear was thrown at him and she thought he died from that wound. But if she said he died on his bed, she cannot excuse herself; therefore we do not say a migo. See Tosafot s.v. mi amrinan; at first glance these two comments contradict one another. Bach left it requiring study why, if she established there was war, she is not believed through a migo that he died on his bed; these words of Tosafot escaped him.
The same applies whenever she says, etc. Chelkat Mechokek is unsure whether the Rambam agrees to this, because the Rambam holds that even when she says, I buried him, she is not believed, and presumably she buried him far from the war, yet even so it does not help. This is not comparable, because with I buried him the reason is that since he died in war, she does not wait and lies by saying, I buried him. The Taz wrote: in the Maggid Mishneh the reason is stated, for it is the practice that people who go without weapons to watch the war are not harmed. We learn from this that in those wars where the practice is that if one side is victorious they also kill the attendants and those who come near the war, they too are in danger, as we have heard, because of our many sins, in the decrees from 5408 onward. There is no distinction in them, and anyone who was there is included in actual war. End quote.
