Even though they contradict one another. Chelkat Mechokek notes that this ruling is disputed: Shiltei Gibborim, citing Riaz, holds that if one witness says "he died" and another says "he was killed," this is a contradiction, following Rambam's view that contradiction about the substance of the event is like contradiction in formal interrogation and voids the testimony. Rosh, Tur, and the Shulchan Aruch do not follow Rambam here and treat it like contradiction in secondary inquiries. Beit Shmuel suggests, first, that even Rambam may agree here, since this resembles one witness saying "a black maneh" and the other "a white maneh"; and second, that for permitting an agunah it is enough that one witness says the husband is no longer alive, unlike monetary cases that require two witnesses. Pitchei Teshuvah observes that Beit Shmuel's second explanation appears to conflict with the later seif, which validates contradictions in secondary inquiries but implies that contradictions in formal interrogation would not be valid. He explains that, according to this approach, even contradiction in formal interrogation would permit her under the law of one witness; the later seif means only that when contradiction is merely in secondary inquiries both witnesses remain valid as a pair, with consequences for later rules. Noda B'Yehuda siman 46 argues that the Rosh, Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and later authorities overlooked an explicit Yerushalmi in Ha-Ishah Shalom, where R. Yehudah and R. Shimon concede regarding ordinary witnesses that such contradiction matters; their leniency concerns only rival wives. Since the Bavli does not contradict this, one cannot be lenient in a matter of forbidden relations. The Rosh's actual case may still be correct because the contradiction concerned whether someone was present at burial, not the body of the testimony. The Vilna Gaon likewise writes sharply that the proof from the Mishnah is mistaken and that the Rosh permitted for a different reason, namely contradiction only in secondary inquiries. Later authorities, including Maharit's responsa, Korban HaEdah, Beit Meir, Kehillat Yaakov, Yad HaMelekh, Chemdat Shlomo, and Berit Avraham, discuss the issue. In Noda B'Yehuda Tinyana siman 56, a respondent attempts to defend the Rosh from the Yerushalmi and notes that Ra'avan also ruled like the Rosh. Noda B'Yehuda rejects the defense, but nevertheless writes that since Ra'avan and Rosh are lenient, and Rif and Rambam are silent, one who relies on these two pillars of halacha cannot be stopped, especially because the Yerushalmi's statement is attributed to R. Yochanan while Rav disagrees, and perhaps the rule that the law follows R. Yochanan over Rav applies only in the Bavli. Machatzit HaShekel rules at length like Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch, as do Rashdam, Tzemach Tzedek, Maharam Lublin, and Maharchash, and he is inclined to be lenient even in real contradictions in formal interrogation where another ground for leniency exists. Yeshuot Yaakov distinguishes between valid witnesses and disqualified witnesses: two biblically valid witnesses may be relied on despite contradiction, but where rabbinic belief is extended to otherwise invalid witnesses, the Sages did not institute reliability when they contradict one another. Noda B'Yehuda Kamma siman 41 and Responsa R. Akiva Eiger siman 87 discuss agunah cases with contradictory details of day, place, and burial; R. Akiva Eiger finds many grounds to be lenient but hesitates to rely on them against Beit Shmuel and the explicit Yerushalmi. He also rejects a proposed majority argument where two witnesses contradict a third, since if the contradiction is in interrogation they may constitute one invalidated testimony; however, where burial details are not essential to the testimony, the contradiction may be only in secondary inquiries. Beit Shmuel adds that a contradiction between one witness saying the husband drowned and another saying he was killed depends on this dispute. If the drowning was in waters with an end, Shiltei Gibborim would treat it as contradiction; if in endless waters, even he may agree that there is no contradiction, since the man might have emerged and later been killed. According to the Shulchan Aruch, she is permitted in all such cases. Noda B'Yehuda Tinyana siman 57 discusses whether a witness who says the man was hanged contradicts another who says he died naturally, and notes several complicating factors, including the majority likelihood of death by execution by hanging, the credibility of the witnesses, and local practices of execution.
