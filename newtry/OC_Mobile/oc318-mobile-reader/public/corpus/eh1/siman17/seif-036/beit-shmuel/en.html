One who is taken out to be executed. See the end of siman 141. Several authorities hold that there is no distinction between a Jewish court and a gentile court; in all cases such persons are in the status of both living and dead. This is also the Rif's view, and therefore he did not cite the sugya at all, but only the Mishnah. Even according to the opinion that one taken out to be executed by a gentile court is presumed dead, the witness who testifies must be Jewish; so write Tosafot in Gittin 83 and the Rosh, and so it appears below in siman 141. The Chelkat Mechokek writes that the sugya speaks only regarding a get and terumah, but one does not permit his wife to remarry, because this resembles seeing him cut up or crucified. This does not seem correct to me, for the Rosh brings proof for the Rif from the baraita: if one heard from a Jewish court that so-and-so was executed, his wife may marry. This implies that if the witness says merely that he was taken out to be executed, she may not. Therefore he rules regarding a get that the man is doubtfully dead and doubtfully alive; it is clear that there is no distinction in the matter. In all these cases, if she married she must leave, as the Rivash writes there. The case of a ship that sank is where the witness did not remain there, and therefore she must leave. In this law of one taken out to be executed there is a dispute among the authorities, and one may say we follow the lenient view, as written above.
