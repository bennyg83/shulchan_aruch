{Rama: And some distinguish.} See Beit Shmuel, who wrote that this is based on the Rosh's explanation. The Rosh explains, they saw them immediately and stated signs, as referring to one who saw the drowning, for then he speaks by approximation. Therefore visual recognition alone does not help, but only visual recognition together with signs, and he saw them immediately. This refers to signs that are not absolutely distinctive; then he must see them immediately. But if there is a clearly distinctive sign, he does not need to see immediately. See also Tosafot there; from their words too this reasoning is proven, that if signs are not Torah-level, visual recognition must be joined, and without this, even where it is clear that he drowned, a non-distinctive sign does not help if signs are not Torah-level. In the book of the Gaon Noda BiYehudah, may his light shine, siman 43, he wrote in this language: I say that if he stayed over them until their souls departed, then by Torah law she is permitted and rabbinically she is forbidden even ab initio. If so, since there is only a rabbinic prohibition, we certainly rely on signs. A proof is that the Rosh in a responsum, kelal 2 siman 16, challenged the Rambam, who ruled regarding kilayim that one who leads animals together is liable for lashes only with one impure and one pure animal, but not with two species both impure or both pure. The challenge is from Chullin 79, where R. Abba said to his attendant, when you bring me mules to the carriage, choose those that resemble one another. Evidently he holds that we do not suspect paternal seed and that signs are Torah-level. If there were no Torah prohibition, how could he resolve from this that signs are Torah-level? And even if it were rabbinic, we would rely on them in a rabbinic prohibition. Nor can one say that the Rambam disagrees and holds that even in a rabbinic prohibition we do not rely on signs if they are not Torah-level, for certainly one cannot distinguish in this. In Yevamot they said: are signs Torah-level or rabbinic; the practical difference is returning a get by signs. Thus their language mentions specifically a Torah prohibition. End quote. I am astonished that he did not pay attention to what is explained here in Tosafot and the Rosh himself to the contrary. Their language certainly implies that according to the Ramban's method in seif 34, that in waters that have no end one must also stand there long enough for his soul to depart, that incident too was so. For otherwise, what forced them to say that if signs are not Torah-level, the case here speaks of absolutely distinctive signs? Let them say that they stood there until their souls departed. Necessarily, their view clearly implies that even where the drowning is known with certainty, nevertheless she is not permitted through a rabbinic doubt alone. In my humble opinion, the proof from responsum Rosh is not one. First, a rabbinic prohibition that has no basis except a decree is not comparable to a rabbinic prohibition arising from a Torah doubt, where one can say the Sages treated it like a definite Torah prohibition for ab initio practice; see the words of Issur VeHeter in the Shach, Yoreh Deah siman 110. Further, there no prohibition had been established, and we already hold that a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently. Thus, even simply, the Rambam has a basis for permission. The Rosh did not wish to ask why he needed to examine at all, since a rabbinic doubt is lenient, because where it is easy to clarify, failure to do so is like closing one's eyes and intentionally creating a rabbinic doubt. But it is obvious that there is no proof that signs are Torah-level, for even if they are only rabbinic, since it is impossible to clarify more, it is permitted because of a rabbinic doubt. This is unlike drowning, where the Sages forbade her because of doubt; at any rate she stands in the presumption of rabbinic prohibition, and you wish to permit her by rabbinic signs. Tosafot and the Rosh understood from the sugya that we say: whatever the Rabbis enacted, they enacted like Torah law, and she is permitted only by joining visual recognition, as if he had not drowned. See siman 132, Gittin 65a, and Pesachim 30b. According to this, it appears that one can rescue the Rambam from the Rosh's question. Even according to the Rosh's method it is difficult, for there the Gemara concludes that even R. Yehudah is uncertain whether we suspect paternal seed. If so, even though we hold like R. Yehudah, as explained there in Tosafot that the halachah follows R. Yehudah regarding mules, still there is a double doubt: perhaps we suspect paternal seed and all mules are permitted with one another; and if you say we do not suspect paternal seed, perhaps both are offspring of mares or both of she-donkeys. What he said to examine was only to clarify what can easily be clarified; see Shach, Yoreh Deah siman 110 s.k. 66. Since we establish the halachah like R. Yehudah, certainly this doubt cannot be considered a lack of knowledge, for it is a doubt for everyone. What proof is there from this that signs are Torah-level? Therefore the proof must necessarily be in this way: it implies that R. Abba relied ab initio on this examination and was not concerned at all to separate them from the time of birth, those born from mares and those born from she-donkeys, so that they not become mixed. It is certainly forbidden to mix them in order to create a double doubt. Necessarily, he relied on the signs because they are Torah-level. If so, even according to the Rambam it is resolved, for even a rabbinic prohibition may not be mixed in order to create a rabbinic doubt.
