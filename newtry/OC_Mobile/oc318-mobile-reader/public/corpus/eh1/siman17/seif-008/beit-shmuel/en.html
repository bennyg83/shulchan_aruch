But if a witness came. So wrote Ramban and Ran, unlike Shiltei Gibborim at the end of Yevamot; see siman 115, where I wrote about this. Chelkat Mechokek is unsure whether, if she spoke in the name of a male or female slave, they can contradict her. Responsum Shevut Yaakov siman 32 ruled that they cannot contradict her. See these laws in Kuntres Agunot simanim 159, 160, and 297. If two witnesses say something in the name of one person, that one person cannot contradict two witnesses. If the first witness comes and says that he told him falsely, Chelkat Mechokek wrote that he is believed, since the first statement was outside bet din. He also wrote that the woman must investigate well so that the first witness does not retract. Certainly his intent is as good advice, that she should investigate so she does not come to stumbling; but by law we are not concerned that perhaps the first will retract and say, I spoke falsely, for if so, how could one permit on the basis of a witness when the first witness is not before us? Rather, he said this as good advice.
And the bet din need not send for him. So it implies in responsum Ri Vav, who wrote: are all the Mishnayot and all the poskim who ruled that we permit based on testimony heard from another witness speaking only where the first witness is not in this city, and for that reason they stated their words without qualification? Rather, certainly even if he is in the city, one need not send for him. Chelkat Mechokek challenged this from the sugya of perhaps a locust died, but one who examines the sugya will see that Ri Vav learned this law from that sugya. This is the explanation according to his approach: if one hears from children saying that so-and-so died, he may testify, and even if the children are in the city one need not ask them. Therefore the Gemara challenged there: perhaps a locust died and they called that locust by so-and-so's name; therefore one must ask them. If so, why do we not ask them? Rather, the testimony is valid in this way; this is how he explained the sugya. Even according to what I wrote nearby in the name of the Maggid Mishneh, that we do not judge possible from impossible and must ask him how he saw, there is no contradiction from there to this law. Specifically the body of the testimony, how he knew, must be asked if possible. But if the second witness explains his testimony properly, one need not ask the first witness.
