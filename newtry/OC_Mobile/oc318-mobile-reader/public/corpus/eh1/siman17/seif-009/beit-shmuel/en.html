One witness says he died, etc. The Tur cites a responsum of the Rosh that where two witnesses testify that he died, even though there is a contradiction between them, the woman is permitted. That responsum concerned a contradiction about whether a certain person was present at the time of death, and the Rosh ruled that since both testify that he died, this is complete testimony. From there alone one would not know the case where they contradict one another as to whether he died or was killed, for perhaps that is a contradiction. Yet the Rosh learned his rule from the Mishnah in Yevamot: one woman says he died and one says he was killed; Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon say that since both acknowledge that he is no longer alive, they may marry. It follows that even when the witnesses contradict one another in this manner, saying died or was killed, they may marry. The Beit Yosef asks why the Tur wrote this law in the name of the Rosh, when it is explicit in that Mishnah. This is not difficult, for in the Mishnah one might say that only rival wives do not create a contradiction, since they cannot truly refute one another. But where witnesses contradict one another in this way perhaps it is a contradiction. From the Rosh we learn that even such witnesses are not considered contradictory, since both agree that he is not alive. The Derishah and Chelkat Mechokek write similarly. The Rosh's ruling even regarding witnesses appears to rest on Tosafot in the name of R. Yoel, who explains that the case is where we see that both women come to permit themselves and not to contradict one another. According to the first answer there, then, even contradictory testimony of this kind is effective; this follows R. Yochanan's answer there, not R. Eliezer's first answer. Examine the sugya and it will be clear. I have seen in Tzemach Tzedek, siman 59, that he wrote differently. See also Choshen Mishpat siman 30, where it is explained that when witnesses contradict one another in bedikot their testimony stands, but when they contradict one another in chakirot their testimony does not stand, even in monetary matters. If one says he lent barrels of wine and the other says barrels of honey, the Rambam treats this as a chakirah and the testimony is void. The Chelkat Mechokek understood that the contradiction here, whether he died or was killed, is like wine or honey according to the Rambam, and therefore asked how the Mechaber could rule simply like the Rosh. It is more reasonable that the Mechaber holds there is no dispute here between the Rambam and the Rosh. The contradiction here is like "a black maneh" and "a white maneh," which all agree is not a contradiction, because once he saw that the man was not alive he was not so exacting about the cause of death. Moreover, in this matter one witness who says he is no longer alive is sufficient; therefore even if two contradict one another in the investigative details, one of them is nevertheless saying the truth that he is no longer alive, and she is permitted. This differs from monetary law, which requires two witnesses. I saw this also in Penei Moshe I, siman 64. The Shiltei Gibborim, however, writes that if one says he died and one says he was killed, it is a contradiction, while Maharam Lublin, siman 130, ruled like the Mechaber. If one testifies that he drowned and the other says he was killed, the matter depends on this dispute: according to the Shiltei Gibborim she is forbidden, and according to the Mechaber she is permitted. In any event, if the drowning was in water that has an end, it is like one saying he died and one saying he was killed; but if it was in water that has no end, it is not a contradiction, for he may have emerged from the water and been killed, as appears in the responsum of Maharam.
