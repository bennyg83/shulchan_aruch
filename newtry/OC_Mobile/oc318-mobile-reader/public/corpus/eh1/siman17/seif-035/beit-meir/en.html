The gaon Noda BiYehudah wrote in siman 28, s.v. furthermore it appears to me, that one should wonder about the incident of the Diglat, where the Gemara states as a novelty that she married after five days, and asks, according to whom did he act? See there. The difficulty is: how is this after five days case understood? If they saw that this woman's husband drowned, and after five days they found someone in the Diglat and recognized him as this woman's husband, what does it matter that it was after five days? Whichever way you look at it, she is permitted. If the drowned man did not come up again, then he remained there longer than the time required for his soul to depart. Rather, we must be concerned that he came up. If so, I say: if we had not known anything about drowning, and we found in the water a man recognizable by visual identification as this woman's husband, she would be permitted even if the water did not constrict the face, for according to Rabbenu Tam we attribute that it was within three days. If so, if someone drowned before us and then came up alive before us, and afterward it happened that they found in the water a man whose visual identification was like the person who drowned and came up before us, his wife would be permitted. For although he came up then, nevertheless since he is now recognizable by visual identification as so-and-so, we say that he drowned again and died in the water. If so, in the Diglat case, where you say it was after five days and her husband drowned, even if we say that he came up, nevertheless since he is recognizable by visual identification as this man, we say he drowned again and not yet three days have passed, and visual identification properly applies. And if you say that it was after five days because this person drowned five days earlier and had been lying there since then, then it is obvious that she is permitted. See Shulchan Aruch seif 27 and examine carefully. Based on this, he explained that the case was where there were already witnesses by signs that the one who had drowned was the one who was brought up, but they did not know whether he was this woman's husband, and the shushvin recognized by visual identification that he was her husband, though he did not know from the sign. In my humble opinion, this is a remote explanation. As the incident is stated, there is no difficulty at all; the essential element needed for the difficulty is missing. It is also difficult: from where did he know to ask this? Perhaps it was simply as stated. Rather, in my humble opinion, what he wrote, that it should not be better than if we saw with our own eyes that he came up, is certainly better. If we saw that he came up and now someone is found and recognized by visual identification, we ordinarily say that he drowned again within three days and that the identification is true. But where this person drowned five days ago, although if he were not found we would be stringent and be concerned for the minority possibility that he came up, why should we say this leniently by Torah law, when the majority do not come up? If so, when he is found and recognized, we say: how is it possible that this identification is true and he is the person who drowned five days ago? After three days there is no recognition. Rather, this identification is imaginary and false, and there is no testimony, similar to Bekhorot 28b regarding a practical difference to contradict witnesses. What remains is the remote concern that perhaps he came up; but one cannot say that through this testimony it is clarified that he did not come up, for if he did not come up how can they identify him after three days? To say that their identification is true is impossible unless he came up and then drowned again, and for leniency we do not say that he came up. Examine carefully. In the Kuntres Iggun of Maharachash, folio 64, it is stated that the custom is to be lenient like the first opinion, and the second opinion is the view of the Rashba, who was uncertain, as stated in the Beit Yosef. Without doubt those who practice leniency have their reasons. To strengthen the matter I will add support for the custom and say that the Rashba z"l is properly uncertain according to his own view, but according to us there is proof for leniency. It is also stated in the above Kuntres Iggun, folio 45b, in the name of a responsum of the Ran, that what the Rambam wrote, if he remained after being cast from the water onto dry land and became swollen, does not come to be lenient by requiring that he certainly became swollen. Rather, he means that ordinarily, if he remained there, he certainly became swollen even if it is not noticeable. He wrote that according to the Rambam's words it appears that the Rambam is stringent because he holds that he certainly became swollen, whereas the Rashba wrote only that perhaps he became swollen. Perhaps in this they follow their own views, for it is stated in the Shach, Yoreh De'ah siman 110, in the name of Responsa Rashba, that in a rabbinic doubt where a prohibition has an established presumption, we do not rule leniently; whereas Pri Chadash and Minchat Yaakov ruled leniently on the basis of the Rambam. Therefore the Rambam had a difficulty: since he already drowned, she is permitted by Torah law, and only a rabbinic prohibition remains, so why should we be concerned that perhaps he became swollen? He was forced to explain that he certainly became swollen. The Rashba does not need this, because in any case she remains in the presumption of a married woman. It appears at first glance that since all the poskim equate after three days on dry land with remaining after being brought up from the water, according to the Rambam, after three days on dry land he too certainly became swollen, unlike the implication of the other poskim that it is only a doubt. The reason he did not explain this regarding dry land is because it was not needed for the above. However, according to his view, the difficulty is: what does the Gemara ask in the Diglat case, according to whom did they act? Perhaps in truth the reason that one does not testify after three days is only because of doubt; but with a drowned person, where the remaining prohibition is rabbinic, we rule leniently. One must say that the Talmud holds that necessarily the reason is that after three days he certainly changes. For if it were only a doubt, even in a Torah matter it would not be proper to suspect perhaps he became swollen and thereby establish the witnesses as having testified by imagination, especially where there are two witnesses. On the contrary, reason dictates that the testimony of the witnesses clarifies for us the doubt that he did not become swollen. Rather, after three days he certainly became swollen, and the question is sound. It also appears that although the dispute of Tosafot and Rashba concerns the meaning of the Mishnah, nevertheless the main conceptual dispute depends on this: according to Tosafot, the testimony removes us from the doubt of after three days, and we ordinarily say it was within three days; according to the Rashba, because of the doubt we are concerned and establish the witnesses as testifying by imagination. If so, the Rashba, following his view, can remain uncertain, and nevertheless the Gemara's question is proper. But according to the Rambam, the above difficulty arises, and one must say as above: he plainly holds that because of doubt we do not establish witnesses as testifying by imagination. From this he proved that after three days he certainly became swollen. Consequently, in a doubt whether it was within three days or after three days, we also do not establish the witnesses as testifying by imagination, and we say it was within three days. If so, according to the agreement of Pri Chadash and Minchat Yaakov with the Rambam, there is room to be lenient.
