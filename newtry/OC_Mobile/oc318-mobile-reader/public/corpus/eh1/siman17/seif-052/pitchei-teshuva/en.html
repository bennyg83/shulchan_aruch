Even if it is not authenticated. See Baer Heitev, who writes that some authorities nevertheless hold that authentication is required, but authentication by one witness is effective; ordinarily one witness does not authenticate a document because it is like testimony from the mouth of testimony, etc. This is the language of the Beit Shmuel, se'if katan 29. See Responsa R. Akiva Eiger, siman 92, who writes that his words are unclear; see there. See also what I wrote below, siman 97, se'if katan 2. Regarding Baer Heitev's further statement that if a writing came from afar the Chelkat Mechokek is lenient, see Seder HaTzvi, se'if katan 38, who discusses this and concludes that in practice the main view appears to be the ruling of the Chelkat Mechokek: in a case of agunah, where no other testimony can be obtained, one may rely on the authorities who do not require authentication, since in this case there is no basis to suspect forgery. But where the woman herself is not believed, such as where there was a quarrel and the like, so that there is reason to suspect forgery, the matter requires study; see there. He means this even if two witnesses are signed on it. See Responsa Pnei Aryeh, siman 20, who writes that a letter coming from afar through the postal service is worse and requires authentication by Torah law. It is only where someone brings a document in his hand and says that it was handed to him by a Jew in a distant place, and he found written in it that so-and-so died, that one might have thought, according to the Ramban and Rashba, that authentication is unnecessary. But where a letter comes through the post, since the postal official in the place where the letter is deposited receives sealed and closed writings and does not care who brings them, even if it is ultimately clarified that they were forged it can never be discovered from whom the evil came. Therefore we suspect forgery by Torah law until it is authenticated. If it is written in a Jew's handwriting, perhaps one may be lenient in a case of agunah, as the Chelkat Mechokek wrote; but in his case the writing was that of a gentile woman, etc.; see there. In his index he writes that the Shakh in Choshen Mishpat, siman 46, is also of this opinion. See also Kehillat Yaakov by the gaon of Lisa, who is very stringent about a writing that comes from afar and requires authentication at least by one witness. In his opinion, even if it is written in a Jew's handwriting, one should not be lenient even in a case of agunah, unlike the Chelkat Mechokek, because this is a matter that is not at all likely to become revealed, and how can one witness be believed in a matter of forbidden relations? See there. Perhaps, however, if two people are signed on it in such a way that it is clearly recognizable that one person did not write both signatures, he too would agree. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 33, who discusses at length the dispute among the authorities on this law and concludes that the main view is the one that does not require authentication. This is obvious according to those who hold that one witness in testimony permitting a woman is believed by Torah law; see what I wrote above, se'if katan 13. But even if we say that one witness in testimony about a woman is believed only rabbinically, nevertheless we say that authentication is not required. He concludes that if two more rabbis agree, he too will join in permitting; see there. Responsa Kehillat Yaakov by the gaon of Karlin, siman 11, also tends toward leniency, that authentication is not required. He brings a proof from Yevamot 115b, the case of Yitzchak the exilarch, where "they sent from there," and the language "sent" implies that they sent by writing in a letter. In Bava Batra 38 it is explained that Hispania is a year's journey from the Land of Israel, so certainly no one knew how to authenticate a letter coming from there; evidently authentication is not required. But according to Responsa Maharik, siman 30, also cited in Responsa Galia Masekhet, siman 70, letter 7, the explanation there is that they sent from Cordoba, not from Hispania, and according to that there is no proof. In particular, the Kehillat Yaakov writes to permit in his own case, where the letter was in the handwriting of three people constituting a court that had accepted testimony. Some authorities hold that authentication by a court does not itself require authentication even regarding bills of divorce, as Rema writes at the end of siman 142; see Responsa Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, siman 136. All the more so in testimony about a woman, to testify that her husband died, and especially where it is difficult to obtain authentication, the woman should be permitted. Still, a court of three should be seated to permit her; see there. In Responsa Chatam Sofer, simanim 44 and 49, he too discusses this at length. It is clear there that his opinion follows the Chelkat Mechokek: in a case of agunah one may rely on an unauthenticated document, but where it is not a case of agunah it is not proper to rely on it without authentication, even if three are signed on it. But if a letter was written to a known addressee and the answer came back through the post, that is sufficient even if only one person signed it, for in such a case no authentication is needed. He had already written this in siman 43: any writing sent by post to known owners, where a reply comes from them back by post, is plainly from its owners and involves no forgery, as every understanding person sees, and it needs no authentication. See there. At the end of siman 45 he also writes this reasoning and mentions that the gaon in Responsa Beit Ephraim also wrote so, and that cases had already been decided several times on the basis of this reasoning. This is also explained further at the end of siman 94; see there and see the next se'if katan.
