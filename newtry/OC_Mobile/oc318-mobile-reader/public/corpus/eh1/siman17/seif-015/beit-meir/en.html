One witness says "he died," etc. The Beit Shmuel and Chelkat Mechokek understood, etc. Here too, since the witness saw that he was no longer alive, he is not careful about the precise cause by which he died. Even when one witness says that he died by the sword and another says that he died by an ariran, their testimony is invalid only because this is treated as a matter of derishah, formal examination. In my humble opinion, that is because liability or exemption depends on the matter: if the death was by a sword no medical assessment is needed, whereas if it was by another instrument assessment is required, as is stated in Hilkhot Rotzeach 3. The same may explain the Gemara's answer in Sanhedrin 41a regarding a cloth with which he was strangled, if it was fit to strangle with. The words of Tosafot in Chullin 11b, who write that sword and ariran are not so closely connected with the body of the testimony, require examination. This is also explicit in Rambam, chapter 1 of Hilkhot Edut: the chakirot and derishot are the matters that constitute the essence of the testimony, by which one becomes liable or exempt. Here, by contrast, there is no practical difference between "he died" and "he was killed," so it is not called derishah. Accordingly this must also be Rambam's reason for treating wine and oil as a contradiction in derishah: according to one witness the defendant is exempt from wine, and according to the other he is exempt from oil. The same is clear from Ran in Sanhedrin, in the fifth chapter, where he writes that the case in which one says "barrels of wine" and the other says "oil" is a contradiction. Although in chapter Chazakat HaBatim we say that people do not distinguish carefully between wheat and barley, that is where one says that he consumed wheat and the other says barley; there the contradiction is only in bedikot, for the matter on which the chazakah depends is equally significant whether it was wheat or barley. See there. Thus there is no real difference between Ran and Rambam except that Ran calls the contradiction of wine and oil a severe bedikah that invalidates even in monetary cases, while Rambam includes it among the chakirot. When Rambam also lists, among the chakirot, the food with which one ate on Yom Kippur, it must be because there too there is a practical distinction, for example whether the food was unfit for eating. So too, after analysis, each matter that he mentions has legal consequence. At first glance there is support for Rambam's view that whatever defines the substance of the testimony is called derishah. At the end of Yevamot the Gemara asks whether Rabbi Tarfon does not require derishah from the baraita that examines the witness: did the lion speak about the person concerning whom he testified, or about the village? The Gemara establishes it according to the one who requires derishah. Thus that question is within the class of derishot, because through it we know about whom he is testifying. Ran's responsum 47, however, writes that even a contradiction about the name is considered a contradiction in bedikot and is valid in monetary law. That is because the identity of the person about whom they testified was known apart from the name: everyone knew that the husband had no more than one brother, and both witnesses testified to the death of the husband's brother. The name therefore made no practical difference. In this light the words of the Shulchan Aruch are correct. The second answer brought by the Beit Shmuel in the name of the Penei Moshe, that in testimony permitting a woman to remarry one witness suffices even if the witnesses contradict one another in chakirot, is difficult. The Shev Yaakov, responsum 18, agrees with the Beit Shmuel himself, who in section 21 rules decisively that when there is contradiction in chakirot the testimony is void. Also, Bach in responsum 94 thought that in testimony concerning a woman, where the sages were lenient because it is a matter likely to become known and because of the woman's own careful inquiry, even contradiction in chakirot does not void the testimony. But the Shev Yaakov already wrote that the language of Ran and of Shulchan Aruch section 21 does not imply this. The Baer Heitev, however, writes that if one witness says he died in one place and another says he died in another place this is not a contradiction, citing Mahari Karo. This requires checking in the work itself, for it is proved in Bach. Know also that against the Penei Moshe's reasoning the Noda BiYehudah, responsum 41, objected from Sema, Choshen Mishpat 30:9, who writes that the testimony is void and that no oath is even required, although one witness also suffices for an oath. He answered that in monetary law real testimony is required, and without a witness judgment cannot be rendered; truth of the matter alone does not suffice. The Torah invalidates the testimony of relatives even in the case of Moses and Aaron, although the matter would certainly be true. Since these two witnesses contradict each other and one of them is lying and invalid, the other's testimony is also void. But in testimony permitting a woman we do not require formal witnesses; we require knowledge that the husband died. Therefore, if many witnesses testified and one was found disqualified by sin, it appears to him that the woman is nevertheless permitted. So too, when they contradict each other in chakirot, since according to both of them he is at least dead, and one of them is telling the truth, she may be permitted through the true witness, whoever that is. His statement that in monetary law testimony rather than truth is required needs examination, for we rule that witnesses were created only against falsehood, as appears in Choshen Mishpat 191:1. Only in bills of divorce and betrothal do the acts have no force without witnesses; in monetary matters, the truth of the matter is plainly the essence. He must mean that a Torah-level oath can be imposed only by a valid witness. As to the decree concerning relatives, the decree must mean that their testimony is considered as though it had not been given, so that the truth of the matter is not known through them. From the Itur cited by Shakh, Choshen Mishpat 33:9, it also sounds as though the decree even treats Moses and Aaron as suspect of lying; otherwise what does he mean by saying that once the ruling has been issued, it stands, and that a person is inclined to maintain his prior statement? If he was never suspected of lying but was merely disqualified at the time of testimony by the royal decree, and now that he has become distant the decree no longer applies and his testimony begins and ends in fitness, why invalidate him? It would seem that the decree is to suspect even Moses of falsehood. Yet that cannot be correct, as is clear from Choshen Mishpat 46:35, where others testify that they recognize the signatures. The reason is that disqualification through kinship is not because one is suspected of forgery, but because of the King's decree. If one is not suspected of forgery, he is certainly not suspected of testifying falsely. The words of the Itur therefore require examination. We must explain with difficulty: since a person is inclined to maintain his prior words, and we know even before he testifies again that he will repeat his first testimony, it is not considered a new testimony but one continuous testimony; once the matter has been ruled on, it has been ruled on. This is satisfactory if judgment has already been rendered. But if judgment has not yet been rendered, why follow the first testimony? Let us follow the second, which begins and ends in fitness, and rule by it. This explains Shakh's statement that there is no reason to distinguish whether judgment has already been rendered. According to his implication, that there is suspicion of falsehood, he wrote well; according to what we have said, it is resolved. If so, because the decree is that their testimony is treated as nonexistent, and the truth of the matter is therefore not known, one might also say in testimony concerning a woman that through the rule that when one witness is found a relative or disqualified the entire testimony is void, the testimony is deemed nonexistent and the truth is not known. Nevertheless the Noda BiYehudah spoke well that in testimony concerning a woman the rule of "one of them was found a relative or disqualified" does not apply. According to those who hold that by Torah law one witness is valid in testimony concerning a woman this is obvious, for the analogy "just as two..." does not apply to such testimony. Even according to most authorities who hold that by Torah law two witnesses are required, and therefore the analogy could have applied by Torah law, once the sages validated all those who are Torah-disqualified except one disqualified by a Torah transgression, a person disqualified only by combination is no worse than a woman or slave, since he himself committed no transgression. The author of the responsum, in responsum 45, wrote this as obvious. Thus, if Sema's reason were clearly based on "one of them was found disqualified," the answer would be good. In my humble opinion, however, this requires examination, for it appears that his reason is that with respect to the testimony in which they contradicted each other we treat both of them as disqualified. This is like Rosh in chapter Zeh Borer, law 34, who writes on Ramah's words that at first glance even a Torah oath should not be required, because according to the lender one of them is testifying falsely; the testimony is contradicted, and we treat both as disqualified for that testimony. If his reason were "one of them was found disqualified," what was difficult? To invalidate through that rule they must have intended to testify; perhaps Ramah was speaking where they saw the loan without intending to testify. Although Sema had to distinguish this way, on further examination Sema 30:25 writes on Rosh's words that we do not say these witnesses are considered contradicted and their testimony void, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write in the following siman. This shows clearly that he understood Rosh's reason also to be that no Torah oath should result because it is like "one of them was found a relative or disqualified," for in the following siman, subsection 2, he explicitly explains this reason, as he writes there and as appears in Rashba's responsum 322. Still, this is difficult for me, because Rosh rules that two requirements are needed for "one of them was found disqualified": they must also have intended from the outset to testify, as is clear in siman 36 in his name. Moreover, even if they intended to testify, that is not enough here, for one of them was contradicted only at the time of testimony by the lender's own words; at the time of seeing the event both were presumed valid. What has this to do with invalidating the valid witness by his combination with the invalid one? This is especially so according to Shakh, siman 36:9, who writes that intent to testify is of no use when they did not see the event together, for how did they join? That certainly does not apply to Ramah's case, where they testified about different times. One should not force the matter by saying that Rosh wrote according to Ramah's view in Tur siman 36 that testimony in court alone suffices, for Rosh never brought that view of Ramah even in tractate Makkot. Furthermore, even according to Ramah there is no difficulty according to Rif's view in siman 36 that the valid witness must recognize the invalidity of the other. Even according to those who disagree, the witness who is contradicted and invalidated only at the time of testimony was truly valid beforehand, and the valid witness was an anus, not even inadvertent. Why then did Rosh answer by distinguishing between witnesses who contradict each other and witnesses contradicted by the lender? He could simply have said that even if the witnesses contradict each other in chakirot, the valid witness should not be invalidated by combination with the invalid one because he was an anus. It therefore seems to me that Rosh plainly means that for the testimony for which they were invalidated through contradiction they are entirely void in their own right, though the source for this still requires examination. This is surprising regarding Sema: according to his own reasoning, how could he rule that law simply in siman 30:9, when almost all decisors agree that intent to testify at the time of seeing is required? Tosafot in Zeh Borer 31, s.v. "she-beit Shammai," although they imply that intent to testify is not required, answered in other ways, and in Bava Batra they indeed changed the reason to lack of intent. According to my reading of Rosh, the Penei Moshe's answer does not stand. See, however, Urim VeTumim, who undermines Sema's very rule on the basis of the sages of Lunel; the matter requires examination. I later found a great novelty in the book of the Noda BiYehudah, responsum 46. He brings a clear proof for the Shiltei Giborim from the Yerushalmi on this mishnah in Ha-Ishah Shalom. The Yerushalmi records: Rabbi Yaakov bar Acha in the name of Rabbi Yochanan, and Rabbi Chila in the name of Rabbi Eliezer: Rabbi Meir admits in the first case. He found a teaching: even that case is in dispute. What is the difference between the second and the first? In the first they did not make this woman's statement count at all against the other. Rabbi Elazar said: Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon admit regarding witnesses. What is the difference between witnesses and a rival wife? They did not make the rival wife's words count at all against her fellow. Rabbi Yochanan said: if Rabbi Elazar said it, he heard it from me. The Penei Moshe explains that "Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon admit regarding witnesses" means the case of one witness saying "he died" and one saying "he did not die," and likewise explains "from whom did he hear it." The Noda BiYehudah writes that he erred, for Rabbi Elazar in the Yerushalmi says that Rabbi Meir admits in the first case. If so, the opening case, where one says he died and one says he did not die, is unanimous; Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon are not mentioned there. How then can one say that they "admit regarding witnesses," which implies that although they disagree regarding rival wives they admit regarding witnesses? They do not disagree regarding rival wives at all in that case. Rather, "they admit regarding witnesses" means where one says "he died" and the other says "he was killed." Even if we emend the text to align it with our Talmud, so that Rabbi Yochanan says Rabbi Meir admits in the first case and Rabbi Chila in the name of Rabbi Elazar found that even it is in dispute, nevertheless the conclusion remains. The Yerushalmi says that Rabbi Elazar heard it from Rabbi Yochanan, and according to Rabbi Yochanan Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon did not disagree regarding rival wives in the case of one saying "he did not die." It must therefore mean that they admit regarding witnesses where one said "he died" and one said "he was killed." The Noda BiYehudah concludes that, with due respect to Rosh, Tur, Beit Yosef, and all the later authorities, the Yerushalmi was hidden from them and the words of the Shiltei Giborim are primary. In my humble opinion, although his explanation is clear, as I will explain, the reason remains difficult. Since Rabbi Yochanan holds that Rabbi Meir admits in the first case because the contradiction of a rival wife counts for nothing, and according to the Yerushalmi Rabbi Elazar also holds this way, Rabbi Meir's disagreement where one says "he died" and one says "he was killed" must follow Tosafot's reason: since both women come to permit themselves, we cannot treat either as intending only to ruin the other; rather, they may have spoken by conjecture. Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon's reason must be that their words agree on the essential matter, that he is not alive. If so, why distinguish and say that regarding witnesses they admit? What is different? Their words still agree on the essential matter. The reason of contradiction by a rival wife is one Rabbi Meir himself admits, according to Rabbi Yochanan; he holds only that where both come to permit themselves that reasoning does not apply. These are precisely the words of the Beit Shmuel on Rosh, that this follows the answer of Ri. See the sugya and you will understand. Thus, contrary to the Noda BiYehudah's astonishment at Rosh from logic, the simple explanation is as Beit Shmuel wrote: according to Rabbi Yochanan, the matter is proved intrinsically, for there is no reasoned basis to distinguish for Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon between witnesses and rival wives. According to the Noda BiYehudah's explanation, this difficulty falls on Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Elazar in the Yerushalmi. Moreover, I will bear my shame before all our masters from Rosh onward: shall we say that the Yerushalmi escaped even Rif and Rambam? They are the ones who illuminate our eyes in both Talmuds by extracting law from them. Would they have ignored this Yerushalmi set out in its place? Rather, later in the Yerushalmi it says: we learned there, one who had, etc. Rav said that contradiction of testimony within testimony does not void it; Rabbi Yochanan said it does void it. Rabbi Yochanan follows his own view, for Rabbi Chiyya said in the name of Rabbi Yochanan: if the counter was established and one says "from the purse" while the other says "from the bundle," this is contradiction of testimony within testimony, and even Rav admits that the testimony is void. Where do they disagree? Where there were two groups, these saying "from the purse" and those saying "from the bundle": is contradiction of testimony within testimony void, or has it not been voided? Rav's strength is from the mishnah in which Rabbi Shimon and Rabbi Yehudah say, since both admit that he is not alive, they may marry. But did he not hear what Rabbi Elazar said, that Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon admit regarding witnesses? What is the difference between witnesses and a rival wife? They did not make the rival wife's words count at all against her fellow. The mishnah then challenges Rav: both chakirot and bedikot, when they contradict one another, their testimony is void. Rabbi Mana answers for Rav that this refers to one witness against one witness. From this it is explicit, as the Noda BiYehudah explained, and it is surprising that Penei Moshe did not look from the end of the passage back to its beginning. Yet it is also explicit that Rav and Rabbi Yochanan both hold that with one witness against another, when one says "from the purse" and the other "from the bundle," the contradiction is considered in the body of the testimony and the testimony is void. They disagree only about two groups. This is exactly what our Talmud calls contradiction in bedikot, such as one saying a black money pouch and the other saying a white one, which is valid in monetary cases, as explained in Choshen Mishpat 30. This shows that the Yerushalmi disagrees with the conclusion of our Talmud and holds that even in monetary law, contradiction in bedikot voids the testimony. Therefore Rabbi Yochanan, following his own view, properly rules that Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon admit regarding witnesses; and Rav, following his own view, who did not wish to distinguish between rival wives and witnesses, properly derives from that mishnah a distinction between one group and another group. But according to the conclusion of our Talmud, just as we judge in the case of the money pouch that the testimony remains valid in monetary law, the same is true for testimony concerning women, which is treated like monetary law: even with one witness against one witness, if one says "he died" and the other says "he was killed," their testimony remains valid for all opinions. I have already shown that even for Rambam this is only bedikot. For this reason they excluded the Yerushalmi from practical law and stated the mishnah simply, to teach that there is no distinction, even in one witness against one witness, as Rosh rules. However, Rosh and Tur in Choshen Mishpat 145 distinguish between wheat and barley on the one hand and wheat and legumes on the other, saying that the latter does not establish a chazakah because people do not confuse all such things. According to him it is somewhat difficult, for "he died" and "he was killed" should perhaps resemble legumes and barley. This can be answered with difficulty and requires further study. In any case, we learn from the Yerushalmi that since Rabbi Yochanan, following his own view, says Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon admit regarding witnesses, although the witnesses are really comparable to one group against another, and because even in one group against another he holds that the testimony is void, it follows that he agrees with Rosh that with respect to the testimony for which they contradicted each other we treat both witnesses as invalid for that testimony. Since the law is known to follow Rabbi Yochanan against Rav, it follows for us as well that in contradiction in chakirot, like wine and oil, Beit Yosef wrote correctly that even for a single witness's oath the witness does not stand. See the Urim VeTumim cited above, for his words are not compelling, since Ran in Sanhedrin holds exactly like Rambam that monetary witnesses who contradict each other in chakirot are entirely void, unlike the ruling of the sages of Lunel. Examine this carefully.
