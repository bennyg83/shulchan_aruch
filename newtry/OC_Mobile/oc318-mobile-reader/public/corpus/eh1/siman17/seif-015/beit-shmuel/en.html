A gentile who spoke innocently at first, even though they questioned him afterward. This is specifically in the same session, as ruled in responsum Maharshdam siman 23. In responsum Mishnat Binyamin siman 105 he wrote in the name of Maharam Alashkar and Binyamin Ze’ev that even afterward it is considered innocent speech. Chelkat Mechokek wrote: from the language of the gloss it appears that if a Jew asked a gentile, where is so-and-so, and he answered, he died, and a long time afterward the gentile related innocently that so-and-so died, he is not believed. But in truth this is a great stringency; because they asked him last year, should he not be believed now? See responsum Mahari ben Lev part 3 siman 46.

Where is our companion, etc. This implies that even if they did not leave their companion ill, nevertheless whenever they asked, where is he, it is not considered innocent speech. See responsum Mishnat Binyamin siman 109 and responsum Tzemach Tzedek. In practice, all these matters that they wrote there from their own reasoning require analysis. See the Mordechai at the end of Yevamot, who explains the Yerushalmi: if one woman was mentioned before the gentile and he said that her husband died, this is not innocent speech; but if he asked about one city that was under siege and said, so-and-so from that city died, this is innocent speech. Responsum Maharam Lublin siman 130.
But if they asked another gentile. Chelkat Mechokek and Taz explain that this gentile was not present at that same session when they asked. But if he was present at the same session, even though they did not ask that gentile, it is as though they asked him. According to this one must say that what is written, although he was with the gentile, means that he was with him afterward, not at the time of the question. Beit Yosef wrote in the name of his teacher that if a Jew asked a Jew, and a gentile was present at that session and answered that he died, this is innocent speech. According to Chelkat Mechokek, one must distinguish: if they asked one gentile and a second gentile was present at that session, it is as though they asked him; but if one Jew asked another Jew, even though the gentile who answered was present at that session, it is not as though they asked that gentile.

There was doubt whether it was innocent speech. Likewise, in every place where there is a dispute, we rule stringently. See below, where it is explained that according to the Beit Yosef’s interpretation, the Rambam holds that with one witness in war or in a quarrel, where the question was not resolved, if she married she need not leave; according to his interpretation, the question concerns the case after she married, and he understands the Rambam leniently, that she need not leave. According to this, one must say that a dispute is not comparable to an unresolved question, because this doubt is somewhat like a lack of knowledge. Below I wrote more about this.
And likewise wherever there is a dispute. See responsum Mishnat Binyamin siman 44 and siman 105, who wrote in the name of responsum Maharam Alashkar siman 36 that he holds that in a dispute we rule leniently. Chelkat Mechokek challenged him and explained the words of Maharam Alashkar differently, and so it is in responsum Avodat HaGershuni. However, there in responsum Maharam Alashkar, folio 43b, it appears like the words of Mishnat Binyamin. The Taz brought in the name of the new responsa of Maharam and responsum Maharitatz siman 188 that they also wrote leniently, and so ruled the Taz.
But if the gentiles ask. I have already written that the Beit Yosef infers from the Ran's responsum that he disagrees with this law, and according to what I wrote this is necessarily so. At first glance it is difficult: nearby the Rema writes that in a dispute we follow the stringent view, so how did he rule here like Terumat HaDeshen and not like the Ran? It seems to me that he holds that the Ran does not disagree with Terumat HaDeshen. The fact that the Ran did not permit the case in the responsum cited by the Beit Yosef, where gentiles reported before a judge in response to the judge's question, was not because he holds that when a gentile asks another gentile it is not speech in the course of conversation. Rather, the judge's question is different, for he asks him to speak as testimony. Still, the fact that the Ran did not permit on the basis of the emissary's question to the apostate's widow proves that he holds that when a gentile asks another gentile it is not considered speech in the course of conversation. One cannot answer, as the Chelkat Mechokek does, that there we fear perhaps the gentile asked in the name of a Jew, for I have already shown that we do not suspect any concern that is not compelled and for which there are no grounds. Nevertheless, in this matter one may be lenient, for I have already written that according to several authorities we follow the lenient view in a dispute.
And he stated compelling estimates. See responsum Mishnat Binyamin siman 68: sometimes we permit even ab initio if it is evident that he spoke truthfully, such as a murderer who confessed without torture that he murdered this person; it is evident that he spoke truthfully, for otherwise why would he obligate himself for nothing? Granted, what he said under torture can be explained as said because of suffering. In responsum Shach part 1 siman 56 he was stringent in this, and Rabbi Mahari Betzalel also ruled stringently and disagreed with Maharik, root 121, who was lenient in this. This is the language of the Taz: if there is doubt whether the gentile spoke innocently or by way of a question, and there are compelling estimates, we should follow the lenient path and say that he was speaking innocently, even ab initio. So it appears to me. End quote.
