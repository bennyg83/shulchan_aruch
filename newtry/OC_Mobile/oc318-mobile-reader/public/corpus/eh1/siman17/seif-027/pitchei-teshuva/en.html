Who is now her mother-in-law. See Baer Heitev and Machatzis HaShekel, s.k. 18, 19, and 20, who argues at length to uphold Chelkas Mechokek both regarding the first law, where she is not fit for yibbum, etc., and regarding the second law, that in countries where yibbum is not practiced, etc. He concludes accordingly as a matter of law, unlike Beis Shmuel, Responsa Avodas HaGershuni, siman 67, and Tzemach Tzedek, siman 88. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 86, also upholds Chelkas Mechokek. See further Responsa Bris Avraham, simanim 39 through 56, and Responsa Chemdas Shlomo, simanim 9 and 10. At the end of his discussion he cites Tzemach Tzedek, siman 88, also cited by Beis Shmuel below in siman 158, that even according to the Rif, Rambam, and Shulchan Aruch, who hold that a yevamah may testify for her only when she testifies that this woman's husband, who is her son's brother, died, if this woman's husband already died and she is bound to the witness's son for yibbum, the mother may not testify that her son, the yavam, died. Everyone agrees that she is not believed in that case. Accordingly, even nowadays she may not testify in such a case. Responsa Avodas HaGershuni, siman 67, writes that the law of a future mother-in-law teaches the same rule and reasoning regarding a future stepdaughter, namely the daughter of her husband's brother. She may not testify for her, because she anticipates that perhaps the husband will die and the woman will fall before her father for yibbum, consume her mother's property, and become hated by her. Thus she intends now to ruin her. Just as those who are stringent regarding a future mother-in-law must include her within the category of mother-in-law so that the count of five women remains exact, so too a yevamah's daughter is included within the category of stepdaughter. Korban Nesanel on this sugya, letter 21, states this plainly, and it is also explicit in Beis Shmuel below, siman 158, seif 4, in the name of Tzemach Tzedek. The same would apply to the daughter of a future mother-in-law, meaning the daughter of the yavam's mother, who is included within the category of the daughter of her mother-in-law. Kovetz Edus of Maharach Shach, folio 33b, leaves unresolved, according to those who are stringent regarding a future mother-in-law, the law of a future mother-in-law's daughter and a future stepdaughter. Beit Meir leans toward leniency and concludes that the silence of the early authorities is sufficient grounds for leniency in a case of agunah, especially nowadays when yibbum is not practiced. See there and also siman 158. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, at the end of siman 64, raises a further uncertainty. A mother-in-law may not testify because she hates her daughter-in-law, saying, 'This woman will consume all my labor.' The same concern applies to the daughter of the mother-in-law and the daughter of the father-in-law, either because their mother hates her and they therefore hate her too, or because she consumes the produce of their father and mother. What if the son married after his father's death and inherited nothing from him? Perhaps the mother-in-law may then testify for her daughter-in-law, since the concern that she will consume all her labor does not apply. If the marriage occurred during the father's lifetime, even if he later died and the son inherited nothing, perhaps the hatred had already become fixed in the mother-in-law's heart because during the father-in-law's lifetime she worried that the daughter-in-law would consume all her labor, and although he was poor he might have become wealthy. But if the son married only after his father's death, and the father died poor, there is no cause for hatred, so why should she not testify? The same question applies to the mother-in-law's daughter where her brother married this woman only after both parents died and inherited nothing from them. Perhaps the Sages did not distinguish within these categories of women. That reasoning may suffice for leniency, but not necessarily for stringency. See there.
