She does not leave. And she may marry another man who does not know whether her husband died, because once they permitted her on the basis of one witness, there is no doubt at all. The reason we require here specifically that they permitted her, while below in siman 178 regarding sotah the Tur ruled that even if they did not issue the ruling based on the witness, nevertheless he is like two, is answered by Tosafot in Yevamot and Ketubot: sotah is different, because there one witness is believed by Torah law, so the witness is considered like two even if the court did not rule based on him. But in a matter where he is not believed by Torah law, but only rabbinically, he is not made like two unless the court ruled based on him.
Because of malicious talk. So it is in the Gemara, chapter 2 of Ketubot. But the Rambam holds that according to Rava's resolution there, this was rejected; see Kesef Mishneh and Bedek HaBayit. Tosafot there explains that even if they permitted her, there is still a full prohibition against marrying, but the permission helps so that if she married she need not leave. According to this, it appears that if they did not permit her, and the witnesses came one after another and she married, she must leave; otherwise why was the permission taught in the Mishnah? It is forced to say that the Mishnah teaches only that ab initio she may not marry even after permission. According to this one must explain what Tosafot wrote afterward, s.v. one witness, that when they came one after another she need not leave, which implies even if they did not permit her; that follows the answer of Ri. See Chiddushei Halakhot, for according to Ri's answer, after they permitted her there is no prohibition, only malicious talk. So also wrote the Rosh and the Tur, that there is no prohibition after they permitted her.

She need not leave. But she may not marry ab initio. Although one witness testifies that he died and she says, "I am certain he died," nevertheless she may not marry; but if she did marry, she need not leave. Even though there is suspicion that he testified in order to marry her, she need not leave because of this suspicion, as stated in siman 12. This is the Rambam's view. The Ramban, Rashba, and the Rosh in a responsum hold that she must leave. The Ran raises a difficulty from the sugya in the second chapter of Yevamot, where it says that if one testifies about a man that he died or was killed, she may not marry. Rabbenu Tam answers that the sugya there speaks where there are three testimonies saying that he died, so there are two witnesses apart from the man who would marry her; so too writes the Rashba. It follows that they hold that in Yevamot she would otherwise have to leave, for if not, there would be no difficulty. According to the Rambam, who holds there that she need not leave, it is unnecessary to establish the case as involving three testimonies; even if there is only one witness she need not leave. The Bach infers in siman 12 that the Rosh holds that where a witness testified that her husband died and she married him, she need not leave because of suspicion. This is incorrect, for the Rosh in Responsa, rule 32, siman 4, cited by the Beit Yosef in this siman, writes that where one witness says he died and one says he did not die, and she married the permitting witness, she must leave, because we set one against the other and the husband remains presumed alive. If the Rosh held that she must leave because of suspicion, she would have to leave even without the contradicting witness. Rather, he certainly holds that if one witness comes and testifies that her husband died and she married him, she need not leave; only when there is a witness contradicting him does he hold that she must leave.

But if she too says that he died. And another woman also says that he died, and she married or they permitted her, and then two women came and said that he did not die, we say that the two women who came afterward cannot remove her. Although with two against two, where they are valid witnesses, we hold that she must leave unless she married one of her witnesses, nevertheless with two against two where they are women or disqualified witnesses, she need not leave. This is specifically when she already married, or if they had already permitted her, for then the first two are treated like two valid witnesses and she is permitted to marry another.
But the husband who married her is not believed. In Nimukei Yosef it says that the husband is not believed to testify about himself, "I am he." It appears that Rema holds that the woman is believed even if she has already remarried and said nothing before the marriage, and that if another woman says that he died, she too is believed. But the husband who married the woman is not believed after the marriage. See the sugya at the beginning of HaIshah Rabbah, where it is clear that she is believed even after marriage. There the case is that she married on the testimony of a woman, and afterward one man came and said that he was her husband, and two women also said that he was her husband, while she and the other woman said, "This is not he." This is treated as two against two. Although she had already married, she can still say, "This is not he." One should not say that there the reason is that, if he truly were the husband, she would not have the audacity to deny him, for we hold that after betrothal and after marriage she does have such audacity. According to Rashi there, it seems that even if she says "my husband died," she is believed more than many disqualified witnesses, because a person is believed about himself in matters of prohibition more than one hundred disqualified witnesses. The reason she nevertheless needs another woman there to say, "This is not he," is that in that case she does not know whether her husband died; she only says, "This is not he." Therefore another woman is needed to say that her husband died. The Rambam, Ramban, and Rashba, however, do not hold this way; see the Maggid Mishneh there.

And if she married one of those who testified, etc. The wording is surprising, for here there is only one witness, and the Derishah already asked this about the Tur. However, it appears that in the Tur one can explain it, because there he wrote two cases: either one valid witness said he died and two valid witnesses came and said he did not die, or one relative said he died and two relatives said he did not die. She must leave, even though she married one of those who testified; this means one of those cases. But here in the Shulchan Aruch it is difficult: how could he write that she married one of those who testified, since he speaks where there is only one witness? Perhaps one can answer that when he wrote one valid witness, this includes even two brothers who testify that he died, for they count as one valid witness; then the wording, that she married one of those who testified, is understandable. There is another difficulty according to those poskim who hold that where one witness is contradicted, even if she married she must leave; if so, even in such a case she must leave. This language, if she married one of those who testified, is the Rambam's language, following his own view that even with one witness contradicted, if she married she need not leave. But according to the other poskim, the difficulty remains as above.
And a gentile speaking innocently. His law is like that of other disqualified witnesses. In all these laws, a woman, a gentile speaking innocently, and testimony from the mouth of another witness are equivalent, both leniently and stringently. See Kuntres Agunot siman 157, and see responsum Maharshdam and responsum Maharam Lublin siman 133.
