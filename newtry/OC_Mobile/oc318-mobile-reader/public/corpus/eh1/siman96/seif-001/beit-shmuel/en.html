Only with an oath. Although we hold there is a presumption that a person does not pay within the time, nevertheless with a woman who has a ketubah by enactment of Chazal we are more concerned that perhaps he gave her bundles for payment. So wrote Tosafot in Perek HaSholeach and at the beginning of Bava Batra, and the Rosh there. One should not ask: if so, why does she collect with an oath, and how is this different from support for his daughters, which is also by enactment of Chazal and is not collected from purchasers even with an oath, where the concern is also for bundles? Tosafot answer that with daughters there is a greater concern, because he wants them to have abundance. It appears that nichsei melog and nichsei tzon barzel, to which the enactment of Chazal does not apply in the same way, do not create concern for bundles, and therefore no oath should be needed even where the property is no longer extant. According to this, what the Rambam and Tur wrote that she inherits nichsei melog and nichsei tzon barzel can refer even when they are not extant, and so wrote Responsa Rashdam, siman 134. But the Maggid Mishneh and Beit Yosef explained that they speak where the property is extant. In practice one can say they wrote well, because Tosafot and the Rosh gave another answer there: we are concerned that close to death he gave her bundles, not before the time. According to this, there is concern even by nichsei melog and nichsei tzon barzel, and therefore we require specifically that they be extant. Although according to this answer there is no concern where he died suddenly, as he would not have given bundles before death, in siman 90 I wrote that this is disputed. We are concerned in sudden death according to the first answer, and in nichsei melog and nichsei tzon barzel that are not extant according to the second answer, so we do not extract from orphans. Still, according to both answers, if he died suddenly she collects nichsei melog and nichsei tzon barzel even when not extant, whichever way you look at it, unless there is concern that she herself took bundles before his death. If so, even nichsei melog and nichsei tzon barzel cannot be collected. This depends on what is written below: if the oath that she did not take bundles herself must be sworn without relying on gilgul of the oath that her husband gave her bundles, then even if he died suddenly and there is no concern that he gave her bundles, there remains concern that she took them herself. But if that oath applies only through gilgul, then there is no concern where he died suddenly. See siman 90. Nowadays, when everything is included in one sum and they write that he owes her such-and-such amount, everyone may agree that she needs an oath even if all is extant, since her debt is for a sum of money. However, Responsa Ralbach and Responsa Mahari Beit HaLevi do not imply this. See Responsa Maharshal, siman 49, where it is explained whether a ketubah is collected from a loan that her husband lent before the marriage.

But if she seized, etc. There are three kinds of seizure. First, if the woman seized during her lifetime and her heirs are now in possession, we do not say that a person cannot bequeath an oath to his sons. Second, if the woman seized and the heirs are not in possession, but the property is deposited with the beit din, Terumat HaDeshen did not decide the matter in practice; he wrote that since the money deposited with beit din is held for both, it would appear that the woman’s heirs acquired it, for they do not need to extract, and with a slight reason we reject the rule that one cannot bequeath an oath. At least it would appear they divide. Third, if the heirs seized after death, this is found in several places in the Mordechai. It is explained there that if there are no witnesses and no seeing of the seizure, according to all it helps. If there are witnesses who saw it, the decisors dispute it. The Ran and Ra'avyah hold that even then seizure helps, because we hold that a judge who acted like Rabbi Elazar, his act is not reversed, and we hold that a person may take the law into his own hands. Therefore even seizure with witnesses helps, since he acts for himself like Rabbi Elazar, who says a person bequeaths an oath to his sons. So it also implies in Choshen Mishpat, siman 108, that we hold like the Ran, and so wrote Ba'al HaTerumot, Gedulei Terumah, and the Taz. See Responsa Ben Sasson, Responsa Maharit, Responsa Maharam Mintz, and Responsa Shevut Yaakov. When the heirs are in possession they need no oath at all. If beit din gave her property for the ketubah and she had not sworn on the ketubah and died, the heirs acquired according to everyone; this follows from the law nearby, if she sold without an oath. Responsa Shevut Yaakov writes that when there are creditors of the father, the heirs can say: we do not want our mother’s oath, for Chazal instituted the oath for the heirs’ benefit, and we do not want Chazal’s enactment; they then inherit the ketubah and need not pay the father’s creditors from the ketubah money.

But if she was divorced. The reason is that specifically an oath against orphans is subject to the rule that a person cannot bequeath or sell an oath. But an oath that is not against orphans, such as with a divorcee, a woman whose ketubah was impaired, and the like, may be bequeathed and sold. However, if he died before she collected the ketubah, her oath then became against the orphans and she cannot bequeath it. If the husband claimed at the time of divorce that he had already paid the ketubah before giving the get, Responsa Rashba in the name of the Rif and Ba'al HaTerumot writes that this is not a valid claim, because a person does not pay before the time. According to Tosafot, cited above, there is concern that perhaps he gave her bundles before the time; but the Rif holds that he gives bundles only close to death. Therefore he rules that the claim is not valid. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 78, where there is a dispute whether, when the borrower claims he paid within the time, the lender must swear. It seems from here that the Rif holds he need not swear, and the Rashba agrees, as does Ba'al HaTerumot and Ri cited by the Tur. The Bach rules there that the lender need not swear before payment; the borrower must pay, and afterward the lender must swear hesset. But from these decisors it implies he need not swear even after payment, since they wrote that it is not a claim. At first glance the main law seems like these decisors, for an oath of hesset is only a rabbinic enactment, and in a dispute we leave the law as it stands. One might ask from Choshen Mishpat, where there is a dispute whether he can make the lender swear despite the presumption that one does not pay within the time, while in siman 68 the Tur and Rambam rule that if a husband says he did not find virginity he need not swear, because of the presumption that a person does not trouble himself with a feast and ruin it. One must say these presumptions are not equal. It also implies from the Rif’s responsum that if the husband claims after the get that he gave bundles before the get, he cannot make her swear, even though if he said he paid after the get he could make her swear; we do not say a migo to impose an oath.
(ט) If he died after her. But if he died after the get and afterward she died, her heirs receive nothing, for immediately after his death she needed an oath against the orphans, and one cannot bequeath an oath to one's heirs. So too it is stated in the above responsum of Rashba. In all these laws where one cannot bequeath an oath to one's heirs, they do not tear the ketubah. Shas; Choshen Mishpat siman 108.
