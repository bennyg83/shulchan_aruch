That she waived to the creditor. The Rosh wrote in a responsum, klal 36, cited by Beit Yosef, that since she does not inherit her husband and has only a debt against him, before she collected the ketubah she has not acquired the property, and she has no power to give or waive it. If her husband had a loan document and she returned it to the borrower, saying, "I receive this toward my ketubah," perhaps this can be compared to selling movables. But oral waiver certainly is ineffective. With a written waiver it might be compared to a gift of movables, though one can distinguish. See Chelkat Mechokek. If he left no other property and she can collect only from this debt, then that debt is liened to her under Rabbi Natan’s law; her waiver is effective even though she has not yet sworn, because the debt belongs to her by biblical law, and Chazal merely instituted an oath. Nevertheless, this is not conclusive: is the lien of Rabbi Natan better than her lien on her husband’s property, all of which is liened to her, and most decisors hold that lien is biblical? The Yam Shel Shlomo writes that the Rosh also holds lien is biblical, and nevertheless she cannot waive.
