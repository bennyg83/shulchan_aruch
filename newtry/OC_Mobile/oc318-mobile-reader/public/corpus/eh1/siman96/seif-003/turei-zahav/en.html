But if she seized, etc. To clarify this law, with the help of Heaven, we must copy what is found in the Mordechai, for from there we learn it. In Perek Kol HaNishba'in the Mordechai cites Sefer HaMitzvot: if a widow died before swearing, her heirs inherit nothing from her ketubah, because she has nothing until she swears, and she cannot bequeath an oath to her heirs. Some distinguish and say this is only where they seek to extract from the husband’s heirs when those heirs are in possession; but if the widow was in possession of the husband’s assets and died before swearing, then certainly we say a person bequeaths an oath to his sons so that the widow’s heirs can retain the money. He brings proof from the case of a business document found against orphans, where we plead for the orphans that their father returned it with a migo of unavoidable loss, even though the father himself would have been believed only with an oath. Thus a person can bequeath an oath to his sons. This is one place in the Mordechai where seizure helps. A second place, in another version of Perek Kol HaNishba'in, says the opposite: see Perek Chezkas HaBatim after the case of Rabbi Abba’s ingot, where he explained that a person cannot bequeath an oath to his sons even where they seized. A third place, in Perek Chezkas HaBatim, in a responsum to Maharam about the widow Serach bat Asher’s claim against Reuven over land, movables, and books of her grandfather Yaakov, also implies that seizure did not help where the property remained in the heirs’ possession and her seizure was compared to an artisan’s holding. A fourth place, also in Perek Chezkas HaBatim, concerns Reuven who married Leah, had a son Chanoch, died, and Leah married Shimon without swearing and brought Reuven’s books into that marriage. When Leah died and Chanoch claimed from Shimon, Maharam answered that Chanoch must bring proof to extract from Shimon what he holds, because Leah herself could have retained it with an oath to collect the ketubah, and we also plead for her heirs and those who come through her, like Shimon. Although Leah herself would have needed an oath, Shimon retains what is in his hand without an oath, for we do not impose an oath on him on a doubtful claim, and even the heirs’ oath is not needed because that oath is only when they collect, not to exempt themselves. This proves seizure helps for heirs and those who come through her. A fifth place in the Mordechai, Perek HaKotev, reports an incident where a widow seized movables and books for her ketubah. Several authorities ruled that if she sold or gave them before swearing, the heirs can take them back, whether land or movables, even if she seized during life, unless they claimed them from her in her lifetime and she refused to return them. But Ra'avyah ruled in practice that if she sold or gave, it stands, because the matter depends on judicial discretion and many Geonim ruled like Rabbi Elazar, not like Rav and Shmuel. Rav Hai Gaon likewise had a tradition that they divide. Rabbenu Simcha brought proof that if, according to Beit Shammai, a shtar standing for collection is considered collected, all the more actual seizure helps according to Beit Hillel. Ra'avyah answered that actual seizure is worse according to Beit Hillel because it was seized in doubt, perhaps bundles had been given. Maharam wrote that although Ra'avyah held the widow’s gift stands, the Geonim who ruled that the gift before the oath is invalid are primary, because the property remains in the possession of the orphans, and even if she later swore, at the time of gift she had no power. From all these passages the law must be weighed. The key distinction is whether the widow or those coming through her were already in possession, whether the possession was before or after death, whether there were witnesses to the seizure, and whether they are extracting or merely retaining. Where the heirs or those coming through the widow are in possession and seek only to retain, there is strong basis that seizure helps; where they seek to extract from the husband’s heirs, the rule that one does not bequeath an oath remains a major obstacle. This is the root of the dispute brought by the decisors.
