But if he became deranged between writing and delivery and then recovered, or after part of the get was written he became deranged and they did not write until he recovered, there is no concern. So ruled Beit Yosef. However, Tosafot imply that even in such a case there is concern, and Darkei Moshe ruled that it is proper to be concerned for them; bedieved, however, one may be lenient. If they did not examine a deathly ill person who was speaking at the time of writing and delivery, Beit Yosef wrote that perhaps it is valid. What Darkei Moshe wrote that the Rosh in Seder HaGet invalidates is not so; there he speaks where it was known he was not mentally sound. It implies that even if they did not examine him when he commanded them to write, the law is the same. If he was not sound at the time of writing, it depends on the dispute above: according to the Rambam it is only rabbinic, and according to the Tur and Shiltei Gibborim it is biblical. If he was not sound at the time of delivery, perhaps even the Tur agrees. If he became mute in his illness, the matter depends on a dispute. The Yerushalmi explains that the Mishnah at the beginning of Perek Mi SheAchazo, which says that one who became mute requires three examinations, speaks specifically where he became mute while healthy. But if he became mute because of illness, he does not require three examinations, since we attribute his muteness to weakness. Several decisors hold this way, including the Itur. According to him, even one who became mute while healthy is not suspected of insanity, because if there were such a concern, examination would not help, since perhaps he is sometimes sound and sometimes insane. Rather, the examination is to determine his form of communication. Thus if he had already commanded them to write and then became mute, even while healthy, no examination is needed. This is unlike Beit Yosef’s presentation of the Itur. According to other decisors, however, one who became mute raises concern that he became insane and requires examination, specifically if he became mute while healthy. If he became mute from illness, there is no such concern. But Rashi and the Ran explain the Mishnah as speaking where he became mute from illness, and even then we are concerned that he became insane; all the more if he became mute while healthy. According to this approach, if he commanded while speaking and then became mute, and they wrote without examination, one should not be lenient. Beit Yosef suggests that in a case of illness, where examination is impossible, all may agree bedieved that it is valid, but this is not correct according to that approach. Perhaps according to the Rambam, where the concern is only rabbinic, there is room to be lenient, but this is not certain. According to the Tur, if he was not sound at the time of writing the get is biblically void, and so ruled Shiltei Gibborim; therefore one should not be lenient.
