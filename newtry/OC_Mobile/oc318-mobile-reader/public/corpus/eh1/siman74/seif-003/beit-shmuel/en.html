She vowed not to adorn herself. See Yoreh Deah, siman 234: according to everyone the husband can annul it, either as a vow of personal affliction or as a matter between him and her. Therefore, when he did not annul it, we say he hates her and he must divorce her immediately, even if she vowed never to adorn herself and she will remain forbidden to adorn herself even after divorce. According to this, these two laws are properly grouped together: whether she made it dependent on marital relations or vowed generally, she leaves immediately because he hates her. The Perishah, however, writes that we say he hates her only when she made it dependent on marital relations. Therefore, according to him, the two laws are not identical. If she made it dependent on marital relations, she leaves immediately even if she vowed forever and remains forbidden after the get; but if she did not make it dependent on marital relations, we do not say he hates her, and she leaves only where she will be permitted to adorn herself after the get. The Taz agrees that we say he hates her even where it was not dependent on marital relations, while the Shach follows the Perishah. The Rambam’s language about vows from fruit, where the choice is in his hand, is difficult according to these approaches, and the Chelkat Mechokek answers that it teaches he must pay the ketubah even though she is a vow-taking woman, because he failed to annul her vow. The matter remains difficult in parts, as explained there.
