It is not collected. At the end of Perek Yesh Nochalin this is a question: where she borrowed before marriage by oral loan, do we collect from the husband? If his law is like an heir, we collect; if like a purchaser, we do not. The conclusion is that sometimes the husband’s law is like an heir and sometimes like a purchaser: where there is no loss to another, his law is like a purchaser; and even where another would lose, if that person caused his own loss, his law is like a purchaser. But if another would lose and did not cause his own loss, his law is like an heir. Several decisors hold that here his law is like an heir because of the lender’s loss. The Rif, Rambam, and Rosh hold the question was not resolved, for perhaps the lender harmed himself by lending orally to a woman who stood to be married. The Mechaber ruled that it is not collected from the husband, meaning one cannot extract from him; but if the lender seized, we do not remove it from him. See Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 17. All this concerns whether the husband must pay from what she brought him; from his own property, according to everyone, he need not pay, as written in Tosafot, Perek HaMekabel, the Mordechai there, and Responsa Maharik, shoresh 18, unlike Rabbeinu Baruch cited by the Mordechai. The Rashba explains this sugya as speaking even while she is alive, implying that even in her lifetime his law is like a purchaser. The Mechaber’s language also implies this. The Sema compares assessment and repayment, and the matter is discussed in Choshen Mishpat, simanim 103 and 112. In practice, with nichsei tzon barzel his law is like a purchaser even in her lifetime, and seizure by the lender does not help; with nichsei melog, seizure can help. If the lender seized the fruits of the land, it does not help, because the fruits belong to the husband in exchange for redemption. This also shows that the language "brought in" applies to nichsei melog, as the Rashbam and Rashba write in this law.
However, if the money is intact. So wrote the Rambam at the end of chapter 26 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh, and so wrote the Maggid Mishneh there in the name of Rabbenu Chananel and Rav Hai, even regarding a loan made after marriage. But the Maggid Mishneh wrote that this requires study, because even where the money is intact, it is as though it was spent, since a loan is given to be spent. So wrote the Nimukei Yosef, that even if the money is intact he need not pay. What he wrote afterward, that one must return intact money or an item that came from it, refers to a case where she sold nichsei melog; the Chelkat Mechokek was not precise and did not see what the Nimukei Yosef wrote at the beginning of the sugya.
The law is with him. As written in Choshen Mishpat, siman 73: if the borrower wastes his assets or wants to go overseas, the lender can restrain him even within the time.
He collects from property. Whether from nichsei melog or from nichsei tzon barzel.
Her husband is obligated to pay. Terumat HaDeshen compares this to Perek HaMekabel, where a person brings a rich person’s korban for his wife. There it means he must bring it from his own funds; therefore he must pay a fine on her behalf from his own funds even if she brought him nothing, so that she have atonement, like a korban. This matter is disputed: according to Tosafot, Mordechai, and Hagahot Maimoniyot, he must bring her korban even if the obligation began before marriage; according to Rashi, only if she became obligated while under him. The Chelkat Mechokek writes that this applies only if she transgressed inadvertently, but if intentionally, all the more if rebelliously, he need not pay for her, lest she impose obligations on him every day. It is surprising that Responsa Maharam Mintz explained the sugya in Perek HaMekabel as a korban for a vow, against Rashi and Tosafot. Terumat HaDeshen himself concluded that it requires study whether the case resembles the proof, and it is surprising that the Rama ruled that the husband must pay. The Taz therefore writes that in his view one should not extract money from the husband. What that responsum writes about a woman who conducts business in the house and vows charity, that he must pay for her, means that although he can prevent her for the future, he must pay what she already vowed.
