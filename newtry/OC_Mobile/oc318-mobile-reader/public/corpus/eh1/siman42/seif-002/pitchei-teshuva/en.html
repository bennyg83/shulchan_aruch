See Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 112, regarding one who wants to betray his betrothed, and the community leaders have the ability to force him through the city governor to enter the chuppah, whether there is concern here of kiddushin against his will. The Rav who asked inclined to permit, because the Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel challenged the Rama’s words in Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 205, that if one was coerced to buy he does not acquire. Thus the only remaining doubt is because they act improperly, whereas here, on the contrary, he is the one acting improperly, and it returns to the law of other coerced acquisitions, which are effective, unlike the Rama. He answered that in practice he also agrees to coerce him, and many such acts were already done. Although in his novellae to Bava Batra 48b he supported the Rama’s words in the name of the Ba'al HaItur, that one who is forced to take does not acquire, nevertheless this case is different. It is not comparable to one forced to take, because regarding a korban it is clear in Bava Batra that we compel him until he says, "I want," since he wants atonement. Tosafot there explain that whenever one is obligated to do the act, it is like a sale. Here too, since he obligated himself through cherem and penalty, and even if he pays the penalty he is not released from the cherem, and certainly in the case where he cannot pay, he plainly needs atonement, and the kiddushin are his atonement. Thus it is like a korban and everyone agrees that he may be coerced. Still, according to this it should be no better than a korban itself, where he must at least say, "I want." In my humble opinion this is not a concern, as the Maggid Mishneh writes at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot Ishut: whenever he performs the act, it stands, meaning that he need not specifically say "I want"; rather, as long as he performs the act we say he did it willingly. Chazal excluded only that one does not seize collateral for an olah as one does for a chatat or asham, but he must give or say "I want to give." So too, when one is pressured to betroth a woman, there is no greater act than saying, "You are hereby betrothed to me," and we attribute this to his will. This also removes the Lechem Mishneh’s astonishment at the Maggid Mishneh there, and so the Migdal Oz writes explicitly. Therefore even the Ra'avad may disagree only where the Rambam’s language is general, even where he did not say "you are hereby betrothed to me," but they were merely engaged in that matter. But when he performs an act showing "I want," it is clearly effective. Therefore he agrees that one may coerce someone to kiddushin in this case, and there is no concern. If the concern is that no peace will result between them, the truth is certainly with him, and he told the bearer that in his opinion it is better to separate those attached. End quote. See there.
