(א) Even if both of them admit the matter. It appears clear that even if they say they intended kiddushin, it does not help, since there were no witnesses. So wrote Beit Yosef in the name of Rashba in chapter HaZorek: even one witness in the morning and one in the evening do not help; there is no kiddushin here, for even if he had relations with her for the sake of kiddushin, his kiddushin is not kiddushin, since one who betroths before one witness is not treated as having effected kiddushin. Do not object from seif 3, where she says "I intended only play," implying that if she said "I intended kiddushin" she would be betrothed even though she did not know of the witnesses. There, at least witnesses exist, only she did not know of them; here, where there truly is no testimony, it does not help even if they intended kiddushin.
(ב) If both admit. It appears that it is enough that they admit they intended kiddushin, and this teaches that we do not say there is no matter of ervah with fewer than two witnesses in this sense. But if they say they intended only joking, there is no reason to be stringent where there is only one witness, for that is like contradicting the witness entirely. This is also clear from the responsum of Rashbatz cited by Beit Yosef, who concludes that where there is contradiction between them, there is no concern with one witness, as Rashba wrote regarding one who claims "I was joking with you." My teacher and father-in-law wrote likewise.
Words of the glossator: There in the above seif, "but if one contradicts the witness," etc. Bach distinguishes in a claim of joking between one witness and two witnesses: with one witness he is believed to say he was joking, but not with two witnesses. Taz agreed. Chelkat Mechokek wrote that his words are not compelling for leniency, for just as with two witnesses the claim of joking is nothing, since matters in the heart are not matters, so too with one witness; what does it help that she has a miggo? The reason is not merely belief, but that words in the heart are not words, and even if we know they intended to joke, we are concerned with what was spoken. Beit Shmuel also objected, comparing it to one who says "I grabbed it, but I grabbed what was mine," who is not believed by a miggo that he could have denied. In my humble opinion, these rabbis were not precise here. The stringent view is Semag's, because since the halachah was not decided, he is concerned for Rav Pappa's view that we do suspect kiddushin before one witness. Rashbatz explains that Semag is stringent specifically where both admit, but not where there is contradiction. The Gemara's discussion of deriving "matter, matter" from monetary law implies that according to the view that one witness creates concern, that derivation is not accepted; still, it does not follow that one witness is believed against denial. Therefore Rashbatz's distinction remains sound.
