If she contradicts the witness, there is no concern. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 60, who concludes that this is the main law: one witness who is contradicted is certainly nothing. Although Responsa Maharam Padua, siman 32, was inclined to be stringent even in this, Chelkat Mechokek, s.k. 4, wrote that Maharam Padua himself retracted in siman 37. Although Chacham Tzvi, siman 115, wanted to say he had not retracted, one who sees Maharam Padua’s words in siman 37 will see that it was simple to him that one contradicted witness is nothing. The Gemara also proves this explicitly, that one need not be concerned for one witness who is contradicted, even according to the view that one is concerned for kiddushin in the presence of one witness. See there at length, and see Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, siman 76, Shivat Tzion, siman 77, and Sha'ar HaMelech, chapter 9 of Hilchot Ishut, law 51. What Ba'er Heitev brings, that one distinguishes, etc., until the Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel disagree with him, see Responsa Bach, siman 37, Beit Meir, and Tumim, siman 61, s.k. 2. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 104, who writes that the Beit Shmuel’s explanation of the Rashba, that if she says "I was joking" it is considered contradiction only where she says she explicitly said so at the time of kiddushin, is difficult in the Rashba’s language. From the Tashbetz it is explicit that the Rashba speaks even where she claims joking in intent, not explicitly; see Chatam Sofer, siman 99, who cites Tashbetz, part 1, siman 70. So too writes Beit Meir. Regarding the Chelkat Mechokek’s main question on the Bach, that what difference is there between one witness and two witnesses regarding a claim of joking, since unspoken thoughts are not legally effective, it appears in my humble opinion that when the man who betroths claims he intended joking, this is indeed unspoken intent and is not effective. But when the woman claims joking, it is effective. The reason is that unspoken intent is ineffective only where the act itself does not point to the inner intent. Here, where a woman is betrothed before one witness, the logic leans that she intended joking, for what foolish woman would betroth herself to a man and forbid herself to everyone when the husband could deny the witness and be exempt, while she would be chained forever? Therefore the circumstances themselves indicate joking, and it is no longer merely unspoken thought. But for the man there is no such indication, because if she denies it he can marry another or divorce her. Thus his claim of joking is not effective. He also discusses a case of young people on Shabbat where one gave a ring to a girl and said kiddushin words. Since she denied hearing the kiddushin and said she thought it was play, and because there was only one witness who was contradicted, along with the fact that it occurred on Shabbat and in a playful setting, he permitted the man to her relatives. In siman 99 he discusses another one-witness case where the word "li" was not heard and concludes there was no concern of kiddushin. See what I wrote above, siman 27, s.k. 14.
