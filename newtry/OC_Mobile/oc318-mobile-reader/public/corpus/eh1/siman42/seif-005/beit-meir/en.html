If he betrothed before witnesses, etc., she is betrothed. This derives from Kiddushin 43, where the school of Rabbi Sheila said: an agent cannot become a witness. What is the reason? If one says it is because he did not say to him, be a witness for me, then if he betrothed before two witnesses but did not say, you are my witnesses, would that too not be kiddushin? One could still say that although he need not say you are my witnesses, nevertheless the witnesses must at least intend on their own to testify; but if they came merely to see, they are not witnesses and she is not betrothed. Even so, the Gemara asks because the statement that an agent cannot become a witness was taught absolutely. Why not distinguish: where they intended on their own, in that case even an agent becomes a witness? Since the poskim do not require designating witnesses even ab initio, and did not worry that perhaps those who see will not intend to testify, so that he would be betrothing without witnesses, it follows that they hold that even a witness who merely sees becomes a witness. This is also explicit in Shulchan Aruch, Choshen Mishpat siman 36 seif 1, and it is from the Rambam. In my humble opinion, the Rambam's words are founded on his method in explaining the sugya in Makkot 6, where we ask all of them, as explained there in the Shach. It implies that the reason we need the inquiry whether they came merely to see is because one of them was found to be a relative; but with valid witnesses this is unnecessary. Even according to Rashi, whose implication is that we ask only the relative, the same is heard; otherwise Rava's question would make no sense, for this question would be necessary in every testimony. So too it appears from Responsa Rashba siman 1073, who wrote: witnesses need not be specially designated; anyone who saw or knew and testified, we accept from them, for testimony is established by seeing without knowing and by knowing without seeing, as with three who entered to visit a sick person, who may write if they wish, even though they were not designated to testify. This needs no proof, and even regarding kiddushin, which is ineffective without testimony, specially designated witnesses are not required. Since it was obvious to him that they need not be designated, he did not consider distinguishing between witnesses who independently intended to testify and those who came only to see. In my humble opinion this is also compelled from Shevuot 34b, where one said to his fellow: I counted a maneh for you at the side of this pillar, and Rav Nachman said he is established as a denier; Rava said to him, etc. When the witnesses saw that he had urinated, they certainly did not intend any testimony. How then did Rav Nachman think to accept their testimony and make him a denier through them? And why did Rava need to answer as he did? Necessarily there is no distinction. However, in Responsa Penei Moshe part 3 siman 25 he wrote: I am astonished how one can validate witnesses who did not intend to testify, for there is an explicit sugya in Keritot 12b: Abaye said, Rabbi Meir concedes to the Sages that if two said to him, do you know testimony for so-and-so, and he said I do not know, he is exempt, for if he wished he could say, I did not intend for testimony. It is explicit that if he said I did not intend, there is no substance to his testimony. In my humble opinion this is not explicit, for one can explain that he may excuse what he swore, that he did not know, because he did not focus on it and it was for him a matter not placed upon him, and he said not with awareness, as Shevuot rejects above. What Birkat HaZevach wrote against this interpretation does not even begin as a difficulty. Penei Moshe, however, brings support for his reading from Tosafot Yeshanim, s.v. I did not intend, which says: from here Rabbi said, and so ruled in practice, that witnesses who heard the matter from the principals but did not intend to testify and were not brought there as witnesses have no testimony if they later come to testify in bet din. Thus it is explicit that if they were not designated as witnesses and did not intend to testify, there is no substance to their testimony. How then did the Rambam rule that we rely on such witnesses, since they are not capable of giving testimony? Tosafot Yeshanim wrote there that we derive it from the verse and he is a witness, implying one who intends specifically. In truth, the Rosh and Rif did not bring this law. I am astonished that all the later authorities did not bring this, for it is an explicit undisputed sugya and requires study. End quote. According to what I wrote, the Rambam's ruling is compelled from the Talmud in two places. In my humble opinion, even according to Tosafot Yeshanim the halakhic ruling is correct, because in the two places it is compelled from Rava; and even if Abaye disagrees, the halakhah follows Rava against Abaye. Yet since they did not note Abaye's words at all, it seems to me that they did not explain them like Tosafot Yeshanim but as I explained, in order to minimize dispute. Still, so that the ruling of Tosafot Yeshanim not contradict all the poskim, I would press to explain it unlike Penei Moshe's understanding. Since the language says from here, etc., that witnesses who heard, etc., it does not sound like he speaks about an actual event; had that been so, he should have said, and so he ruled regarding witnesses, etc. Since he wrote witnesses who heard, it sounds as though he came to teach a law, and he should have taught it even regarding witnesses who saw an event. Rather, it seems that he ruled this specifically about witnesses by hearing. Perhaps it was in a case where a claim of joking or inducing an admission was relevant; alternatively, one may press to distinguish for some reason between witnesses by hearing, who require intent, and witnesses by seeing. He explains that the case of one who says do you know testimony means that they know it through hearing, so that the simple meaning is that if he did not intend for testimony he is not capable of giving testimony. What Tosafot Yeshanim wrote in the preceding comment, that we derive from and he is a witness that he must intend specifically, requires study in any case, for the verse also says or saw. According to the above one must press that he said it only as a support: and he is a witness refers to testimony by hearing, and or saw refers to an act. However, from the Responsa Rosh cited in Choshen Mishpat siman 51, where he wrote that the one witness who heard the will from outside joins with the other to testify even though they did not designate him, it implies that even with testimony by hearing, wherever joking or inducing an admission is not relevant, there is no difference whether he heard with intent to testify or heard incidentally, since he does not distinguish. The Beit Yosef already brought this in siman 36 as support for the Rambam's ruling. From all this, in my humble opinion, the words of Tosafot Yeshanim do not stand as halakhah unless they concern a case where joking is relevant. See Choshen Mishpat siman 81 seif 9; their words require study. Nevertheless, in my humble opinion it is proper ab initio to designate witnesses for kiddushin as for gittin. Aside from the above, the reason is that relatives usually stand present at kiddushin, and one must be concerned for what Ritva wrote in Kiddushin there in the name of his teacher, that regarding kiddushin there is reason to say that even if the relatives came merely to see, all the testimony is void; see there. His reasoning is exactly opposite the reasoning of Beit Shmuel s.k. 8. I later saw that according to the Rosh's approach there is no need for this, for although Ritva wrote that many disagree with his teacher, he concludes that one should be stringent for his view. Likewise in Makkot there he brings that it is an ancient custom to designate two witnesses for kiddushin, and writes that the reason is concern for Rashi's explanation, which is the first opinion in siman 36 cited above, that by intent alone, even without joining in testimony in bet din, all testimony is void when one of the relatives intended to testify. The Derishah there also concludes that it is proper to do so. The Shach there, s.k. 8, wrote simply that for us nowadays it is certainly valid, because we designate valid witnesses specifically for that purpose. Perhaps he refers to the custom brought by the Rama z"l in siman 31, that under the chuppah we ask the witnesses, etc.; this is like designating them as kiddushin witnesses. In my humble opinion, however, it is more proper to designate them explicitly to intend to testify about the essential kiddushin, and I believe this is the custom in Ashkenaz. What Beit Yosef, Sma, and Shach attribute there, that the Rambam's view follows Rashi and Rashbam and not the Rosh, at first glance requires study, for the Rambam's language, how do they examine the matter? When many witnesses come to bet din, one group says, etc., implies that he is particular also that they come to bet din as one group. The printed text places a verse break before one group and attaches one group says, but in my humble opinion this is a scribal error, for says refers to one of the judges, as explained in the Shulchan Aruch, and this requires study. Gloss: but, etc., until they see the giving. The Beit Shmuel wrote: see the Rama, etc., and in Choshen Mishpat siman 90, etc. With respect, he was not precise here. From there, in my humble opinion, there is proof to the opposite. The Rama did not write that in monetary law a matter is established by knowledge except in the case stated in Shevuot 34, by the admission of the litigant. This is not estimation, nor even near certainty, but actual certainty, because the admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses; in capital cases it is a Scriptural decree that a person is close to himself. In Choshen Mishpat siman 90, the Rama's law is learned from the case of one who was wounded, as stated in the Tur: just as we say regarding one who was wounded that if the matter is one by which he could have wounded himself, he takes without an oath, so too, etc.; this is testimony established by knowledge without sight. Thus he learned the law from the wounded person and based it on the verse or knew. On the contrary, the sugya in Shevuot makes clear that even according to Rav Acha, the case of the biting camel is called knowledge without sight, and we do not rule like him in that, as explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 408, but like the first tanna. In the wounded person and in the Rama's equivalent case, the first tanna agrees. It is therefore proven that even in monetary law we do not judge by knowledge without sight in the simple sense, meaning estimation based on the majority; that is Rav Acha's reasoning, as stated in Perek HaMokher Peirot. Rather, only an assessment like the wounded-person case, which is truly close to certainty, is accepted. In this, monetary law differs from capital law. This is exactly the view of Hagahot Maimoniyot and Rashba, who distinguish between near certainty, where she is doubtfully betrothed, such as where he threw money to her and it reached the airspace of the courtyard and we do not know whether it entered the partitions, where even though it is remotely possible that wind moved it, since it is close to certain she is doubtfully betrothed, and a case where he said he would betroth her and entered with the money in his hand with her into a room, which is not close to certain but only a strong inference like the camel; in such a case we are not concerned. The exception is kiddushin by intercourse, which is also close to certain because of the principle of fire near tow. Darkei Moshe further says that it is impossible to see like a brush in a tube; he means that the Torah's ways are ways of pleasantness, and presumably the Torah did not designate kiddushin by intercourse through literal sight, but through sight joined with knowledge close to certainty. Even Rashba in Responsa siman 1193 wrote only: furthermore, even if the etrog leaves her hand and she admits she received it from him for kiddushin, it does not help because there was no sight, but only something like knowledge. He means that without her admission, the sight of the etrog is only something like knowledge, not clear knowledge like the wounded person, but estimated knowledge: since he said see the etrog that I am giving, and then it emerges from her hand, ordinarily the estimate is that he gave it for kiddushin and she accepted it. Nevertheless, if she had claimed I accepted it as a gift, she would be believed; only through her admission does it become clear knowledge. Her admission does not matter, as he concludes, because with kiddushin it is effective only through witnesses whom she cannot contradict. See there and examine carefully.
