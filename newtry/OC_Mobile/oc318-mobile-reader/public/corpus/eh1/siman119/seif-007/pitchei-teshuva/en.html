Not to divorce. See Beit Shmuel below, s.k. 12, who wrote that if Rabbenu Gershom’s cherem is comparable to an enactment of Chazal, then nowadays even if he transgressed and divorced, she is not divorced. But from the Rama’s gloss here, which says that if he transgressed and divorced her against her will nowadays and she remarried, the man is no longer called a transgressor, it is explicit not like this; she may marry another through this get. This is also clear from the Beit Shmuel himself above, simanim 77 and 79, where he compares the enactment of Rabbenu Gershom to the prohibition of divorcing a first wife without cause; if one transgressed and divorced a first wife, she is divorced, and so too here. Beit Meir also writes that the Rama is not like the Beit Shmuel. He cites Maharshal, whose responsum implies that nevertheless the divorce did not help him to permit him to marry another. Beit Meir notes that the Rama wrote only that after she remarries he is no longer called a transgressor, which implies that before she remarries he may still remain under the ban. In the responsa at the end of Beit Meir, siman 4, he discusses a case of a man who divorced his wife by trickery against her will and concludes that although she may be divorced and permitted to marry, the husband may still be obligated to return and remarry her or remain in violation until she marries someone else. He compares this to the rule in Temurah that whatever the Torah says not to do, if one does it, it may not take effect for purposes of releasing the transgressor from the obligation. Therefore, beit din should rule that he continues to violate the cherem each day until he restores the situation or she marries another. Since she remains bound to him in this respect, he may also remain within the prohibition of marrying two wives, and no one should give him another wife because of the possible cherem. Get Pashut, s.k. 24, writes that bedieved if he divorced her against her will, perhaps the get is not valid even bedieved, but he notes that the wording of the enactment quoted in siman 102 does not clearly say this. It appears even Maharam Mintz did not decide to void the get absolutely, but treated it as doubtful. See there also whether a woman who consented at the beginning of writing the get and then retracted must pay the expenses; he writes she need not pay, and he caused his own loss. See also what I wrote below, siman 144, s.k. 6. Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, siman 129, cites Knesset HaGedolah, who writes simply that if he divorced her against her will, although he violated the cherem, the get is a get. The phrase in Maharam Mintz that "the get is nothing" means regarding the husband’s obligations to her, not that the get is halachically void.
