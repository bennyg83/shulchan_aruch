(א) Unless he found in her a matter of ervah. The Tur brought the dispute: Beit Shammai holds that one may not divorce her unless he found in her a matter of ervah through two witnesses, and this is "ervat davar." Although she is then already forbidden to him, and if so why is the verse "her first husband may not return to take her" needed, Tosafot answered in the name of the Yerushalmi that it is to make him transgress two negative commandments. Beit Hillel holds that even if she burned his food, meaning she burned it through fire or salt, he may divorce her, and they derive this from the word "davar." Do not ask why the word "ervah" is needed; the Gemara answers that if only "davar" were written, I would have thought that if she was divorced because of ervah she would be forbidden to marry another, so Scripture teaches otherwise. R. Akiva holds that even if she did nothing wrong toward him, but he found another more attractive than her, he may divorce her. The Tur wrote that we rule like Beit Hillel, who are more lenient than Beit Shammai, so that actual ervah is not required, but more stringent than R. Akiva, who permits even without wrongdoing. Rather, it is permitted only where she sinned against him, which is like ervah. This is the explanation of Shulchan Aruch's words, which are Rambam's language: "unless he found in her ervat davar" means either ervah or davar, namely wrongdoing against him. I do not know why he did not explain what "ervat davar" means.
(ב) But without this we say: whoever divorces his first wife, etc. At the end of Gittin we say: "If he hates, send away." Rav Yehudah says: if you hate her, send her away. R. Yochanan says: the one who sends is hated, meaning, as Rashi explains, hated before the Omnipresent. They do not disagree: one refers to a first marriage and the other to a second marriage, as R. Eliezer said, whoever divorces his first wife, etc. The verse says: "And this second thing you do: covering the altar of Hashem with tears," etc., "because Hashem testified between you and the wife of your youth, whom you betrayed, though she is your companion and wife of your covenant." The Tur wrote: in what case is this said? With a second marriage; but with a first marriage one should not be quick to divorce her, as R. Eliezer said, whoever divorces his first wife, etc. Therefore he should not divorce her because she sinned against him. But if he found in her a matter of ervah or immodesty, it is a mitzvah to divorce her, as it is written, "Drive out the scoffer and strife will leave." This is difficult: if he found immodesty in her, even a first wife may be divorced, and the distinction between first and second wife applies only where she sinned against him; with a first wife he should not divorce her, whereas with a second wife even if she merely sinned against him and no immodesty was found in her, he may. Since this is the Tur's distinction, why did the Gemara distinguish between first and second marriages? It should have said: they do not disagree; one case is where he found immodesty, and then "if you hate her, send her away," while the other is where no immodesty was found but she sinned against you, and then the one who sends is hated before the Omnipresent. This is indeed a difficulty: from where do we know to distinguish between the marriages when there must in any event be another distinction? It seems the Gemara is precise from the verse, "and you say: why?" What kind of question is this? It is a foundation of our faith that there are no sufferings without sin, and one may not question the Holy One, blessed be He. Rather, the verse indicates that they were asking because they thought they had acted lawfully; from here the Gemara learned the distinction between a first and second marriage.
