An apostate Jew who betrothed. See Noda BiYehudah Tinyana siman 162, concerning a troubling case in an Islamic country: a young man came to a community presumed to be an unmarried Jew, persuaded a widow to marry him, stayed three weeks, married her with chuppah and kiddushin, then took all she had and fled. It was then learned that he had been an apostate for years, leaving the woman in danger of permanent agunah. A rabbi suggested annulling the kiddushin from the outset. Noda BiYehudah responded forcefully that this must not be accepted. In that case, the man behaved as a Jew for three weeks and betrothed her as a Jew, so all would agree we must consider that he may have contemplated repentance; his later return to apostasy does not prove his state retroactively at the time of kiddushin. Even when the apostate is presently before us as an apostate, no one takes account of the dissenting view cited by Beit Yosef in the name of Ba'al HaItur. The settled halakhah is that when an apostate Jew betroths, his kiddushin is valid, and Kenesset HaGedolah writes that even one who disagrees regarding yibbum concedes that his kiddushin is kiddushin. Nor can one argue that because she did not know he was an apostate, she did not betroth herself with that in mind. Even by a man, where the maxim that he is content with any wife does not apply, and even where the prohibition has no permitted remedy, all authorities agree that kiddushin to a woman forbidden by a lav whom he did not recognize nevertheless takes effect. All the more so for a woman, who is presumed content with any husband, and where the prohibition can be remedied because he can repent at any moment, the marriage is not a mistaken kiddushin. The conclusion is that she is a married woman and remains an agunah until she obtains a get from him.
