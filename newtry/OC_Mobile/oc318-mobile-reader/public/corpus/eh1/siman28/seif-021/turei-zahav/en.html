With respect to prohibitions of benefit, the Rosh's view is that if there is a biblical aspect, whether because of chametz itself or because of the time, she is not betrothed; but where there is no biblical aspect she is betrothed. Rambam holds that even where there is no biblical aspect she is not betrothed. The Shulchan Aruch's doubt is therefore a doubt between these two rabbinic sides. Where the Tur cites the Rosh regarding chametz in the sixth hour, it should mean rabbinic chametz in the sixth hour. This resolves the Beit Yosef's wonder that the Tur's words are obscure. Consider one who found an object within his four cubits, and another person picked it up. We rule that a person's four cubits acquire for him by rabbinic enactment. If the one who acquired it through four cubits then betrothed a woman with it, we might have said that she is not betrothed, since biblically the finder who lifted it acquired it, for four cubits do not acquire biblically, and it would be theft in the first man's hand. Yet we do not find anywhere that such kiddushin is invalid; on the contrary, the rule is stated simply that four cubits acquire for him, and it becomes like his own money for all purposes. We must therefore say that the Sages have power to make property that is not his under Torah law into his by means of hefker beit din, divesting the one who lifted the lost object. So too with a get, the Sages enacted that a woman's four cubits acquire for her because of the concern of agunah, as Tosafot writes. The divorce is effective even against the biblical baseline. If so, why should the Sages not likewise have power regarding rabbinic prohibitions of benefit to divest the benefit from him and treat it like a biblical prohibition of benefit? Moreover, the Rosh writes in a responsum that if a court enacted that kiddushin performed without the father and mother should not take effect, and someone violated the enactment, the kiddushin is void because the Sages divested him of the money. Although Rema cites nearby that it is only a concern of kiddushin, that is a stringency and not a leniency. The same reasoning would apply to a rabbinic prohibition of benefit, and likewise to kiddushin with a gift on condition that it be returned at the beginning of the next siman: the Sages divested the money and made it ownerless, so the kiddushin should not take effect. A responsum of Rashba also implies from chametz that, in any rabbinic prohibition, kiddushin should not take effect at all because of hefker beit din. The matter requires practical analysis.
