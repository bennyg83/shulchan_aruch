She is not betrothed. For he holds that a craftsman does not acquire rights in the improvement of the utensil, and he holds that wages accrue from beginning to end, so every perutah becomes a loan. Even the final perutah becomes a loan before he gives the object into her hand. Although we rule that wages are payable only at the end, nevertheless the name of wages applies to each perutah, since wages accrue from beginning to end. But if wages existed only at the end, they would not become a loan at all, and the name of wages would not apply until he gave the object into her hand. So it is in Rashi and the Ran, unlike the Perishah. If, when he gives her the object, he says, be betrothed through the benefit of forgiving the loan, its law is as written above. Tosafot wrote in Perek HaGozel, Bava Kamma 99, in the sugya there: if he said, be betrothed with the wages that I will do for you, she is not betrothed until the money reaches her hand. The Gemara asks, what money? If you say that same money, meaning the object, does this imply that R. Meir holds that same money does not suffice? Rather, with what does R. Meir betroth her? Ri answered that it is logical that she is not betrothed by his forgiving her his wages, since he cannot claim his wages from her in court until he returns the object to her; his forgiveness is not considered like giving her utensils until the time comes when he can claim them in court. End quote. It is proven from this that the case is where he says, through the benefit of forgiving the loan, for they wrote this according to the view that a craftsman does not acquire the utensil, and obviously simple forgiveness of the loan would not help. Necessarily, then, it speaks where he says, through the benefit of forgiving the loan, and nevertheless it is not kiddushin while the utensil is in his hand. According to this, one may say that if she owes him money that he lent her and the due date has not yet arrived, it does not help if he says, through the benefit of forgiveness, since he cannot claim it from her in court. However, one may say that there it is different: as long as he has not returned the object to her, there is no loan at all. In any case, we hear that if he does not give her the object, there is no loan at all, and it does not help even if he says, through the benefit of forgiving the loan. Although the Ritva wrote that if the craftsman's utensil was lost through unavoidable accident, the owner must pay the craftsman his wages even though he does not return the object, and the Shach in Choshen Mishpat siman 126 brought his words, there it is different: while the object is in his hand there is no loan at all until he returns the object to her hand. According to this, what is written nearby, if the utensil is still with him, etc., also speaks where he returns the utensil. However, what is written, he did work for another, seems to contradict this. One must say that there the owner who owes him became obligated to him through a kinyan, and then it is like a loan whose due date has arrived. This is the meaning of, and he became obligated to him. So too the Bedek HaBayit in Even HaEzer there wrote, to reconcile the words of the Shulchan Aruch there in seif 17 and seif 18, which appear to contradict one another; he explained that what is written, and he became obligated, means an obligation through kinyan, and then it is like an actual loan.
And some say betrothed. Beit Yosef in name Tosafot R. Yehuda: when gives vessel must say behold betrothed with wage of this vessel; what said first with wage of work I will do and she said no — not kiddushin like get if said to wife behold divorced.
According to everyone she is betrothed. This is the language of Ri"u, folio 185: my teacher R. Eliezer wrote that it appears to him that according to everyone, if he had already made bracelets and rings for her and afterward betrothed her with his wages, she is betrothed, since he holds them for his wages and it is like betrothing with a loan for which he has a pledge. This is unlike the case, make bracelets for me, where she is not betrothed, for there he does not hold them for his wages. So too they said: with the wages I have done for you, she is not betrothed; Rashi explained, because he had already returned the bracelets to her. End quote. In Darkei Moshe he brought his words briefly and wrote: although we wrote above that it must specifically have come into his hand as a pledge, it appears that here everyone agrees that any craftsman who holds the utensil in his hand is like one holding a pledge for his wages. End quote. His intent in saying, although we wrote above, etc., is that there the Tur brought in the name of the Ramah that regarding her pledge it must specifically have come into his hand as a pledge, as I wrote above. At first glance the words of Darkei Moshe are astonishing, for the Tur wrote there that if it did not come into his hand as a pledge she is not betrothed; as long as he did not reveal his intention that he holds it as a pledge, she is not betrothed. Ri"u, however, speaks where he holds it for his wages. If so, what difficulty did the Tur's words pose for Ri"u's words? This gloss too implies that even if he does not hold it for his wages it is like a pledge, and this accords neither with the Tur nor with Ri"u. It is also difficult to settle the sugya, where it establishes their dispute as whether wages accrue from beginning to end. According to his understanding, it is difficult either way: if the utensil is still in his hand, why is she not betrothed, since it is like a pledge in his hand? And if he already returned the object, then even according to the view that wages are owed only at the end, once he returned the object the wages immediately became a loan, so why is she betrothed? Ri"u's words are proper because he speaks where he holds it for his wages. But according to the explanation that he understood the case to be where he does not hold it for his wages, it is difficult to settle the sugya. The Ran holds that this applies specifically when he claims his wages from her and holds it for his wages. The Ramban holds that it is not a pledge unless it came into his hand specifically as a pledge; even with a craftsman it does not help that he reveals his intention to hold it for his wages, for in any case it is not like a pledge since it did not come into his hand as a pledge. Thus there are three views in this law: according to the Ramban, it is not a pledge unless it came into his hand as a pledge, with no distinction between a craftsman and another person; according to the Tur and Ri"u, when he reveals his intention that he holds it for his wages, it is like a pledge; and according to the Ran, when he claims his wages from her and holds it for his wages, then it is like a pledge.
