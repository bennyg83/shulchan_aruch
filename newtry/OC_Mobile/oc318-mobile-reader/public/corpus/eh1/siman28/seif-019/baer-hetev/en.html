A woman. Then we say that he resolved in his heart to give it to him in whatever manner would be effective for kiddushin. If it would not be effective in the language of borrowing, it should be in the language of a gift; at the very least, the gift should be on condition that it be returned, so that she is betrothed, and he will acquire it from the woman and return it, or return its value. For one who gives a gift to another on condition that it be returned, if the recipient does not return the object itself but only its value, that is considered a return. In that case he need not inform her that he borrowed the object, but may betroth her with the object, since he can return the value of the object to the lender. So it is in the Tur in the name of the Rosh. Chelkat Mechokek wrote that according to the second reason, if he did not return its value it is retroactively theft; only according to the first reason is it possible that she is betrothed, for if the language of kiddushin was not effective, his intention was to give it as an absolute gift. In practice this requires analysis, whether it is definite kiddushin or doubtful kiddushin if he did not return the money. If he did not inform him that he wished to betroth with it, the Rosh holds that she is not betrothed unless four conditions are met. First, he lent it to him for a known time; otherwise he could demand it immediately, and there is no benefit worth a perutah, for perhaps he will demand it at once. Second, he must give him permission to lend it to another. Third, he must inform the woman that it is borrowed until such-and-such time. Fourth, the benefit of adornment for the whole time it is borrowed to him must be worth a perutah. See Beit Shmuel, Bach, Perishah, and Kenesset HaGedolah 59a. The one who betroths is not believed to say that he borrowed it without the owner's knowledge, because that would make him a thief, and a person does not render himself wicked; R. Yosef Adarbi, siman 10. If he gave him one object on condition that he return another object like it, according to everyone she is betrothed, for he did not betroth her with a borrowed object but with his own object. See Kenesset HaGedolah 58a.

An object. On this law, whether rental for a day is considered a sale, see Shulchan Aruch, Choshen Mishpat 175 and 313, and see what Beit Hillel wrote. The practical difference is this: if rental for a day is considered a sale, he can betroth with the body of the object, and he must inform her that he rented the object. If not, it is like borrowing, and he cannot betroth with the body of the object. If he has a pledge from a Jew, he cannot betroth with the benefit of adornment, because that is like interest; but he can betroth with the body of the pledge, and he must inform her that it is a pledge in his hand. If he has it by way of sale, then by law he need not return it to the borrower, it is like his own, and he can betroth with the body of the object. See the responsum of the Rosh, who wrote that ab initio one should be careful not to betroth with a borrowed object, even if he borrowed it in order to betroth with it. In Darkhei Moshe he wrote about this that the custom has already spread to betroth with a borrowed object, meaning where he borrowed it for the purpose of betrothal. In any event, whoever arranges kiddushin must be careful to ask the groom whether the ring is his, or whether he borrowed the ring. If he borrowed the ring, one must ask him whether he informed the lender that he wished to betroth with it. This applies also if he borrowed it from the husband, or from a woman who has a husband in town. Beit Shmuel and Beit Hillel.
