Has acquisition etc. — Rambam view holds R. Yitzchak even pledged at loan time acquires pledge; Rambam wrote partial acquisition; therefore some say though not fully acquire from R. Yitzchak where pledged at loan — still acquired partially since has partial acquisition in body of pledge.
And if he sold it, etc. This refers to a gentile's pledge. Mahari Weil wrote to the questioner that, although one might think that because this is rabbinic theft it should not effect kiddushin, like chametz in the sixth hour, he was hesitant. Here it is not theft but more like withholding repayment of the gentile's loan, which is permitted; although it may be forbidden where it causes desecration of the Name, that does not make it theft. We find many matters that are forbidden ab initio, such as the dust of interest, yet if one betroths with them the kiddushin is not thereby void. Moreover, even if she cannot sell the pledge, one can sometimes evade the gentile by saying it was lost, in which case it becomes permitted in his hand, and perhaps there is not even desecration of the Name. This requires analysis. If the issue is that there is an issur but not theft, what would be different if it were theft from a gentile? Theft from a gentile is also prohibited, yet the prohibition is rabbinic according to Mahari Weil's own discussion. If kiddushin can take effect there, why not here? And if one says that it can indeed take effect even with actual theft from a gentile, why did Rema rule that if one betrothed with a gentile's pledge she is not betrothed, following Mordekhai, because a Jew does not acquire a gentile's pledge? At minimum it should be a doubtful kiddushin. This remains difficult. The glossator cites Beit Shmuel: when he has a pledge, he must inform her that it is not his but a pledge, because she can say, "I do not wish to be betrothed with something that is not his," as we find regarding a borrowed ring and a rented utensil. I do not understand this comparison. In a borrowed or rented object he has no proprietary claim in the object itself; it must be returned at the end of the period without payment. A pledge, however, is acquired by him indefinitely unless it is redeemed, and even if it is redeemed, the redemption is like a sale back for money. It is therefore considered his, and there is no mistake in the kiddushin. We do not say that if a man betroths with an object and later must sell it, the kiddushin was mistaken because she assumed no such circumstance would arise. Furthermore, if one must disclose that an item is a pledge, why did the Tur not write that rule here, where the law of a pledge appears first, from which one would have learned it all the more so for borrowing? Even regarding a borrowed object, the Darkhei Moshe cites authorities that if the woman later says, "Had I known it was not yours I would not have accepted kiddushin," her words are ineffective because unexpressed intentions are not legally operative. Although this may conflict with the responsum of the Rosh, it is possible that the Rosh spoke only of borrowing; with a pledge even the Rosh would agree that her later claim does not help, since it is treated as his. Similarly, where one betroths with a gift that must be returned, she is betrothed even though he did not tell her, and we do not say she can object that it was not truly his. Accordingly, when Rema rules that one who betroths with a gentile's pledge does not effect kiddushin, he is speaking even where he did not tell her it was a pledge, parallel to a Jew's pledge. How, then, did he give it as kiddushin if it is not his? It must be that he intended to seize it, making it comparable to theft from a gentile. The Taz and Chelkat Mechokek's question therefore remains: why should she not be betrothed?
