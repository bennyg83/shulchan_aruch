With stolen property. If there is contradiction between the man and the woman, the man saying that the money was his and the woman saying that it was hers, the presumption is that whatever is in a person's possession is his; Maharit, part 1, siman 138. Whether the betrother is believed if he says that the money was not his: see Responsa Mahar"י Adarbi, siman 250, Responsa Beit Yosef, siman 18 in the laws of kiddushin, and Kenesset HaGedolah 56b. A worker hired to a householder for one day who, in the middle of the day, took from the householder's money without his knowledge and not as payment: this is ordinary theft, and she is not betrothed. Maharival, siman 101. Reuven owed Leah money and took out the money to pay Leah, placed it on the table, and Shimon came and betrothed Leah with it: this is ordinary theft, and she is not betrothed; Maharil, there, siman 101. If he betrothed her with the dust of interest, or with the find of a deaf-mute, minor, or legally incompetent person, she is betrothed; Mahar"י Weil, siman 138. If one said to Reuven, "My property is yours, and after you to so-and-so," where the law is that ab initio he should not sell, if he betrothed a woman with it she is betrothed; Mahar"י Weil, siman 138. If he sold movable property and took money, but the buyer did not draw or lift the movable property, and the seller betrothed a woman with that money which he received from the buyer, she is betrothed; Sema, Choshen Mishpat 198:3.
With theft. See Shach Choshen Mishpat 368 who elaborated plain theft among Israel — owners despair; gentile — no despair; robbery even among Israel no despair.

A gentile. The Shach asked in Choshen Mishpat 348:1-2: Rambam and Maharshal hold that one who steals from a gentile is obligated to return it and that it is forbidden by Torah law; see there, and see siman 359. If so, why did Rama write here that she need return it only because of sanctification of the Name? And when Rama presents this law in the name of Mahar"י Weil, siman 138, if you examine there you will see that it is not so. Chelkat Mechokek wrote likewise. I found in Sefer Nachalat Tzvi that he resolved this as follows: this applies specifically while the gentile's stolen object is still in the thief's hand, when he must return it; but if he transgressed and sold it or gave it to another, he need not make efforts to take it back and return it, except because of sanctification of the Name. See Beit Shmuel. I am astonished: if this were not so, why did Rama write below in this siman, paragraph 12, "if the lender sold it to another and he betrothed with it"? He should have said even if the lender himself betroths with it, she is betrothed. Examine this carefully, and see there what he wrote in the name of Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel.
Only, etc. This means where it was her stolen property after despair, and she did not say yes, and there had been no prior matchmaking between them. Nevertheless, she is betrothed rabbinically, because despair effects acquisition rabbinically. If it were ordinary theft from others, how could one find a case of despair alone? As soon as he betroths her, there is both despair and change of possession, as the Tur wrote in the name of the Ramah. See Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel. In Nachalat Tzvi he wrote that the case is where the owners despaired after he placed it in her hand, so that there is only despair alone, and it always speaks of theft from others. He says that it is proven there that with her own stolen property, even if she despaired she is not betrothed unless he first arranged a match with her, from the fact that the Talmud does not answer, when it asks "but Rav spoke of her own stolen property," that Rav was speaking where she had not despaired. This is not precise. If Rav were speaking even where she had despaired, the Gemara would have no question there from "Did Rav say so? But did he not say..." for R. Yochanan spoke of stolen property where the owners had not despaired. Moreover, Tosafot there, s.v. "and did he not say," do not imply that Rav speaks even where she despaired, for they ask what Rav came to teach, since it is a complete Mishnah that where the owners did not despair it is not his. So too the gloss to Derishah in the name of Maharshal asks this. See Kiddushin 52a. The difficulty of Nachalat Tzvi is unfounded from the start, according to the conclusion that the text is explaining "he betrothed with it." It speaks, for example, where he snatched a sela from her hand; since he snatched the sela from her hand in her presence, she does not despair, as stated in Sema, Choshen Mishpat 368:4. On this basis the proof of Bach in the words of the Ramah is also rejected; examine it carefully. In any event Bach concludes that with her own stolen property after despair, where she did not say yes and where there had been no prior matchmaking, she is betrothed rabbinically. So too Kenesset HaGedolah 58b; see Beit Shmuel.
