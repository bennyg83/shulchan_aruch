Seif 1 One who betroths a woman with stolen property, etc. NB: See the later commentators, who discussed this at length. I found in Rambam's language in Hilchot Issurei Mizbeach, chapter 4 law 15, that if he gave her something that was not his, he did not disqualify it, because a person cannot prohibit something that is not his unless the owners despaired. This proves that if the owners despaired, the law of etnan takes effect. But there too the difficulty arises: a person cannot prohibit something that is not his, and despair alone does not acquire. Even if Rambam holds, according to Yam Shel Shlomo, siman 362, that despair acquires rabbinically, how could he write without qualification that once the owners despaired it is an etnan? If she owed a korban and offered this animal, she would have to bring another korban; but since by Torah law it is not an etnan, this would be chullin brought into the Temple courtyard. If Rambam holds like the poskim that something rabbinic can become Torah-level, the matter is settled. But according to Maggid Mishneh's reason, that since she acquired it through him, he too acquired it, implying that rabbinic acquisition does not become Torah-level, this passage is difficult. Here Maggid Mishneh's reason cannot be said, for in kiddushin everything depends on her ability to receive benefit; any benefit can effect kiddushin, and therefore once she has benefit through him she is betrothed, even if the object itself is not his. But by etnan, the actual object must be his. Rambam's language implies that the disqualification depends on him: he did not disqualify it, meaning not that she disqualified it by receiving it, but that he disqualifies it by his giving, as appears from many similar formulations there. If even after despair it is not his, how can he prohibit it by Torah law? From this it appears there is proof for what I wrote in my youth in my novellae to Hilchot Kiddushin: the correct reading in Maggid Mishneh is not "otah" but "oto" - he acquires the object itself through her. Since he alone can enable her acquisition, and the acquirer needs his transfer, the robber has a monetary benefit-right in it; in such a case everyone agrees that a benefit-right has monetary status, so it is as though he acquires the body of the object. Thus Maggid Mishneh wrote that he acquires "it," namely the object, and therefore etnan can also take effect upon it. This is precise. See Beit Shmuel s.k. 9, who wrote regarding R. Yochanan's statement that if one stole and the owners had not despaired neither can consecrate it, nevertheless with the consent of both it becomes hekdesh. See my work Milei DeNezikin on Bava Kamma 98b, where I brought proof from R. Meir Sirkis, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet, Bava Kamma 96b, on the Mishnah of stolen chametz, that he holds like Beit Shmuel on this point; otherwise his words are astonishing. Examine carefully. Regarding whether rabbinic kiddushin becomes Torah-level kiddushin, see the proof I wrote in this law, siman 37, in my eighth edition, siman 214, with God's help.
