Or by coercion. The prohibition of coercion violates do not covet; so wrote the Rambam, and so ruled the Shulchan Aruch, Choshen Mishpat siman 359, unlike the Sema in siman 34, who wrote plainly that he does not violate do not covet. Nevertheless, he is disqualified for testimony only rabbinically, as written in Choshen Mishpat siman 34, because people think that do not covet means only without payment. Regarding what Beit Yosef wrote in Choshen Mishpat siman 34 about the law that a coercer is disqualified rabbinically, where he asked from what appears in the Gemara, that people understand do not covet to mean without payment, and he inferred from the word understand that the truth is not so, I do not know what was difficult for him. In any event, people understand it as without payment, and therefore he is not disqualified. So too the Rambam holds: he wrote that one violates do not covet, yet he holds that a coercer is disqualified rabbinically; necessarily the reason is that he thinks do not covet means without payment. So too it implies in responsum Rivash siman 311, which Beit Yosef brought there. If you say that Beit Yosef's question concerns the Tur's language, for he wrote that a coercer is a robber rabbinically, implying that he does not violate do not covet by Torah law, then it remains difficult how he resolves this question by saying that Tosafot wrote that this is indeed the truth. The Tur does not hold like Tosafot, for in siman 359 he brought the Rambam's view that one violates do not covet even when he gives money. The reason she is not betrothed is that in any case the object is not his and he cannot betroth her with it unless it is known that the owners despaired of it.
She is betrothed. In Choshen Mishpat 356 it is explained, according to the Rambam's position, that if one bought from a notorious thief, even where there was despair and a change of domain, the purchaser must return the value of the object to the owners. According to this, it is impossible to say that our case concerns a notorious thief, for if so, with what does he betroth her? Rather, it concerns a thief who is not notorious. Yet this is difficult, because here it implies biblical betrothal, while there the Beit Yosef writes that in the case of a thief who is not notorious, by strict law the purchaser would have to return the money, and only because of the market enactment need he not return it. If so, she is not betrothed on a biblical level. I have heard this difficulty raised in the name of the Gaon R. Yaakov of Lublin. One may answer according to the Beit Yosef's view that even though ma'amad sheloshtan is a rabbinic enactment, it nevertheless creates biblical betrothal; so too here, it is biblical betrothal. But according to Rema, who holds that ma'amad sheloshtan does not create biblical betrothal, the difficulty remains. There is another difficulty either way. If he revealed to her that the object was stolen, the market enactment does not apply and she must return the money. If he did not reveal that it was stolen, she can say, "I do not wish to be betrothed with stolen property," as stated in Kiddushin 53. One cannot say that the case is where he accepted responsibility to remove the claim of the robbed owner, for in such a case there is no change of domain, as stated in Choshen Mishpat at the beginning of siman 361. One may say that the case is where he told her that it was stolen, and nevertheless she need not return the object itself, as stated nearby. Therefore he betroths her with the benefit of using the utensil: the body of the utensil is hers, and she need only return its value. She has benefit from the fact that the utensil is hers. This seems to settle the words of the authorities, but the Talmudic passage is still not settled. The Talmud records a case in which a man betrothed with stolen money, and it implies that if ordinary theft is presumed to involve despair, the betrothal is valid. This is difficult as above, for with stolen money the robber certainly must return the stolen money. There is a further difficulty from Rema's ruling in Choshen Mishpat 356 that nowadays stolen property is returned even after despair and change of domain because of the law of the kingdom. If so, with what does he betroth her, since she must return it? It resembles a borrowed utensil, with which one cannot betroth unless he informed her that it was borrowed. If the case is where he told her it was stolen, then she must return it without compensation, because the market enactment does not apply; by Torah law she must return the money, and because of the law of the kingdom she must return the utensils. Thus she must return it for nothing. Perhaps one may say that even if he did not inform her that it was stolen, it is not comparable to a borrowed utensil, which is not his at all. Here he betroths her with something that has undergone despair and change of domain; Beit Hillel writes similarly. Still, the difficulty remains that if he did not reveal it, she may say, "I do not wish to be betrothed with stolen property." See Choshen Mishpat there: if one bought from the thief before despair, he must return the object and the owners return the money to him. If he knew it was stolen, the market enactment was not made for him, and he must return the object without compensation. Where there was despair and change of domain and he knew it was stolen, the law is not explicit. The Bayit Chadash writes in this siman that he acquires it, and so writes the Shakh there. It appears that according to the Rambam he need not return the object to the owners, since he acquired it through despair and change of domain; but he must return the value, for he is no better than one who bought from a notorious thief. Even by strict law, one who buys from a non-notorious thief must return the money, except that because of the market enactment he need not. Where he knew it was stolen, the market enactment was not made for him, so by law he must return the money. Consequently, nowadays, because of the law of the kingdom, if he knew it was stolen he must return the utensil to the owners without compensation; the same applies if he bought from a notorious thief. From this we learn the law of betrothing a woman with property from a notorious thief, or where she knew it was stolen and he betrothed her with the object. According to the Rambam, it is not betrothal, because by law she must return the money, and she agreed only to the full value of the object. It is like a deposit that is diminished. One should not say that since he betroths her generally with the object, when she knows it is stolen she agrees to be betrothed by the benefit of using the utensil before the owners come, for women are not legally expert and she assumes that the object need not be returned. The practical result of these distinctions is as follows. If he betrothed with stolen property after despair, informed her that it was stolen, and betrothed her with the benefit that the utensil is hers, the betrothal is valid. If the utensil has no such benefit, because utensils like this are common, it is not betrothal. If he betroths her generally with the utensil, he is not a notorious thief, and he did not inform her that it was stolen, then she need return neither the utensil nor the money, and she is betrothed. Although by strict law she should return the value of the utensil, because of the market enactment she need not, and she has acquired it. This is biblical betrothal according to the Beit Yosef, as written regarding ma'amad sheloshtan. However, if she said, "I do not wish to be betrothed with stolen property," it is doubtful betrothal. Nowadays, because of the law of the kingdom, she must return it; nevertheless on a biblical level she acquired the object, and the robbed owner must give her the value of the object, so it is biblical betrothal, provided she does not know it was stolen. If he told her that it was stolen, it is not betrothal at all, because in such a case there is no market enactment. She must return the money by Torah law, and since he betrothed her generally, she thought he was betrothing her with the full value of the object; therefore the betrothal is void. If he said that he betroths her with the benefit that the utensil is hers, the betrothal takes effect even nowadays, although she must return the object because of the law of the kingdom. If he obligates himself to remove the robbed owner's claim, there is no change of domain and it is not biblical betrothal. After he silences the robbed owner, one may say that the betrothal then takes effect, as the Ritva writes on the sugya at Kiddushin 52. See further below what I write.

Theft from a gentile. According to Rashi in Sanhedrin 57, robbery and theft from a gentile are prohibited only rabbinically, because of chillul Hashem. See Rashi in chapter Lulav HaGazul, Sukkah 30, and responsum Shevut Yaakov siman 2. So too it implies in Tosafot in chapter HaGozel, Bava Kamma 113; see Yam shel Shlomo. However, the Rambam at the beginning of Hilkhot Geneivah, the Semag, and the Tur, Choshen Mishpat siman 348, hold that it is a Torah prohibition. Yet all this concerns the robber or thief himself. But one who purchases from him before yeush is not under a Torah prohibition, for withholding from a gentile is only rabbinic, as Kesef Mishneh wrote at the beginning of Hilkhot Gezeilah, the reason being that it came into his hand permissibly; so too with a purchaser. See what Chelkat Mechokek wrote nearby. In Shach siman 348 he left the rabbi's words requiring study, but according to what I wrote this is settled. However, regarding what he wrote that one need return it only because of kiddush Hashem, at first glance it appears that there is a rabbinic prohibition, as with withholding.
After despair alone. This means where he stole from her after despair, and she did not say yes, and no prior shiddukhin had occurred between them. Nevertheless, she is betrothed rabbinically, because despair acquires rabbinically. This is how the Bach understood, and so too the Chelkat Mechokek wrote. According to this, one must say that in such a case she cannot say, I took what is mine, since she had despaired of it. But their words are not compelling, and it also does not imply so from the Ramah's words in the Tur. One may say that here it speaks of theft after despair where he accepted responsibility for it, and then it is not stolen property, as written there in Choshen Mishpat. Nevertheless, the Bach ruled that if he stole from her after despair, she is rabbinically betrothed. See Choshen Mishpat siman 392; in the Tur there it is explained that despair alone does not create kiddushin even rabbinically, but only doubtful kiddushin. According to this, here too it is rabbinically doubtful kiddushin. See Yam Shel Shlomo, Perek Merubah siman 7, where he wrote that according to the Rosh, if despair acquires rabbinically, then it is Torah kiddushin, because he acquires through court confiscation, and another man's kiddushin does not take effect on her. But since according to several poskim despair does not acquire even rabbinically, it is doubtful kiddushin; according to this, it is a Torah doubt. We hear that he too agrees with the Beit Yosef's view, that something acquired rabbinically, when used for kiddushin, creates Torah kiddushin. But the Beit Yosef does not hold like the Maharshal regarding despair, because theft after despair is not comparable to ma'aser sheni. In theft after despair, although he acquires it rabbinically, nevertheless by Torah law he must return it; if so, he betroths her with something that is not his. This is unlike ma'aser sheni. So too it is proven below, for the Beit Yosef challenged Ri"u, who wrote that with ma'aser sheni it is rabbinic kiddushin, but regarding theft he did not challenge him. Necessarily one must say this distinction. According to Ri"u, who holds that with ma'aser sheni it is not Torah kiddushin, all the more so with theft after despair. With this it is also clear why there is no difficulty from what is written in Choshen Mishpat siman 356, that regarding a Jew we presume he has despaired, while here he wrote specifically where it is known that she despaired. The Bach answered that here, regarding Torah kiddushin, we do not say that presumably she despaired, because the presumption that one despairs is only rabbinic. Yet according to the Beit Yosef's method this would still be difficult: anything acquired rabbinically and used for kiddushin creates Torah kiddushin, so why must it be known that she despaired? But according to what I wrote, it is well resolved by the Bach's answer. In Darkei Moshe, regarding ma'aser sheni, he brought Ri"u's view and not the Beit Yosef's view, implying that he holds the main law is like Ri"u, that it is only rabbinic kiddushin; so too the Bach ruled. This also implies from the Tur's words in the name of the Ramah, that if he betrothed with stolen property through despair alone, it is not kiddushin, for the reason above. However, one can say that there is another reason there: she can say, yes, I took it, and I took what is mine, and therefore it is not kiddushin at all, even rabbinically.
