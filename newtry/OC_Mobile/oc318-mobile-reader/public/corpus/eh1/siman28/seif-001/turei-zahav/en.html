She is betrothed rabbinically. This implies specifically after the owner's despair, for the Shulchan Aruch has already ruled that despair is not assumed unless it is known. Therefore, in an ordinary case before known despair there is no betrothal even rabbinically, and she does not require a get. I wrote this to exclude the words of my father-in-law, who, because of a difficulty raised by Maharshal, maintained that a get is required. I already wrote in Choshen Mishpat that the difficulty can be resolved, and that if one betrothed a woman with stolen property before it is known that the owner certainly despaired, no get is required, as all the authorities rule here. Even if some great authorities were said to require a get, Mahari Weil records a practical ruling that no get is required, and Maharshal's teacher and others agreed. No later authority is found requiring a get in such a case. Since even according to the stringent view the concern is only rabbinic, and the authorities clearly rule leniently, there is no basis to require a get. Indeed, stringency here would cast aspersion on earlier practical rulings, much as the Gemara at the beginning of Gittin warns against stringencies that call earlier gittin into question. The Tur cites Ramah: specifically with property stolen from another she is betrothed after despair, because there are both despair and a change of domain; but with her own stolen property, if he had not first arranged the match, even after despair she is not betrothed, because there is no change of domain. The words "and if he stole" are the Tur's continuation, not Ramah's. This resolves the Beit Yosef's question why the Tur attributed the ruling to Ramah when it is explicit in the Talmud: Ramah teaches that in her own stolen property, despair does not help. There is, however, a broader difficulty. In Choshen Mishpat the Rosh rules that if a thief betrothed a woman with stolen property after despair, she requires a get out of doubt, because perhaps despair alone acquires biblically. If the very act of betrothal were considered a change of domain, then after despair she should certainly be betrothed, not merely doubtfully. It cannot be speaking of her own stolen property, because if he had arranged the match she would certainly be betrothed, and if not, there would not even be rabbinic kiddushin. It must concern property stolen from others, and even there the Rosh apparently does not treat the act of kiddushin itself as a change of domain, for at the time of giving the money must already be his. It follows that there is a dispute between the Rosh and Ramah. Although the Tur cites both in different places, here, in the laws of kiddushin, his ruling is primary: the betrothal and the change of domain can occur together, just as elsewhere we find acquisition and domain arriving simultaneously. Rambam's language also implies definite kiddushin after known despair, because of the change of domain. Thus Ramah's statement is a real novelty. One should not object from Tosafot in Kiddushin, who ask what Rav adds beyond the Mishnah. Rav was commenting on the Mishnah, which deals with a case before despair, as the sugya itself indicates. My father-in-law again wrote that rabbinically a get is required in every case and claimed that this is the gloss in the Shulchan Aruch, but that is inaccurate. The gloss concerns only a case after definite despair. In an ordinary case, we have a settled rule that ordinary theft is not presumed to involve despair by the owner, and she is not betrothed at all, even stringently.
