With a loan and a perutah. The Tur writes: Therefore, if she said to him, "Make me bracelets and earrings, and I will be betrothed to you for the wage of the labor," she is not betrothed, because wages accrue from beginning to end. The meaning is that since betrothal is effected only with money and not with a loan, this wage too has the status of a loan. Rashi explains in Kiddushin 48 that according to the view that wages accrue from beginning to end, whenever each perutah's worth of work is completed the employer becomes obligated to the artisan for that portion of the wage. Therefore, when he returns the article to her, the wage is retrospectively a loan. According to the view that wages are owed only at the end, when he returns the article and waives the wage for the sake of betrothal, there is no prior loan at the moment of return. The essential point, then, depends on the return of the completed work. Only then does the payment become due according to the view that wages are payable at the end. According to the view that wages accrue from beginning to end, the obligation has attached already, even before she has received the object; as soon as he performs work worth a perutah, that perutah becomes a loan against her retroactively when he later gives it to her. The same applies even if the entire wage is only one perutah. But according to the view that wages are not owed until the end, no loan rests upon her until the object is returned to her, for only then can he collect the wage from her. This is not like building a wall, where each row becomes payable as it is made, because the wall already stands in the owner's domain and is not in the worker's hand. Here the due date depends on transferring the object back into her domain, and then, specifically, the wage is treated retrospectively as a loan. Rashi likewise writes that once they have finished the work and must return it in court, it has already become a loan. The Perishah explains that according to the view that wages accrue from beginning to end, even if he gives the object immediately upon completing the work, the final hammer-blow is not worth a perutah; otherwise it would be a case of a loan together with a perutah, in which she would be betrothed. I am astonished at this. If so, why did the Gemara need to formulate the dispute about a loan and a perutah in a case where he added a jewel of his own, as the Tur also writes nearby? It could have been found in every case where the final blow was worth a perutah. Furthermore, below in siman 38 the same issue appears regarding "I will speak for you to the ruler," and there it is impossible to say that the final speech is not worth a perutah. Nor can one say that the final blow is never worth a perutah; if the whole wage were only one perutah and there were only one blow, would she then be betrothed? Certainly not, for the wage still has the name of a loan. Rather, Rashi repeatedly makes clear that the matter depends on the return, whereas the Perishah focuses on the final blow. In truth the giving is the act of betrothal. The difference is this: according to the view that wages are owed only at the end, the loan begins only from the moment of giving onward, not retrospectively from the time of labor; according to the view that wages accrue from beginning to end, the giving causes each perutah, even a single perutah, to become a prior loan retroactively. This is unlike the case in Avodah Zarah concerning building a structure for idolatry, where the wage is permitted because wages accrue from beginning to end and the prohibition takes effect only at the final hammer-blow, which is not worth a perutah. There the completion itself is the final blow, and there is no later act of giving. In betrothal, however, everything depends on the giving, which is the essential act of kiddushin. Tosafot Rid writes that the baraita's phrase, "for the wage that I will do with you," states the legal case, but the man must say at the time of giving, "Be betrothed to me for the wage of this vessel." If he said at the outset, "Do you wish to be betrothed to me for the wage of what I will do for you," and she said yes, but at the time of giving he said nothing, she is not betrothed, just as with a get one must say at the time of giving, "This is your get." The Tur's cases of giving the vessel for its improvement, or adding a jewel of his own, would accordingly require such a statement at the time of giving. I find this difficult. The Gemara says that "for the wage I have done with you" is ineffective, while "for the wage I will do with you" is effective. If a new declaration were required at the time of giving, he would necessarily have to say then, "for the wage I have done with you," and the case would collapse into the first clause. Rashi also writes simply that when he finishes and gives it to her, she is betrothed, without mentioning a new declaration. It therefore seems that no new declaration is required. Since one who was already speaking with her about betrothal and then gave without specifying can effect betrothal, all the more so where he stated explicitly at the outset that the labor would be for kiddushin. At the time he gives the vessel, no further words are needed; the giving plainly relies on the first declaration, even though time has intervened.
