She is doubtfully divorced. According to the Maggid Mishneh, the doubt is whether this man can be transferred from being the husband’s agent for delivery, appointed by the husband, into becoming the woman’s agent for receipt, since the husband’s agency has not yet been completed. This implies that he understands the phrase "the agency has not returned to the husband" to mean that the agency has not yet been completed. According to Rashi, the doubt is because there is no time to return and inform the husband that he performed his agency. One should not ask why here she is doubtfully divorced while in siman 142, seif 13, it is not a get at all. Here, at the outset there was an agency fit to return to the husband; there, there was never an agency fit to report back that the agency had been completed.

(ד) And some say that the woman cannot, etc. In the Talmud two reasons are explained. The first reason is concern for the husband's disgrace if she herself does not receive the get; according to this there is a Torah-level concern. In the responsum of Rashba cited by Beit Yosef at the end of the siman, it is likewise explained that the husband may not appoint an agent to give the get into the hand of the woman's receiving agent because of the woman's disgrace, when he himself does not wish to give it into her agent's hand. What the Rav wrote in the gloss, that if she said to receive it from her husband one may not receive it from his agent, must mean that when she said to receive it from her husband there is insistence according to everyone, even according to the view that is not concerned for disgrace. But according to Rav, who is concerned for disgrace, even if she did not say to receive it from her husband, one may not receive it from his agent. So wrote Taz. The second reason is that it resembles a courtyard that comes afterward, meaning as if he placed it in another's courtyard and she then acquired that courtyard; according to this, there is only a rabbinic concern. There are also practical differences: if the husband is not particular, then according to the reason of disgrace there is no objection. If she appointed her agent at the outset, then the concern of a courtyard that comes afterward does not apply, but the concern of disgrace still applies, even though she had appointed the agent beforehand. If she was preoccupied when the get was brought to her and because of that preoccupation appointed an agent for receipt, some say that in such a case there is no concern for the husband's disgrace. Ran wrote that even in such a case there is concern for the husband's disgrace. Responsa Rivash, siman 55, wrote that the essential law is that a woman can appoint an agent; therefore, where there is some concern of prohibition if he sends it by an agent to her hand, it is preferable that she appoint a receiving agent to receive it from his agent.
