(ב) And if the sender denies, etc. Necessarily one must say that the sender denies and says, I gave it to you as a deposit, while the agent says it was for divorce. For if he says, it fell from me and you found it, the Maggid Mishneh wrote in the name of Rashba that he is not believed. If so, it is difficult: when they are not in one city, why can he contradict him? According to Rif's approach, as written in the responsa and in Rashba, the sugya speaks of an agent for delivery, and according to all opinions when they are not in one city the agent is believed; yet here he wrote plainly that he can contradict him. One should not say that here it speaks where the Get is not in the agent's hand, and therefore he can contradict him, because then it is difficult: where is the Get? If the Get is in her hand, the Mechaber wrote below that when the Get is in the woman's hand it is as though it were in the agent's hand. If the Get was torn, then even if the sender admits he gave it for divorce, she is in doubt, as written below. Nor should one say that the sugya speaks where it is known that he is her agent for delivery, and likewise below, and only there do we say he cannot contradict him unless the reasoning assists him, namely, if it was for divorce he should have given it into her hand; therefore when they are not in one city he cannot contradict him. But if we do not know at all whether he is his agent, he can contradict him. For in any event he is a third party holding the matter, so why can he contradict him and say he did not give it for divorce? Nor should one say that it speaks where he says it fell from me and you found it, and he is not a third party at all; for although Rashba holds he is not believed to say it fell from me, Hagahot Maimoniyot does not hold so, but follows the Rosh, who holds that he may say it fell from me, as written in the responsum cited by Beit Yosef in Choshen Mishpat siman 55. What the Tur wrote below in siman 152, that when the Get is in the woman's hand the husband cannot say it fell from me, may be because a Get in the woman's hand is different. And what the Tur wrote there in Choshen Mishpat, that if he says to the third party, you stole it from me, he is not believed, may be because a claim of theft is different, but he is believed to say it fell from me. Yet Hagahot Maimoniyot wrote this law, and wrote there that the husband can contradict him when the Get is not in the agent's hand, as written nearby. If he held like the Rosh, it would be understandable; even when the Get is in the agent's hand he could also contradict him and say it fell from me. Rather, necessarily he does not hold like the Rosh. I also wrote nearby that it is proven not like the Rosh, who is concerned for falling, and not like the poskim who hold that he is believed to say I gave it as a loan. Perhaps it speaks where the Get is not in the agent's hand and not in the woman's hand, but the agent delivered the Get to her before witnesses of delivery, the witnesses are before us, and the Get was torn. Therefore, if the husband admits that he is his agent, she is divorced; but if he denies him, even though they saw the Get in his hand, it does not help. This is as written below in the gloss, that what they saw the Get in the agent's hand does not help. Necessarily one must say that when he delivered the Get to her, he did not say that he was the husband's agent; for if he had said so at that time while the Get was in his hand, the agency was in his hand and he would have been believed when he said he was an agent for delivery, and the husband could no longer contradict him, as written there in Choshen Mishpat. Hagahot Maimoniyot wrote that the husband can contradict him because the third-party holding left his hand; this implies that if the Get is in the agent's hand, he cannot contradict him. At first glance this is difficult: if the Get is in the agent's hand, why can he not contradict him? The husband has a miggo that he could object and tell him not to deliver the Get to her; if so, why is he not believed to say he gave it as a deposit with a miggo that he could object? One must answer that a third party is believed even where there is a miggo to nullify the matter. So too it is implied from what is written in Choshen Mishpat, that the third party is believed like two witnesses; if so, it is like a miggo against witnesses.
However, the agent is believed, etc. So wrote Ri"v, and the Rosh also wrote at the beginning of Gittin that even if the husband contradicts him, the agent is believed. This cannot mean that the husband says the get fell from him, for the Rosh holds he can say that, as written nearby. Rather it speaks where the husband says he gave it as a deposit. Darkei Moshe explains that the agent is believed when the get is in his hand. If the get is not in the agent’s hand, the Rama’s words would be difficult, for why should he be believed and why are we not concerned that the husband will come and contradict him? Therefore the Rama must be speaking where the get is in the agent’s hand, even though the Mechaber speaks where it is not. It must also be speaking of an agent for delivery from place to place; otherwise the husband could contradict him. The Rosh and Ri"v appear to hold that when an agent for delivery brings a get, he cannot be contradicted even in the same city, because since he is believed to say "it was written and signed before me," he is believed also to say he is an agent for delivery. However, the Tur and Beit Yosef do not imply this. From the Rosh it further seems that this credibility applies specifically where he must say "it was written and signed before me." In Eretz Yisrael, where that declaration is unnecessary, he is not believed to say he was appointed agent for delivery, though Ramah holds he is believed even there. The Rambam also appears to hold like Ramah, as Rivash explains, that the agent is believed to say he is the husband’s agent even where the get is verified and he does not say "before me." All this concerns an agent for delivery; an agent for receipt is not believed to say he was appointed if the get is not in his hand.
And the custom is to require, etc. Meaning that when he brings a get from place to place, a written authorization is not required by strict law, because he is believed to say he was appointed before witnesses; the requirement is only customary. But in one city, according to the decisors who rule like Rav Huna, authorization is required by law. It is difficult, however, that even from place to place there is concern that the husband may say, "it fell from me and you found it," or "I gave it to you as a loan," in which case he is not an agent. It must be that the Mechaber here follows Ba'al HaTerumot, who holds that even if the husband says he gave it as a loan, the agent has the law of a third party, unlike Tosafot and other decisors; all the more, the husband is not believed to say it fell from him and the agent found it.
