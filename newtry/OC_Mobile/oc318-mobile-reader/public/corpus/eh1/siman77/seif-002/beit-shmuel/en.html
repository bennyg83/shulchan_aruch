If the husband wanted to divorce her. But we do not compel him, unlike the view of the Rambam and Rashbam. See Maggid Mishneh, chapter 14, halacha 1.
And she takes her worn-out property. There are four approaches in this sugya: the Rambam’s, the Rosh’s as cited by the Tur, the Ramban and Rashba’s, and the Ran’s. According to the Rambam, the sugya of Rav Zevid’s daughter-in-law speaks of a moredet who says, "I want him but I want to pain him." The Gemara says that with nichsei tzon barzel, if she seized, we do not remove it from her, and if she did not seize, she did not acquire. Rivash writes that the husband need not seize; if she did not seize, it is as though he seized. Nichsei melog she receives even without seizure. The Rambam holds there are daily announcements for four weeks, with warnings before and after, and then the beit din consults her. Immediately after four weeks she has no ketubah, though the get is delayed twelve months. In "ma'is alai," he divorces immediately and the Rambam holds we compel him, though all decisors disagree. In nichsei tzon barzel and nichsei melog, whatever is extant she takes even without seizure. According to the Rosh, after the final enactment both types are delayed twelve months. In "I want him but pain him," they announce her, and after twelve months she loses ketubah and conditions of ketubah; before then she has them, except no support and her earnings are hers. In both cases nichsei melog are hers without seizure, and so is land of nichsei tzon barzel; movable nichsei tzon barzel she receives only if she seized. According to the Ramban and Rashba, "I want him but pain him" has only the Talmudic law: after four weeks’ announcement she loses; seizure does not help for nichsei tzon barzel, while nichsei melog need no seizure. Rav Zevid’s daughter-in-law speaks of "ma'is alai," where we wait twelve months; during that time she loses nothing except support, and afterward loses ketubah and conditions. For nichsei tzon barzel, seizure helps; for nichsei melog, seizure is not needed; and no announcement is made for "ma'is alai." According to the Ran, in "ma'is alai" she even has ketubah, nichsei tzon barzel, and nichsei melog without seizure, and the get is delayed twelve months. Rav Zevid’s daughter-in-law speaks of "I want him," where seizure is required for nichsei tzon barzel, and after four weeks’ announcement she loses ketubah; if she returns it does not help, but Chazal enacted not to divorce her until twelve months. Regarding seizure, see siman 93: seizing a key does not help, nor seizure through an agent. The Chelkat Mechokek rules that if her father seized, it is like seizure through an agent, unless the dowry had not yet reached the son-in-law and remains with the father. The Ran’s responsum explains that seizure helps only for the very item she brought him, not another item; money of dowry is like another item, because "praise of her father’s house" does not apply to money, unlike the Bach. Hagahot Maimoniyot compares this to siman 53: if her father did not promise the dowry except for his daughter’s benefit, and she says "ma'is alai," he need not pay it; according to that reason it applies even if she says "I want him but pain him" when she leaves him. If the dowry is held by a third party, the laws depend on siman 53.
And she takes nothing from the husband’s property. In Rivash, siman 30, it is explained that gifts he sent her, and gifts given because of his father or relatives at the time of marriage or afterward, must be returned. Regarding the kiddushin ring, see siman 30; regarding gifts, see siman 118.
And some say even nichsei tzon barzel. This is the view of the Rosh, Ramban, and Rashba, that Rav Zevid’s daughter-in-law speaks of "ma'is alai," and in the conclusion seizure is needed for nichsei tzon barzel, while nichsei melog need no seizure.
That even nichsei melog, etc. The Chelkat Mechokek already wondered about this, for we do not find such an opinion among the approaches listed. According to everyone, seizure is needed only for nichsei tzon barzel, while nichsei melog need no seizure. Responsa Ralbach, siman 37, cites another view that in "ma'is alai" seizure helps specifically for nichsei melog, but not for nichsei tzon barzel. It is also difficult that the Rama brought here opinions regarding seizure of nichsei tzon barzel and nichsei melog but did not bring the view of the Rosh, Ramban, and Rashba that the get is delayed twelve months and during those months she receives ketubah. The Mechaber ruled simply that if he wants to divorce he need not pay the ketubah. Regarding what wording counts as "ma'is alai," see Responsa Maharam ben Sasson, siman 186.
They consult her. This means that the beit din itself warns her before the final ruling. So wrote the Ran. According to him, if she returns, we still reduce her ketubah by seven dinarim for every week she remained rebellious, so that she not scheme. Hagahot Maimoniyot and the nearby gloss do not imply this; one must say that since they announce her, we are not concerned for scheming.
And she loses her ketubah. This is the Rambam’s view: immediately after four weeks she has no ketubah, but they ask him not to divorce her immediately because it is degrading to Jewish women for their husbands to divorce them because of quarrel, as the Maggid Mishneh writes. So wrote Semag in the name of Rabbenu Tam, and this is also the view of the Ramban and Rashba, as above, and the Ran and Shiltei Gibborim. According to the Rosh, she loses nothing until twelve months. Nearby the gloss rules like the Rosh, which is astonishing against all these decisors. Even in the Rosh it is not certain; the Tur wrote it in his name. What the gloss brings nearby from Responsa Rashba, that if he divorces within twelve months he must give ketubah and nichsei tzon barzel, was not said about this moredet but about "ma'is alai."
Even if she is pregnant. Meaning that he is not obligated in support because of the fetus in her womb. But once she gives birth, she is not obligated to nurse the child. See the responsum itself.
Her husband inherits her. Although according to the Mechaber she lost her ketubah after the announcement, he nevertheless inherits her. So too according to the Rama after twelve months, and this is agreed by all opinions: even where we compel him to divorce, if she dies before the get he inherits her, as written by the Rif and other decisors in the laws of the Metivta. Although he is exempt from all obligations, she is the one who rebelled and loses, while he does not lose. It appears he eats the fruits even after twelve months, and so Responsa Ralbach writes in the name of the Rashba. Darkei Moshe writes that according to the Rosh, after twelve months he does not eat the fruits; this is also inferred from that responsum. But if he loses his fruit rights, it is difficult why he inherits her, unless the Rosh holds that a husband’s inheritance is biblical.
And her burial. The Rosh wrote that before twelve months he is obligated in her burial because he is obligated to give her the ketubah. It follows that after twelve months he is exempt, since he is exempt from the ketubah. So wrote Darkei Moshe. According to the Rambam’s approach, after four weeks the same law applies. Bach wrote that the reason he is exempt from burial is that Chazal enacted burial in exchange for nichsei tzon barzel, not nichsei melog, and here it is uncertain whether she acquired the nichsei tzon barzel. Therefore he is exempt. However, it appears that if she already seized or acquired the nichsei tzon barzel and after her death he inherits them, he is obligated in burial, unlike the implication of the Bach.
The husband has no claim over her. This implies he does not eat the fruits, but he inherits her, as stated. According to the Rashba, who holds he eats the fruits, he can certainly protest that she not sell anything. According to the Rosh, who holds he does not eat fruits and the marriage is as though absent except that he inherits her as a relative, he cannot protest; so wrote Responsa Ralbach. Responsa Mahari Weil, however, rules that according to everyone he can protest, and so rules the gloss nearby. Bedieved, if she sold, it requires study whether he can void the sale. When she says "ma'is alai," he can always divorce her and she has no ketubah. Her law at every moment is like a moredet of "I want him" after twelve months. According to the Rambam, there she acquired nichsei tzon barzel without seizure, so he is certainly obligated in burial. According to the Ramban and Rashba, the sugya speaks of "ma'is alai" and it is doubtful whether she acquired nichsei tzon barzel; this creates a double doubt to obligate him in burial, because perhaps the halacha is like the Rambam, and perhaps even according to the Ramban she acquired them under Talmudic law. Since by double doubt we extract money, he is obligated in burial.
He gives her her nichsei tzon barzel. This is specifically according to the Rosh. According to the other decisors, she loses immediately after four weeks. What he wrote, "but not what he wrote for her," see the Tur, siman 65, which does not imply this, but rather only where she has no ketubah she has no gifts. I already wrote at the beginning of the siman that even if he has relations with her by coercion, she has the law of a moredet.
And some say that nowadays, when men do not marry, etc. Meaning that in Talmudic times they delayed twelve months because he had no loss, since he could marry another woman. Nowadays we do not delay. The Chelkat Mechokek wrote that even according to those who do not permit him to marry another, nevertheless they permit him to divorce her against her will and pay the ketubah. If so, what is written that if she does not want, they permit him to marry another, means all the more that they permit him to divorce her against her will. What is written nearby that after twelve months she must accept the get against her will is because she has already lost her ketubah and has no claim. Within twelve months, even though he can divorce her against her will, he must pay her ketubah.
Specifically with a married woman, etc. So wrote Maharik at the end of shoresh 63. However, perhaps this is not limited to an arusah; rather, if the matter was done before beit din and they agree that she wronged him, it will not lead so much to frivolity. The reason we do not permit him to marry another is concern that each man will say she rebelled against him. If the matter comes before beit din or the city leaders, that concern is absent. What the Rama wrote, "and it appears to me," applies even to a married woman. See Darkei Moshe, siman 154, in the name of Teshuvot Maimoniyot, end of Hilchot Ishut, that even with an arusah they do not permit him to marry another.
We do not compel divorce. Meaning we do not compel him to divorce either one. He is not obligated to divorce the second, since according to the Ramban she is permitted even lechatchilah; and he is not obligated to divorce the first, because she rebelled against him. So wrote the Chelkat Mechokek.
Before her illness. The Rosh brings a Yerushalmi that says specifically if she rebelled before becoming niddah, and the Rosh disagrees. The Maggid Mishneh also brings a dispute about this. The Ran and Shiltei Gibborim explain that the rebellion must precede her illness. The Rama brings a dispute regarding illness; perhaps he holds this is a greater novelty, that not only with niddah, where there is a set end, must she rebel before becoming niddah, but even with illness she must have rebelled before. The dispute he brings appears to be that Maharam holds there is no distinction, while the Ran and Shiltei Gibborim disagree. From the Rama’s language it appears that he holds mainly like Maharam, since the Rosh agrees with him.
Even if her husband was a sailor. Not only if he is at home and can bring his conjugal interval closer, but even if he is leaving home and does not intend to return for six months, the days of rebellion begin immediately. Chelkat Mechokek.
And she rebelled in order to pain him. If she rebelled and said "ma'is alai," she has no loss, for presumably an arusah has no ketubah. Rather this refers to a moredet who says, "I want him but I want to pain him," and they announce about her.
And some say that also a yevamah, etc. This follows the decisors who hold that the mitzvah of yibbum takes precedence; they write a letter of rebellion against her. See below, siman 165.
