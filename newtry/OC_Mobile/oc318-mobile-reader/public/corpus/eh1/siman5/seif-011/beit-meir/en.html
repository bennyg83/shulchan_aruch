Hagah, "and some say." See Responsa Pnei Yehoshua, part 14, siman 3, who inclines to be lenient in every case. He asks that according to Rashi's approach that agency for a gentile exists stringently, why did the Gemara need to ask whether instructing a gentile is a shevut in cases of negative prohibitions? Let it be forbidden because of agency, as though the Jew did it himself. One could answer that there is no agency for transgression, but that works only according to the view that the rule depends on whether the agent could choose to act; according to the view that where the agent is not subject to the prohibition there is agency for transgression, the difficulty remains. At first I wished to say that regarding Shabbat, with a gentile, agency for transgression does not apply, because only where the act itself is repugnant before the Omnipresent, such as castration or muzzling, does agency for transgression fit; the transgression has occurred. But Shabbat labor is prohibited so that Israel and all that is his should rest. The labor itself is not inherently a transgression, as shown by the fact that one may place a pot by the fire before Shabbat and let it cook on Shabbat, and conversely a gentile who rests on Shabbat is liable. Thus a gentile acting for a Jew on Shabbat would not be a case of agency for transgression, since the Jew is resting. Similar distinctions exist regarding agency for mitzvot, for bodily mitzvot such as tefillin cannot be fulfilled through an agent. However, this answer is difficult from the law that one does not recite Havdalah over a lamp lit on Shabbat even by a gentile, because it did not rest from forbidden labor, implying the act is considered a transgression. One can still press that it is not a full transgression for agency, but has the trace of transgression, as the Shulchan Aruch implies. Yet Rashi at the beginning of Mi SheHechshich explains the Gemara's question as based on agency: why was he permitted to give it to a gentile, since the gentile is his agent on Shabbat? Rashi did not explain simply because of the shevut of instructing a gentile. Ran in chapter HaSocher Et HaPoalim also explains this as proof that shevut by instruction applies even before Shabbat. This implies that according to the Rosh, who limits the shevut of instruction to Shabbat itself, one must explain the issue as agency, and stringently there is agency for a gentile. Still, in my humble opinion this is no proof that there is agency for transgression even where the agent is not obligated, for just as there is no agency for a gentile generally yet stringently there is, so too even though there is no agency for transgression generally, stringently there may be. This also fits the Rosh's approach: if instruction before Shabbat is not shevut, why did the Sages forbid renting a bathhouse to a gentile at the beginning of Ein Ma'amidin? It must be because of agency, as Rashi and Tosafot explain there. Nevertheless, this raises why Rav Ashi in Shabbat 150 asks from shevut of instruction rather than from agency. The answer is that instruction on Shabbat is more severe, containing both agency and shevut, and the law of agency stringently is not explicit in the Mishnah. Rashi's proof from the Mishnah of a gentile who came to extinguish is not merely agency, for the Sages were lenient with agency in Mi SheHechshich because a person is distressed over his money; if so they should have been lenient at a fire as well. Rather, instruction on Shabbat has the additional severity of shevut of speech, and they did not permit it even for monetary loss. Thus Pnei Yehoshua's difficulty on Rashi is resolved. My sharp son-in-law R. Yehudah later showed me that Ran himself at the beginning of Mi SheHechshich sensed my question about a fire and answered well according to his approach. Rashi, however, did not accept that distinction and therefore explained as above. The upshot is that the laws cited by Rashba and Ran about one who accepted Shabbat early and whether another Jew may do labor for him depend on the principles of agency and obligation, and the matter requires study. I add only that the difficulty from Tosafot in Shabbat 121 can be answered: Rav Ashi's proof from the Mishnah of "do not extinguish" need not be limited to Torah prohibition; according to the conclusion it can refer to extinguishing not needed for its own sake, itself rabbinic, and so it proves the breadth of the shevut.
