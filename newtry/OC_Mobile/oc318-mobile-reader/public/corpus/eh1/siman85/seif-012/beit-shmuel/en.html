(א) They gave it to me as a gift. We are not concerned that perhaps she stole it from her husband, as we are concerned regarding a found object, as written above, because a gift becomes known and one cannot testify falsely that it was given as a gift. When she says this, we say that she certainly is not lying. Ba'al HaTerumot there, and the Maggid. As for the Rambam’s ruling in chapter 9, halacha 9, that if shtarot are issued in her name she is not believed to say they are hers and must bring proof, as written in Choshen Mishpat, siman 62, that case is different, because it discusses where she conducts business in the house. So wrote Ba'al HaTerumot, gate 6. One can also say that movable property is different, as written in Ba'al HaTerumot, gate 51, and in the Tur there in the name of the Ramah.
(ב) They are from your labor. Meaning, perhaps they are from her labor, and it is not speaking where he makes a definite claim. Therefore, he does not have an oath against her.
(ג) She must bring proof. For the Rambam holds that all money found in the woman’s hand is presumed to be nichsei melog.
(ד) Specifically with hidden money. This is what the Tur wrote in siman 86 in the name of the Rash and the Rosh: if the husband seized money and she says, "it is mine" or "it belongs to so-and-so," she is believed when the money was not hidden. All the more so here, where the money is in her hand, that she is believed to remove it from him, for there, when she says it belongs to so-and-so, he does not inherit it and does not eat the produce. The Perishah discusses this at length and wrote that even according to the Rash and the Rosh she is not believed to say "it is mine" to remove it from the husband. What is written there, that she is believed to say "it is mine," means it is mine in the status of nichsei melog. He overlooked what the Maggid Mishneh wrote at the end of chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh and at the end of chapter 7 of Hilchot Sheluchin in the name of the Rashba and several decisors, that she is believed to say it is mine and the husband has no right in it. So too wrote Nimukei Yosef. But the distinction made in the gloss between hidden money and open money is not a distinction we have found in this. The Derishah and Bach discuss this at length. What the Bach wrote does not appear to me. So wrote Maharshal: according to the Rash, she is believed even with hidden money. According to those decisors who hold that she is believed to say that he has no right in it, they necessarily do not hold of the presumption that whatever is in the woman’s hand is presumed to be nichsei melog. However, there is difficulty from the responsum of the Rashba brought by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 86, which implies that the Rashba does hold of this presumption, while here he holds that she is believed to say that he has no right in it. One must say that the responsum speaks where she conducts business. See Gidulei Terumah there. According to these decisors, even if she bought land from him, she too is believed to say that it was given to her as a gift on condition that he has no right, and he does not eat the produce. The statement in the Talmud that he eats the produce speaks where she does not make such a claim. One can also say that when she bought land and he wrote a shtar in her name, she no longer has a migo that it belongs to so-and-so. Therefore she is not believed to say that he has no right, for she is believed only through a migo that it belongs to so-and-so.
(ה) Specifically when she does not conduct business. This also applies to the first clause: when she conducts business, she is not believed to say it was given to me as a gift; rather, it belongs entirely to the husband. See the responsum of the Rashba brought by the Beit Yosef, and see Kesef Mishneh. When he deposits with her, it is like conducting business, as written in the Rosh and Tur, siman 86. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 62. It is explained in Tosafot there that when she conducts business, she is not believed to say "they are mine" or "they belong to so-and-so," even if the husband died and there is no one who claims with certainty against her, as written regarding notes and shtarot issued in her name. There it discusses where her husband died and the orphans claim perhaps, as written in the responsum of the Rashba brought by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 86. However, where she conducts business and says, "you gave it to me as a gift," one can say that she is believed because she cannot brazenly lie, according to the reasoning of the Maggid nearby.
(ו) And she has a migo, etc. She is believed in every case, and even when she conducts business in the house. Perhaps even according to the Rambam’s approach, that he holds whatever is in the woman’s hand is presumed to be nichsei melog, nevertheless she is believed. Although it is a migo against a presumption, since in any case she is holding it, the issue of a migo against a presumption is an unresolved question, and we do not remove it from her hand, as written nearby. The Rav Rama wrote this law in the name of Responsa Maharam, as written in Darkei Moshe. At first glance this is difficult: how did he learn this law from Responsa Maharam? There the case was that she said, "you gave it to me," which involves brazenness. From where does he know she is believed when she did not say "you gave it to me"? One can answer that there, brazenness was not relevant, because that case involved her rebelling against him, and then she certainly is brazen before him. In the responsum of the Rashba brought by the Beit Yosef, end of seif 7, this law is also explained, but in Responsa Maharam it is explained that even when she conducts business, she is believed with a migo. However, from Tosafot in this sugya it implies that when she conducts business she is not believed, even though she has a migo. For they wrote that when one who conducts business deposited an item, she is not believed to say it is mine or belongs to so-and-so. There she has a migo, since she could give it to so-and-so, as written in Tosafot there. See Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 62. In the gloss there it is written: a woman who wrote that the money in her hand belongs to others, if it is known that she had money, she is believed. The Sema explains that this discusses where she has no migo. But if she has a migo, she is believed even where it is not known that she has money, even if she conducts business in the house. This follows the view of Maharam. The Shach challenged him and brought these Tosafot, which hold that with one who conducts business, a migo is not effective. But he challenged the Sema for no reason, for the Sema wrote only to explain there the words of the Rav Rama, and the Rav Rama ruled here that she is believed with a migo.
(ז) You gave it to me as a gift. The Maggid wrote that she is believed because she is not brazen enough to lie in his presence. Ba'al HaTerumot asked: why is she not believed when she says, "it was given to me as a gift on condition that he has no right," with a migo that she could have said, "you gave it to me"? He answered that we do not say a migo that weakens the claim, for according to her claim he does not inherit her, while when she says "you gave it to me," he does inherit her. The practical difference from this is that if her husband already died, she is believed. The Shach, siman 62, challenged him that his question is not a question, because we do not say migo here since it is a migo against a presumption, for everything in the woman’s hand is presumed to be nichsei melog. He discusses this at length. This does not appear to me, for although everything is presumed to be nichsei melog, nevertheless she is holding it, and a migo against a presumption is an unresolved question. We do not remove it from her hand when she has a migo. Therefore his question was proper. One should not say that the Shach holds that even what is in her hand is considered as if it were in the husband’s hand and she comes to extract it, for that is not so. If she were considered the one extracting, then the oath would always belong to the husband, for he is the defendant. Rather, certainly, whenever she holds it, she is the defendant, and when she has a migo she is believed and we do not remove it from her hand. The whole law of the Rambam discusses where she has no migo, for when money is found with her, she can no longer give it to another, and although she could have given it earlier, that is a retroactive migo. She also can no longer say that it is hers on condition that he has no right in it. When she says, "you gave it to me," she is believed without a migo, because she cannot be brazen, as written in the name of the Maggid. However, the Maggid’s reason is still difficult, for we hold that where there is monetary benefit, one can be brazen, as written at the beginning of siman 17. Perhaps he holds that maintaining what is in her hand is different, and in this kind of case we say she cannot be brazen. Also, one should not compare one kind of brazenness to another. It is different when she is his wife; she cannot be brazen even because of monetary benefit, unlike there, where she wants to leave him. In Gidulei Terumah he challenged Ba'al HaTerumot: this is a migo of brazenness, to say "you gave it to me," and we do not say a migo of brazenness. In the Shach’s gloss he wondered about him, for it is explicit in the Ran that we do say a migo of brazenness to exempt from money; only to exempt from an oath we do not say it. The proof is from those goats that ate shelled barley, where he is believed to claim they ate up to their value with a migo that he could have said, "I bought them," even though had he said "I bought them," he would have had to be brazen. It is possible that Gidulei Terumah holds that she is believed to say "you gave it to me" because she cannot be brazen, as the Maggid wrote. Learn from this that although it involves monetary benefit, nevertheless she cannot be brazen. Therefore he properly asked: what is Ba'al HaTerumot’s question? We do not say a migo of brazenness. Necessarily, here, "you gave it to me" is brazenness, even though it involves monetary benefit.
(ח) To me as a gift. It is explained in Ba'al HaTerumot and brought in Kesef Mishneh that with movables known to belong to the husband, she is not believed that the husband gave them to her as a gift. Even according to what was written in the name of the Maggid, that she cannot be brazen, nevertheless regarding an item known to belong to the husband she is not believed. She is believed only regarding something that is not known to belong to the husband, but whatever is in the woman’s hand is presumed to be nichsei melog. There, the presumption that she cannot be brazen is effective.
