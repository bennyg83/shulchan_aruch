They should not be sold. So ruled the Rambam in chapter 22 according to his version. In the first clause, Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel disagrees and says he should not sell, and the halacha follows him. Regarding olives, Rabbi Yehudah disagrees, and the halacha does not follow him. Regarding whether she can compel him to sell, see the Tur. Where he is not permitted to sell, she cannot compel him to sell. But where he can sell, the Ramah holds that when he does not want to sell, she can compel him to sell. According to this, a goat and other things that he cannot sell, and he uses until the principal is exhausted, she too cannot compel him to sell. Only regarding old slaves, the Ramah holds that he can sell against her will, and she too can sell against his will. According to the Rosh, although he can sell against her will, nevertheless she cannot compel him to sell. This is not comparable to where money fell to her, because there they necessarily must buy something with the money, and then each one can prevent buying something that does not produce fruit. However, something that does not produce fruit and has no use, and is fit to be sold, she too can compel him. This dispute is learned from the Mishnah: if slaves fell to her, according to the first Tanna he can sell against her will. The Rosh holds that the first Tanna is concerned for his benefit, as written in Tosafot. Although there is a disadvantage for her because of the honor of her father’s house, they are not concerned for her disadvantage, only for his benefit. From this we learn that everywhere we follow his benefit. Even according to the Rambam’s approach, that regarding slaves we are concerned for her benefit because of the honor of her father’s house, nevertheless regarding olives, where the honor of her father’s house is not as noticeable, as written in Perishah, he ruled that we hold like the first Tanna. It automatically follows that we are always concerned for his benefit, except where the honor of her father’s house is recognizable. According to the Ramah, what the first Tanna says, "he should sell," means that either one can compel the sale, and she too can compel him. According to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, he cannot compel her because of the honor of her father’s house. According to the Rosh, one must say that what is taught regarding a goat and other things, that he eats until the principal is exhausted, means if he wants, he eats; and if he wants, one can say that he may sell. So too it appears in Tosafot, s.v. "the rule." If an old tree fell to her, he is not permitted to cut it down, as written in Perek HaMekabel. The Ran wrote that this is because the exhaustion is visible.
