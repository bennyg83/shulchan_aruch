Section 6, "one does not ask." The custom is to speak to women when entering the synagogue in a manner resembling requests for good, and they answer. Kiddushin 76 implies that when she answers an inquiry of peace, the issue of a woman's voice applies, as the Beit Shmuel cites. Perhaps, however, this is like what the decisors wrote regarding greeting a mourner, that in our time it is not considered a formal inquiry of peace. So too here: the Talmud was concerned only with a greeting of "shalom," not with the phrases now customary. The practice of going after a circumcision to say "mazal tov" to the woman who gave birth is not a form of asking a woman's peace that must be avoided. It is rather a prayerful wish that the matter be auspicious for the mother's family as well, for the Sages said that when a son is born in a family, the whole family is healed. Although lineage is written "by their families, by their fathers' houses," Scripture also calls Mordechai both a Judean and a Benjaminite, teaching that merit can be associated with two tribes. Thus ancestral merit applies also through the maternal side, and a child can bring merit to the mother's family above. Therefore one may honor one's mother after her death through Torah, good deeds, Kaddish, and prayer; the measure of good is greater than that of punishment. Punishment is visited through fathers only when children maintain their ancestors' deeds, whereas kindness extends to thousands through fathers and mothers alike. I wrote elsewhere that anything not asked as an affectionate inquiry of peace is not included in the prohibition. People customarily ask how women are doing in health and success, pray for them, and bless them; our greetings are also lighter than the greetings of the Sages' era, as is clear in Shulchan Aruch regarding the twelve months of mourning. Nevertheless, I do not usually go solely for this purpose if I can avoid it, because of how people view it. When I attend the circumcision meal, I say "mazal tov" there more than once; presumably she answers "may you be blessed," and her blessing is not light in my eyes on her day of joy in a mitzvah. Just as the "mazal tov" for her includes her family, perhaps her response of blessing stands for her whole family. That day begins the days of purity after the birth of a male, indicating a change from judgment to mercy. When I do not attend the circumcision meal, I rely on saying "mazal tov" several times in the synagogue, intending all the families; presumably the souls of the mother's family answer blessing to those who awaken good fortune for them. Then I am like most townspeople, who do not go to say "mazal tov," but only invited guests and relatives go.
