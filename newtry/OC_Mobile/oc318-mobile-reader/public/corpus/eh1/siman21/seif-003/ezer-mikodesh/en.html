(I also found) what I wrote elsewhere regarding praying for a woman even in her presence, that this is not like asking after a woman's welfare. When she is not present there is plainly no concern at all; on the contrary, one is obligated to pray, even when she knows of it. But even in her presence there is no concern, as is explicit with Elijah the prophet and Elisha the prophet, peace be upon them, who prayed on behalf of the women of Zarephath and Shunem, of blessed memory, and the like, and it appears that their prayers were in their presence. Asking that she have children is also a prayer for her welfare, and requests for sustenance are likewise included in prayer for welfare. Rather, the prohibition is only a conventional greeting of peace, in the worldly style of showing affection to one another; that is what is withheld from women. For this reason the simple custom is to go and say "mazal tov" to a woman who has given birth after the circumcision, and likewise to say "mazal tov" to women at a wedding and the like. Only an inquiry of peace is an especially affectionate form, as in "for the sake of my brothers and companions I will speak peace," which indicates fellowship and intimacy; this is avoided because of emotional closeness, whereas other forms of blessing and prayer for them are proper. Similarly, the custom of blessing female relatives, such as nieces, by placing the hands over their heads in the priestly-blessing fashion is not objectionable so long as there is no bodily contact, hand to hand. Even where parts are uncovered, if flesh does not touch flesh there is no objection, for we find the Sages' concern only in the case of counting coins from his hand into hers in order to derive pleasure, hand to hand. Looking, however, is forbidden even at clothing when he knows the woman and recognizes her form, for sight is the first agent of desire, under "do not stray after your eyes." Touching a garment is not included in "whoever touches her," and the stringencies of distancing are especially strict with a menstruant, more than with others, as is known from the permissibility of eating from one bowl with another man's wife. Even with one's menstruant wife there is sometimes no concern in touching the garment she is wearing. Thus looking is more severe than touching clothing; if there were a real trace of prohibition in such contact, the holy tanna would not have carried the bride. Carrying her on his shoulders, however, has an element resembling embrace, and one who is not wholly unmoved by it should refrain. The later authorities forbade writing, "Say shalom to your wife." Some, however, write, "From my wife to your wife, shalom," and I have done so several times. Although I have not now seen an explicit source permitting it, reason indicates that it is permitted: just as one's agent is like himself, and therefore writing "say shalom to your wife" is the husband's greeting through an agent, so too when the wife makes her husband an agent to convey greetings on her behalf, it is only a woman greeting a woman. Even if he did not hear it from his wife, he may write it presumptively, because when he tells her to do so she will certainly agree, and one may confer a benefit on a person in his absence, since conveying welfare to Jews in particular is a mitzvah and a benefit. Telling my wife to greet her friend is plainly unobjectionable, for he is not greeting in his own name. Some write, "my wife extends peace to your wife," which is still lighter, since it can mean an inquiry after past welfare. The wording "from my wife to your wife, regards" or "peace" is preferable, to avoid the appearance of falsehood; it may be understood as explicit even when stated generally. It appears that a son may ask after the welfare of his mother who is married to his stepfather, and a father after his married daughter's welfare, without concern, as between man and man. Although I have not now seen this stated explicitly, reason supports it: if, with respect to seclusion, which is far more severe and rooted in Torah concern and ancient enactment, father and daughter and mother and son are treated as man with man, all the more so here. Likewise, a granddaughter and further descendants, and a grandmother and further ascendants, have the same status for seclusion as son and mother or father and daughter, and the same follows for inquiries of peace. For minors below the age of seclusion prohibition, the same boundary applies to inquiries of peace. Although asking peace is forbidden even in public, the two matters are equivalent by this argument; only when many are present, or when her husband is present, is an inquiry of peace different, for it creates closeness for another day.
