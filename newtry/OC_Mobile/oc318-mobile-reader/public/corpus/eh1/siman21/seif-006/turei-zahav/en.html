Derishah wrote that it seems only lying in her lap is forbidden, but examining his head while he is sitting may be permitted. However, Responsa Rashba siman 1188 implies that even examining his head is not proper, for the Gemara compares this to pouring water over the hands. Rashi explains that everyone knows there is no evil inclination in the World to Come, and therefore there is no immodesty there. This implies that the primary issue is lying in her lap. But if so, why did they need to mention examining his head? It appears to me that there were two reasons Abraham did not wish them to enter: first, because a person should not perform affectionate acts with his wife before others, for reasons of modesty, like the case of one who was intimate with his wife under a fig tree and was flogged; and here too lying in her lap resembles intimacy. Second, even examining his head alone is improper because it can arouse thoughts in others by reminding them of marital relations. Abraham answered that even lying in her lap was not forbidden there, because in that world no modesty is needed and everyone knows there is no marital use; if so, there is no prohibition for him, and all the more for others. From this it is proven that even examining his head alone is not proper before others. It appears there is a distinction: examining the head alone is problematic before others specifically with one's wife, because whoever sees it is reminded of their marital closeness and may come to improper thoughts. With a daughter, sister, or granddaughter, where there is no association of marital relations, it is permitted before others. But lying in her lap is certainly forbidden with all of them, even not before others, for it is a great closeness and touches the boundary of arayot; even with an unmarried woman, even examining the head alone is forbidden. This appears correct to me.
(א) But it is permitted to inquire of her husband. Maharshal wrote that based on this, people permitted the wording of our letters of greeting, in which they mention the wife, though the custom is still to be stringent and not mention her by name. My teacher and father-in-law wrote that this refers to the beginning of a letter, where one writes "and to your wife," etc.; but at the end of a letter, where people commonly write "say greetings to so-and-so," it is forbidden to write "say greetings to your wife," for that sends greetings to her through her husband, which is forbidden. It appears to me that all this is because it displays affection through asking after her welfare. But where there is a need, for example she was unwell or the like, one may write, "inform me of your wife's welfare."
