(א) On the definition of chuppah in our times. And see above (the correct reference is the beginning of) siman 54, where it is written that in our times chuppah means standing the groom and bride under a canopy spread over poles. Accordingly, until that moment it is not considered chuppah for any purpose that depends on chuppah — unlike the view cited by Rema in siman 54 that the bridal veil (hinuma) constitutes chuppah. There is also another practical difference, as I wrote in Yoreh De'ah siman 342 — see there. It appears this follows the view of the other authorities cited by the Beit Yosef here in the name of the Ran, that chuppah does not mean yichud (seclusion). And all that is stated here — that chuppah without intimacy acquires — refers to a virgin. But for a non-virgin, chuppah does not acquire, as stated in siman 63; see what I wrote there on this matter.
(ב) "A mortally ill woman etc." — Analysis of the Rosh's responsum; challenge on the Rama. A mortally ill woman etc. This is copied from the responsum of the Rosh (kelal 54): Reuven divided his property and left land as a dowry on condition that if she died before marriage the land would remain as hekdesh. She became mortally ill, and in order to divest the land from hekdesh her relatives went and placed her in her bed during her illness (under the chuppah) with one of her relatives, performed the seven blessings so he would inherit her — and she died of that illness. He had not been intimate with her. The Rosh concluded that he does not inherit her, since she was not fit for intimacy — comparable to a case of entering chuppah and becoming a niddah. The Rosh replied that this chuppah is entirely void: first, because she was not fit for intimacy as stated; and second, we follow the presumed intent of Reuven, who wanted to benefit his soul by giving to hekdesh, not that his heirs should inherit — he only reserved it so his daughter could marry, and certainly it was not his intent that someone obtain it through artifice. Therefore the presumed intent is proven and the land is hekdesh. As for what the responsum says "she was not fit for intimacy, as stated" — the "as stated" refers to the questioner's formulation, but not entirely in his sense, because the questioner compared her to a niddah, which is not the Rosh's view, since the Rosh holds a niddah's chuppah is a valid chuppah for inheritance, as written in the Tur in his name in this siman. Rather, the Rosh must hold that the mortally ill case is worse than a niddah: with a niddah, at any rate, the husband intends to be intimate with her after she purifies, and on this basis we say the chuppah validly acquires since the whole purpose of the chuppah is that. But here his only intention is to inherit her, not to be intimate with her, since she is in mortal danger — therefore even the Rosh agrees he does not inherit her. I have great difficulty with the Rama here. The Rosh in that responsum only divested the heirs in the specific circumstances where the woman had no property of her own — the father had provided for her marriage through this arrangement, and if she died before marriage it would go to hekdesh. Here it was as if she had not married, since she was not fit for intimacy, and the intent to divest hekdesh through this arrangement was not as if she had died a single woman. But this is different from a woman who already has property of her own and intends that whichever man betroths her should be her heir through the kiddushin — why should his actions not benefit him? For even a dying person's verbal bequests stand in financial matters, and all the more so here where she performed a significant act and accepted kiddushin for this purpose — certainly it constitutes full kiddushin. Furthermore, it seems that even in the Rosh's case there was full kiddushin, and one who is intimate with her during her illness incurs the death penalty — only for purposes of inheritance over property she did not acquire except through the marriage in accordance with the father's declaration is this not considered ordinary marriage. But here, since the property is already hers, it seems practically that he inherits her; see what I wrote in siman 90, seif 4, for clear proof.
The annotator's words: The Beit Shmuel in subsection 4 writes "do not argue that one must return etc." — the Taz also challenged this, and on account of it wrote that if she had property the husband acquires it by reason of waiver etc., but did not answer the challenge of why the dowry need not be returned. It seems more reasonable that waiver does not apply here, since one may say the chuppah was performed in the expectation she would recover etc. Similarly according to the Rambam's view etc. — so too here. End of annotation. I do not understand what moved him to write these things. First, the Rama may be dealing with a case of mortal illness meaning recovery from this illness is impossible and she will not be fit for intimacy, deteriorating progressively until after a year — even so, the husband does not inherit her after a year since she was not fit for intimacy from the day she entered the chuppah until after her death. Furthermore, one must examine the Rama's words: "Even though she was brought with the bed under the chuppah so that her husband would inherit her, it is not chuppah at all, since she is not fit for intimacy at all and her husband does not inherit her." He began with one statement — "so that her husband would inherit her" — and concluded with two: "it is not chuppah" and "her husband does not inherit her." He should have written only: "Since she is not fit for intimacy, the husband does not inherit her" — why add "it is not chuppah at all"? Rather, the Rama derived two things from the Rosh's responsum: (1) it is not chuppah at all making her his wife, since she was not fit for intimacy, and (2) her husband does not inherit her. He wrote simply that "the husband does not inherit her" — this applies for whichever reason is applicable, whether because she was not acquired through this chuppah since she was not fit for intimacy, or because the dowry must be returned. The clause "her husband does not inherit her" does not depend on the preceding "since she is not fit for intimacy" — the latter clause refers to "it is not chuppah at all," not to the bed issue. And regarding the Rosh's responsum, the challenge has no basis at all, since the responsum states: "In order to divest the land from hekdesh, her relatives went and placed her in her bed with one of her relatives, performed the seven blessings so that he should inherit her" — one can understand "so that he should inherit her" to mean that her heirs should inherit her after her death, since she had a marriage, and it would leave hekdesh, and they would inherit her. One can also say that the chuppah was with one of her relatives who would inherit her anyway — for it says "placed her in her bed with a relative" and "performed seven blessings," implying the marriage was with one of her relatives. If so, he is her heir regardless, and the question was whether the arrangement could remove the property from hekdesh. Furthermore, even if they brought her to chuppah with someone who would not otherwise inherit her, the law is that the dowry would not revert to her heirs if there were a full marriage — for we hold all financial conditions are valid even in Torah matters. If so, all the more if he stipulated before the marriage that if she dies within the year the heirs do not return it — such a condition would certainly be valid and he need not return it. Here, since they brought her to chuppah precisely so the husband would inherit her when she dies shortly after, there is no greater stipulation than this — this is tantamount to a condition not to return the dowry, which is a valid condition made before the marriage. Accordingly, even if one takes the Rama's words at face value — that the husband does not inherit her since she was not fit for intimacy — the challenge that "one should say he must return it anyway within the first year" has no force, since the very reason they brought her to the chuppah was so he would inherit her, and this is as if they stipulated not to return the dowry, a valid condition made before the marriage. Furthermore, the Rama wrote simply: "A mortally ill woman, even though she was brought with the bed under the chuppah" — implying no distinction between what she herself brought and what her father gave her; in all cases the husband does not inherit her. The Taz's main challenge is: if she had property of her own, why does the husband not inherit it by reason of her waiver? So the challenge on the Rama from "he should return it anyway since it is within the first year" is entirely without basis, since the Rama means the husband does not inherit even what she herself brought in — and therefore the only applicable reason is that she is not fit for intimacy. Even his statement that "it seems more reasonable that waiver does not apply here, since one may say the chuppah was performed hoping she would recover" — this is not intellectually satisfying: if so, why did she rush into the chuppah and not wait until she recovered? And do not say she was compelled by the husband's wish — she should have realized he was doing this only in order to inherit her, since she is not fit for intimacy and he refused to wait for her recovery; certainly his intent was to inherit her, and she should not have entered the chuppah. Since she entered the chuppah with him, she certainly acquiesced and waived, and he acquires through waiver — since she entered the chuppah for this purpose. And this is unlike the Rambam's case of a niddah who entered chuppah, where he does not inherit her — for there she did not consider the matter and did not waive, whereas here she considered it and waived. As for what the Chelkat Mechokek proves from the ruling in Yevamot (folio 57) that there is chuppah for those forbidden in marriage — even though she will never be fit for intimacy — that is no proof at all. There the reason is not that chuppah does not acquire when she is not fit for intimacy, but rather that she is penalized since she is preserved for a forbidden intimacy and chuppah is close to intimacy. The first tanna holds that even from erusin she may not eat terumah, even though erusin involves no acquisition — clearly it is a penalty. The Talmud explicitly states (folio 58) that chuppah does not acquire there. See what I wrote in siman 7.
