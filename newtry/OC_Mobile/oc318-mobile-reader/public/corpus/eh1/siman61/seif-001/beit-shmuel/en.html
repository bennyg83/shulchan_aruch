(א) "But if he brought her to chuppah etc. and afterward brought her to chuppah etc." But if he brought her to chuppah and she became a niddah while in the chuppah, we say the beginning of the chuppah acquires. So ruled the Rosh; see also what I wrote below in the name of the Mordekhai and the Hagahot Asheri.
(ב) "She is still as betrothed." She is still as betrothed. He does not inherit her and does not become impure for her — so wrote the Maggid Mishneh (ch. 10), since so hold the Rambam and some Geonim. It appears likewise that she does not have the other conditions of the ketubah. This also seems correct from what is written in siman 55: according to the Rambam, even if one writes a ketubah for a betrothed woman it does not avail, as he wrote it only on condition of bringing her into marriage — therefore she cannot collect from encumbered property. And since the case where she became a niddah during chuppah remains unresolved (teku) as to whether she is considered fully married, if she is not considered fully married the writing does not avail and she has none of the ketubah conditions. The Chelkat Mechokek challenged the Rambam, writing that his words are puzzling: in Ketubot it is taught — Ravin posed the question: if a woman entered the chuppah but was not yet intimate, what is the law? Does the affinity of chuppah acquire, or only the affinity of intimacy? Rav Ashi then posed: if she entered chuppah and became a niddah, what is the law? If you say the affinity of chuppah acquires — that applies to a chuppah fit for intimacy, but perhaps a chuppah not fit for intimacy does not acquire. Or perhaps there is no difference — teku (unresolved). The Beit Yosef responded: as is well known, the method of the Geonim and the Rambam is that when a question is posed in the "if you say" formulation, it is as if the first question is thereby resolved — namely, that the affinity of chuppah acquires. Ravin's question was not about inheritance, since when a father hands his daughter to the husband's agents the husband inherits her — all the more so with chuppah — so Ravin's question must concern other matters. If so, why did Rav Ashi's second question address inheritance? These two questions are linked in the "if you say" form and are bundled together. One cannot say that handing her to the husband's agents is superior to entering chuppah without intimacy — for on folio 48 the Talmud takes it as obvious that entering chuppah without intimacy makes her more properly called "married" than being handed to the husband's agents. For there it states that if the father handed her to the husband's agents and she committed adultery she is executed by strangulation like a married woman, not by stoning like a betrothed woman — since "to commit adultery in her father's house" excludes one handed to the husband's agents. The Talmud then challenges: perhaps it is the case of entering chuppah without intimacy that is excluded from "in her father's house" — since once she entered chuppah she is considered married even without intimacy — but one handed to the husband's agents, who has not yet entered chuppah, is not excluded and is still considered like a virgin. This makes plain that to the Talmud, entering chuppah without intimacy is more clearly "married" than being handed to the husband's agents. If so, since one handed to the husband's agents is inherited by her husband as stated in siman 56, all the more so one who entered chuppah without intimacy is inherited — examine the matter closely. To what I wrote in siman 36 there is no difficulty — all the questions concern inheritance: even though one handed to the husband's agents is inherited, that is by reason of her waiver (mechilah), whereas a chuppah in the father's house poses a question, since one may argue this is worse in that regard as I wrote there. Nor is there proof from the questioner who asked "perhaps the case of entering chuppah should be executed by strangulation while the case of handing to agents by stoning" — for certainly regarding marriage the case of handing to agents is inferior, but regarding inheritance it may be superior by reason of waiver. Do not ask why the Talmud did not resolve the matter from the baraita regarding one whose bread was baked — for there one may say the text refers to a case where he was intimate with her. A prima facie difficulty against the Rambam's view that chuppah means yichud (seclusion): what is the Talmud's question about a niddah's chuppah? If seclusion acquires, obviously it does not acquire with a niddah, since seclusion with her is forbidden — how could one imagine that he acquires her through a prohibited seclusion, as the Ran challenged at the beginning of Kiddushin regarding how one acquires through a forbidden act? Similarly, how does a forbidden seclusion acquire? One may answer that the case involves a woman who was pure, he secluded with her, and afterward she became a niddah — contrary to the Rosh whom I cited at the beginning of siman 67. The Bach and the Chelkat Mechokek similarly derived from the Mishnah in the chapter "Even if" that even if she became a niddah in the middle of her chuppah, the onset of chuppah acquires. One may also say the Talmud deals with a case where he betrothed her through intimacy — in which case he is permitted to seclude with her once he has already been intimate, as stated at the beginning of Ketubot and in Yoreh De'ah siman 192. From there it is also clear that a bride must count seven clean days before the chuppah, because we are concerned she may have seen blood out of excitement. If she had her chuppah within the seven clean days, the Maggid Mishneh holds it is nonetheless a valid chuppah even according to the Rambam, since it is only a concern, not a certainty — see there. The Rambam also wrote that a Torah scholar is permitted to marry immediately, and she counts the seven clean days after the marriage. The Maggid Mishneh explains that he is permitted to seclude with her since she is not certainly a niddah. The Beit Yosef there disagrees and holds that seclusion is forbidden, and "permitted to marry" means the marriage is valid. This is apparently puzzling: if seclusion is forbidden, what marriage did the Rambam permit? For according to him chuppah means seclusion, and if seclusion is forbidden, what marriage is there? From siman 55 it is clear that after kiddushin she is acquired through the beginning of intimacy. Thus according to the Rosh cited above, if she was pure for the duration of the beginning of intimacy and afterward became a niddah, the chuppah is valid. But if she was a niddah before the seclusion, the matter remains unresolved (teku) — with practical significance regarding seizure of property, for those authorities who hold that seizure avails here as well. Meaning: for the Rambam, seizure is required for all matters; for the Rosh, seizure is required only for the additional ketubah amount. It appears from the Mechaber's words that he follows the Rambam as primary, since he cited the Rosh's view in the name of "some say" — indicating that he takes the first opinion, which is the Rambam's view, as primary.
(ג) "And per the view that a niddah's chuppah etc." And per the view that a niddah's chuppah etc. Note that according to the Rambam as well, post facto (bedieved) the chuppah is valid even if a ketubah was not written, as the Maggid Mishneh wrote (ch. 10) — and it is not analogous to the niddah case, as the Ran wrote at the beginning of Ketubot. Rather, according to the Rambam, one must write the ketubah before chuppah so that nothing be lacking, even something that is only rabbinic. But according to the Rosh, one may perform chuppah even ab initio without a ketubah.
(ד) "Since she is not fit for intimacy at all." Since she is not fit for intimacy at all. The Rosh's responsum gives two reasons for this ruling: (1) she is not superior to a niddah's case, in which the husband does not inherit her; (2) the specific case involved: Reuven divided his property and set aside land as a dowry on condition that if his daughter died before marriage the land would go to hekdesh. We follow his presumed intent, that he wished to benefit his soul by giving to hekdesh, not that his heirs should inherit — he only reserved it in order that his daughter might marry. All the more so it was not his intent that someone obtain it through artifice. Therefore the presumed intent is proven and the land is hekdesh. The Rama ruled simply that the husband does not inherit her when she is mortally ill, since she is not fit for intimacy at all. It appears he distinguishes on his own reasoning that her condition is worse than a niddah's — for otherwise the Rosh's responsum would contradict his own ruling that a niddah's chuppah is a valid chuppah. The Derisha likewise wrote so in siman 30. Then one should say he must return the dowry within the first year. One may answer: since she arranged the match after her father's death, and when she herself brings in the dowry it need not be returned, as stated in siman 118. But do not argue from this that if she brought the dowry herself, why then did the husband not acquire it by reason of her waiver, since she had chuppah with him? The Taz raised this challenge and, on account of it, wrote that if she had property the husband acquires it by reason of her waiver. In the Rosh's responsum the case was where the father pledged the dowry — and the daughter's waiver does not avail for that — but he did not answer the first challenge: why does he not return the dowry if it was the father who pledged it? It seems more reasonable that waiver does not apply here, since one may say the chuppah was performed under the assumption she would recover, and she did not intend to waive anything. What is meant by "so that her husband would inherit her" is his intent, but not hers. Similarly, according to the Rambam's view, if she was a niddah and had chuppah he does not inherit her, and we do not say she waived for him — so too here. Do not infer otherwise from what is stated at the end of siman 55 regarding a sick woman whom he inherits — that refers to a case where she was fit for intimacy, as the Bach also wrote.
(ה) "That the blessings are not me'akev etc." That the blessings are not me'akev etc. This is the view of the Rambam and the Rosh, as I wrote in siman 54. There I wrote that according to many decisors, a bride without the blessings is forbidden as a niddah — meaning the blessings are me'akev (indispensable). So too wrote the Shitta Mekubetzet (folio 469). However, it is possible she is not exactly like a niddah since the prohibition is only rabbinic, and moreover, he has the ability to recite the blessings.
(ו) "The custom that the groom and bride fast." The custom that the groom and bride fast. Maharam Mintz gives two reasons: (1) because it is a day of forgiveness for them and their sins are pardoned — according to this reason they should complete the fast; so too wrote the Bach in Orach Chayyim at the end of siman 562. (2) Lest they become intoxicated and their minds not be settled — according to this reason they need not complete the fast; so too ruled the gloss to the Shulchan Aruch. If the father accepts the kiddushin on behalf of his minor daughter, he must fast according to this second reason. On days when one does not fast, as noted in Orach Chayyim, one must be careful not to pursue excess food and drink.
(ז) "He need not recite the seven blessings again." He need not recite the seven blessings again. So wrote the Maggid Mishneh on Kiddushin, and in the responsum of Mahariv (siman 7); so too ruled the Rambam (ch. 6) regarding a niddah's chuppah. See Darkei Moshe siman 62. The reason, as the Maggid Mishneh wrote, is that blessings are not me'akev, and a bride without blessings still has valid chuppah, as stated in the Terumat HaDeshen. Above I noted that according to many decisors the blessings are me'akev. The Maggid Mishneh on Kiddushin (folio 663) gives other reasons. It is puzzling regarding the responsum of Maharam Mintz (siman 49) who ruled that he must recite the seven blessings again. See the responsum of Mahariv there, who wrote that a new ketubah need not be written, since the first ketubah remains valid. See also the Responsa Maimoniyot at the end of Hilkhot Ishut, and see siman 28, siman 42, and siman 150. The Bach in the following siman wrote that where there was no kiddushin at all according to law, then he must recite the seven blessings. See the end of siman 68, where I wrote in the name of Tosafot that if the kiddushin was done in error, he must betroth her anew.
