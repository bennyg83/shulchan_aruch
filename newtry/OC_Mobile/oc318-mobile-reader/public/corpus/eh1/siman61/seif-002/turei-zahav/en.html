(א) "It is proper etc." — Two views on the niddah's chuppah; proof for the Rambam. It is proper etc. There are two views in this matter: the Rambam holds that a niddah's chuppah is not a valid chuppah, while the Rosh holds it is valid chuppah for all purposes except that she does not collect the additional ketubah amount. The Ran cited both views at the beginning of Ketubot and wrote that since our practice now is to perform chuppah without seclusion, we do not mind whether she is a niddah or not; nevertheless it is proper that she not enter the chuppah until she is purified — end of his words. The Shulchan Aruch's words here follow accordingly. There is proof for the Rambam from what is stated at the beginning of Ketubot: "If one had his bread baked and the groom's father died — they bring the dead to a room and bring the groom and bride to chuppah etc."; and "One whose wife became a niddah — he sleeps among the men." For purposes of mourning the Talmud first cites the case of bread already baked, meaning the mourning takes effect before the chuppah — only because the bread was already baked. For the niddah case it specifies "his wife," meaning after she entered the chuppah. The Talmud compares the two regarding guarding against seclusion, but for entering the chuppah: if she already became a niddah after the bread was baked, we do not say he should bring her to chuppah and wait for intimacy until after she purifies even at the cost of a financial loss — implying one should not make chuppah when she is not fit for intimacy. This is in accordance with the Rambam's view. According to the Rosh, who disagrees with the Rambam, the Tanna simply did not mention the prohibition of a niddah entering chuppah, because it is not the practice to separate the chuppah far from the intimacy — but there is no prohibition. My father-in-law of blessed memory raised a challenge here against the Rambam and struggled to answer it — but this was unnecessary, as there is no difficulty at all; the matter is straightforward as I have written.
(ב) "And now the custom etc." — Reason for the practice; text of the wedding blessing. And now the custom etc. The reason is that we hold like the other views I mentioned above in the name of the Ran, that chuppah does not mean seclusion, and even so it constitutes marriage since he transferred her into the groom's domain. At that time — in the text of the blessing, "May Hashem our God soon be heard in the cities of Judah": the Tur wrote "let Him cause to be heard," but it appears that the Tur's text is not the authoritative one, since in Jeremiah (ch. 33) it is written "yet again shall be heard" etc., and so it appears in the Gemara, in the Rosh, and in the Rambam; and so is the practice. Therefore one who changes this follows the inferior version. As for the phrase "who causes the groom and bride to rejoice" — the word "for" there does not refer to speech between them in their union, but rather that Hashem should cause both of them to rejoice in their sustenance; at the end it refers to rejoicing in the union, as Rashi explained.
