(See Beit Shmuel §4) — The mortally ill woman; niddah's chuppah; definition of seclusion; analysis of the Rosh's responsum. Seif 1 (see Beit Shmuel, subsection 4). The Beit Shmuel of blessed memory in seif 1 wrote regarding the mortally ill woman as the Derisha and Prisha of blessed memory wrote in siman 90, and not as the Chelkat Mechokek of blessed memory — even though the Chelkat Mechokek presented a consideration based on the majority of the sick recovering. One may say that even though the majority of the sick do recover, the majority do not recover immediately, and she may remain ill all her days. We also hold a distinction in the law of medical expenses the husband must bear between an illness with a defined course and one without. A niddah — he knows with certainty she will become pure, and a niddah is "bread in his basket," as written in the last responsum of the Rama of blessed memory — see there. Not so with a sick woman: there is no "bread in his basket" and there is no affinity of chuppah at all when it is not certain she will be available to him for intimacy. All the more so here where his intent was plainly evident — that he did it only for her inheritance. As for what the Chelkat Mechokek wrote regarding the case of those forbidden in marriage — the Derisha and Prisha wrote that there his intent was toward a forbidden intimacy and he therefore had the affinity of intimacy; unlike a case where she is permanently unfit for intimacy with him. So too is the view of the Bach of blessed memory, that the Rosh of blessed memory did not retract from his view that a niddah's chuppah is fully valid chuppah. Particularly since one who seeks to establish inheritance or other matters through chuppah is seeking to remove property from its presumed owner — and for this reason the Rosh wrote there that Reuven commanded for his soul's merit that his heirs should not inherit (in the wording of the Rosh's responsum as cited in the Beit Yosef — Rashi does not write that the groom was a relative; in the Darkei Moshe siman 61 it is written that he was a relative, but in any case certainly he would not have been an heir) — only under the condition he stated was the property removed from their presumption. In light of this one must carefully study what the Beit Shmuel wrote regarding returning the dowry to the father who gave it — writing that the case is as if she brought it herself and the father did not give it — see there. Perhaps one may say that included in the condition he stated — that if she died before the kiddushin the land would go to hekdesh — since this is not a valid chuppah, her waiver avails nothing on her part. And presumably there were also kiddushin there together with the chuppah; for the language of the will was that he gives it to her for nissu'in and did not say for kiddushin. Such is the custom of the Jewish people in Spain, may God protect it: they first perform kiddushin and then she prepares herself for nissu'in for twelve months as in Talmudic times. And then the dowry was given; and so the term "nissu'in" is fitting, as the Rosh wrote in his language. It seems this is also the intent of the Taz of blessed memory, who wrote only regarding property of her own that she waived — meaning that apart from the reason of the presumed intent of the will, based on the language of the will itself one may also say she did not acquire through that chuppah. At this time one must study at length what is implied by the Beit Shmuel of blessed memory — that seclusion according to the Rambam of blessed memory means specifically that no person be present and she be fit for intimacy at that precise moment — and why ordinary seclusion should be inferior to handing her to the husband's agents, where there is no aspect of fitness for intimacy. The Taz implies that in the case of handing over while walking there is an advantage over being together; perhaps this too applies through some such means. Perhaps one may say that the Rambam's term "seclusion" includes the groomsmen as well, and even a few others who came only on account of the joy of the chuppah — as implied by the language of the Orechot Chayyim cited in the Beit Yosef there. This would resolve the Talmud's expression "she was not secluded" (perhaps this means the measure of seclusion defined at the end of Even HaEzer; and in any case from the standpoint of chuppah, any brief moment suffices — or where people were entering and leaving, or the doorway was open to the public domain, in which case there is no prohibition of seclusion; and where people were entering and leaving perhaps the concept of seclusion after acquisition does not apply — this requires study). This also resolves several challenges the later authorities raised regarding how a niddah's chuppah is possible since she is forbidden in seclusion (and according to the above this too is resolved — for where people are entering and leaving there is no prohibition of seclusion, and for chuppah any brief moment suffices, since they do not seek union — only the seclusion recalls the affinity resembling marital relations). And what is stated regarding a widow — "would he be intimate before Shabbat?" — is resolved by the case of handing her to the husband's agents. In the discussions of the later authorities on this there are many points to study. Perhaps the affinity of chuppah must be present on her side as well. Regarding the mortally ill woman, the Beit Shmuel wrote that she did not intend toward inheritance; while regarding a niddah he wrote that his knowledge avails — and there is a logical argument that if he did not know he would still have the affinity of chuppah and it would avail. This would resolve why a niddah's chuppah avails, and yet the Talmud appropriately asked "If she became a niddah, what is the status regarding her maintenance?" And similarly why they cite "his wife became a niddah," as the Taz noted. This would also resolve the challenge the Chelkat Mechokek raised against the Mordekhai regarding a case outside her veset cycle — for one may say that since it is uncommon he still had the affinity of chuppah. Regarding intimacy without blessings not being like a niddah — I have now seen that even the Beit Shmuel wrote that he can recite the blessings; the intent appears to be that no action is lacking on his part. And without a ketubah perhaps one may similarly say no action is lacking — according to those who hold that kinyan suffices; and one may also say it suffices if he declares before witnesses that he accepts the obligation, as in Choshen Mishpat 40. And if the obligation is not definitive it is like a conditional obligation (asmakhta) and requires a kinyan like a surety after a loan — but perhaps this is like a surety at the time of the loan by reason of the benefit of trusting him; and so too is the benefit of marriage. In any case it is a beit din obligation; only in a place where the custom is not to write a ketubah — who would write it? Perhaps saying "write it" suffices and no action is lacking. As for not being concerned about the bridal veil (hinuma) and the dissolution of the bundle in a quarrel, as the Taz wrote — one may say this is because chuppah alone is only a doubtful kiddushin, and a combination of uncertainties regarding the chuppah makes it a double doubt (safek safeka) combined with the presumption she is unmarried. And some say kiddushin after chuppah — the kiddushin takes effect retroactively. See the Beit Shmuel; and see what I wrote above — the question as a rental and sale for a specified time regarding the site of the chuppah — I see that the Taz wrote about this; it requires careful study. In the last responsum of the Rama of blessed memory it is written that according to the Rambam one must say that even though a Kohen Gadol is forbidden to marry two women, this prohibition is overridden on Yom Kippur by the rabbinic restriction that one does not marry on Yom Kippur — see there. This requires study, since at first glance it appears the prohibition of marrying two women is Torah law — so how did the Rabbis establish a rabbinic restriction against a positive Torah command? For we do not find that the Rabbis overrode Torah law except in a case of passive refraining (shev ve'al ta'aseh), where they have the authority to do so — but not in a case of active fulfillment (kum va'aseh). One must therefore say they hold it is only an asmakhta and only rabbinic that a Kohen Gadol is forbidden to marry two women; and in any case that is how it stands in our Talmud — even if the Yerushalmi holds it is Torah law, see there. Perhaps there is a distinction between when it applies during Temple service and regarding Torah law. Perhaps there is also some proof from the fact that this prohibition is not counted among the commandments, although for the Rambam this is not much of a proof since anything derived by interpretation is not counted by him — yet it is of Torah origin. And to say that what is not explicit in the Torah the Rabbis may override even in a case of active fulfillment — at first glance this seems incorrect. There, perhaps there are ways through conditions in the kiddushin and divorce of the wives that would not conflict with the said prohibition — but the plain meaning of our Talmud there does not involve conditions. Perhaps one may resolve what is written there on the basis of seclusion fit for intimacy, according to the Yerushalmi in accordance with the Rambam's view — that it is possible to marry through handing her to the husband's agents, if this is effective even for a niddah as the Beit Shmuel wrote in siman 57; and if it effects full marriage. So too is implied by the Taz that it is fully marriage; and in Yevamot 48 it is explicit that through handing over, the punishment changes from stoning to strangulation. And even according to those who hold that handing over does not acquire all acquisitions, one may nonetheless say that for this purpose it is full marriage and she is called "his household" — and what is lacking in acquisitions is actually preferable, since thereby it is not as much like performing an act of acquisition on Shabbat. (As the later authorities wrote — that through acquiring the additional ketubah by intimacy after a niddah's chuppah for all opinions, by reason of the Talmudic doubt — [to say it is a rabbinic doubt regarding acquisition on Yom Kippur, perhaps a niddah's chuppah also acquires the additional ketubah — there is also no rationale for this, since in any case from doubt one does not collect from him, and it would be a certain acquisition; so is implied by the later authorities — and perhaps according to those who hold that seizure in his possession is effective, one may say it would be a doubtful acquisition, for perhaps she will seize; and this is not like a doubt whether she will have assets to collect from, nor like acquisitions he acquires from her that perhaps will not take effect in practice due to some cause — for there the acquisition takes effect immediately; unlike the above, where there is doubt from the side of the acquisition's taking effect whether it took effect at the beginning through chuppah] — it is not like performing an act of acquisition on Shabbat since he does acquire. And it appears their intent is that the obligation already took effect, only it was conditional upon bringing her into marriage; and in fulfillment of a condition, "performing an acquisition" does not apply [as if he stipulated "if it rains"] since not all acquisitions take effect. And perhaps on this point our Talmud and the Yerushalmi disagree — because it follows from the dispute between Shmuel and Rav Asi regarding handing over, whether it fully acquires. One may say the Yerushalmi, because it holds that handing over does not acquire so fully, permitted it on Yom Kippur — or because it holds that marriage is effected by handing over, "entering" applies without the need for seclusion fit for intimacy. But the reasoning inclines as written in the last responsum, that the sun naturally sets and no action is lacking — as stated above.)
