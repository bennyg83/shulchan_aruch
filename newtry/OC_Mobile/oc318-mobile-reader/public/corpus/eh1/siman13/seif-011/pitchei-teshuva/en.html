He betrothed her unintentionally. See Ba'er Hetev, who wrote that this applies specifically where he did not know, etc. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 25, who wrote that the same applies if they acted according to the ruling of a sage who permitted giving the child to a wet nurse and making her swear according to the view of Rabbenu Tam, but did not permit giving the child to a wet nurse who was married to her husband; if on their own, without the sage's ruling, they gave the child to a married wet nurse, this is not called inadvertent. Since the prohibition of a nursing woman is already known, any purported permission one makes without asking a sage is called inadvertent only in the weak sense and is treated stringently, unlike the gaon Maharam Barabi, who distinguishes in this; see there. See also Beit Meir's comments. Chatam Sofer, siman 146, wrote that even if he acted according to a sage's ruling, and Beit Meir concluded that an ignoramus who relies on a sage's ruling is treated as inadvertent, nevertheless he must clarify before us who ruled so for him and why he relied on that ruling rather than asking the sage of his own city, whose rulings he usually follows; otherwise there is suspicion here. We hold similarly in Yoreh Deah regarding a butcher, that not everyone can excuse himself by saying he was inadvertent. Although a woman is believed to say "a sage permitted this to me" and need not clarify, that is where we do not see an erroneous ruling before us and therefore believe her. As for Beit Shmuel's statement that if he knew the prohibition but erred as to whether the intercalary month is included, he is considered intentional, it appears he copied Terumat HaDeshen's words only as an example. For the law itself he wrote below, s.k. 22, that after the fact, if she did not wait the intercalary month, we do not ban him and do not separate them, implying even where it was fully intentional.
