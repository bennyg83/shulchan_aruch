Even if they came into the groom's hand. Beit Meir writes that if the assets came into the daughter's own hand, the practical law requires study whether that is considered as though they came into the groom's hand. Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, Choshen Mishpat siman 49, treats it as obvious that it is considered as though they came into the groom's hand. The case there involved a man who married off his sister, promised four hundred Reichsthaler and a synagogue seat, gave the groom three hundred at the wedding, and had the synagogue seat recorded in the communal register in the couple's name. For the remaining hundred he undertook to provide the couple with food and lodging for a year, which he did. After eight months in his house, his sister died leaving a young daughter. The question concerned the wife's clothing and jewelry still in her brother's house: since the husband had not taken possession of them, could Rabbenu Tam's rule apply? Noda BiYehudah rules that where there is surviving issue, Rabbenu Tam's takkanah does not apply, only his law concerning a groom's dowry. Once the daughter acquired the items, her husband inherits her; after the woman acquires them, the father's presumed intention no longer matters, for otherwise one could say the same even after delivery to the husband. Therefore the husband inherits all his wife's estate, and the synagogue seat as well if she acquired it by use during her lifetime or by formal acquisition. Pitchei Teshuvah notes Noda BiYehudah's textual comments on Mordekhai and Nimukei Yosef. Panim Me'irot II:144 rules differently. With clothing and jewelry given to the daughter but still in her father's house, one may say that the father intended only that his daughter enjoy them. Even if they were handed to her at the chuppah, this differs from a dowry delivered to the groom, which he may spend or give away; clothing and jewelry cannot be sold or pledged by the husband without her permission and remain in her domain. When they are presently in the father's possession, he may say "I hold like Rabbenu Tam"; if the husband is in possession, he may say "I hold like Rashi." Panim Me'irot further writes that whether the husband's inheritance follows the daughter's acquisition is a dispute between Tosafot and Mordekhai, and the father may rely on Tosafot.
