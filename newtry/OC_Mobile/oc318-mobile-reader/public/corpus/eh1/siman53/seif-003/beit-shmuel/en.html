The father cannot retract. If she died, Maharshal and Bach wrote that the heirs did not acquire; rather, it returns to the father, because he gave on condition that she marry and the condition was not fulfilled. Her strength is only that if she is alive, even if there is no yavam, she acquired the right to marry another. This is not like the nearby law, where she did not acquire the right to marry another, because there the father pledged at the time of kiddushin, and the presumption is that he pledged only if she would marry this man or his brother, since the first kiddushin had not yet lapsed. But here, when he pledged or gave before kiddushin that she should marry, if she was once betrothed and the fiancé died, she acquired the right to marry another. There is no difference between pledging and giving. The rule is: if one gives to his daughter without specification, it is a complete gift, and if she dies and has a son, the son inherits. If he pledges on condition that she marry a specific person, she has no monetary right except if she marries that person. If he pledges on condition that she marry, without saying a specific person, then if she became betrothed to one man, he died, and she married another, she acquired the money; but if she did not marry and died, her son did not acquire. If he pledged at the time of kiddushin, even without specifying, she has no right to the money except if she marries the one who betrothed her or his brother. This appears primary, and so wrote the Taz. The Derishah wrote that even if she died, the heirs acquired, since he gave it to her and she had already become betrothed. One should not bring proof to these laws from one who vowed to marry off an orphan, as discussed nearby and in Yoreh Deah and Choshen Mishpat, siman 253, because here, where it is his grandchild and heir, it is different. However, from Maharshal and Bach, who wrote plainly that if she died the heirs do not acquire, it follows all the more so that if one vowed to marry off an orphan, the heirs do not acquire. I do not know how they wrote this so plainly and were not troubled by the law of one who vowed to an orphan.
One who vowed money. See siman 118 regarding the enactment of Shum; there I wrote that this case should not be compared to that law.
Some say the husband acquired them. This law derives from Ketubot 47, regarding one who wrote for her produce, clothing, and utensils. Rashi explains the sugya as speaking of a betrothed woman, and therefore it says that if she died, the husband did not acquire and does not inherit. But if she was married, the husband acquires, even though everything is still in her father’s hand. This is the view of the Rambam, chapter 22, and the Ramban in Milchamot, who writes that the Rif also holds this way. Tosafot hold that the sugya speaks even after nisuin, and nevertheless if she died the husband does not inherit because it had not yet come to the groom’s hand, for the assessment is that the father wrote it only so that his daughter should benefit. This applies even if she died several years after the marriage and even if she had children from the husband. The reason we do not say such an assessment regarding one who pledged support to his wife’s daughter is that where the person owed is alive, we do not apply that assessment; but here, the obligation was to his daughter so that she benefit, and she died. See siman 96. Hagahot Ashri writes that nichsei melog that never came to her hand, such as if her relative died in her lifetime, are inherited by the husband. So wrote Responsa Maharil, siman 61, and so below at the beginning of siman 90. Gifts given in honor of bride and groom that did not come to the groom’s hand are different, but nichsei melog given by her father are like dowry. Perhaps even gifts the father gives to groom and bride are like dowry, for whatever the father gives is presumably given only so she should benefit. But inheritance that fell to her, or gifts from others, have no such assessment, and the husband acquires. In siman 118 I wrote in the name of Responsa Maharam Mizrachi and Rashdam that anything not explicit is acquired by the husband, though there the husband was in possession.
Even if they came to the third party. In the above Responsa Maharil, this law is divided into three cases. The responsum in Teshuvot Maimoniyot, that if it came to the third party the husband did not acquire, speaks where they deposited it with the third party not to give it to the husband until a set time, and the daughter died during that time. The Mordechai’s ruling that they divide speaks where it was deposited without specification. If the third party was appointed for the benefit of the couple to profit for them, then even if they set a time and the daughter died during the time, the husband acquired. Hagahot Maimoniyot also writes that even if they agreed that the father-in-law should do business with the dowry money for the husband’s benefit, the husband acquired.
And similarly if he set up guarantors for him. Then he completed the transfer and conveyed it as though he had taken it from his hand and put it into the groom’s hand. See Hagahot Maimoniyot. However, in Responsa Rama, siman 89, he wrote that even if he set up guarantors, the husband did not acquire. I do not know why he ruled here plainly that guarantors are effective. He further wrote there that even a kinyan is not effective and he did not acquire.
And he converted it into a loan obligation. So wrote Terumat HaDeshen: even if he gave a debt document, the husband did not acquire. The reason is the assessment that he pledged only so his daughter should benefit. This resolves the difficulty that with a debt document one can obligate himself even where he was not previously obligated, as in Choshen Mishpat; here it is worse because of the assessment. Shiltei Gibborim wrote that if he converted it into a loan, the husband acquired. In Responsa Rama it is written that this is a doubt of law and we do not extract. However, according to Tosafot in Ketubot 9, in such a case we extract money because there is a double doubt: perhaps the halacha follows Rashi, or perhaps it follows Rabbenu Tam; and even if it follows Rabbenu Tam, perhaps when it was converted into a loan he acquired. So ruled Responsa Ben Lev, siman 21, and Responsa Rashach, part 1, siman 16, that money is extracted on a double doubt. See Responsa Maharil Beit HaLevi, siman 22. The Taz ruled that if he gave a mamarni note as practiced in Poland, where the lender is not named, the law follows Shiltei Gibborim, because as soon as the note leaves his hand he is obligated to pay whoever produces it, and he must then pursue his son-in-law; it turns out that the father is the one extracting from the son-in-law.
But if the groom seized. See Responsa Maharik, shoresh 94, Responsa Rav Betzalel, siman 35, and Responsa Maharil Beit HaLevi regarding saying kim li. The Sema, siman 25, brings Maharik, who wrote that in other disputes one cannot necessarily say kim li like that view; only in this dispute between Rashi and Rabbenu Tam, where Rashi’s reason is logical and Rabbenu Tam was later, do we follow possession. However, at the end of Ketubot he ruled that in every dispute one may say kim li, and so ruled Responsa Beit Maharil HaLevi. Nevertheless, one may say kim li only where at least two decisors hold that way; whether the Rosh and Tur count as two, see Choshen Mishpat, siman 77. The Rambam and Maggid Mishneh count as two. The Rambam and his teachers count as one. One cannot say in one place kim li like this view and also kim li like that view, and if there is a dispute whether kim li may be said, one cannot say kim li like the view that allows kim li. Darkei Moshe cites Terumat HaDeshen that seizure helps only if the groom seized before the doubt arose, meaning before his wife died. Even if the groom had his father-in-law’s items during her lifetime as a loan, and after her death wanted to seize them, it is not effective. So wrote the Taz. Beit Hillel records that the Shach, in Tekafo Kohen, ruled regarding an incident in Vilna that seizure was effective and not like Terumat HaDeshen. He also derives from Terumat HaDeshen regarding the Polish enactments about a debtor who flees: if a creditor received payment from the fugitive within twelve weeks before he fled, he must return it so all creditors divide it; but if an item was deposited with a creditor and the fugitive did not demand it before fleeing, the creditor need not return it, and his seizure is effective.
And there is no distinction in all this, etc. In truth, we have not found any decisor who wrote that if one pledged to his son and died he is exempt from giving. The Chelkat Mechokek also asked how the Rav knew this law. It is possible to force a slight resolution, but the main point is that this gloss belongs at the end of the siman, as Beit Hillel wrote.
Rabbenu Tam further enacted, etc. See Tosafot there, the Mordechai, and Hagahot Maimoniyot, where the enactment was for both man and woman. In Hagahot Maimoniyot to Kiddushin he wrote that it was not enacted for the man. Darkei Moshe writes that the custom in the enactments of the communities of Shum is not to distinguish between daughter and son: in the first year everything returns, and in the second year half returns. Responsa Maharil cites Rabbi Yakir’s uncertainty whether they enacted, when the husband dies, to uproot the ketubah as well. Perhaps since they uprooted the husband’s inheritance, which is biblical, all the more so they uprooted the ketubah. The main thing is to make a compromise according to the sages of the place. Maharam wrote that he had not heard that they enacted to uproot the ketubah. Rabbi Azriel wrote explicitly that Rabbenu Tam enacted only regarding him, not regarding the woman. Yet the Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot speak of every man and woman, and Maharik, shoresh 73, wrote that the custom is that if the groom dies during the first year, all the money is returned to the groom’s heirs except for a small amount given for the ketubah. It follows that even from the basic ketubah of one hundred or two hundred, they deduct something, and since he did not set an amount, it implies he follows Rabbi Yakir that it is given according to the sages of the place and the circumstances. I wrote in the previous siman in the name of Maharik that if his son died and she fell before his second son for yibbum, the father still acquired by force of this enactment and everything is returned to him; in the second year, half is returned. If he left only the amount of the ketubah, half is returned to him and she loses half. If he left much, she certainly cannot take more than the ketubah; but she takes the ketubah amount, and if half reaches the ketubah and additional ketubah, she takes that as well. All this does not speak of the dowry, which is a complete debt, and the additional third appears to be like a dowry debt and she does not lose it. Rather, this discusses the one hundred or two hundred enacted by Chazal or what he added voluntarily. See siman 118 regarding whether the enactment also applied to nichsei melog. If she died during the first year and he returns everything, he is not obligated in burial if he does not inherit the nichsei melog. See Teshuvot Maimoniyot and Maharil.
During the first year. Responsa Rashdam, siman 125, wrote that if the year was a leap year and she died in the thirteenth month, since this is not explicit in the enactment and by law the husband inherits his wife, we say the enactment applied only to most years, which are twelve months. If she died in the thirteenth month, the husband acquired. However, Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 68, ruled that wherever they taught "a year," the leap month is included.
Without surviving offspring. See Responsa Ben Sasson, siman 19, who discusses this at length and rules that the enactment was that the child live thirty days after her death according to the enactments of Solitina. In truth, however, when a child was born after thirty days, even if he dies immediately after her death, his law is that of a viable child. It appears that regarding the enactments of Shum too, if he lived after her death even one day and he was already thirty days old, the husband acquired. See siman 118.
Everything returns to the father. See siman 118 for these laws. The Mordechai states that he is not obligated to return it immediately, but within thirty days, the usual beit din time. Within thirty days it implies that he may support himself from the dowry, and so wrote the Chelkat Mechokek.
And so is the simple custom in these countries. According to this, even if the conditions did not state the customary language that because of absence or quarrel the matter should stand according to the enactments of Shum, it is as though it was written. So wrote the Chelkat Mechokek, and so it appears in Darkei Moshe, siman 55, because everyone marries according to the custom. However, this is specifically where he knows the custom, as the Rashba wrote in a responsum.
But what he spent. The Agudah in Perek Na'arah Shenit writes that they later enacted that where she died within her year without a viable child, he must return to the givers of the dowry or their heirs whatever remains in his hand from the dowry, excluding what he spent on her illness and burial. It follows that expenses for medical care and burial are deducted from the dowry. If he had business losses, it appears those too are deducted. If he also had his own money in the business, the loss is divided between them. The tithe removed from the dowry sum also appears deductible.
The heirs can protest. Meaning that they can prevent him from selling, namely nichsei melog during the above time, even if she agrees that he should sell.
