And Rabbeinu Tam. He explains that the husband did not acquire the dowry, meaning because of the presumed intention. In Choshen Mishpat I raised a difficulty between the case of a groom's dowry, where we follow presumed intention, and the Mishnah at the beginning of Ha-nosei, where one who undertakes to feed his wife's daughter remains obligated even if his wife dies. There I answered, based on Tosafot, that according to the sages of Rabban Gamliel the reason the groom does not acquire the dowry is that the father wrote at the time of the arrangement that the property would come with his daughter from her father's house to her husband's house. Therefore, although she acquired it, so long as an act remains lacking, namely bringing it from the father to the husband, the husband has not acquired it. In such a case we follow presumed intention, because the groom is not in possession. This means specifically where the father had put him in possession by giving it to him; taking it on his own does not help according to Rabbeinu Tam, even during his wife's lifetime, unless he can say, "I hold like Rashi." See Terumat HaDeshen. The glossator cites Beit Shmuel, who writes that Tosafot speaks even where she was married; nevertheless, if she died, the husband does not inherit what had not yet come into his hand, because the presumption is that the father wrote it only so that his daughter should benefit. Beit Shmuel distinguishes the undertaking to feed the wife's daughter: there we do not say that he obligated himself only because of his wife, since the person to whom he obligated himself, the daughter, is alive. Here, by contrast, he pledged to his daughter and she died. This requires clarification. According to him, the presumption must be that only the daughter should benefit and not the son-in-law. For if the presumption also included the son-in-law's benefit, then the son-in-law is alive, and why should he not at least acquire his half? We do not say that he wrote to him only for the daughter's sake, since he is alive. All the more so if the father pledged directly to the son-in-law: even if the daughter died, he should have to give to the living son-in-law, just as one who pledged food to his wife's daughter remains obligated and we do not say it was only because of the wife. One cannot distinguish by saying that in the wife's daughter case the wife does not benefit from what is given to the daughter, whereas here the father wanted his daughter to benefit. If the daughter were alive, he would be obligated to give even if the son-in-law took all of it for himself and the daughter did not benefit from it at all, for example if the son-in-law was wealthy and did not need the pledge. If so, when the daughter dies he should still be obligated to the son-in-law, since the pledge was to him and he is alive. Nor does Rema distinguish between a pledge to the son-in-law and a pledge to the daughter; on both he writes that according to Rabbeinu Tam the husband did not acquire it. Tosafot itself says that we are witnesses that the father's intent was to give the dowry only on condition that his daughter benefit from it together with the husband. They explicitly include the husband's benefit. If so, according to Beit Shmuel, he should receive at least his half, since he is alive. Moreover, the Gemara asks whether the dispute follows Rabbi Elazar ben Azaryah and the sages. According to Beit Shmuel, the Gemara could have answered that all agree with the sages, and that the reason she collects everything is that the person to whom the obligation is owed is alive, like the wife's daughter case. The Gemara's answer, distinguishing his obligation to her from her obligation to him, also becomes reversed according to Beit Shmuel. Therefore, in matters of presumed intention there is no distinction between whether the person to whom he obligated himself is alive or not. The Taz's difficulty remains: why, in one who undertakes to feed his wife's daughter, do we not apply presumed intention? See there for his answer. The related difficulty raised by Beit Shmuel regarding a half-male-document is likewise resolved by the Taz's answer.
Even if the father made a document upon himself, etc. In Darkei Moshe, before this he brings in the name of Hagahot Alfasi that if she converted it into a loan against him, it is as though it came into the groom's hand. Afterward he brings the view of Terumat HaDeshen siman 321 that this does not help, and so the Rama ruled here. The reason of Terumat HaDeshen there is that since the matter depends on the reason, even if she converted it into a loan against him, that reason still applies. In my humble opinion, if the father gave a promissory note to the groom according to the practice nowadays in this province, one should rule like the above Hagahot Alfasi. With such a promissory note, a person obligates himself even though he owes him nothing at all, as stated in Choshen Mishpat siman 3: if he writes upon himself that he obligates himself to whoever brings this writing, even though it is truly known that he owes him nothing, nevertheless he is liable because he obligated himself toward him. So too with a promissory note. Since when the father once placed money in the groom's possession we say afterward that the father fully resolved and transferred it to him in every way, the same applies here: as soon as the note left his hand he is liable to anyone who brings it against him, even though he owed that holder nothing at all. There is no greater resolve and transfer than this, for if someone else presents it, he will have to pay him, and he will then return to his son-in-law for having given the note to another. It follows that the father is called one who extracts from the groom, as appears clear to me.
