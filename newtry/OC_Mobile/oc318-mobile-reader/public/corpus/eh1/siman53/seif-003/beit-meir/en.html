One who pledged money to his son-in-law at the time of kiddushin and gave it to him before the chuppah, etc. The reason he chose this specific case, where he gave it to him, and did not write simply that one who pledged to his son-in-law and the daughter died is not obligated to give it to her son, is this: if she has a son, she is either a widow or divorced, and it was not the first marriage; even if she had not died, she would not have acquired it. Therefore he stated, and he gave it, for certainly he could no longer retract had she remained alive, since he had already transferred it. One might have thought that even though she died, it should be returned to her heir; he teaches that nevertheless, what he gave was only to fulfill what he had pledged, and every one who pledges, etc. The Bach wrote in explanation: although the groom is in possession, this possession is nothing. The implication is that he stated it this way as a novelty, that the groom may not keep possession of it. But this is no novelty, and it is explicit in Ketubot 48 that although her ketubah is in her husband's house, if she dies her father inherits her. Rather, the main novelty is that we should not say that only there, where the father is the heir, but if she has a son it should return to the son. He innovated this only in a case where he had already given it, as above, and this is clear. But one who gives to his daughter plainly so that she marry with it: here, where it is not at the time of kiddushin, he was precise to write, one who gives, for with a mere pledge she would not acquire. The Beit Shmuel s.k. 8 wrote: but here, where he pledged, is not precise, or where one vows to the poor; and once she became betrothed, the father cannot retract. This implies that before she became betrothed, even if he is alive and she wishes to marry with this money, he can retract. This is astonishing, for since he gave it to her on condition, how can he retract? In the Bach's explanation of the Gaon's words, it appears that for this reason he wrote, in place of, and since; although in the abridged rulings of the Rosh it is explained that specifically after she became betrothed. Further, all the later authorities agreed that even if she became betrothed, nevertheless with respect to this she did not acquire it, so that if she died her son does not inherit her. Only in the name of the Derishah does the Beit Shmuel bring that even her son inherits her, and Sefer Atzei Arazim supports the Derishah. He also asks from Choshen Mishpat siman 253, where if one said, give 200 to my daughter for a dowry, then if she died her heirs inherit; this is itself the Beit Shmuel's question in brief. He proved from this like the Derishah. I do not know what he accomplished by this, for in any case, even according to his method, there they disagree with the Gaon here. There, according to the Rashba and Ritva, even if she was not betrothed the money is hers, whereas here the Gaon made the law depend specifically on her becoming betrothed. Therefore, in my humble opinion, there is a difference between the Gaon's language here, that she should marry with it, and one who says, for a dowry or for her marriage. There the Rashba's reason applies, that he is only indicating the purpose; see the Sema there. But the phrase, that she should marry with it, sounded to him like language indicating complete insistence, that he gives only if she marries with it. It is stronger than an explicit condition, because he makes his gift depend on an act, and it does not take effect at all until she marries. See siman 120, Chelkat Mechokek s.k. 22, who wrote that any language of explicit condition is like a condition of if, which does not take effect until it is fulfilled, and not from now. However, the Gaon necessarily innovated that specifically regarding one who pledges to his son-in-law at the time of kiddushin, where the main acquisition is that through his joy he resolves and transfers ownership, we estimate that he pledged only on condition that she enter the chuppah, for this is the completion of the joy. But regarding a gift before kiddushin, where if he had not said that she should marry with it she would have acquired it immediately, and only because of his condition it does not take effect until the condition is fulfilled, we do not follow presumed intention but the implication of his language. For regarding what he removes from his own hand, even with a dowry, we do not follow presumed intention. He holds that although in the language of the Mishnah chuppah is called marriage, as we learned, once she is married the husband has greater rights, nevertheless in common language even kiddushin is called fulfillment of the condition, so she acquired it and the father cannot retract. A proof for the Gaon's view in this is from the Tosefta in the Rosh, Perek Shnei Dayanei Gezeirot, where it says: but if ben Yehudah betrothed a woman in the Galilee, we compel her to leave, for on this condition he married her. Thus even erusin is called marriage in the language of the condition; see there, and in Rivash siman 177. In any case this is a simple proof for the Gaon. But before kiddushin he can retract, because she did not acquire at all until the time the condition is fulfilled. If so, it certainly follows automatically like the Derishah's view. The view of the later authorities requires study. Either way: if her son does not inherit her, this shows that at kiddushin the condition still was not fulfilled, so why can he not retract? And if we compare it to a condition from now, why specifically if she became betrothed? Even if she was not yet betrothed but was ready and standing to become betrothed, it should be so. According to them, the Gaon's words are law without reason, and require study.
