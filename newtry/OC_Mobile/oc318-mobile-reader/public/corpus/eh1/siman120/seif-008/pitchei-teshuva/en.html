They recognize and know. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, siman 123, where he was asked by the gaon author of Or Chadash about a case in which one agent came with a letter from the rabbi of the holy community of Yass: a man named Binyamin, called Wolf, son of Shelomo HaKohen, had married there a woman Leah daughter of Chaim HaKohen, called Zelig, and because of necessity had left there; therefore wherever the agent would find him, he should request from him a Get. From the rabbi's handwriting it was recognizable that he was precise with names, for he was careful regarding the woman's father to write HaKohen before the nickname, as explained in Shulchan Aruch, siman 129 seif 7 in the gloss. This agent found in Poland a man who had married a woman in Yass. The agent himself did not recognize the man, for he had never seen him; only after he investigated after a man of such a name, they showed him that man. That man had not remained in Poland thirty days, but had spent the previous winter in a certain city, and a man came from there and recognized that this man had been there in the winter. He said that there he was not called Wolf at all, but was called Binyamin by people, and was called to the Torah as Binyamin Ze'ev; in the collection documents in the husband's possession his name was written Binyamin Ze'ev, and so he signs himself. If so, there are two changes here, etc. The rabbi who asked wrote that regarding writing the names we should press and direct matters to write Binyamin Ze'ev called Wolf. But the difficulty is that it is explained in Shulchan Aruch and Beit Shmuel that the witnesses must recognize that this man, as his name is written in the Get, has a wife whose name is as written in the Get. Here there is no facial recognition, and necessarily the witnesses rely on this: since we know this man is called thus, and from the man's words and from the rabbi of Yass we learn that a man with such a name has a wife with such a name. But on the contrary, here we know a man whose name is Binyamin Ze'ev, while the letter from Yass says Binyamin called Wolf, so at least there is no identifying sign. Noda BiYehudah answered him: regarding what you wrote about writing the names, that you would write Binyamin Ze'ev called Wolf, my opinion is not so. Rather, one should write Binyamin called Wolf, demitkarei Binyamin Ze'ev, since in the place of delivery he certainly was not called Ze'ev at all. If time permitted, it would be more correct for him to remain here thirty days, be called to the Torah as Binyamin, and be called so by people; then the place of writing would match the place of delivery. But if the agent is pressed to continue on his way, one should write the first formula. Yet the fact that they do not recognize the husband is difficult. Although in a time of danger one writes even though they do not recognize, we learned this only for a time of danger, not merely for pressing circumstances; and what danger is here? See the new Responsa Bach, siman 92, who did not write so, and it will be cited below in seif katan 15. Regarding what is clear from the questioner's words, that if there had been no change in names at all and the man's name were established here as the name mentioned in the Yass letter, it would be simple to rely on this: to me the matter requires study according to the Tur, which is the opinion of the some say view brought by Rama, that even the scribe must recognize. In my humble opinion the reason is that although the facts are correct and true, if the scribe does not recognize this at the time of writing, it is not called written for her sake, etc. Now, if he recognizes the husband, it is enough that he knows the husband has a wife with such a name; if so, he writes for her sake wherever she may be, for this husband is her husband. But if he knows only that the woman being divorced has a husband with such a name, if he does not know this is her husband, even though he knows his name is such, perhaps he is another man and has no wife with such a name. Even if afterward it becomes clear that this is the man, since it was not clarified at the time of writing, it is not called written for his sake. He then wrote that nevertheless, if there had been no change in names, it would have been proper to combine the fact that sometimes they write even when they do not recognize, especially since I saw at the end of his letter that there is a reliable man there who recognizes that this man married a woman in Yass, though he does not know his wife's name. If so, his name, his father's name, and the city Yass would have sufficed to recognize the man. But now that there is a change in names and the sign works against us, I cannot permit.
