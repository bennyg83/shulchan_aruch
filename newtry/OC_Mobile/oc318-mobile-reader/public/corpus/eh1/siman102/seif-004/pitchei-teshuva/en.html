Principal and addition. See Baer Heitev. Regarding what he wrote about the customary addition of a third among us, and that Beit Shmuel wrote, etc., that its law is like the ketubah: in the gloss of R. Akiva Eiger on the Shulchan Aruch, he wrote: see Responsa Ba'ei Chayei by the author of Knesset HaGedolah, Choshen Mishpat siman 202, who wrote that this too depends on a dispute among the great authorities whether the added third is included in the ketubah addition or in nichsei tzon barzel; see Responsa Rama, siman 54. What Baer Heitev wrote, regarding one who died owing rent to a landlord and movables were found in the house, that the landlord takes precedence: see Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 133, in an exactly similar case. He is uncertain there whether this is called the landlord having seized first. Even according to what we hold in Choshen Mishpat siman 313:3, that one who rents a courtyard to another retains the courtyard as the landlord's domain for acquiring found objects, nevertheless according to Tosafot in Bava Metzia 11b, we require specifically that the landlord's house open into the courtyard, so that he is considered standing beside his field. If so, in the present case it is not the landlord's courtyard, even though he too lives in the same house in a room opposite the rented room. It appears to me that Tosafot's reasoning applies only to the courtyard, since the landlord also enters and exits there and can guard it. But regarding the rented room, where the renter has authority to lock it and prevent the landlord from entering, it is certainly not guarded for the landlord. Although the Shach in Choshen Mishpat there explained that Rambam holds that in every case it is considered the landlord's courtyard, his words are not compelling in a way that decides Rambam's view. There is also room to question seizure by means of the courtyard here, for since a courtyard acquires by agency, as concluded in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, and as Rif and the Rosh explain there, even for an adult male a man's courtyard does not acquire as his hand but as his agent, one can discuss whether acquisition by courtyard helps here, since it is like seizing for a creditor where it harms others, which does not acquire. He also wrote that one should discuss in this case the right of residence in the house that her husband had until the start of the coming year: perhaps she too may live there for free, since her husband acquired the house for residence and it is mortgaged to her as land for her ketubah. Although it is also mortgaged to the landlord for rent from past years, nevertheless at the start of this year, when the husband acquired residence rights for this year, it is like after-acquired property both regarding the landlord for his debt and regarding the woman for her ketubah, and it depends on the dispute of the poskim regarding one who borrowed, then borrowed again, and then acquired property, where he wrote after-acquired property to one creditor but not to the other.
