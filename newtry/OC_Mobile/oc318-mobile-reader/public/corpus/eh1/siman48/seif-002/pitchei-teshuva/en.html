See Ba'er Heitev, who wrote that this applies even if there are witnesses, etc. See Mishneh LaMelekh, ch. 9 of Hilkhot Ishut, law 15, who cites Responsa Maharival, part 2, siman 85, where he was uncertain about a woman who said, "I was betrothed before so-and-so and so-and-so," and those two came and said that she was not betrothed before them: do we say that she has rendered herself a prohibited entity through her own admission, or not? He brought remote proofs from the rule that anyone who says, "I did not borrow," is as though he said, "I did not repay." He then cited the dispute of the early authorities regarding testimony heard from another witness where the first witness contradicts the second; see there. He wrote that none of those proofs is comparable to this case. In truth, however, this law appears clear from the responsum of Rashba cited in Yoreh De'ah siman 1, regarding a butcher who said about a particular animal that he had not slaughtered it, and witnesses then came, etc. This is also clear from another responsum of Rashba cited by Beit Yosef in this siman, concerning one who claims that he betrothed a woman and, at the moment of giving the object, said to her, "Be betrothed to me with this," even though witnesses contradict him and say that at first he gave it to her as a deposit, etc. So too it is clear from the words of Riaz cited in Shiltei HaGibborim, ch. 2 of Ketubot. Although from the writings of Mahari in siman 226 it appears at first glance that he disagrees, on careful examination they do not disagree. Mahari speaks where the one who betrothed retracted and gave no acceptable excuse for his retraction; but if he stands by his first words, even Mahari agrees that as to himself he has rendered the matter a prohibited entity, even when witnesses contradict him. Rashba and Riaz would also agree that if he retracted, even without giving an excuse, his retraction is effective where there are witnesses who support that retraction. These are his words; see there. Yet in ch. 3 of Hilkhot Yibbum, law 13, Mishneh LaMelekh himself took it as settled that Mahari's words oppose Rashba's words, and that Mahari holds that against witnesses one is not believed even to render oneself prohibited. He wrote there that one cannot force the interpretation that Mahari spoke only where she retracted her admission by which she had forbidden herself, for there is no reason for such a distinction: since she did not give a proper excuse before beit din for her first statement, what difference does it make if she later retracted? See there. See also Sha'ar HaMelekh, ch. 9 of Hilkhot Ishut, law 16, who cites Responsa Rashbatz, part 2, siman 279, responding about a woman who said, "I was betrothed before so-and-so and so-and-so," and the witnesses contradicted her, that she is not forbidden to his relatives, for she rendered herself prohibited only through those witnesses, and they have contradicted her; see there. Sha'ar HaMelekh writes that it is clear from his words that Rashbatz does not disagree with Rashba: only where she says "I was betrothed before so-and-so and so-and-so" and those witnesses contradict her do we not say shavya anafshah chatikhah de-issura, because she made the prohibition depend on those very witnesses, and a matter of ervah is not established with fewer than two witnesses, even if both parties admit it. But in the case of the butcher, where this reason does not apply, Rashbatz agrees that we say shavya anafshah chatikhah de-issura even against witnesses. From all this, there is no place for Mishneh LaMelekh's challenge to Maharival, as is understood. At first glance it seems that the main distinction is not between other prohibitions and matters of ervah, but that even within matters of ervah themselves one must distinguish between a case where those same witnesses on whom the prohibition was made to depend came and denied it, and a case where other witnesses came. This is compelled by Sha'ar HaMelekh's statement that there is no place for Mishneh LaMelekh's challenge to Maharival; otherwise the responsum of Rashba cited by Beit Yosef in this siman, concerning one who claimed that he betrothed the woman, would still remain difficult. According to this, the words of Mahari in his writings cited by Mishneh LaMelekh may also be settled: Mahari was speaking where those very witnesses before whom he claimed to have betrothed her came and contradicted him, whereas Rashba was speaking where other witnesses came; each master stated one case, and they do not disagree. Consequently, it may be said that Rashdam and Rashach cited here by Ba'er Heitev, that even where witnesses contradict him, etc., also speak in such a case, where other witnesses came. But if those very witnesses before whom he said he betrothed her came and contradicted him, as in Maharival's case, perhaps they too would agree that we do not say shavya anafsheih chatikhah de-issura, as in the words of Rashbatz cited above; this requires study.
