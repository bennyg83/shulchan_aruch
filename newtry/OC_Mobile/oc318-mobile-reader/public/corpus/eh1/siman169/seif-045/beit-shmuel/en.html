He intended and she did not, etc. See the Rosh there, who asked: since we hold that a yevamah who spit should perform chalitzah, why did the Talmud press to find a way in which the yevamah would be forbidden to the yavam? Why did they not tell her to spit before them, which would forbid her to the yavam? He answered that in the Talmud’s case the yavam wanted to do yibbum and did not intend chalitzah. Therefore, even if she spit, she would not disqualify herself to him. He brought proof from removing the shoe of one of the brothers, where the concern is that perhaps both intended it. But if one did not intend, there is no concern. It seemingly implies that since he compares these two laws to one another, and spitting speaks before beit din, the same applies if she removed the shoe in beit din and did nothing else: we require specifically that both intended. It appears from his words that the Talmud said that if one of them intended it is chalitzah stringently only with a complete chalitzah, as the Talmud says, "remove the shoe, and thereby you will acquire her," where it is a complete chalitzah but he did not intend. There we hold that it is chalitzah stringently. But with shoe removal alone or spitting, even before beit din, if one intended and the other certainly did not intend, it is not chalitzah at all and she is permitted to the brothers. Only when it is not known to us do we suspect that perhaps both intended. According to this, the Tur’s version is fine: perhaps they intended for chalitzah. Therefore, in the above Talmudic case, even if she had spit before beit din and intended for chalitzah, it would not be chalitzah at all and she would be permitted to the brothers. So it also appears from Tosafot’s explanation in this sugya, that we are concerned perhaps both intended. The question of the challenger, "but it was taught: whether he intended or she intended, it is invalid chalitzah until both intend," means until it is known to us that both intended. Here we are concerned perhaps they intended. The answerer replied: stringently, we are concerned perhaps they intended, but to permit her to the world she is not permitted until we know they intended. The whole sugya speaks of the concern that perhaps both intended, but if we know that one of them did not intend, she is permitted to the brothers. See what the Bach wrote on the Tur; it is not precise. But according to what the Nimukei Yosef wrote, the sugya speaks where we are concerned perhaps she intended. The Gemara asks: but was it not taught, "until both intend"? It answers: stringently we are concerned even if one of them intended, it is chalitzah stringently. Although we do not know that one of them intended, we are concerned perhaps one intended. If so, even if one of them certainly did not intend, nevertheless with shoe removal she is invalid to the brothers, even outside beit din. To resolve the Rosh’s question, why they did not tell her to spit and intend for chalitzah so that she would be forbidden to the yavam, one can say that he holds that with spitting, the intent of one of them is not effective; only with shoe removal did Chazal suspect that she is invalid to the brothers. But according to this, it is difficult why he needed to prove there that spitting which she spits among the brothers is not effective even to disqualify, for even in beit din it is not effective with intent of one of them, and all the more so among the brothers. Necessarily, among the brothers there is no concern that perhaps both intended, for the whole Talmudic discussion is whether perhaps she intended, but there is no concern at all regarding both intending. The words of the Rif and Rambam, chapter 4 of Gittin, according to the Nimukei Yosef, that they wrote "we are concerned perhaps they intended," and what the author wrote "perhaps they intended for chalitzah," imply specifically where there is concern that both intended. It appears that he wrote imprecisely. It emerges that if she removed the shoe not before beit din and one of them did not intend chalitzah, the above decisors disagree. If she spit in beit din and he did not intend, according to the Rosh it is not effective at all, and perhaps the other decisors also agree to this. If she removed the shoe in beit din without intent, I wrote that according to the Rosh too it is not effective unless it was in a case of complete chalitzah. However, this can be pushed aside somewhat, and one can say that the Rosh intended to compare spitting in beit din without one of them intending to shoe removal outside beit din without one of them intending, but shoe removal in beit din, even without intent, disqualifies.
