And she removed the upper one. According to our version, it is explained that if she removed the upper one and the lower one remained, this is "from upon that which is upon." At first glance it appears that "from upon that which is upon" is an invalid chalitzah and disqualifies. Therefore, what the Rosh wrote, and what is brought in the gloss nearby, that if she removes both they are valid, appears to speak where she removes both together at one time. Otherwise, when she removes the upper one, it is "from upon that which is upon," an invalid chalitzah, and she must go back to all the brothers. If she tore the upper one, but it still remained and because of the tear she could remove the lower one, the question was not resolved, and it is an invalid chalitzah. It implies that even if she tore most of the heel or most of the shoe of the upper one, so that he cannot walk in it, nevertheless, since it remained and there was no exposure of his foot, this is the question. For even where most of the shoe was torn, we hold that it is chalitzah and disqualifies. Therefore, in this kind of case, one can say it is as though the upper shoe remains. See the above responsum. According to the Rambam, the question in the Talmud is where she removed the upper one and tore the lower one. If so, it is chalitzah and exposure of the foot. Even if she did everything at one time, it appears that the question remains, since the chalitzah was with the upper one. However, if she tore the upper one entirely and removed the lower one, it is an excellent chalitzah. The Maggid and Beit Yosef wrote that the Rambam had another version. However, in truth, one can learn the Rambam’s law from our version too.
