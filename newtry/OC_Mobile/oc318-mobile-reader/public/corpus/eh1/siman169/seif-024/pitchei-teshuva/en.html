All the words of Maharam Lublin in responsum siman 9 are correct in this, that in every respect it is better to do it one after the other. In Responsa Maharam Lublin there it is explained that if we follow the Beit Yosef’s ruling, that she should remove both shoes at once with both hands, there is concern that perhaps she will intend for the one that is not truly the right foot, as Mahari Weil wrote regarding two gittin given at one time. But when she removes one after the other, there is no concern, like one who gives two gittin one after the other. See there, and see Sefer Get Pashut above, siman 129, s.k. 104. It appears to me there is also another reason: according to the Beit Yosef, it emerges that from one of them she removes with her left hand, while lechatchilah we require it to be with her right hand, as below, seif 30. She should say explicitly that regarding the foot which the law treats as valid for chalitzah, she intends for the mitzvah of chalitzah, and regarding the one that is not considered valid for chalitzah according to the law of our Torah, she does not intend for the mitzvah of chalitzah at all, but only as one occupied with removing a shoe from a stranger. There is no concern of invalid chalitzah that would require returning to all the brothers, since she says explicitly that regarding the one that is not according to law, she does not do it for chalitzah at all. Furthermore, the main law that an invalid chalitzah requires returning to all the brothers is not agreed upon, and Tosafot wrote that this applies specifically where the invalidity is because of weakening of the zikah. See Sefer Beit Meir. Nevertheless, where there is another brother, even a minor brother, rather than the left-footed one, she performs chalitzah from the other brother. So we acted here in practice. Nevertheless, my words count as nothing against the Torah of the Ramban of blessed memory. Therefore, where there is another brother, even several parsaot away, one should not act against the Ramban. This is astonishing, because if so, why did he need to say that the main view is like the Bach and Maharam Lublin? This is explicit in the language of the Beit Yosef in the explanation of Seder Chalitzah, seif 40, where he wrote: it appears to me that if he has another brother, she must remove both feet at once, etc. See there. What the Gaon author of Get Mekushar there wondered about the Bach, Maharam Lublin, the Beit Shmuel, and the Rav arranging the chalitzah, who all wrote that they should intend that if it is not according to law it should not count as chalitzah at all: he wondered that this is like a condition, and what benefit is there in a condition, since we hold that there is no condition in chalitzah? He concludes there that this is like an error before the ruler. At first I wanted to resolve his question, because the reason that there is no condition in chalitzah is explicit in the Gemara: because we require something that can be performed through an agent. If so, one could say based on what the Mishneh LaMelech discusses in chapter 6 of Hilchot Ishut, end of law 1, that the laws of conditions are required only for a condition about the future, such as if I do this or if you do this, but for a condition about the past, such as if I did this or if you did this, the laws of conditions are not required. See there, and Sefer Yad HaMelech there. Accordingly, one could say that we also do not require that it be possible through an agent. I found explicitly in Responsa Brit Avraham, siman 15, that he wrote this regarding one who betroths on condition that she had not had relations with one disqualified to her. See there, brought briefly above, siman 6, seif 17, s.k. 16. If so, here too, this condition, if this foot is valid for chalitzah, is not a future condition, and it need not be possible through an agent, so this condition should work well. According to this, a new law would emerge regarding what is below in seif 50: if he made the condition depend on the past, such as if you gave my father two hundred zuz in such-and-such place, or the like, the condition would effectively void the chalitzah. However, the conclusion of the Mishneh LaMelech there is not so, but rather that even for a condition about the past, the laws of conditions are required. Furthermore, from Tosafot in Ketubot 74, s.v. "condition," it is clear that this matter, that it must be possible to perform through an agent, is not dependent on the other laws of conditions. See there. This is astonishing regarding the above Responsa Brit Avraham, who did not mention the conclusion of the Mishneh LaMelech or the above words of Tosafot. In truth, in his own case he did not need this at all, according to what is explained in Ketubot there regarding betrothal through relations, that all forms of becoming betrothed are compared to one another. If so, one can say the same regarding prohibited betrothal. So it is explicitly explained in Responsa Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, Yoreh Deah, siman 147. See there. However, see Sefer Yeshuot Yaakov below, seif 50, s.k. 57, where he resolves the change between the sugyot in Yevamot 53 and 106, on which Tosafot there, s.v. "Ravina," already stood. According to his words there, one can also resolve the above question. One can also say according to what the Ramban wrote in his novellae to Bava Batra 126, brought in Sefer Beit Meir above, siman 120, seif 4, that a condition is effective in nazir even though it is impossible to perform through an agent, see Tosafot Nazir 11a, s.v. "on condition," because we learn from the condition of Bnei Gad and Bnei Reuven only cases like it, namely a condition between one person and another. But a condition between himself and himself is a condition in every respect. The reason makes sense, for he stipulated of his own will and wishes to fulfill the condition; therefore even a doubled condition is not required. See there. This reasoning is sufficient here too: the rule that there is no condition in chalitzah applies to a condition that she give you two hundred zuz, as below in seif 50, which is a condition between one person and another. But here, "if this foot is valid for chalitzah," is like a condition between himself and himself. This requires study. See further in Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, siman 156, where he was asked about an incident in which one performed chalitzah for his yevamah. Before the chalitzah they saw that he controlled both feet, and he performed chalitzah with the right according to Torah law. Afterward, when the yevamah became engaged, the chalutz challenged the chalitzah and said that in truth he was left-footed. What he had not said at the time of chalitzah, he did deceitfully, because he knew the law that the yavam and yevamah divide the deceased’s assets, and the yevamah had said that the deceased left nothing. Therefore he also acted deceitfully and performed an invalid chalitzah, so that she would make a match and the conditions would publicize how much she was bringing as dowry, and then he would go and reveal before the beit din that it was an invalid chalitzah. Then she would need another chalitzah and be forced to divide the assets with him. The questioner asked whether he is believed now to invalidate the chalitzah. He answered at length and concluded that this woman should be permitted to the public, and we do not pay attention to the words of the chalutz who creates a rumor that he is left-footed. First, he is not believed because a person does not make himself wicked. If it were true that he is left-footed, he performed an evil act, because if he had died suddenly, a stumbling block would have come through him in the prohibition of a yevamah to the public, since there were other brothers. Although Tosafot in Bava Metzia 3 wrote that a person does not make himself wicked, nevertheless, since he does not want to bring non-sacred animals into the Temple courtyard, he is believed; so too here, one might say he is repenting and does not want to cause her to marry with an invalid chalitzah. This is not so. Tosafot wrote this reasoning only regarding himself, but he cannot ruin others, etc. Although this permission is not entirely clear, because perhaps "a person does not make himself wicked" applies only to something the Torah called wicked, etc., see Urim VeTumim, rules of migo, letter 105, and Responsa Brit Avraham, siman 13, brought somewhat above, siman 6, s.k. 16, there is another permission here: even if we believe this chalutz, who says he is left-footed, there is no certain invalidity in this chalitzah, because the Semag and Semak hold that even a left-footed person performs chalitzah with the right of ordinary people. Even the decisors who hold that he performs chalitzah with both do so because they are uncertain whether we follow his own right. Only the Tur holds that he performs chalitzah with his own right. If so, even if he is left-footed, there is only doubtful invalidity here. From now there is a double doubt: perhaps he lies in his right and is not left-footed at all; and if he is left-footed, perhaps we follow the right of ordinary people. It is also a reversible double doubt, etc. Although there is a presumption of prohibition, and many are stringent not to rely on a double doubt, nevertheless here there are three doubts, for even if she performed chalitzah with the left, there is no clear halacha that it is invalid, etc. He discusses this at length, part of which was brought above, s.k. 22, and see what I wrote there in the name of Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger. He concludes: since there are three doubts here to permit her to the public, she should be permitted, especially since I already wrote a reasoning not to believe him to contradict his first words at the time of chalitzah, that he is not left-footed, if the arrangers of the chalitzah acted as customary and asked him at the time of chalitzah whether he is left-footed. Nevertheless, although she is permitted, one should not rush to permit. He should instruct the chalutz’s neighbors to watch him when he goes out and comes in, in a way that he will not sense that they are paying attention to this, for without doubt he will be caught in his lie. If despite all this he always acts deceitfully, do not pay attention to him and permit the woman. But because of the seriousness of the matter, he should also ask another well-known Rav. End quote. In Responsa Chatam Sofer, part 2, siman 91, he was asked by his father-in-law, the Gaon Rabbi Akiva Eiger of blessed memory, about a similar case: a chalutz received a large sum of money to perform chalitzah and told the beit din he was not left-footed. The beit din tested him, and four times he stepped first with his right and two times he stepped first with his left, and they judged him to control both feet. He performed chalitzah with the right. After the chalitzah he spread a rumor that he was left-footed and wanted to pressure them to give him more money. Rabbi Akiva Eiger wrote that his view inclines to leniency, because in that case there were no other brothers. If he had said he was left-footed, they would have let him perform chalitzah with both feet, and the beit din told him that they also had a left shoe prepared. If so, it was in his power to perform a proper chalitzah, and he was believed in his words that he was not left-footed, and afterward one does not retract and testify anew. Even if the credibility is based on "it was in his power," we say regarding it that one does not retract and testify anew, etc. Also, it is a double doubt: perhaps he is not left-footed, and perhaps the halacha follows the decisors that a left-footed person performs chalitzah with the right of ordinary people; and even according to those who prohibit, it is an unresolved question. Also, there is room to say that a person does not make himself wicked because he took money unlawfully. However, he did not want to decide to permit until his son-in-law, the Gaon, the author, agreed. He, of blessed memory, answered at length, and the substance of his words was to agree to permit that woman to the public. What his father-in-law the Gaon wrote, that even one believed by reason of "since it was in his power" and "what would he gain by lying" is also subject to "one does not retract and testify anew," is certainly clear, for this is an explicit Talmudic passage in Kiddushin 64a, etc. What he wrote that there is a double doubt here, at first glance there is room to question this, because there are four views here, etc., see the explanation of Seder Chalitzah, seif 40. If so, the doubt that perhaps the halacha is that a left-footed person performs chalitzah with the right of ordinary people has most decisors against it. On the contrary, there is a double doubt to be stringent: perhaps he has no remedy, and if he has a remedy, perhaps we follow his own right. However, the words of the Gaon Rabbi Zalman Emrich of blessed memory in his book Shishah Zera'onei Arugah are fit to join, for he wrote strong proofs that a left-footed person does not mean one who normally begins with his left foot, for that is of no concern at all. Rather, itter means blocked, from the force of that limb, like one whose right hand is weakened. Here too it means the strength of his right foot is weakened until he drags his right foot after his left, etc. See Rashi, Shoftim 3, on the verse "itter yad yemino." This appears to have hit the truth. Although Heaven forbid to act on this alone, nevertheless it is fit to join to a double doubt, that perhaps all the decisors hold this way, and in our case there is no left-footed person at all, etc. Also, what his father-in-law wrote, that there is room to say a person does not make himself wicked because he took money unlawfully, is correct. One can add that when he took his wage as a worker and must complete his work, he is no longer believed to say that he did not perform his work properly, unless he said so the first time he was found, as the Ran explains in Kiddushin 68b and the Shach hinted to in Yoreh Deah, siman 127, s.k. 20, etc. Furthermore, it appears to me that here, since they tested him in beit din and four times he stepped first with his right and two times with his left, if one would think that he is left-footed and deceitfully lifted his right to mislead the beit din, then a deceiver is careful in his deceit and would always have been careful to lift his right. This is how we hold like Rav Zevid in Bava Batra 167b, and even Abaye said his view only where over time they called him and he answered. But here, in the very same session where he was engaged in his deceit, he certainly would have been careful. Therefore, from the fact that he was not careful, learn that he is not left-footed. This reasoning appears correct and is fit to join. He concludes: nevertheless, it appears to me it is good to say to him: You are not believed, and this woman is permitted. But if you know in yourself that the truth is with you and you are concerned for causing a stumbling block, and you want to perform chalitzah for her a second time without any money, without any expense, and without any delay to cause her suffering, then she will perform chalitzah for you. Then, if he listens and agrees to this, perhaps she is forbidden to marry by law until she performs chalitzah again, for he is believed, since what would he gain by lying and also by degrading himself? In Sanhedrin chapter 2, chalitzah is considered embarrassment and degradation. It is somewhat like "since you are believed to lose your payment," etc. It is also not a case of making himself wicked, because he speaks by way of repentance. Therefore one should be concerned. Who would place his head between the great mountains to act leniently, all the more so since those great authorities who are stringent are the majority in wisdom and number? One should not say that where there is pressing circumstance, as here, where the yavam is distant from the yevamah, we may rely on the Semag and Semak. This is not so. First, it is not called pressing circumstance unless there is no remedy for the matter, as the Radach wrote in bayit 3, that where travelers are common, it is not pressing circumstance merely because of waiting three or four months. If so, in our case, where the chalutzah has not yet married and the yavam wants to perform chalitzah, it is not a pressing circumstance. Furthermore, the Radach wrote there that specifically in rabbinic matters do we say that he is worthy to rely on in pressing circumstances to be lenient. If so, in our case, where those great authorities who invalidate hold that they invalidate biblically, it is not relevant to be lenient in pressing circumstances. All the more so in our case, where we see that he intended to invalidate the chalitzah so that she would submit to judgment, since they asked him at the time of chalitzah whether he was left-footed and he answered no and forced himself to mislead them by lifting his right foot.
