And she is accustomed to this. Ba'er Heitev writes that the Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel wondered: if they did not warn her, etc.; and if they warned her, etc. From Darkei Moshe, who explains the Rashba as speaking where they warned her, it implies that the Rama requires that she be accustomed even after warning. But Beit Shmuel, s.k. 11, writes that this is specifically in the Rashba’s case, where there was room to attribute the act to anger and quarrel; therefore even with warning it must be habitual, for then we do not attribute it to any external cause. Likewise below the Rama writes of a woman accustomed to seclusion, where one might attribute it to her business dealings. But where there is no such cause, even one time makes her one who violates Dat. Beit Meir explains the Rashba simply based on Terumat HaDeshen, siman 242, who was uncertain whether habitual conduct perhaps does not require warning. To resolve the Rama, he writes that regarding loss of ketubah, everyone agrees it depends on warning, not on habit. But because the Rama later rules that one who violates Dat can be divorced against her will, and that may apply even without warning, he derives from the Rashba that without warning she is called a violator only if she is habituated. Thus after warning there is no distinction whether she is habituated. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 101, writes that from many early authorities there is strong support for the Rama that if she is not habituated, there is no mitzvah and he cannot divorce her against her will. In siman 100, Beit Meir writes that two times is called habitual for this matter. Beit Meir also disagrees with Mahari HaLevi, who concluded that if she is accustomed to violate Dat with witnesses, beit din compels divorce. Beit Meir writes that there is no coercion by beit din for one who violates Dat, even habitually and with witnesses; the difference is only that when she is habituated, even without warning he is obligated and commanded to divorce her against her will, while one time does not make her a violator except after warning. Noda BiYehudah’s case above, where she served as a niddah for over half a year, also concludes only that it is a mitzvah to divorce her and he may divorce her against her will, not that beit din compels, though that case involved her admission and not witnesses. Regarding cursing, it is reported in the name of the Gaon Maharach of Brisk that this applies specifically when she curses with a Divine name or substitute name, as in the laws of one who curses himself, his fellow, or his parents. The Talmudic phrase about a lion may itself be a Divine reference; this requires study.
