And that he ate it based on her statement. See Chelkat Mechokek, who infers that witnesses are also needed that he ate it. Regarding serving him as a niddah, one must then force the explanation that he either admits it or witnesses found her serving with him. Beit Meir challenges this: according to the Ra'avad, who holds she is not believed against herself even to lose the ketubah, the case can be established only where witnesses found them, which is very forced. Furthermore, even according to those who disagree with the Ra'avad, the Ran writes that the Mishnah was not established as a case of admission because that is uncommon. If so, it is difficult to establish "serving him as a niddah" only through admission or the even more uncommon case that witnesses found them together. Therefore Beit Meir writes that the Shulchan Aruch’s language is not exact. It is enough to have witnesses that she said, "I am pure," or "it is tithed," and then he is believed that he stumbled through her. Specifically where there are no witnesses at all she can claim she never said it; and even if she said it, before he ate she can say, as the Rosh writes, "had he wanted to eat, I would have separated him." But once there are witnesses that she told him, he is believed that he stumbled through her. What the Shulchan Aruch says, "if there are no witnesses and she denies that she fed him," means she denies that she said anything to him. Responsa Mahari HaLevi initially also wanted to say that witnesses are needed only that she told him, and he brought proof from the Ran. Later he wrote that from the Tur’s wording, "and there are witnesses that she told him it was corrected and that he ate it," it implies the same by niddah: witnesses must testify that she said "I am pure" and that he had relations with her then. He rejects the proof from the Ran by saying the Mishnah could speak where he says he had relations with her then and she does not deny that, but says she was pure. He further writes that if there are witnesses that he had relations with her after she was established as niddah, but we do not know whether she said she was pure, it appears at first from the Tur that this is insufficient. Nevertheless, with niddah one can distinguish from untithed grain: with grain she can say she did not know she fed him or ask why he ate it; but with niddah, the husband relies on her and she knows herself, so witnesses that she said "I am pure" may not be required. She cannot simply say the husband knew too unless she brings witnesses, as above.
