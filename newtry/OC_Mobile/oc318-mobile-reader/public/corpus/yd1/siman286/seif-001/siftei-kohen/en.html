Provinces. They are surrounded by mountains and valleys for several parasangs, and one can leave them only through gates, such as the land of Hagar. Rashi.
A cattle stable. Although it is a place of filth, its foulness is nevertheless not excessive. The Derishah writes that the general rule is as follows: where there is a concern of sanctity, such as a synagogue, or where there is a place of filth, an actual dwelling is required, one in which people live constantly. Where neither concern applies, however, such as a hayloft and the like, each such structure is considered a dwelling even if people do not actually live there, since they regularly enter and leave it.
And poultry coops. This is a built room made for geese and chickens.
And a woman's house, etc. We do not interpret "your house" as excluding her house, because Scripture states, "so that your days may be prolonged," and women require life just as men do. They are therefore obligated.
All of them are obligated. See Sema, Choshen Mishpat, siman 427, seif katan 2.
But a house, etc., is exempt. This is because part of it belongs to a non-Jew, and also because of danger, lest the non-Jew say that the Jew is practicing sorcery. Courtyard gates and city gates are likewise exempt for this reason, because of danger, as stated in the Gemara. This is unlike the Ateret Zekenim, who writes that the reason is the verse, "so that your days may be prolonged," since non-Jews do not desire this, for they do not observe the Torah, etc.
Likewise, courtyards or towns, etc. Where some non-Jews reside, even if most residents are Jewish, the gates are exempt because of danger. For this reason mezuzot are not affixed to street gates, such as in the holy communities of Krakow and Prague, where gates were erected in the Jewish streets and closed at night, yet mezuzot were never affixed to them. The reason is the danger of the ruler's accusations, which were always liable to be directed against the Jewish people. There is also another reason not to affix mezuzot there: they would certainly be removed and disgraced, thereby causing sacred writings to be treated contemptuously. From this we learn that when a Jew leases a concession from a nobleman to sell spirits, beer, or mead, and the nobleman's servants enter the premises every day and are accustomed to taking mezuzot and treating them contemptuously, mezuzot should not be affixed to those entrances unless the entrance is protected from the non-Jews so that they cannot remove it. So writes the Bach.
