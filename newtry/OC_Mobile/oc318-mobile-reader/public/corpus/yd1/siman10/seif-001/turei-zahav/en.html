And therefore one should peel it. Explanation: it is a full obligation—not as the Shulchan Aruch implies, that it is only hiddur mitzvah; and so Rabbenu Yerucham: one should be stringent by Torah law. The rishonim asked: why is this different from a knife of Kutim, for we hold a plain vessel of Kutim is not ben yomo—and why different from a radish of chiltit in the chapter "One may not stand," and sharp things in siman 96 forbidden with a Kutim knife because its sharpness spoils it and makes it praiseworthy—implying that without its sharpness it would be permitted? The Rosh and Tosafot answered: here it speaks where the knife is known to be ben yomo; and the Rashba wrote in the name of Rabeinu Yona: here it is forbidden because of smeared prohibition visible on the knife, and whatever is visible is not spoiled even in many days; but with a radish of chiltit there is no concern of visible prohibition, for there it speaks of buying from a shopkeeper, and a plain shopkeeper has clean vessels, and there is only concern of absorbed prohibition—and in this, if it is not ben yomo, it helps. And in Perishah he challenged the Rosh, who is lenient here regarding fat on the knife, from siman 96 regarding radish cut with a knife, etc.; and he answered: here it speaks of a plain vessel of Kutim—as we say regarding it, the plain is not ben yomo, so too we say its plain is well wiped; but there it speaks of an Israelite vessel—as we say its plain was ben yomo, so too its plain is not wiped. And he was not precise—for in siman 122 seif 4 he ruled explicitly that even with an Israelite vessel we say their plain is not ben yomo. And one must explain in the Tur, which brought the dispute of the Rosh and Rashba, in what they argue and why the Rosh was not also concerned for visible prohibition on the knife and why he relied on leniency in a Torah doubt—and he said: he holds that even with visible prohibition there is reason for spoilage, in which all agree, as proved in many places—why different from a piece of prohibition? And I found in Mishmeret HaBayit, who wrote: this answer—that the reason is grease visible on the knife—I hesitate about somewhat; for a recognizable thing, smeared slight grease on the knife's sides, is spoiled somewhat even temporarily, and all the more when it is not ben yomo; and they do not bring proof from a piece of meat and abundant fat standing alone for smearing only; and furthermore through iron it receives taste and is spoiled, and experience testifies to this, end of his words; and it appears Tosafot and the Rosh also hold thus here, therefore they needed another answer. And one must understand the Rashba's and Rosh's views here: the Rashba holds beit hashchitah is called boiling on one side—that the knife can receive from beit hashchitah grease, blood, and anything, but it is not fully boiling regarding expelling the knife's absorption into beit hashchitah; receiving into the knife is easier than expelling from it; therefore if he slaughtered with a Kutim knife there is no concern it will expel into the simanim what the knife absorbed—for beit hashchitah has no power to boil so much; rather concern is from what is visible on the knife from prohibition already cut; in this, sticking in the ground helps; therefore the Rashba called this "somewhat boiling." And in Derishah and Perishah he understood from "somewhat boiling" that therefore the knife absorbs in beit hashchitah somewhat—and from this entered pilpul; and he did not have Tur Orach Chaim before him, who wrote explicitly as I mentioned that it does not absorb at all from knife to simanim; and what the Tur wrote in siman 105—that for the Rashba heat in beit hashchitah expels—means one side we mentioned, from prohibition to knife, but not from knife to simanim; and in Perishah he did not notice this distinction. And it appears clear that also the Rosh, who argues with the Rashba and establishes it in ben yomo, also holds beit hashchitah does not absorb from the knife's absorption, but absorbs from what is visible on the knife—all the more when ben yomo, as he wrote; therefore sharpening and sticking help the knife, as in the primary source Tosafot brought in the first chapter of Chullin (daf 8): you might say: if it absorbs the absorption, what does sticking help for what is absorbed? Rather, as I explained, both hold the primary concern is from what is visible on the knife; but for the Rosh, even in this it must be ben yomo, and for the Rashba ben yomo is not required in this—as explained clearly after investigation; and with this what they ask is resolved: why did the Tur bring the Rashba's plain view that sharpening and sticking help, since for the Rosh they do not help—and according to what I wrote, it is fine.
Permitted to slaughter with it in such a manner, etc. Explanation: if he sharpened on a whetstone or stuck in hard ground; and it appears the Rama does not speak here except of a plain knife; but where it is known to be ben yomo he should not do so l'chatchila—rather as written shortly in the name of the Mordechai; and so concludes Orach Chaim.
