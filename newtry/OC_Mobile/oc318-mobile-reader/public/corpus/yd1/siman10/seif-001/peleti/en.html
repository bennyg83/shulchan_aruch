That it spoils—Tosafot challenged: we say in Pesachim one slaughters for avodah zarah on Shabbat—they were concerned about repair, removing it from eiver min hachai; and I did not understand the question: on Shabbat we require spoilage, and this is not spoilage—for what he spoils in shechitah he repairs by permitting from eiver min hachai; but here it is truly not called spoilage; nevertheless it is also not called benefit—for his wage comes out in his loss, and he does not benefit from avodah zarah; so what if it is not spoilage, since he does not benefit? Perhaps this is Tosafot's intent—see there.
And forbidden to slaughter a dangerously ill animal—if he slaughtered, the Tur brought in the name of the Rosh that such slight benefit is permitted b'dieved; and the Rosh's proof: from what the Gemara asks and could derive from grease prohibition—and it came to teach that all the meat is forbidden; and if because of grease, only the cut place is forbidden; and if it asks on the heter that it is permitted to slaughter with it, etc.—why does it ask? It needed to teach the meat is permitted, and the power of heter is better to teach that although forbidden initially because of grease, nevertheless if he slaughtered it is not forbidden b'dieved, etc. On this Maharsha wondered and wrote: I do not know what he says—for nevertheless it asks well: it is not taught permitted to slaughter and cut, for l'chatchila it is forbidden because of grease even relying that he will peel, lest he forget and not peel, as the Rosh himself wrote. And I did not merit to understand Maharsha's words; and one must explain their words: why did they not briefly ask—should it not be permitted to slaughter relying that he will peel? Nevertheless for the Rosh's difficulty there is no answer, for there is no prohibition b'dieved; and it teaches that for spoilage l'chatchila it is permitted from doubt—what does it challenge and derive from grease prohibition? We know that for grease it is known permitted l'chatchila to slaughter relying on peeling; but if because of avodah zarah benefit it is forbidden, it teaches spoilage; if so, it should have challenged the Rosh either way. And one can say: granted, if permitted—as taught, the main intent is the animal is permitted and was not forbidden like a dangerously ill animal—it is not difficult because of grease, for there the prohibition is only a peel's worth, and the language "permitted" nevertheless is fine: even because of grease, permitted l'chatchila to slaughter relying he will peel; and if it is difficult how it teaches plainly permitted, implying even without peeling—say the intent is to permit the animal; whether he must slaughter relying on peeling is not discussed; but if b'dieved it is permitted in any case, the whole intent of "permitted" is to instruct he may slaughter l'chatchila without delay—it is difficult, for there is grease; in any case it should say he must slaughter on condition he will peel, for all his intent is initial permissibility. And for this intent one can say in the Rosh's words: as the Rosh wrote explicitly—what we say "slaughters with a gentile's knife, peel" means where it is known to be ben yomo; otherwise a plain gentile vessel is not ben yomo and would be permitted without anything. Accordingly one can say: why does the Gemara ask and derive from grease—and it needed to push with a forced change, cutting wood or building a brick, to say it speaks of a plain knife that is not ben yomo and would be permitted, and even if we say a plain knife has grease smeared—we could say wiping; and why need a brick, and all the more the Rosh does not hold fat is smeared on the face. And thus the Gemara's difficulty is l'chatchila: l'chatchila forbidden, and l'chatchila forbidden to slaughter because of grease. And the Rosh's claim is correct: nevertheless it teaches because of benefit of avodah zarah utensils—for because of this grease, permitted l'chatchila to slaughter relying he will later throw away enough for the absorbed peel from grease—and it is not relevant "lest he forget and not peel"—what of it, there is no Torah prohibition, and if he eats without peeling he does not violate a Torah prohibition of noten taam lifgam, which is permitted; and one may rely on this and the question is fine. Unlike below with a ben yomo knife, where it is fine to forbid slaughter relying on peeling afterward, lest he forget and violate a Torah prohibition. And one can understand in the Rosh why he began with the difficulty "and derive from grease"—it refers to forbidding slaughter of a dangerously ill animal, not immediately as Rashi truly explained that the difficulty refers to permitting spoilage; see Maharsha. And it is possible the Rosh holds: Rav's "peel" with a gentile knife—one can say as Semag: Rav's reason is noten taam lifgam is forbidden. Accordingly one can ask: why does the Gemara ask and derive from grease—perhaps Rav only says peel because Rav holds noten taam lifgam is forbidden and they were stringent like Beit Shammai; but for us, who hold noten taam lifgam is permitted, only l'chatchila forbidden—they are not so stringent with heat in beit hashchitah; and if it says regarding avodah zarah: how does Rav hold, whether noten taam lifgam is permitted or forbidden—perhaps his doubt, and for Rava Rav Nachman is clear that Rav holds noten taam lifgam is forbidden—and so Rashi must say in Pesachim daf 46; therefore there is no proof from "peel"; therefore the Rosh explained the Gemara's difficulty "and derive" refers to the dangerously ill animal case—and this is what Rava says, and Rava is uncertain how Rav holds regarding noten taam lifgam—see there. And from this it appears the Rambam, who rules not like the Rosh, because he holds as I wrote in laws of meat and milk: therefore noten taam lifgam is permitted because it is not in the manner of their benefit; but for avodah zarah, even not in the manner of their benefit, possibly forbidden, as Tosafot Pesachim 26, s.v. she'ani heikhal, and Avodah Zarah 43, s.v. amru lo. If so, even noten taam lifgam is forbidden, and the Gemara asks and derives well, etc.; and the Rosh's proof and the Rambam's words are correct.
Repairs—and the Shach's view: even the Rosh concedes at least knife-wage benefit must be given; and Peri Chadash challenged him: the Rosh's view regarding bread baked in an oven—all is forbidden—is not understood in his words, for the Rosh distinguishes bread benefit from knife benefit; and the Shach holds the distinction is we require only knife-wage benefit to slaughter—if so, what proof from bread? Indeed it is difficult: still the Gemara asks "and derive," etc.—peeling suffices; therefore knife-wage benefit is needed; and for a dangerously ill animal where knife-wage is expensive, much must be given—rather we learn the Sages did not penalize in this. And were I not afraid they would laugh at me in the study hall, I would say: this "to eat through shechitah" is a mitzvah—"and you shall slaughter" not "and you shall stab" as in the desert; we were only commanded the mitzvah of shechitah, and so we bless; if so, mitzvot given to enjoy are like lulav and similar, permitted in an asherah. With this one can resolve the Rif's words, who omitted the entire statement regarding an avodah zarah knife—and they wondered why. According to what I said it is fine: he holds mitzvot given to enjoy were given; but according to what we hold, mitzvot were not given to enjoy—all this is void.
He rinses beit hashchitah—this is the Rambam's ruling, like the Rif, who rules not like Rav who said peel; and they already wondered why he ruled like Ravah in place of Rav. And if we hold with a knife that slaughtered treifah in cold—as they wrote, the printers' error—and truly their error is compelled: if one law applies to a gentile knife for slaughtering treifah, why did Rav and Ravah establish their dispute on a gentile knife, and R' Elazar and Ravina on a knife that slaughtered treifah, and not state their words in one manner? And furthermore, do R' Elazar and Ravina argue in Rav and Ravah's dispute—what do they argue? Let it teach the halachah follows Rav or Ravah! And it is strained to say both argue according to Ravah, that rinse—Rav's colleague holds nevertheless there, after he slaughtered; but here l'chatchila to slaughter another animal with it, Rav's colleague holds in hot—this is strained; and also the Gemara's challenge: every animal has eiver min hachai for R' Elazar's colleague, and it does not ask Rav, who holds peel—if so, every animal absorbed eiver min hachai and the knife needs hag'alah. Rather we learn there is room for the printers' error; and it appears to me to distinguish between "peel" and treifah knife—and then the difficulty on the Rif is in place: why he ruled like Rav. From Semag negative commandment 37 it appears the reason is noten taam lifgam, and Rav forbids in such a case noten taam lifgam. And this does not suffice for the Rambam—for the Rambam wrote: if he peeled, this is praiseworthy; shall he say so in noten taam lifgam laws, where the stringent is praiseworthy? It is strained to say it speaks of grease smeared on its back, and the Rambam according to his view that even smearing on the surface becomes noten taam lifgam, as explained there in Hilchos Ma'achalot Assurot chapter 16; nevertheless his view is good to peel, for visible grease is here—and all this is strained. But in the Rif one can seemingly say: therefore he ruled not like Rav, who holds Rav for his reason that noten taam lifgam is forbidden. But it is difficult: whence for the Gemara to say Rav and Ravah argue whether beit hashchitah is cold or hot—say they argue whether noten taam lifgam in such a case is forbidden or permitted! Granted for Rashi in Pesachim daf 46, for his reason, s.v., who wrote Ravah holds noten taam lifgam forbidden—see there in Tosafot, s.v. amar rav, why we do not establish Rav and Rava's dispute on this. One can say: otherwise Ravah, who holds noten taam lifgam requires rinsing for all, would establish Rav and Ravah's dispute on ben yomo—whether noten taam lifgam is permitted or forbidden; or the Gemara's question is difficult to establish the dispute thus. On the contrary, Rashi's proof that he rules like Rav is from the difficulty: halachah follows Rav—and then difficult halachah against halachah, for we hold noten taam lifgam is permitted; therefore the Gemara establishes the dispute elsewhere, and it speaks of ben yomo, as Tosafot wrote. But for the Rif it is difficult—and strained to say he holds if they argue about noten taam lifgam they argue in general; or even Ravah holds noten taam lifgam forbidden and holds nevertheless rinse; and truly one can say: since we hold noten taam lifgam permitted, rinsing is also not required—only the Rif was stringent—and think. But truly I did not know the wonder: did not the Rashba cry out: how can beit hashchitah be hotter than kli sheni heat, which does not cook—and from this he was compelled to say kli sheni absorbs and expels in any case? But the Shulchan Aruch below simanim 68 and 105 does not hold thus—the difficulty is in place; and Peri Chadash in siman 68 wrote: indeed it is difficult for the view that therefore peel is because beit hashchitah is hot; but for the view of knife pressure—and truly this too is strained, that knife pressure would absorb more than kli sheni heat. And even if we say like Beit Shammai—what shall we say in what most authors wrote? See below those simanim regarding metal vessels: even kli rishon does not cook (and according to Rabbenu Tam and Rosh it cooks and absorbs everything as one) until fire passes beneath it; and shall heat in beit hashchitah be more than kli rishon heat, without fire passing beneath—and therefore the Rif holds everything reverts to those views in Yerushalmi chapter Kirah and Ma'aser Sheni, that kli sheni or pouring cooks even in copper vessels, and equals other matters; but according to our conclusion there, in metal vessels even kli rishon does not help to absorb—how can heat in beit hashchitah act to expel from a shechitah knife's absorption and require peeling? Therefore he ruled halachah not like Rav, and only rinsing is needed. And it is possible to distinguish: kli sheni heat comes elsewhere incidentally, and all the more if it will stand and cool—therefore it lacks power to absorb; but heat in beit hashchitah has heat in its essence, for the bloods boil and have power to absorb. Nevertheless, that even kli rishon does not cook until fire passes beneath is difficult: if its power is not as good as beit hashchitah or knife pressure—and I said in my novellae: it is proved there is not so much heat in beit hashchitah—for otherwise how do we slaughter the Pesach? The shechitah knife heats beit hashchitah, and afterward the knife is spun for peeling beit hashchitah—and spinning is because of meat; see Pesachim: a drop fell from its gravy, etc.—Tosafot challenged thus regarding a spit; and thus it is not hot, only for Rav who holds "upper man"—if so, the knife is not heated at all and not spun for Pesach, as appears there in the above view; and fine to say beit hashchitah heat is strong. Therefore Rav holds peel; if so, for us who hold "lower man," there is none—proved there is no hot beit hashchitah; therefore he ruled rinse—and think. And truly it is correct and clear: although peeling is required, he holds only stringently that the rabbis were stringent—and therefore there is no proof for beit hashchitah, for rabbinic decree there is no difficulty or comparison; and if so, in any case rabbinic decree, and in a place of doubt we are lenient—no place to be stringent.
In a place of pressure, etc.—and if he stuck in the ground or rinsed the knife so there is no visible grease, and the knife is ben yomo: the Rashba's view—nothing is needed, for there is no heat in beit hashchitah to expel anything from the hard knife (and this is somewhat as I wrote regarding metal vessels requiring fire beneath). So the Bach for the Rashba's view, and so Peri Chadash—and not like the Shach on the Rashba; the investigator in Tur Orach Chaim will find this explicitly, and elaboration here is unnecessary. However, the Mordechai at the beginning of the first chapter of Chullin holds: specifically when not ben yomo, sticking suffices; but for ben yomo, sticking does not help and hag'alah is required. And in Tosafot Chullin 8, s.v. shelivnah, who wrote: therefore with an avodah zarah knife a brick is required, and sticking does not suffice—in order to reconcile even if beit hashchitah is boiling. And if Tosafot held like the Mordechai, the rule should require peeling and sticking should not help—they should have said briefly: the Gemara comes according to the conclusion that hag'alah is required and sticking does not help—what difference if beit hashchitah is boiling or cold, finally sticking does not help by law, and shall the Gemara be against halachah? Rather Tosafot clearly hold: since we hold halachah like Ravina in cold because beit hashchitah is cold, as Rashi wrote explicitly—only sticking is needed, for beit hashchitah does not absorb or expel the knife's absorption; if so, even with that avodah zarah knife, sticking suffices; therefore Tosafot wrote a brick is needed to reconcile even for one who holds beit hashchitah is boiling—namely R' Elazar's colleague, who holds hot; and this is also resolved according to him, and for his view sticking does not help. And if so, it is proved Tosafot do not hold like the Mordechai, only according to what we hold like Ravina, sticking suffices, as the Rashba's view. And Rashi wrote, s.v. vehalachta: even in cold regarding a permitted knife he ruled halachah for the permitted side, that it is not forbidden from this absorption, and it is hard to absorb except through boiling; but regarding a forbidden gentile knife, in the earlier dispute he did not rule, because the knife and meat are soft to absorb, end of his words. And so the Rosh wrote there; and thus according to the plain meaning: Rav forbids because the meat is soft and absorbs forbidden grease; but in Ravina's matter, where the knife absorbs treifah through heat in beit hashchitah, and likewise in second shechitah beit hashchitah expels from the hard knife—we do not say so. And so the Rashba wrote: and if so, with a gentile knife that was stuck or wiped well, there is no concern of grease on the surface, only from absorption in the knife—heat in beit hashchitah has no power to expel and re-absorb; sticking helps and hag'alah is not required. These are exactly Rashba's words, following Rashi. Indeed in the Rosh's words one can wonder: the above Rashi holds like the Rashba regarding a gentile knife with grease smeared on its face, therefore peeling is required, for beit hashchitah absorbs forbidden grease into soft meat. But the Rosh, who wrote a gentile knife speaks where known ben yomo—otherwise it would be noten taam lifgam and permitted—we learn he does not hold grease on the surface; if so, there is no absorption and expulsion in beit hashchitah, and here the knife is hard—and strained to divide between absorption and expulsion, for the rule is: as it absorbs, so it expels; and why should the Rosh hold: even though grease is smeared, nevertheless if the knife is not ben yomo, even smeared grease is spoiled, as the Rambam and his support hold—for the Tur siman 128 wrote without any dispute that visible matter is not spoiled; and if his father disagreed, how plain against his view? And it is clear this is the Rosh's view: we are not concerned that fat is smeared on the face, as appears from his words; if so, the difficulty is in place. And truly we must be precise in Rashi: if Rashi's intent is only to distinguish Rav from Ravina, as appears from the Rashba—there soft matter and in Ravina hard knife—why did Rashi always mention forbidden and permitted language, saying regarding a permitted knife he ruled halachah for the permitted side, etc., but regarding a forbidden gentile knife in the earlier matter he did not rule because knife and meat are soft to absorb—what did Rashi seek here? Forbidden or permitted—all depends on soft and hard; and if permitted is also soft, peel. And it appears certainly besides soft/hard distinction, there is a broad distinction between Rav and Ravina: certainly through heat in beit hashchitah the knife absorbs or expels a slight thing, for knife pressure or pressure is not great; therefore with a knife that is inherently permitted, only that he slaughtered treifah with it and it absorbed slightly through heat in beit hashchitah, and afterward when slaughtering another animal, heat in beit hashchitah expels from this second slight matter—it is a small of a small and we are not concerned. But with a forbidden gentile knife with much absorption—even if beit hashchitah expels only slightly, there is still a small amount and peeling is required. Therefore also with a knife with which he cut boiling, it is forbidden and requires peeling—for although the knife absorbed only slightly, this slight matter is expelled explicitly to boiling and forbids. And this explanation you will find the faith of these words almost in the Rashba's and Razah's novellae. Therefore Rashi may have intended: why he precisely distinguished forbidden from permitted—look and you will see as words only seemingly difficult; therefore it appears certainly the Gemara's plain gentile knife speaks of ben yomo, as Tosafot and Rosh wrote, or of not ben yomo only not wiped and grease smeared on it. In every case Rav speaks, and for that case where it is not ben yomo and we judge because of smeared grease—there is no distinction between forbidden and permitted, for smearing itself is a slight visible matter, as all poskim wrote; if so, if beit hashchitah's heat does not expel and absorb only slightly, it is still a small of a small and still difficult to permit—therefore we distinguish soft from hard: with soft, beit hashchitah absorbs more—therefore with a gentile treifah knife. This is in that case; but in another case, not Rav's smearing matter, only ben yomo knife—there is no soft/hard distinction, for heat in beit hashchitah is needed to expel the knife's absorption for Rav. And one must say the second answer: although it is hard and beit hashchitah absorbs only slightly—what of it, the knife was forbidden beforehand and its absorption is much, unlike a treifah knife that absorbed only slightly; therefore heat in beit hashchitah does not expel from hard either except slightly—and it is a small of a small, and we are not so stringent—and think. And it is precisely analyzed in Rashi's language—and if the Rosh wrote: with a knife that slaughtered, forbidden to cut boiling without hag'alah, even though he ruled there is no heat in beit hashchitah controlling the hard knife—for according to the one who says beit hashchitah is boiling, even for Ravina, and since there is some absorption, the boiling cut afterward fully absorbs and forbids (and what he wrote below for the one who says cold, etc., I will write in the next s.k.)—if so, it is clarified for us: with a ben yomo knife, even clean of smeared fat, nevertheless heat in beit hashchitah expels slightly into the slaughtered animal, even though the knife is hard, and peeling is required, not sticking—and this is the Mordechai's view above; nevertheless one may be lenient, since in any case the Rif and Rambam rule not like Rav and Rashba, and Tosafot permit sticking—one may rely.
