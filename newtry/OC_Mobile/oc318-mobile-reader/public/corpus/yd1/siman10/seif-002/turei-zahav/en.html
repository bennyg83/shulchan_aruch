Soiled with blood. He explained in Tur Orach Chaim that beit hashchitah is like boiling for this—that it absorbs through the knife's pressure even if not soiled with blood—and not like Rashi; and his proof from that a knife with which he slaughtered is forbidden to cut boiling—and the reason is nevertheless beit hashchitah boils somewhat and absorbs blood in the knife; nevertheless permitted to slaughter with it because the simanim are busy expelling blood and do not absorb, as in the Gemara.
Permitted to slaughter with it another time. It implies without any rinsing or wiping; and so Maharshal concludes; and it is wondrous from what is in Chullin (daf 8): there is an opinion regarding a treifah knife that hag'alah in hot water is required, and it asks on it: why is treifah different, that it absorbs prohibition—for when he slaughtered kosher it also absorbed eiver min hachai, and the knife should require hag'alah even when slaughtering kosher? And it answers: when does the knife absorb? While the animal lives; and when does it live? At the completion of shechitah—at that moment there is no eiver min hachai—learn that the heter is only because the absorption is only at the end; but if the prohibition is not from absorption but only from what is visible on the knife, it certainly forbids even before completion of shechitah—for this does not need heating; and according to the one who says beit hashchitah is cold, there is prohibition from what is visible on the knife; if so, when slaughtering kosher it should be forbidden to slaughter afterward, since visible prohibition of eiver min hachai is on the knife. And it appears we only ask if the concern is taste absorbed in the knife that will return and absorb in the simanim—we also find in kosher absorption of forbidden absorbed matter from eiver min hachai; but if the concern is only what remains visible, this does not apply to eiver min hachai—for there is no substance of eiver min hachai, only mixed in blood; and since blood does not absorb because simanim are busy expelling their blood, therefore no prohibition even from eiver min hachai; rather they were concerned about treifah animal grease that adheres (in the matter) to the knife, which is something that adheres and joins even at completion of shechitah—in this they were certainly concerned and required kashering the knife, as above. And I saw in Hagahah in the Agudah first chapter of Chullin: also when slaughtering kosher, wiping the knife is required because of eiver min hachai; and therefore it does not ask for the one who says regarding a treifah knife in cold: there is eiver min hachai prohibition, as it asks for the one who says in hot—therefore, end of his words; and Orach Chaim brought and strengthened his words to say: even according to the conclusion that it absorbs only at completion of shechitah—meaning it does not absorb so much that hag'alah is required; but in cold nevertheless it absorbs somewhat before completion of shechitah; and therefore he ruled: even when slaughtering a bird, wiping with something hard is required. And to me it appears as I wrote: otherwise sticking in the ground would be required, like one who absorbs treifot frequently—and we do not find sticking except for a knife that frequently has treifah; and furthermore, how did the Torah command any shechitah—shall we say that at the beginning of shechitah it absorbs somewhat from eiver min hachai prohibition and forbids at completion? Rather it is clear this has no basis and no one is concerned for it at all.
To cut cold with it. He did not need to write this law—for even for treifah slaughter, rinsing helps even to slaughter with it again, as seif 3 says; rather he mentioned cold law here since he mentioned boiling law; and it is simple that if he cut cold without rinsing the knife first, he rinses that cold afterward.
Pouring suffices for him. Because beit hashchitah is not fully boiling, as the Tur wrote in the name of the Rashba.
Even though it is not kli rishon. With kaf, not bet—meaning kli sheni.
