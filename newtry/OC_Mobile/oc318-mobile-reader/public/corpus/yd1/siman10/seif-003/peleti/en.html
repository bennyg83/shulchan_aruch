Until one rinses it when cold—for we ruled above with a gentile knife like Rav who said peel, requiring peeling, as I explained what Rashi wrote and Be'ur Rosh's words and what difference cutting boiling has. And truly in my humble view in Tosafot one can say simply and think: all the reasoning that beit hashchitah is boiling or knife pressure absorbs and expels is one opinion, not decided whether it expels and how much—much or little—or nullified in a small amount. If so, general rule for us: in min b'aino mino it is forbidden from the Torah and poured until the measure cannot stand; and treifah doubt is Torah stringency; and in min b'mino it is kosher, rabbinic doubt lenient. If so, slaughtering with a treifah knife—even if it absorbed kosher treifah grease—it is min b'mino, and we doubt whether it absorbed; plainly kosher, rabbinic doubt. But with a gentile knife—if it absorbs, it is min b'aino mino, doubt stringently, law with Rav who peels—and think. Therefore with a knife that slaughtered and blood is on it and he slaughtered boiling—it is min b'aino mino, blood in other things—even Ravina concedes hag'alah is required—and think. With this one can explain Tosafot on a gentile knife: known ben yomo—and not as the Rosh's printers in Avodah Zarah chapter 2, noten taam lifgam forbidden l'chatchila (and one can explain the Rosh's words: even though he used "after he did," for Rav who holds noten taam lifgam forbidden; the Gemara bringing the dispute, although we hold noten taam lifgam permitted, is because of l'chatchila). If so, this is rabbinically forbidden, not the law of peel—and think. All is reconciled: the Rosh's view that wiping a thick slice does not help when cutting cold with a knife that slaughtered, and we hold wiping helps when slaughtering treifah; see Shach and Kereti s.k. 18—according to what I said: slaughtering treifah is min b'mino, cutting cold min b'aino mino—they were stringent. Rashi, who did not answer so, goes to his view that min b'mino is not nullified by sixty, and min b'aino mino rabbinic—taste is not like essence, not Torah. But since we do not hold thus, the matter is correct and clear. And one can say: nevertheless difficult from that he slaughtered with it, forbidden to cut boiling—and if it is min b'aino mino and Torah, all boiling concern applies; and absorbed blood is cooked or roasted blood from the Torah permitted—its law equals min b'mino. Truth in my novellae: one can say what is taught forbidden to cut boiling, and if he cut, rinsing suffices, and what is taught cold requires rinsing—cold is stricter than boiling; and if hypothetically he cut an apple and blood is visible from the knife, he is pressed—this is eating Torah prohibition; but if it adhered to something boiling, in that boiling thing which is boiling kli rishon it cooked—and cooked blood is rabbinic—here rabbinic doubt. In my humble view this answer suffices greatly. With this also what all acharonim strongly challenged on the Tur—if he did not rinse when slaughtering treifah, he rinses beit hashchitah afterward—for it is no worse than a gentile knife where we hold peel—is fine according to what I said: if treifah grease adhered to the knife and from there to kosher animal, it is doubt and min b'mino, like noten taam lifgam and kosher; unlike a gentile knife, min b'aino mino, Torah doubt stringently.
And if accustomed—this is Tosafot's answer: why does avodah zarah require sticking here and not? Answer: here it is only by chance. Therefore he ruled: if accustomed, sticking is required. But Rabbeinu Tam divides another way: here absorption is in cold, and avodah zarah absorbs in hot; and Tosafot challenged from Shmuel's knife, etc., we learn he holds beit hashchitah boiling—nevertheless in cold they said rinsing required; answer: blood is different, it slides slick. The Mechaber ruled like Tosafot, not Rabbeinu Tam; therefore he distinguishes accustomed from chance in general. If so, we learn he does not hold Rabbeinu Tam's view to distinguish blood that slides from other things—and if so, difficult above seif 2 with a knife that slaughtered: he does not distinguish chance from accustomed, never requires sticking—and we were because blood slides, Rabbeinu Tam's answer that Tosafot do not hold; if so, difficult against itself. The Shach in s.k. 15 gave the reason blood slides and did not notice this is only according to Rabbeinu Tam—but Tosafot do not hold. Strained to say: he knew blood slides acts so it does not adhere much—even many slaughterings, it is like slaughtering by chance; but regarding not needing sticking at all and use through cold, Tosafot do not distinguish—and this is strained. More likely: sticking in the ground acts in two aspects—if thereby everything adhering with ultimate adhesion on the knife is removed, so even the outer thin peel peeled falls off in sticking (see Shach s.k. 7). If we come to judgment on what adheres on the knife's face, certainly distinguish blood from other things—for blood is scraped and slick, no adhesion at all, as the Ra'ah wrote there daf 16. But if we say it is nevertheless absorbed into such a thin outer peel, hard to say blood is scraped, etc.; and conversely, if to remove the peel, there is difference between absorption in hot and in cold—as understood, Rabbeinu Tam's view; but if what adheres externally on the knife's face—what difference hot from cold? If so, certainly Tosafot do not distinguish hot from cold—and they hold sticking certainly removes what is smeared on the knife's face, and certainly distinguish blood from other things—and think. But for Rabbeinu Tam, who distinguishes absorption in hot from cold, and therefore holds the thin outer peel absorbs—and in this Tosafot do not distinguish blood, for according to them it slides slick—therefore they challenged him from a knife that slaughtered blood, and Rabbeinu Tam answers: nevertheless we say this reasoning, and infer cold blood does not absorb so much—indeed he inferred it does not absorb—and therefore the Shach's words are correct: for all we say this reasoning blood slides slick—and think.
