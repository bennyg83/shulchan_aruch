That is, until after the first Shabbat. Whether near or distant. The Bach wrote that all the more so it is forbidden to go to a seudat mitzvah, such as a brit milah, until Motzaei Shabbat; see there. In Responsa Masat Binyamin, siman 83, he wrote as follows: Regarding relatives of the deceased who are not obligated to mourn but nevertheless show some signs of mourning, I have seen different local customs. There are places where only relatives invalid for testimony to the deceased show mourning, while those valid for testimony do not show mourning at all. There are places where even third with fourth show mourning. Where the custom exists, it exists; where there is no custom, it appears that only those invalid for testimony should show mourning, like Terumat HaDeshen, siman 298. Nevertheless, it appears that not all relatives are equal in this; the closer relative takes precedence. Those relatives who by law would have been obligated to mourn fully, as we say "anyone for whom you mourn, you mourn with him," although nowadays they do not practice so, nevertheless in actual mourning they must show mourning on the day of death or the day of hearing, if within thirty days, until the first Motzaei Shabbat. They should not change from weekday garments to Shabbat garments, nor wash in hot water, nor wash the head, except in cold or lukewarm water. They should not eat outside the house, neither at a seudat mitzvah nor a social meal. But a white shirt should not be withheld in honor of Shabbat. They also should not change their place in the synagogue. On Motzaei Shabbat everything is permitted, whether he is distant or close. Those relatives who by law do not mourn should not be made stringent regarding washing in hot water, washing the head, or changing to Shabbat garments so much; it is enough for them to wear the upper weekday garment and not eat outside the home. All the more so in those places where even those valid for testimony show some mourning, one should not be so stringent; end quote. What he wrote to permit washing in hot water and washing the head for those relatives who do not mourn with the mourner does not appear so from the words of Terumat HaDeshen and the Rav, for they prohibit washing for relatives of the deceased who are invalid for testimony, meaning second with second, although they do not mourn. Also what he wrote to be stringent that those who mourn should not change at all from weekday garments to Shabbat garments does not appear so from their words; rather, they change only some of their garments. Nevertheless, it appears that in these matters we follow the custom.
