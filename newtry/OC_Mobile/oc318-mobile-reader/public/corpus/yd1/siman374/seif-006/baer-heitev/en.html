Those who astonish. The Shach wrote: although one who wishes to be stringent and mourn for someone for whom he is not required to mourn has permission to do so, as the Rav wrote at the end of the gloss, that is specifically when he mourns completely according to the laws of mourning. But when he mourns only in the mourner's presence, he is like one who mocks the mourner. End quote. It appears to me that “we do not protest” means one who does this on his own, knowing he is not obligated, but observes some mourning in honor of the dead because he loved him, was related to him, because of the dead person's good deeds, or for other reasons. This is fully permitted, as Darkhei Moshe brings from Responsa Rosh, klal 27 siman 9: you asked whether they may wear black for a father-in-law who died, or for a beloved friend, for twelve months. For a father-in-law it is obvious that he may; for a beloved friend too, if he wants to distress himself over him by wearing black or for his honor, he may. So too we find that David accepted the mourner's meal over Avner. End quote. What is called “among those who astonish” is one who wants to do it as an obligation to observe mourning like the mourners and force himself into kinship like actual mourners; that is like mocking the mourner. In Even HaEzer 145:9 in the gloss, regarding a woman divorced conditionally whose husband died, she need not mourn for him, but there is no prohibition if she wants to be stringent on herself to cry over him and follow his bier. End quote. Beit Hillel explained that we are not concerned that people will cast aspersions on the get and say it is not a get. Nevertheless, there is no prohibition; thus he uses the language of her wishing to be stringent in some matter, which is permitted.
For testimony. See Choshen Mishpat siman 33 regarding who is disqualified for testimony. Nevertheless, it appears that this refers specifically to those disqualified for testimony because of kinship of other mourners, not because of kinship by marriage, as below in the following seif. End quote from Shach.
The first. The Bach wrote that all the more so it is forbidden to go to a mitzvah meal, such as a brit milah, until Saturday night. Responsa Mas'et Binyamin wrote that some places have the custom that only those disqualified for testimony regarding the dead show a little mourning, while in other places even third with fourth show mourning; where there is a custom, there is a custom, and where there is no custom, it appears that only those disqualified for testimony should show mourning. Nevertheless, not all relatives are equal for this; the closer relative takes precedence. Those relatives who by law would have been obligated in full mourning, even though nowadays the custom is not so, must nevertheless show mourning on the day of death or on the day of report if it is within thirty days, until the first Saturday night: they should not change from weekday clothes to Shabbat clothes, should not wash in hot water or wash the head, only in cold or lukewarm water, and should not eat outside the home, neither at a mitzvah meal nor at a companionship meal. But one should not prevent a white shirt in honor of Shabbat, nor should they change their places in the synagogue. On Saturday night everything is permitted, whether the relative is distant or close. Those relatives for whom by law one does not mourn should not be stringent in all these matters; it is enough for them to wear the weekday outer garment and not eat outside the house. End quote. What he wrote permitting relatives for whom one does not mourn by law to wash in hot water, etc., does not appear so from the Rav's words, for he forbids washing for those disqualified for testimony, meaning second with second, even though by law they do not mourn. Also what he wrote to be stringent for those who mourn by law not to change at all from weekday clothes does not appear correct; rather, they change only some of their garments. In any event, it appears that in these matters we follow the custom. End quote from Shach.
