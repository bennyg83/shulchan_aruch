Those who astonish. The Shach wrote: Although one who wants to be stringent and mourn for someone for whom he is not required to mourn has permission, as the Rav wrote at the end of the gloss, that is specifically when he mourns completely according to the law of mourning; but when he mourns only in front of him, he is like one who mocks the mourner; end quote. To me it appears that when they said we do not protest, that refers to one who does this on his own, knowing he is not obligated, but practicing a little mourning in honor of the dead because he loved him, was his relative, because of good deeds in him, or for other reasons. This is entirely permitted, as Darkei Moshe brings from Responsa Rosh, klal 27, siman 9: You asked whether they may wear black for a father-in-law who died or for a beloved friend for twelve months. For a father-in-law it is obvious that he may, etc.; and for a beloved friend too, if he wants to grieve for him by wearing black or for his honor, he may. So too we find that David accepted a meal of comfort for Avner; end quote. What it says that he is only among those who astonish refers to one who wants to do this as an obligation to practice mourning like mourners, forcing himself into the category of closeness like actual mourners; this is called like mocking the mourner. In Even HaEzer siman 145 seif 9 in the gloss: a woman who was divorced conditionally and the husband died need not mourn for him, but there is no prohibition if she wants to be stringent upon herself, cry for him, and follow his bier; end quote. Beit Hillel explained that we are not concerned lest they cast aspersions on the get and say it is not a get; nevertheless there is no prohibition. Thus it says in the language that she wants to be stringent in some matter, and it is permitted.
For testimony. See Choshen Mishpat siman 33 regarding who are invalid for testimony. Nevertheless, it appears that this refers specifically to those invalid for testimony because of blood relationship, for other mourning is not because of a marriage relationship, as below in the next seif; end quote of the Shach.
The first. The Bach wrote that all the more so he is forbidden to go to a seudat mitzvah, such as a brit milah, until Motzaei Shabbat. Responsa Mishnat Binyamin wrote that there are places where only relatives invalid for testimony to the dead show some mourning, and there are places where even third with fourth show mourning; where the custom is so, it is so, and where it is not the custom, it appears that only those invalid for testimony should show mourning. Nevertheless, it appears that not all relatives are equal in this; the closer relative takes precedence. Those relatives who by law would have been obligated to mourn fully, although nowadays they do not practice so, nevertheless must show mourning on the day of death or on the day they hear, if within thirty days, until the first Motzaei Shabbat. They should not change from weekday garments to Shabbat garments, nor wash in hot water or wash the head, only in cold or lukewarm water. They should not eat outside their home, neither at a seudat mitzvah nor a social meal. But a white shirt should not be withheld in honor of Shabbat. Nor should they change their place in the synagogue. On Motzaei Shabbat everything is permitted, whether the relation is distant or close. Those relatives who by law do not mourn should not be stringent in all these, and it is enough for them to wear the upper weekday garment and not eat outside the home; end quote. What he wrote to permit relatives who do not mourn by law to wash in hot water, etc., does not appear so from the Rav's words, for he forbids washing to those invalid for testimony, meaning second with second, even though by law they do not mourn. Also what he wrote to be stringent for those who mourn by law not to change at all from weekday garments does not appear so; rather, they change only some of their garments. Nevertheless, it appears that in these matters we follow the custom; end quote of the Shach.
