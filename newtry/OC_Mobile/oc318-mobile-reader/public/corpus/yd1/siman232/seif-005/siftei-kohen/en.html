All produce, etc. Teshuvot Maimoniyot, siman 25, writes that although one who vows from 'the produce of the year' is forbidden only the year's produce and may eat kids and lambs, nevertheless one who says, 'All produce in the world is forbidden to me,' is forbidden from everything. The third chapter of Shevuot indeed classifies as a vain oath a statement that all produce in the world shall be forbidden to him. Moreover, it is possible that everything that grows in the world is included in 'the produce of the world,' including fish, flax, hemp, wool, silk, wood, animals, and many other things that are produced or grow from one another and are called derivatives of produce or things that grow from the ground. This implies that the decisive word is 'all,' not specifically 'in the world.' The proof from Shevuot also indicates this, unlike the apparent implication of the Ateret Zekenim. The Bach objects that this contradicts siman 176, where clothing and utensils are not included in 'produce,' even though they are made from wool, flax, hemp, or silk, and according to some texts the same question applies to wood. There is no difficulty, because there he did not say 'all.' The Perishah makes the same distinction. See also siman 217, seif katan 30.
It is not a vow. If he says that he intended only one or two days, he is believed, for it is evident that this was certainly his intent, since the vow cannot otherwise be fulfilled. All the more so regarding an oath, for otherwise he would have taken a vain oath. If, however, he forbade himself only some types of produce, he is not believed unless there was coercion, as discussed below in seif 14. Bach, end of siman 16. See also siman 210.
But if he said, etc. If he later performs that specified act, some authorities say the prohibition takes effect, because it was possible that he would not violate the condition. The Tur, however, writes that even in such a case it is a vain vow, and the Rosh writes likewise at the beginning of HaMedir in the name of Rabbeinu Tam. It appears that the Rama's statement that some disagree also refers to this point. Nevertheless, one should be stringent, for this is also the view of Tosafot at the beginning of HaMedir, the Semag, and Hagahot Ashri there in the name of Mahari'ach citing Rabbi Yitzchak.
And some disagree. They hold that it is a vain vow. If, however, he said, 'Except for meat,' it is not a vain vow, because meat settles a person's mind, and one can subsist on meat, cheese, and milk. This is stated in Teshuvot Maimoniyot and cited by the Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe. I wrote similarly above in siman 217, seif katan 31, according to the Ran and later authorities. It appears that all the more so, if he said, 'Except for grain,' it is not a vain vow, since 'grain' includes the five species, as explained above in siman 217, seif 8. This is also implied above in siman 208, seif 2.
