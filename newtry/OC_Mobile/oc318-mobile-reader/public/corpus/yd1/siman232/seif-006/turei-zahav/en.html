Likewise, one who says, “If so-and-so was,” etc. The same applies when two people disputed a matter, each claiming that the facts were as he said, and they gave one another a handshake oath that whoever was correct would receive a specified sum from the other. The one who loses is exempt for this reason, for he acted under a mistaken belief. Proof is found in Shevuos 26a, where two students of Rav disputed what Rav had said. Each declared, “I am liable to bring an offering if Rav did not say as I maintain.” They asked Rav, and he said that he had spoken according to one of them. The other said, “If so, I am liable to bring an offering.” Rav answered, “Your heart compelled you,” meaning that you were under duress because you erred in your understanding. The verse states, “a person with an oath,” excluding one who acts under duress. So too, whenever a person errs in his understanding, it is considered duress, and he is exempt from what he obligated himself to pay. Although Choshen Mishpat, siman 203, seif 10, states regarding two who made a kinyan to exchange that some say the acquisition is effective, there they consciously completed and transferred ownership despite the uncertainty. Here, however, he made his obligation depend upon a fact which he believed to be certain and had no doubt about it at the time. We find a similar distinction in Choshen Mishpat, siman 207, regarding asmachta.
