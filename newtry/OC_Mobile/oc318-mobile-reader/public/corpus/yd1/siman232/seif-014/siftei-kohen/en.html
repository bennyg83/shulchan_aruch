Or one who swears, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that although the Mishnah mentions only vows, the Yerushalmi states that the same applies to oaths. The Bach wonders, for the Mishnah itself records a dispute between Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel regarding an oath. There is no difficulty. The Mishnah's oath resembles a vow, where one says, 'All produce shall be forbidden to me forever if I am not of the royal household.' At the moment of swearing he has not sworn falsely, because he can keep the oath by abstaining from produce. This is implied by the wording that one may make vows to robbers, except by means of an oath, while Beit Hillel permits even an oath. The point is that one may impose a prohibition upon produce by a vow, but according to Beit Shammai not by an oath. The Yerushalmi, however, proves that even an oath that is false at the moment it is uttered is permitted under coercion, for Rabbi Yishmael derives from 'You shall not swear falsely in My Name' that one may swear to murderers, bandits, and tax collectors. Otherwise, the Mishnah itself would already establish the rule. The verse too indicates an oath that would otherwise be false. The Beit Yosef and Rama therefore rule below that even such an oath is permitted when he nullifies it in his heart.
All produce in the world, etc. This means that he used wording that does not literally include every product in the world. Otherwise the vow would not take effect at all, as explained above in seif 5. Rather, he forbade himself the fruit of trees and the produce of the ground.
And he thinks in his heart that it shall be forbidden to him only today. If the ruler explicitly requires the oath to apply forever, he should intend: 'For as long as I remain under your coercive power,' unless another necessity or similar condition applies. The authorities write likewise. The Beit Yosef cites Rashbatz that he may formulate any condition in his heart, such as, 'If I do not go to the synagogue today or this week.'
Regarding a coercer, this is permitted. In other contexts, unspoken intentions have no legal effect. This differs from the case above in siman 210 where one intended to say 'wheat bread' but said 'barley bread.' If he simply said 'bread,' he is forbidden wheat bread. Here there was no verbal mistake. He intended to say 'forever' aloud, while intending inwardly that it mean only today, so his spoken words prevail. There, however, his speech was mistaken, because he intended to say 'wheat bread.' His utterance therefore has no force. Strictly speaking, his unspoken intent should also have no force, but Scripture teaches that we follow his intent where his speech does not contradict it, since wheat bread is also called bread. Thus writes the Rosh in the chapter Shevuot Shtayim in the name of Ramban and other authorities.
But if the coercers know, etc. Maharshal writes in chapter HaGozel Batra, siman 17, that if he whispers the condition with his lips, it is permitted. Although there remains a concern of desecrating Hashem's Name, we do not impose such stringency where he faces severe coercion and has no alternative. Verbal nullification is effective only within the time required for a brief greeting, as stated in Hagahot Maimoniyot in the name of the Semag and cited by the Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe.
Therefore Zedekiah was punished, etc. This follows the Rama's position below in seif 15. According to the view that an oath remains binding even under threat of death, the reason for his punishment is straightforward. This is explicit in Pesakim U'Ketavim of Mahari, siman 73. Likewise, according to Mahari Weil, cited below in seif katan 40, that a ban not to disclose a matter takes effect even under coercion, Zedekiah's case is also readily understood.
Nevertheless, if he informed them, etc. Responsa Mahari Weil appears to prohibit even in such a case, since he did not permit it. The Rama understood him this way in Darchei Moshe, but wrote that the matter requires study. Yoma states that if he informed the coercers from the outset that his words contained a stratagem, there is no desecration of Hashem's Name. The Rama therefore concludes that everything depends on the degree of necessity and coercion. It appears to me that there is no proof from that passage. Rabbi Yochanan swore to the noblewoman, 'By the God of Israel, I will not reveal it,' and later taught the matter publicly. The Gemara explains that he meant, 'I will not reveal it to her, but I will reveal it to the people of Israel,' and asks about desecration of Hashem's Name. It answers that he disclosed this interpretation to her from the outset. That case differs because the wording itself bore that interpretation.
Two Jews struck one another, and a governor demanded testimony. If he ordered the community to impose a ban, they may not do so merely to save themselves at their fellow's financial expense when they can reach some form of settlement. This is the wording of the Agudah and Hagahot Mordechai there.
More than his lawful punishment. If the ruler will punish him only according to law, they may testify after obtaining release from the ban. This is the Rama's view. See Choshen Mishpat, beginning of siman 228.
