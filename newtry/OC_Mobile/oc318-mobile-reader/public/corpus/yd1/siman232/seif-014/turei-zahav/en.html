To murderers and tax collectors. The Mishnah mentions another category as well, namely robbers. It teaches the cases in ascending novelty. Not only may one vow to murderers, but even to those who merely seize property, and even to tax collectors, who are not formally called robbers, one may nevertheless make such a vow. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch mention only the greatest novelty, in the manner of “not only this, but even that.”
Without authorization from the king. Otherwise, there is no robbery, because the law of the kingdom is law. Why then would one evade the tax?
All the fruits in the world shall be forbidden to me. One should not ask that seif 5 states that one who says, “All the fruits in the world are a konam upon me,” has not made a valid vow. Why then must he think in his heart, “today”? The answer is that it is nevertheless a vain vow, as written by the Tur and Shulchan Aruch there, and so wrote the Perishah. Although the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 237 cites opinions that one does not receive lashes for a vain vow, there is still a prohibition. Therefore, here he needs a method that avoids even any prohibition. It is obvious that if he forbids only some fruits and did not think “today,” the oath takes effect and requires release. This differs from forbidding all fruits, which after the fact requires no release.
To nullify it in his heart by way of a condition. For example, he should think, “I will do all this only if I do not put on tefillin,” or a similar condition.
And they should nullify it in their hearts. I do not understand the Rama. How does this differ from seif 15, which states that a vow made under physical torment, and certainly under threat of death, has no validity and requires no mental nullification? Here too, it is obvious that refusal to obey the governor’s decree exposes them to physical torment and death. This is not comparable to vows made to murderers and tax collectors. Those people do not kill, but merely seize money, as the Beit Yosef writes in the name of the Hagahos Maimoniyos to distinguish the case in seif 15. This is also implied by the Rosh’s responsum cited by the Beit Yosef, which states that this resembles one who swears that he will eat pork. It is obvious that one who swears to eat pork requires no mental nullification. I also do not understand how mental nullification applies here. One may not think the opposite of what he says, but only a condition. That works in the nearby case, where he says, “All fruits shall be forbidden to me,” and thinks “today,” or where he thinks another condition, such as, “if I do not put on tefillin.” Here, however, where he swears that he does not know that so-and-so had relations with a non-Jewish woman, or a similar matter, what condition can apply when he actually does know? His heart would contradict his mouth. Perhaps he can think, “I do not know whether he had relations with her on another occasion after the coercer ordered that a cherem be imposed upon whoever knows,” etc. The writing stating that they should nullify it in their hearts is from the Beit Yosef, who says that this appears correct and compares it to vows made to murderers. In truth, the cases are not comparable for the reason I wrote. This requires further study.
So that there should not be a desecration of Hashem’s Name, etc. This applies in the present case, which concerns money. In the preceding case, where the testimony would cause a person’s death, we do not make this distinction.
Regarding what is written, “and a coercive governor decreed after the testimony,” this means that he ordered the community to impose a cherem upon anyone who knew the testimony. So it is written in the Agudah, in the final chapter of Bava Kamma: It was found in the name of Maharam that when two Jews, etc., and the coercer went to the synagogue and ordered the community to impose a cherem, they may not impose it in order to save themselves through another person’s money if they can reach a settlement by any means. If they cannot, they should testify and reimburse the injured party for his entire loss, end quote. This is also the Rama’s intent in writing that it is considered duress, meaning that the cherem does not take effect and they need not testify. If, however, there is no way to avoid testimony, such as where the non-Jew knows that this person knows that he struck him, he should testify and reimburse the loss. It appears that if the coercer does not identify the witness but only imposes a general decree because he knows someone in the community has information, the community must reimburse the loss.
