Or slightly less. This follows the opinion of the Ran cited by the Beit Yosef. I do not know why he wrote specifically “slightly.” The Ran wrote: Although they intended three dinars, the seller may give it for less than three and the buyer may give more, because the implication of the vow was only to urge the other party. Although in their hearts they agreed upon three dinars, that was merely an unspoken intention. Nevertheless, the seller may not give it for a shekel and the buyer may not give a sela, because the primary implication of their vows was precisely for that. There is also one who says that even in such a case their vows do not take effect, end quote. Thus, even according to the stringent opinion, he prohibited only an actual shekel or sela, because that is what they explicitly insisted upon. Anything they did not state is permitted, even if it is much more than three. It appears that the Rama explains that the Ran’s stringency concerning an actual sela and shekel was not stated with exact precision. Rather, it teaches that permission applies only to an intermediate amount between the two, close to each side equally, namely three. Slightly less or more is also considered an intermediate amount. A much greater amount, however, is not intermediate, but closer to one side than the other.
For such is the way of merchants, etc. This means that from the outset they intended three dinars, but each intended through his vow not to remain fixed upon his stated position. Their vows therefore do not take effect, because one’s mouth and heart must be in agreement, whereas here his heart did not agree with his mouth when he made the vow.
And there are those who say, even though, etc. Their reasoning was explained in the nearby words of the Ran.
And there are those who are lenient. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that it appears the common practice follows this opinion.
If I reduce it by more than a sela. The Gemara on 21a states: He said to him, “More than a sela,” and the other says, “Less than a shekel.” Is this a vow or merely urging? The phrase “and the other says” means alternatively, that either one of these formulations suffices, as written by the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. The Ran explains: The seller says, “It is a konam if I reduce the price for you by even one perutah below a sela,” and the buyer says, “It is a konam if I increase the price for you by even one perutah above a shekel.” Since they descend to such exactness, perhaps it is a genuine vow. According to another version, the seller says he will not reduce it below five dinars and the buyer says he will not give more than one dinar. Since they are so far apart, perhaps it is a genuine vow, end quote. The Rosh there appears to follow the second explanation, but the Tur and Shulchan Aruch appear to follow the first, for they wrote “slightly less than a shekel.” It is obvious that because of that slight amount they are not considered extremely far apart. Rather, it must follow the first explanation. The reason is that he used wording indicating exact insistence. This explanation is reasonable, for we similarly find in Choshen Mishpat, siman 83, seif 1, that even if one calculated precisely down to less than a perutah, we do not say that because he was so exact he must be speaking truthfully. There, the reason is that he may be acting deceptively so that the judges will believe him. The Gemara is uncertain whether, in a vow stated with such exact language, all of this was merely an effort to urge the other party to believe that he was exact, or not. The second explanation, however, is difficult. What difference is there whether they initially differ by one shekel or by somewhat more? Therefore, the first explanation appears primary.
