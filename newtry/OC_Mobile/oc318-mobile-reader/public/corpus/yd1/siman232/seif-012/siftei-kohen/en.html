His fellow imposed a vow upon him that he eat with him, etc. This concerns a case that is not merely a vow of inducement, as discussed above in seif 3.
Or a river prevented him. The Beit Yosef cites Rabbeinu Yerucham that this means the river rose because of melting snow, an uncommon accident. If it were common, he should have anticipated it and stipulated accordingly; if he failed to do so, it would remain within the vow. Darchei Moshe cites this. This is also proven by the Gemara, which distinguishes an obvious or foreseeable accident. Rashi explains that the absence of a ferry on a particular side is foreseeable, because at times it is unavailable there and one should anticipate that possibility. The Rosh writes similarly. The Mishnah's case of a river preventing him refers to an unusual rise caused by rain or melting snow, which he was not expected to anticipate. It appears that an event that is neither common nor rare also counts as coercion regarding vows. Rashba, responsum 971, states this. The Gemara and the halachic authorities compare vows in this respect to monetary law, where such an event is treated as coercion, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 54. Although Tosafot in Nedarim may appear otherwise, that discussion compares monetary law to bills of divorce, where an event of intermediate frequency is not treated as coercion. We follow the first version of Rava, according to which monetary law is not equated with divorce. Indeed, all authorities write that illness is such an intermediate event, yet here illness is treated as coercion. This confirms the distinction.
These are vows made under coercion. This implies that even if he did not stipulate at the time of the vow regarding every possible accident, we assume that he did not vow with an accident of this kind in mind. This is obvious, unlike the apparent implication of Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 51.
Vows made under coercion, etc. Responsa Maharam Galanti, siman 89, discusses one who fixed a date to pay his fellow and stipulated that if he failed to pay by then, meat and wine would become forbidden to him. If he lost his property before the deadline, he is exempt from the oath. He discusses the matter at length, though the ruling is straightforward.
And if an accident occurred to him, etc. It appears that the Rama refers to a case where the circumstances demonstrate, or an assessment shows, that from the outset he never intended to spend such an enormous sum merely to fulfill his oath. Pesakim U'Ketavim of Mahari, siman 254, writes similarly. Even if one could hire escorts for one hundred maneh and travel safely, this too is considered coercion, as the Gemara indicates when it asks whether one must pay sacks of dinarim. The Beit Yosef and Rashba, responsum 971, cite the same principle explicitly. If the circumstances are not of this kind, however, one must spend all the money in the world rather than violate his oath, unless he can obtain release from it. This is also clear from Rivash, siman 377, and resolves the Bach's objection to the Rama.
But if no benefit would come to him, etc. This is obvious. Maharik's novelty is that even if he previously delivered a formal declaration nullifying this oath, we do not say that because he revealed his unwillingness to swear, the oath is void. Darchei Moshe cites this, and the Ateret Zekenim rules likewise.
A person is believed to say that his oath was taken under coercion, etc. This appears to apply specifically when he vowed or swore only to himself. If he swore to another person, such as in a debt instrument, he is not believed. This is unlike the ruling of Rabbi Yehudah ben HaRosh that one who says, 'I fulfilled the oath I swore to you,' is believed. There, fulfillment is ordinary; coercion is uncommon, and otherwise anyone could claim that he had been coerced. Moreover, a person is not brazen enough to lie that he fulfilled an oath when the other party can contradict him. The Rashba's responsum cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 228 does not prove otherwise. In that case the coercion was known: his sick sister threatened to cause her own death unless he vowed. Therefore, even though he declared before witnesses that he vowed willingly, we attribute that declaration itself to the coercion. If no coercion was known, however, one who swore to another is not believed merely to claim that he was coerced. Where there are supporting circumstances, he appears to be believed.
Or on such and such a condition. He must also have expressed the condition verbally, because unspoken intentions have no legal effect. Thus writes the Kesef Mishneh at the end of chapter 2 of Hilchot Shevuot in the name of the Raavad.
But if he already, etc. Responsa Rashba explains that the same applies if witnesses warned him and he ignored the warning, failed to state his reservation, and ate. All the more so, if he explicitly said, 'Even so,' he is not believed afterward. Rambam rules likewise in chapter 2 of Hilchot Shevuot, halachah 12. He further writes that if witnesses warned him and he said, 'I never swore or vowed regarding this matter,' and after they testified that he had sworn or vowed he then claimed, 'I did so, but my mouth and heart were not aligned,' or, 'I had a condition in mind,' he is not heard and receives lashes. The same principle appears below in siman 234, seif katan 42. His first denial is comparable to one who says, 'I never borrowed,' which is treated as an admission that he did not repay, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 79. Accordingly, even if witnesses support his later claim, it is ineffective, for a person's admission against himself is stronger than one hundred witnesses.
Or a document states concerning him, etc. The Sema, Choshen Mishpat siman 72, seif katan 16, cites Terumat HaDeshen, siman 326. If a person wrote in his own hand that he swore to pay another and later claims that he merely wrote those words but never actually swore, then if the claimant admits this, there is plainly no concern for an oath. If the claimant does not admit it, the monetary law is the same as for any other document that contains no oath. The Sema adds that if the document says he actually swore, he cannot excuse himself by claiming that he did not, and the same applies if witnesses wrote this in the document. It appears that this last statement concerns a case where the claimant does not admit the defendant's explanation. If he does admit it, there is no concern. Still, the Sema's general citation requires study, for Terumat HaDeshen speaks only where the defendant can explain to the beit din why he is legally exempt or gives a plausible explanation. If he merely says, 'I did not swear and will not fulfill it,' then even if the claimant admits that he did not swear in his presence, perhaps he swore outside his presence. Since he admitted the written statement, he has rendered the matter forbidden upon himself. If the claimant says that he swore in his presence, even a proffered explanation must be examined carefully.
