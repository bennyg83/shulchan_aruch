And if an unavoidable circumstance occurred, etc. As stated in Gittin 30a: 'something like a vessel containing three kav of dinars,' etc., according to the opinion that unavoidable circumstances are legally recognized. According to Rashi there, however, this applies specifically where he lacks the money. The Rivash likewise writes elsewhere, as cited by the Beit Yosef, that financial compulsion is not deemed an unavoidable circumstance. The Terumat HaDeshen and the rulings of Mahari likewise write that only danger to life qualifies; see their lengthy discussion. The Mordechai in chapter 3 of Shevuot and the Gemara likewise indicate this. Even if he says, 'Had I known this, I would not have vowed,' he certainly cannot release his own vow, as stated at the end of Nedarim, 81b: 'he shall not profane,' etc. Moreover, even a sage cannot release it on this basis, because it is a newly arisen circumstance. See siman 228, seif 43.
And likewise if he commanded, etc. As stated in Bava Kamma 117a: 'like an ox caught in a trap,' etc.
Nevertheless, etc. As stated in Gittin there, specifically 'a vessel containing three kav,' etc.
One who, etc., is exempt, etc. As explained above, where he lacks the means, all agree that this is an unavoidable circumstance, as Rashi writes in Gittin there.
And if he is able, etc.; and if, when the time arrived, etc., according to all opinions, etc. This follows his approach in siman 225, seif 41, where he distinguishes within the Rashba's rulings between a case in which the person was already obligated, etc. He accepts the second opinion there. Accordingly, he rules here: 'one who swore,' etc., 'and if he is able,' etc. In the second ruling he writes, 'according to all opinions,' because it concerns one who already owed the money independently of the oath. If the creditor released him, he would also be exempt from the oath, as stated in siman 228, seif 39, but the creditor cannot merely extend the time. Where the extension was coerced, even an express release is ineffective, like a coerced gift. This accords with the responsum of the Ramban cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 228, folio 211b, responsum 246: one who swore to repay his fellow by a fixed time, and the ruler compelled the creditor to extend the deadline, etc. There the debt already existed. The Beit Yosef also cites two responsa of the Rashba ruling that he is exempt, but those concern a case where no prior debt existed. What he writes there, that even if the creditor released him, means that he extended the deadline, as above, since an extension alone is ineffective. Alternatively, it means an actual release where the king coerced him, as in the Ramban's responsum. This is the meaning of the wording here, 'and if,' etc., 'or,' etc.
A community, etc. As stated in Shabbos 31b and elsewhere regarding one who ate, etc., because the prohibition is not removed without formal release.
One who swore, etc. As stated in Nedarim 3b. See Rosh there, s.v. lo ipater, etc.
And if he did not, etc.; some say, etc.; and some say, etc. See what I wrote in Orach Chaim 108:8, and see Taz here.
And it is not called, etc. Otherwise, Moshe's oath to Yisro should likewise be considered coerced, for Moshe had fled because of Pharaoh's decree, Yisro gave him residence, and gave him his daughter. See below, siman 228, seif 20. One cannot answer that Tzidkiyahu's oath was taken under threat of death, because danger to life is different, as stated in Gittin 55b regarding the forced sale: due to the coercion of death, he resolves and transfers ownership. See the rulings of Mahari there, and below, seif 14, in the gloss, where this is answered differently. The ruling follows his own position, for in seif 15 he rules against Mahari.
When he is, etc. As stated in Shabbos 88a: 'From here there is a great protest concerning acceptance of the Torah.' Although the oath was made with the consent of the Omnipresent, so that it could not be annulled, as stated at the end of chapter 3 of Shevuot, Rabbeinu Yitzchak asks why the Gemara did not answer that Israel later accepted it under the oath of mutual responsibility. He answers that this too was under compulsion, lest Hashem leave them in the wilderness. Although fulfilling Hashem's will carries great reward, it was still compulsion, and certainly coercion by a person is considered compulsion. This is unlike Nedarim 28a, where one says, 'the produce shall be forbidden,' etc., because that case involves only financial compulsion. See Hagahot Maimoniyot at the end of Hilchot Shevuot and Mordechai, chapter 3 of Shevuot.
He is believed, etc. Just as he is believed to explain the intention under which he vowed, through the questions, 'Do you regret it, and is your heart still with you?' So too with all grounds for release and expressions of regret. We likewise say at the end of chapter 2 of Nedarim: unspecified vows are interpreted stringently, but their explanation may be accepted leniently; for example, one vowed 'by cherem' and then explained, 'by the fisherman's net,' etc. See also Nazir 11a: 'I know that a nazirite,' etc.
Or concerning, etc.; but if, etc. The Rashba writes there that this applies specifically where witnesses warned him and he ignored the warning, saying nothing at the time, and then ate. Certainly, if he said, 'nevertheless,' he is not believed afterward. The Rambam likewise writes in Hilchot Shevuot 2:12 that if one swore not to eat, ate after warning, and then claimed that he had intended only not to go out, and had erred, he is not flogged unless he acknowledged before eating that the oath concerned eating, accepted the warning without objecting, or had already been established as a liar. The Kesef Mishneh explains that this is based on Tosefta Taharot, chapter 6: one is believed concerning himself; if asked, 'Did you vow?' and he says, 'I had a condition in mind,' he is believed. But if he first says, 'I did not vow,' and only when confronted says, 'I had a condition in mind,' he is not believed. The same appears in Torat Kohanim, Vayikra, section 7. The Raavad objects from Nedarim 28a, which states that unspoken intentions are legally ineffective. The Kesef Mishneh answers that here he means that he whispered the condition aloud. Hagahot Maimoniyot answers that the Gemara speaks of an unlearned person, whereas here the person is a Torah scholar, as indicated by Nedarim 20a regarding one who vowed 'by cherem,' etc. He cites proof from Masechet Kallah, where Rabbi Akiva swore while nullifying the oath in his heart. The Mordechai at the beginning of Shevuot, however, writes that Rabbi Akiva actually articulated the qualification, and offers another answer, that the case involved a form of compulsion. The Semag gives a different answer; see there.
And likewise one who, etc. This refers to a case where witnesses establish that he did not swear and he gives a plausible explanation, in which case he is believed, as stated in Ketubot 22a.
