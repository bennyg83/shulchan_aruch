By means of a large expenditure. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, challenged this from the Rama in Orach Chayyim, siman 656, who rules that regarding a negative commandment one must surrender all his money rather than transgress. He therefore removed this ruling from practical halacha. There is no difficulty. Here, he did not transgress at all, for we say that he never swore with the intention of spending a large sum. The fact that his son became ill is effective here. Certainly, his son’s illness could not permit him to violate any negative commandment.
To do something on a specified day, etc. At first glance this appears difficult according to what I explained above in siman 228, seif 41, that there is a distinction between “by a specified day” and “on a specified day,” for with the latter wording no continuing prohibition remains afterward. There is no difficulty. The Beit Yosef cites this ruling here in the name of the Rashba, and the original text indeed reads “by the day.” The Rama does not accept this distinction, and therefore was not precise here to write “on the day.”
And there are those who say that it is not considered duress. This is from the Agudah in the chapter “One who was seized.” It derives the ruling from Gittin 74b. Originally, the buyer of a house in a walled city would hide on the final day of the twelve-month period so that the seller could not find him and return his money, thereby making the sale permanent, as it is written, “If it is not redeemed by the completion of a full year,” etc. Hillel the Elder enacted that the seller could cast the money into a designated chamber, break the door of his house, and enter. The Agudah derives that when one vowed to perform an act within thirty days and was prevented on the final day, this is not called duress. Otherwise, Hillel would not have needed his enactment, for the seller was prevented on the last day. I see no proof from there. Certainly, if it were known to beit din that the buyer hid on the final day, the seller would not lose his right. The problem was that he could not prove it. He would have needed to station witnesses throughout the entire day and search the whole city for the buyer, who could later claim that he had been in some other house together with him. This was practically impossible. Hillel therefore enacted that the seller could place the money in the designated chamber on the final day, an easy procedure. Furthermore, one who studies the Gemara there will see that the primary need for Hillel’s enactment was that otherwise forced payment might not be considered payment, or because payment made outside the recipient’s presence is certainly not payment. One might ask that if it was duress, why was an enactment needed? Since the seller was ready to pay and the creditor was absent, he should not lose his right. This is not a difficulty. The seller wished to enter his house immediately. Hillel therefore enacted that depositing the payment in the chamber should be equivalent to giving it directly, allowing him to enter at once without the buyer’s knowledge. Accordingly, the first opinion is correct, namely, the opinion of the Nimukei Yosef in the chapter “How does the foot cause damage,” in the name of the Ramah. It has clear proof from the Gemara and is reasonable. The principal time for payment is the final day, as though that day alone had been fixed. Since he was prevented on that day, he is certainly exempt from the oath and from every penalty he undertook if he failed to pay that day. So it appears to me.
A person is believed, etc. At the end of siman 228, the Beit Yosef cites the Rashba: Even though he expressed the vow without qualification, and even if afterward he explains it in a manner opposite to what the listeners understood, and even if the listeners believed and recognized that he vowed willingly and without any duress, he is nevertheless believed. Proof is from one who vows by “cherem” and afterward says that he meant the fishing net of the sea, as explained above in siman 208, end quote.
