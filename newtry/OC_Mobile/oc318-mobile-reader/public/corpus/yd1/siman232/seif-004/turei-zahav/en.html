Like those who left Egypt. The Gemara and Tur read, “If I did not see on this road as many people as those who left Egypt.” It appears necessary to read it this way, because the Gemara challenges the statement concerning the beam of an olive press by saying that such a serpent existed in the days of King Shapur, as will be cited nearby, but it does not challenge the case of those who left Egypt by saying that one could indeed have seen such a multitude. Orach Chayyim, siman 224, seif 5, states the law of one who sees 600,000 Jews together. We must therefore say that the phrase “on this road” means that it was publicly known that there were not 600,000 people on that road. According to the reading here, however, the Gemara mentioned above would be difficult.
Like the beam of an olive press. The Gemara asks: Could this not be so? There was a serpent in the days of King Shapur upon which they cast thirteen wagonloads of straw and it swallowed them. Shmuel answered that the Mishnah refers to a serpent whose back was flat like the beam of an olive press. There are many explanations of what “flat” means. However, it is difficult why the Tur did not also write that the case concerns a serpent whose back was flat, and why the Rosh likewise omitted that detail in his rulings. It appears that they explain that the person making the vow did not explicitly say that he saw a serpent with a flat back. Rather, Shmuel means that by saying, “a serpent like the beam of an olive press,” he intended the comparison with respect to the flatness of its back. This is proven from the Gemara’s wording, “We refer to the flatness of its back,” rather than, “The case is where he said that the serpent’s back was flat.” Therefore, the Tur rules leniently that saying merely, “a serpent like the beam of an olive press,” suffices, because this expression already includes the relevant comparison. The Mishnah mentioned it incidentally to teach a law concerning buying and selling, as the Gemara concludes. So it appears according to the Tur. As practical halacha, we rule like the Ran, that even if he made the loaf dependent upon something that is not an exaggeration but an absolute falsehood in everyone’s eyes, the loaf is permitted. He did not intend to create a prohibition, and we find no authority who explicitly disputes the Ran. Accordingly, even if he saw no serpent at all, it is permitted. It appears to me that even the Ran agrees that if he saw no great multitude at all, the loaf is forbidden. Regarding a serpent with a flat back, everyone knows that such a thing does not exist according to the Ran’s understanding, so his statement has no standing from the outset and no one seeks to believe him. With a great multitude, however, he wants people to believe that he saw at least a large crowd and merely exaggerated by calling it like those who left Egypt. If in truth he saw no crowd at all, the loaf is forbidden to him. I saw that the Darkei Moshe wondered why it was necessary that he had at least seen a crowd, whereas with the serpent it is permitted even if he saw none. According to what I have written, the distinction is correct.
