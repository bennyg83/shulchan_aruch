When it was poured. Shach writes that even if a large quantity of permitted wine fell at once upon one drop of yayin nesech, with sixty against it, it is nevertheless forbidden. Something stationary is considered more significant than what falls upon it. When the permitted wine is stationary and the forbidden wine falls into it, the permitted wine nullifies the forbidden wine unless enough falls at once to impart taste. In a major loss one may rely upon the authorities who permit even where permitted wine falls into forbidden wine, provided it fell at once and there is sixty. If it fell gradually, each first amount is nullified and does not revive even after additional permitted wine is added, though in other prohibitions it would revive. All this concerns definite non-Jewish wine. Ordinary non-Jewish wine nowadays is nullified in sixty in every arrangement.
Nullified. This implies even without sixty, following Rambam. We do not rule this way; sixty is required against everything that fell, or else the prohibition revives. Ran writes that although a connecting stream is considered a connection, this means only that the wine above receives the status of the mixture below. Once the wine below was nullified and became permitted, it cannot forbid the wine above.
The stream. Ran implies that this applies only while the stream remains unbroken. Once it stops, the wine in the barrel is no longer connected to the wine in the pit, and the amount that fell is nullified if it cannot impart taste. The reason is that a barrel is ordinarily poured into a pit through a vessel of this kind, so all the wine destined to fall is viewed as already mixed. A small pitcher is not ordinarily poured into a pit, and therefore its contents are not viewed as already fallen and mixed. Shach.
