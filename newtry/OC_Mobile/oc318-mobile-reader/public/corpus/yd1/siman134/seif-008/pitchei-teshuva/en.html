The beverages. See responsum Noda BiYehudah tinyana part 4 siman 56, who was asked about makers of arak called "krambambuli perski" and the like, who put in the pot neveilah meat or slaughtered meat without salting and rinsing at the time of cooking in order to remove smoke and the sharpness of the arak — whether a Jew may do so, and what is the law of the arak made with a gentile; and he concluded that a Jew is forbidden lechatchilah to do so through neveilah or unsalted slaughtered meat, even if there are immediately 60 in the pot — we do not nullify a prohibition lechatchilah; and he said their intent is only to pass smoke and it is not called nullifying a prohibition, for I already explained in Noda BiYehudah siman 26 that this is specifically where it is already mixed but not yet nullified (as I wrote above siman 84 s"k 9 from this); therefore the Jew should salt the meat first and rinse it, and one need not concern that he might drink it with milk, for it is not common; but one must concern that it is customary to serve arak like baker's work kneaded with butter — therefore it appears there should be 60 against the meat, and even if at first there is not, only afterwards when he adds to it, it is permitted. And arak of a gentile: if it is known there is 60, even at the end everything made with a gentile is bedi'eved and batel in 60; and if there is no 60, if most craftsmen mix or half-and-half mix it is forbidden; but if the majority do not mix, then it is forbidden to buy from them in their homes, for it is established; but what shopkeepers buy from them and bring to sell is permitted by reason of "kol d'parish m'ruba parish" — see there. And see Beit Yosef who brings in the name of Rashba in a responsum that anything whose essence is so is not batel, etc. — see there; and in Magen Avraham siman 442 s"k 5; and see responsum Noda BiYehudah the aforementioned siman, who brought the view of great Rishonim that they do not have this reasoning; and he wrote one should rely to be lenient since even for Rashba this is only rabbinic — see there.
