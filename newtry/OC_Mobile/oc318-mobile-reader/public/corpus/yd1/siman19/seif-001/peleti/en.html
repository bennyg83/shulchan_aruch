And he did not bless—the Bach's view: if he did not bless deliberately, the animal is forbidden to the slaughterer; and the acharonim disagree. And there is somewhat of a proof not like the Bach—for it teaches "one who slaughters on Shabbat—it is kosher," see there; it is similar to our case where it teaches—and if so, in what the Mechaber wrote it is permitted according to all; but the slaughterer certainly did not bless, for he is included among those who cut and bless, etc.; and in the Gemara the simple understanding is that it deals with deliberate action; and see in the chapter "Three who ate" in the Rosh and Tosafot; nevertheless it appears permitted in what the Mechaber wrote, even for the slaughterer—and examine this.
And if a defect developed in it—even though we hold regarding a trampled bird in which a defect developed that we slaughter on Yom Tov, why should we be concerned regarding a berachah? For shechita on Yom Tov is not more severe; perhaps he holds like those who say one must examine them—as we learned; but in truth it is forbidden; but according to what we hold in Orach Chaim that it is permitted—why be concerned regarding a berachah? And it must be: since he can bless afterward when it is found [kosher], we do not rely on the presumption; not so on Yom Tov where there is no other way.
He blesses—the Shach brought here Sefer Berachat Avraham, who decides like the Hagahah of the Rosh on berachot on mitzvot: if he did not bless before doing it he blesses afterward—not so Birkat HaNehenin; and he disputes the Rambam, who holds regarding shechita and similar that if he did not bless beforehand he does not bless afterward; and the Shach decided like the Rambam, with proof from that which we say in Berachot: Rabbi [X] forgot and ate and did not bless—he blesses afterward, as we learned: one who immersed and ascended blesses "asher kidshanu..."; and the Gemara rejects: there it is different because the person was not fit, etc.; and the proof is clear—why did it not say we do not compare Birkat HaNehenin to Birkat HaMitzvot? Rather in truth this is not the main point, only it depends on whether the person was fit. And the Peri Chadash wrote there is no proof at all—for if it would answer by distinguishing mitzvah and benefit, it would still be difficult why it cited specifically immersion and not all mitzvot that share one law; and so it appears: in other mitzvot, even though after doing one is obligated in a berachah, nevertheless l'chatchila the berachah is before doing—not so immersion, which requires the reason that the person was not fit, end of his words; and these words have no substance—for if Ravina did not know that in every immersion one does not bless before immersion, daily practice would prove otherwise, that they would immerse and bless afterward, women and men; and the language of the baraita is precise: it teaches "one who immersed and ascended blesses," and does not teach "one who immersed and did not bless should bless when he ascends, Blessed are You..."; and the main thing is missing—therefore the explanation "one who immersed and ascended" is because he could not bless beforehand; if so, Ravina, who does not hold a distinction between benefit and mitzvot, should be difficult to himself: why on benefit and mitzvot do we bless beforehand, but on immersion afterward? Rather certainly he knew the person was not fit, only he holds that if berachot are not by nature [said] after doing, what of it that he could not bless initially—he is discharged without a berachah; rather berachot are by nature also at the end, and the Gemara rejects: they said and they said—in every place berachah beforehand, and where impossible beforehand, afterward; and if so the Shach's proof stands; and also what does the Gemara need to answer a matter that is not true—for even a matter that is beforehand, nevertheless since it is a mitzvah he blesses after doing, and it should have answered: truly we distinguish mitzvah from benefit; and this "before immersion"—Ravina already knew. If so, also Ba'al Berachat Avraham's rejection that the Gemara should have answered is not so—for how can the Gemara give a stumbling block in its words that imply where "person not fit" does not apply one does not bless at the end, especially according to what Ravina knew this view of "person not fit" only he holds it is not worth changing the nature of berachot; if so why is he disputed with the answerers on this view, that amoraim would dispute a view and not explain that in all mitzvot the sages' nature was so? Or it is not so; and the Shach's words are clear and refined; and how much did the Peri Chadash toil at length to reject the Rambam's words and strengthen the Hagahah of the Rosh. And I omitted the words of Tosafot Pesachim daf 115, s.v. matkip, etc., who wrote: he holds a mitzvah requires kavanah—for if not, the above in the first dipping, if he blesses in the second dipping it is a berachah levatallah; and similarly they wrote afterward: and if kavanah is not required, it is obvious he should not bless in the second dipping, for even if he did not bless in the first dipping he exited, and if he blesses in the second dipping it is a berachah levatallah—and this is a berachah on a mitzvah as is known; if so what of it that he already fulfilled the mitzvah—nevertheless since he did not bless initially he may now bless; and what would be levatallah? The matter is clear that he who blesses after the act—it is a berachah levatallah; and truth guides his way; and so it appears from the Ran explicitly that if he exited in the first dipping he should not bless on eating maror—and this is clear like the Rambam's view; and such is the essential ruling: the Peri Chadash wrote that even for the Rambam, within the time of speech after the act he can bless—a matter obvious to him, for us it is very doubtful; for what will within twenty-four hours avail, since the act was already done and one may not bless afterward; and regarding a convert who immersed—the law should have been to bless before immersion, only it was impossible; therefore it was arranged that he bless immediately within twenty-four hours upon ascending. And this for the Rambam's view there is no distinction in this matter between mitzvah and benefit, as proven above from the Gemara; and also that which we hold one swallows liquids without a berachah—he swallows them and does not make a berachah, for he cannot spit out because it is repulsive, and afterward it is after the act—and it is difficult he should bless within twenty-four hours for swallowing; therefore this has no dawn and no essence.
At a distance of four amot—I did not understand the Rambam, who rules in Orach Chaim siman 83 that even a bathroom, if surrounded by partitions, one may read outside the bathroom and need not four amot; and even those who disagree there—see Magen Avraham there—that is a bathroom whose essence and designation is for defecation, and therefore the partitions are also like excrement; but a kitchen—is its essence for excrement? And what of it that filth is placed there, namely blood and discharge of the animal; but its essence is not for that until the partitions are nullified; therefore the partitions for kitchens—and it is obvious he can bless outside; and so in a bathhouse, where it is forbidden to bless inside but permitted outside—Agudah as stated in chapter 3 of Shabbat; and the Magen Avraham brought it in Orach Chaim siman 64: one who wishes to drink in a bathhouse blesses in the outer house—and did not mention four amot at all; therefore it is clear the Agudah's intent is not from the room where they slaughter to distance from the room four amot, but it deals with the room itself where they slaughter animals, where blood and discharge from the animals are—a foul place; this is called a kitchen, for here there is no house prepared for slaughter, but a place of filth—and he distances four amot from it. And see the Agudah's language in the first chapter of Chullin: "One who slaughters who must bless on shechita in a foul place, like a kitchen—he blesses outside the kitchen at a distance of four amot in a clean place," end of his words—behold it wrote outside the kitchen and did not write outside the kitchen-house; and also "in a clean place"; and if it is outside the kitchen-house it is obvious it is clean like all places; rather it is precise: outside the kitchen—simply the place where animals are slaughtered and filth is found—only distance four amot from there; therefore it wrote it should be clean so filth does not spread there; and "kitchen-house" at first that it taught is only as a general example, for there they slaughter; but the essence of the distance is the place where they slaughter—and this is clear.
Four amot beforehand, etc.—if in another room: from the Rambam's words it appears another room is not an interruption; and the Magen Avraham in Orach Chaim siman 166 brought the Agudah's words in Shabbat: one who wishes to drink in a bathhouse, where he cannot bless, blesses in the outer room in order to drink in the bathhouse—for it is like from corner to corner, end of his words—and the same applies here. And the Magen Avraham wondered there: it deals with Kiddush in the place of a meal, as it is written "and you shall call Shabbat a delight"—one may say if his intention is to eat there it is Kiddush in the place of a meal; but regarding interruption—what does it avail whether his intention is for that or not? In any case it is an interruption; and he was aided by the words of Tosafot explicitly who wrote so. And it appears to say that certainly Tosafot and the Rambam gave the measure of interruption as twenty-two amot walking; and proof from that which we say in Zevachim regarding that which we learned: an asham metzora does not have immediate semichah to shechita—and the Gemara challenged: according to the one who says partial entry is not called entry, let him put his hand in and perform semichah; and it answers: distance it to the north; and they stood to calculate—and he walks twenty-two amot: ten amot the priests' feet and ten amot Israel's feet; and they all challenged—the Magen Avraham and Chavat Yair—but rather what of "partial entry is called entry"? He cannot put his hand in adjacent at the side of the opening and bring in the asham and slaughter it immediately—for according to all the courtyard is called north; and they left it requiring further study. And the Agudah certainly holds that in two rooms in any case it is called interruption; only in one room it is not interruption except twenty-two amot. If so the Gemara's words are correct: if partial entry is called entry he cannot put his hand in at all, only perform semichah outside and bring in the animal—since it is in two rooms it is interruption; but if partial entry is not called entry and he can put his hand in, then everything is in another room—he holds it is not interruption; and the Gemara answers: distance it to the north, etc.—and it is twenty-two amot and interruption. And these are the Agudah's words—it deals where there are not twenty-two amot, only from room to room; therefore it is compared to Kiddush in the place of a meal: if his intention is to eat there it is like that place; also here—all interruption is because of room to room: if his intention was on that room it is as if he is in that room and there is no room by itself and no interruption; and if so for this that room interrupts it is like Kiddush in the place of a meal—if his intention is on that room it is like one room; and so regarding bathhouse and kitchen—for since his intention was on that room to drink and slaughter, it is not like another room but like that room, and there is no interruption since there are not twenty-two amot. And if it is still difficult regarding semichah—to perform semichah outside and slaughter inside, and his intention was on inside—then still there is no room to room and no interruption; one may say that since he cannot enter there, what of it that his intention is there—since he cannot enter and partitions separate, it is obvious it is from room to room. And here it is difficult to me from the Yerushalmi in tractate Shekalim chapter 3, as taught: an interpreter was with him so they would not say in his mouth "buyer of shekalim"; and it challenged: "and he shall fill his mouth with water"—Rabbi Tarfon: because of berachah; and they explained he must bless on raising the shekalim; and it is difficult to bless before entering the chamber of shekalim with intention to contribute there, and afterward fill his mouth with water and enter; and if so it appears proven that from room to room is interruption. And it is possible to say, as stated above, that in any case there must not be twenty-two amot of interruption; and the chamber where the shekalim were for contribution was very long, and it does not suffice that between the place of berachah and the place of contribution there be less than twenty-two amot—and it is interruption. In any case we learn from this that all the Rambam's words mean there is no distance of twenty-two amot, but if there is a distance of twenty-two amot it is interruption.
