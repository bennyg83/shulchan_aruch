Or a non-Jew who speaks casually (mesilah le-fi tumo). See the responsum Chavot Ya’ir siman 37, which writes that the same law applies when one who speaks casually (mesilah le-fi tumo) heard it from another who was speaking casually — but if it is not truly casual speech it does not help. Unlike a certain scholar who sought to be lenient even in this case, relying on it combined with the reason of her nursing. He relied on what Maharai wrote in Terumat HaDeshen siman 271 — that even if there is doubt whether the non-Jew speaks casually to enhance the value of his merchandise or not, since it can be understood in two ways, it nonetheless helps combined with the reason of nursing. This is incorrect — it is likewise forbidden to rely on that, God forbid, and we may only combine with nursing a case of true casual speech where it is certain he is not seeking to enhance the sale, as is implied by the Rema’s plain ruling. As for whether one must here also initiate the conversation as in the testimony of a woman in Even HaEzer siman 17 — it appears that in other prohibitions, even those of Torah law, this is not required; only in the testimony of a woman is it needed. A proof is from the case of a non-Jew tasting [food], see there. [See the responsum Chatam Sofer siman 64 and the end of siman 308, where it is made clear that he disagrees — that even in other prohibitions it is not called casual speech except in the manner that is effective for the testimony of a woman.
