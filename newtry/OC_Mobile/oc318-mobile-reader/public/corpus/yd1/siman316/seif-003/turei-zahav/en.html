Or a non-Jew who speaks casually not to enhance [the sale]. In order to clarify a new ruling on this matter, let us set forth the words with heavenly assistance regarding a case that occurred: one had a debt with a non-Jew who said he had no money to pay except one cow, and he led him to the yard and showed him one cow with a calf standing beside her but not nursing it. The non-Jew said: "I will give you this cow without the calf for eight gold coins, as I will raise the calf for myself." The Israelite said: "I think this cow is old and this is her sixth or seventh calf; I want a cow that has calved more often — the more she has calved, the more I will add to her price." The non-Jew replied: "What can I tell you — that she has calved more than this? That is not so. But she is not as old as you say. Believe me, this is her fourth calf, no more." He accepted it from him, and afterward she calved at the Israelite's — what is the law? At first glance it appears to be a doubtful firstborn, since we say in seif 1 that a non-Jew speaking casually is not believed. But I found grounds for leniency with heavenly assistance. We say in chapter 3 of Bekhorot, daf 21: we found [the rule] regarding one who purchases from an Israelite — what is the law regarding one who purchases from a non-Jew? As the Mishnah states there [regarding purchasing] from an Israelite. What did Rav say? It is a certain firstborn — for if it had already calved, he would have boasted about it. Rashi explains: the seller would have said "buy it, for it has already calved." The reason he did not boast is that he thought the buyer wanted it for slaughter, and it is apparent there is a scribal error — for this reasoning belongs to Shmuel not to Rav. Shmuel said: it is a doubtful firstborn — he thinks the buyer wants it for slaughter. Rabbi Yochanan said: it is certainly unconsecrated — for if it had not calved, since there is a prohibition, he would have informed him. Although we do not rule like Rav, as will be explained at the end of this siman, nonetheless we can say that Shmuel and Rabbi Yochanan only disagree with Rav in a case where there is another consideration against the reasoning of "he would have boasted" — namely, "since there is a prohibition he would have informed him." Rav holds this reasoning of "he would have informed him" is not compelling and so we follow the reasoning of "he would have boasted." Shmuel holds these considerations are equal: "he would have boasted" argues toward prohibition, and there is the opposing consideration of "he would have informed him" — which is proof toward leniency — or there is reason to say he thought the buyer wanted it for slaughter and would not boast. Therefore Shmuel makes it a doubt. Rabbi Yochanan holds the reasoning of "he would have informed him" is stronger, and so follows it. But in a case where there is no opposing reasoning to "he would have boasted" — why would we not follow it? Everyone agrees with Rav in that case. And in the case before us: if this cow had calved for the non-Jew and the calf beside her is not hers, he should have boasted about the sale and said she had calved, since there is no opposing consideration here. And what we cited above in the name of Tosafot that a non-Jew speaking casually is not believed — that is from Tosafot and the Rosh there in Rav's words, who holds "he would have boasted." Their language is: "Rashi's explanation: that she had already calved, and would not be in danger of birth again. But a non-Jew even if he said so is not believed — and one can learn from his explanation that a non-Jew even speaking casually is not believed, for he says this to enhance his merchandise." It appears their explanation of "but a non-Jew, etc." is: since a non-Jew, when he does boast, is not believed by us, he therefore does not say it at all — knowing in himself that he would not be believed. This is unlike an Israelite. According to this, wherever it is not for the purpose of enhancing his merchandise — he is believed when speaking casually. That is the reason: since he says the boast he would not be believed, he therefore says it at all; but where it is demonstrable that he is not intending to enhance it — he is believed. Even though the Yoreh De'ah rules simply that we do not rely on a non-Jew's casual speech, and furthermore we say in chapter Hagozeil Batra that a non-Jew speaking casually is trusted only for the testimony of a woman alone — still it requires examination. End of quote. It appears to me that the case before us is superior to a mere case of casual speech and to the ruling of Rav cited above — on the grounds that the non-Jew was certainly telling the truth that this cow had not calved there for him: for if she had calved, he would not have harmed himself, since he saw the Israelite was willing to pay more for a cow that had calved more — he certainly would have told the truth in order to profit. Even according to what we said above in the name of Tosafot that regarding a non-Jew one cannot say "he would have boasted" — that is because there is reason to say he thought he would not be believed, and therefore kept silent. But here, where he is speaking in praise of the animal more than the truth warrants, he should have told the truth and boasted even more. In this case everyone certainly agrees with Rav that "he would have boasted" — for it is a powerful consideration that he tells the truth, and this is strong proof that the truth is with him. We are not coming to permit on the basis of the non-Jew's testimony but on the basis of proof, since he harms himself. I have a proof for this from chapter Hanizkein (Gittin 57): a certain person came before Rabbi Ami and said: "A Torah scroll I wrote for so-and-so — I did not write its divine names for their own sake." He asked: "In whose possession is the Torah scroll?" He answered: "In the purchaser's possession." He said: "You are believed to forfeit your fee, but you are not believed to disqualify the Torah scroll." Another person came before Rabbi Avahu and said: "A Torah scroll I wrote for so-and-so — its parchments I did not prepare for their own sake." He asked: "In whose possession is the Torah scroll?" He answered: "In the purchaser's possession." He said: "Since you are believed to forfeit your fee, you are believed to disqualify the Torah scroll." What is the difference from Rabbi Ami's ruling that he is not believed to disqualify? There, one could say he erred and thought he would only forfeit the fee for the divine names, whereas here, since he forfeits his entire fee and comes to say so, we say he must be telling the truth. Thus before you: from the man's testimony alone there is no credibility to disqualify the scroll — as in the first case before Rabbi Ami — and yet he is believed in the second case on the basis of proof, since he harms himself. So too in our case: certainly we say he would have boasted, and we rely on this that she had already calved by the non-Jew, and this calf she bore for the Israelite is not a firstborn. So it appears to me.
Even so, it is forbidden. The reason is in Piskei Maharai siman 130, and this is his language: as stated in Shaarei Dura regarding a wolf that came and attacked one animal, where Ri wrote that one should not permit by virtue of a sefek sefeka, perhaps it attacked with its tooth, and if you say it attacked with its claw, perhaps it is not this one; since the first doubt is Torah-level, it is not nullified even in a majority, all the more so one against one; end quote. I have room to examine this from what the Beit Yosef wrote above in siman 57: I found written, does Ri disagree with the Talmud, which says sefek sefeka in a Torah matter? He answered there that it speaks where the first doubt became known first. Although I challenged this in siman 110 seif 9 and answered differently, based on the Semag and Semak, that the essential permission of sefek sefeka depends on whether it was possible for the matter to become known in between, in which case it is forbidden, or whether the two doubts are equal, such as in two mixtures; see there at length. All this does not apply here, regarding two kids that were born, one male and one female, where there is a sefek sefeka: perhaps she had already given birth before these were born, and if you say she had not given birth, perhaps the male was born last. Why should we not say sefek sefeka regarding this male and say there is no bekhor here at all? So it is explained above in Shulchan Aruch siman 110 seif 9 in the text, that only where the first doubt is in its body, etc., do we not say sefek sefeka, and in the gloss there he wrote that the matter depends on whether it became known. None of this applies here. Therefore it requires study why we should not follow sefek sefeka here. Certainly, if she now gave birth to two males, and there is a doubt regarding each of them which one was born first, then there is certainly one bekhor among them and it is proper to be stringent, since they belong to one person. This is like the two paths in the first chapter of Pesachim, where if they come to ask at the same time we are stringent; here it is impossible to ask except at one time, because the entire permission is by virtue of sefek sefeka. But with one male and one female, we should have been lenient by virtue of sefek sefeka. Perhaps for this reason the Rama used this formulation in the case of sefek sefeka: “such as where she gave birth to two, and one can say regarding each one that it is not the bekhor,” changing from the formulation used in Piskei Maharai there, namely that she gave birth to a male and female. This is for the reason I wrote; so it appears to me, and it requires study in practice.
