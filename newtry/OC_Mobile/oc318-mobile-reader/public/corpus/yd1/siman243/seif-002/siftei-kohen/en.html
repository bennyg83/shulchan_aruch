And afterward. The community reached a compromise and reconsidered, deciding to hire others, etc. So it appears in Maharam's responsum there.
But a matter, etc. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 163, seifim 4 and 5.
Therefore they are exempt. Ramban writes in his novellae to the first chapter of Bava Basra, folio 8, that when the Gemara says that the unlearned people said, 'Let us flee from here,' and Rebbi allowed them to flee, this applies where the rabbi cannot compel them. But if he can compel them, the law is that he should do so, because once the king imposed the crown tax, everyone became obligated to contribute, and the obligation rests upon each person and his property. The Nimukei Yosef there rules likewise.
From all types of taxes. The same applies to all types of customs duties. Maharam Alashkar cites this in responsum 19 from a responsum of Rabbi Nechemiah addressed to Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Yaakov, author of the Turim, the sons of the Rosh. See there at length.
If the community placed him under a ban, etc. Even if they compelled him and he accepted the ban, it is like vows made to murderers or tax collectors, etc. So it appears in Rabbi Nechemiah's responsum there.
Business dealings. Even if he lends with interest to a non-Jew. So write Maharam Alashkar and Rabbi Nechemiah in the responsum cited there.
For his livelihood. This does not mean only the bare minimum needed to survive. Rather, whenever he engages in work in order to sustain himself, this is called earning his livelihood. Note carefully that the text says, 'and not in order to become wealthy,' rather than, 'and not beyond what he needs for his livelihood.' It is obvious that a person may suddenly face many expenses because of illness or other circumstances, and these have no fixed limit. Tosafot write similarly in Bava Metzia regarding interest charged to a non-Jew. Although according to the first version it was permitted to an unlearned person only for his livelihood, nevertheless it is permitted today because we bear the taxes of kings and officials, and all of this is considered necessary for one's livelihood. In summary, a Torah scholar whose Torah study is primary, and who interrupts his learning only to support, feed, clothe, and provide all necessary needs for his children and household, is exempt from every type of tax and levy, even if he is wealthy. So writes Maharam Alashkar there. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 342, writes: If someone argues that nowadays everything a person continually does to earn money is considered for his livelihood, as Rabbeinu Tam explains in Eizehu Neshech, and therefore interest from a non-Jew is permitted today even beyond one's basic support, answer him according to his own argument. Why did Rabbeinu Tam permit it? Because the taxes of kings and officials have no fixed amount. But this Torah scholar seeks exemption from all taxes and public levies, so he needs to earn only enough for his support. This appears at first to contradict Maharam Alashkar. However, Terumas HaDeshen wrote this only to explain the custom of places that exempt only Torah scholars who head a yeshivah, because other scholars are not sufficiently careful that Torah remain their occupation. It also appears there that those scholars conduct business in order to become wealthy and do not consistently return to their studies when free from their work. See there, and see Maharam Galanti, responsum 117.
Only that he be established as a Torah scholar, etc. So writes Binyamin Ze'ev, siman 252. He further writes that even if he is not particularly important compared with Torah scholars elsewhere, nevertheless, since among the people of his city he is considered a Torah scholar, he deserves exemption from every tax. See there at length.
