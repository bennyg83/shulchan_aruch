Permitted in eating. For it is written "of the behemah you may eat," and we expound: all that is found in behemah you may eat—for its mother's shechitah permits it.
And does not require shechitah. This language is seemingly superfluous; and it appears it teaches us there is no prohibition of eiver min hachai, and it is permitted to eat it even if one cuts from it while it is still alive; and similarly we find in Rashi's explanation at the end of chapter R' Akiva, who wrote there is no prohibition of eiver min hachai since it does not require shechitah—only there is bal teshaktzu, etc.—end of his words. And here there is no bal teshaktzu, for one takes it while still alive and cooks it; for if there were a prohibition of eiver min hachai it would be forbidden once one cut it while alive. And in the Tur it is written here: and its blood is forbidden and its milk is permitted; and the Rosh wrote the reason: its blood is forbidden because it is absorbed throughout the body and is considered like the blood of the limbs of the behemah itself—end of his words. And one must explain his view: why should the milk too not be considered like the milk of the behemah itself? And it appears his view is that the blood in the fetus mixes with the blood of the behemah itself, since the fetus was formed and nourished from it—therefore it is not better than the blood of the behemah itself.
Requires shechitah. Because of marit ayin, lest one confuse it with a complete behemah, since this one too spread on the ground. And one should consider what its law is b'dieved if it was not slaughtered: if we are so stringent because of the decree lest one confuse it—for from the Tur's language, who wrote "and all matters that disqualify in shechitah disqualify in it," it implies even b'dieved; or perhaps l'chatchila they said it disqualifies, as Tosafot wrote at the beginning of HaShochet in that of R' Yehudah who disqualifies until he slaughters the hind legs—and so it appears from that the Gemara permits R' Shimon ben Shezuri even l'chatchila without shechitah; and one need not extend their dispute so much. And there is further proof at the beginning of Gittin: we also find concern for confusion regarding one who brings a get from a country overseas—that it must be "written in my presence and signed in my presence," whereas by law "signed in my presence" alone should suffice; only because of confusion with other validation of documents. And we find there at the beginning of chapter 2 of Gittin that the tanna taught in the extra coming—that it teaches us that even b'dieved it disqualifies if he did not say both, meaning "written in my presence and signed in my presence." From which we learn: without the extra coming we would not disqualify b'dieved, for he did not say both—even though there is concern for confusion. If so, here where we do not find explicit disqualification b'dieved, we have no basis to be stringent to forbid b'dieved. And further: behold Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Maggid and the great teachers that a flawed knife does not forbid at all, as above.
Other treifot. For they are not publicized so much; but regarding shechitah, since they required shechitah for it, we require proper shechitah—so too the Rosh.
Its hooves are cloven. For it is a strange matter and people mention that this wonder is ben pakuah and one will not confuse it. And some are uncertain: he holds like the other language in the Gemara that specifically two strange matters are required—that is, that it was pakuah and it is cloven, that it was slaughtered and an offspring was found in it that is also cloven; and b'dieved it is plain it does not forbid.
