(Shach s.k. 3) For wherever it does not require shechitah there is no issue of oto v'et beno—therefore. And exempt from gifts—for it is written "from your slaughterers of sacrifice"—Tosefta.
In Pri Megadim he was uncertain whether it is permitted to extend a limb from a live ben pakuah to a gentile—for perhaps since it runs and goes, its blood ascends and one redeems with it—it is forbidden to a gentile. And see in Pesachim, where he is precise from R' Natan that it is forbidden to extend a limb from a live animal to a gentile because of lifnei iver—for otherwise it would be permitted for benefit; and if it were so that a limb from ben pakuah is forbidden to a gentile even though permitted to an Israelite, nothing would be proved. And so he wrote in his book Rosh Yosef. However, one can say: there he was precise that it is permitted for benefit from that which he said specifically to a gentile it is forbidden, but to dogs it is permitted—even to dogs of a gentile; and if it were forbidden for benefit, here too there would be lifnei iver, for he benefits the gentile and he too is forbidden to benefit—at least according to R' Meir, who reads "you shall not eat" as benefit. And on this basis the wonder of Tosafot there is resolved; and so it is not found in one that convulses.
(Shulchan Aruch) And if it came upon a daughter pakuah like him—behold his son, etc., like him. And it is proved in the Talmud, Rashi, and Tosafot at the beginning of chapter HaMakishah (daf 69a) that even a nine-month-old live ben pakuah that has a prohibition emerging in one limb—as written in the siman afterward: that it came upon a daughter pakuah like him that also has a prohibition emerging in one limb—for that limb has no remedy through shechitah, as explained in siman 14. Nevertheless, that offspring which it engendered has no prohibition in this limb—for its seed confounds—see there.
