He took a weaving shuttle from it. This means a pointed piece of wood used for weaving. It appears, in my humble opinion, that the author of the Shulchan Aruch did not fully discharge the obligation of careful analysis in these laws, as we shall explain, with Hashem's help. In the chapter Kol Shaah, Pesachim 27a, the Sages taught: An oven heated with peels of orlah, if it is new, must be demolished; if it is old, it must be cooled. If bread was baked in it, Rabbi says the bread is forbidden, while the Sages say it is permitted. If it was cooked over coals, all agree that it is permitted. The Gemara explains the first clause, that a new oven must be demolished and that cooling it and reheating it with permitted wood does not suffice, as a case of zeh v'zeh gorem, one permitted and one forbidden cause jointly producing the result. That Tanna holds that zeh v'zeh gorem is forbidden. The poskim, however, ruled that we do not follow that view, but rather Rabbi Yose in the chapter Kol HaTzelamim concerning planting beneath an asheirah, mentioned below in seif 11. Therefore, a new oven is cooled rather than demolished. Nevertheless, regarding bread baked in it, they ruled like Rabbi, because the improvement produced by the forbidden wood is present in the bread, without assistance from a permitted cause. Shmuel and Rabbi Yochanan disagree there in the Gemara. One says that the improvement of the wood in the bread applies when the coals are glowing, meaning burning. The other says that even glowing coals are permitted, and the prohibition applies specifically while the torch is opposite the bread, meaning that the forbidden wood is burning at the mouth of the oven. It appears to me that the authority who permits glowing coals holds that although a coal of idol worship is forbidden because of “Let nothing of the banned property cling to your hand,” and therefore even dim coals are forbidden there, orlah is different. Even according to the authority who forbids glowing coals of orlah, one must distinguish between orlah and idol worship regarding dim coals. This appears obvious. The Rosh ruled stringently there that glowing coals are forbidden because the improvement of the wood is present in the bread. So too the Tur wrote in his abridgment of the Rosh's rulings there. The Rambam, however, ruled leniently. He wrote in chapter 16 of Hilchos Maachalos Asuros: An oven heated with peels of orlah, whether new or old, is cooled and afterwards heated with permitted wood. If one cooked in it before cooling it, whether bread or another cooked food, it is forbidden for benefit because the improvement of the forbidden wood is present in the bread. If he removed all the fire and afterwards cooked using the heat of the oven, it is permitted, since the forbidden wood is gone. If he cooked bread over coals, it is permitted even though they are still burning. End quote. The Tur wrote in this siman that even according to the view that zeh v'zeh gorem is permitted, if one baked bread while the torch was opposite it, the bread is forbidden because the improvement of the wood is present in the bread. This implies that he rules leniently that glowing coals are permitted. It is surprising that he did not at least cite the opinion of his father, the Rosh, who forbids the bread even when the torch is not opposite it, so long as the coals are glowing. It appears that the Tur did not consider it necessary to cite his father's stringent view concerning glowing coals, because in his conclusion he cites the opinion of the Rosh and the Ramah that even according to the authority who permits zeh v'zeh gorem, here the bread and a garment woven with a shuttle of an idol are forbidden even when only the heat of the forbidden wood remains and there is no coal at all. Therefore, the Tur was not precise in the earlier passage to mention glowing coals, and instead stated the clearer case of a torch opposite the bread, which all agree is forbidden even regarding orlah. One should not infer from this that glowing coals are permitted. All this concerns the law of the improvement of the wood being present in the bread, meaning that only the forbidden cause is present, such as when bread is baked near the torch, over glowing coals, or in a new oven completed with forbidden wood which was not cooled before bread was baked in it. Regarding zeh v'zeh gorem alone, such as when food was cooked in a new oven that had been cooled and then heated a second time, the Tur writes that we rule according to the view that zeh v'zeh gorem is permitted. The Ramah writes that even according to the authority who permits zeh v'zeh gorem, in the case of an idol, bread baked with forbidden wood and a garment woven with a shuttle of an idol are forbidden. So too it appears from the words of my father, the Rosh, who cited the Mishnah as practical halacha. End quote. The forbidden wood discussed by the Ramah is understood as an old oven that was not cooled, where the issue is only zeh v'zeh gorem. The Beit Yosef questions this. Regarding a garden fertilized with manure of idol worship, the Gemara states that zeh v'zeh gorem is permitted. If so, the Mishnah that forbids bread baked with forbidden wood would not follow the halacha. He therefore ruled here in seif 4, based on the Rambam, that the bread is forbidden only when the fire remains inside the oven, because then the improvement of the wood is present in the bread. If the fire was removed, it is permitted, because then there is only zeh v'zeh gorem, which is permitted even regarding idol worship. With this intent, the Rama added, “while the torch is opposite it.” However, the Beit Yosef overlooked the case of a garment woven with a forbidden shuttle. There, one cannot say that the improvement of the shuttle is present in the garment. Although the Gemara explains the necessity of mentioning the shuttle because the forbidden object remains intact, this is not called the forbidden improvement being present in the permitted article. They are two distinct concepts, as the Ran writes in the chapter Kol HaTzelamim: If one baked bread in an old oven with forbidden wood, the bread is forbidden even though zeh v'zeh gorem is permitted, because the chapter Kol Shaah states that the improvement of the wood is present in the bread. It is therefore more stringent than zeh v'zeh gorem, since the prohibited cause is recognizable in the permitted result. This applies while the torch is opposite it, when the forbidden material has not been consumed. Over coals it is permitted, because the forbidden material has been consumed. End quote. The Ran's words show that the concept of the improvement of the wood can apply even where the forbidden object is consumed, and it is not dependent simply on the forbidden object remaining intact. Furthermore, the Gemara should have said regarding the shuttle that the improvement of the shuttle is present in the garment, just as it says regarding bread. Rather, this concept does not apply to the shuttle. The issue there is solely zeh v'zeh gorem, since the shuttle enables the thread to be woven, and the garment is completed through the combined agency of the thread and the shuttle. According to the Beit Yosef, the Mishnah's prohibition concerning the shuttle would likewise not follow the halacha, for the Tanna placed the law of the garment and the law of the bread together, and the Gemara found it necessary to explain why both cases were needed. Were we to rule that the bread is permitted but the garment forbidden, how could the Gemara explain the necessity of both cases together, when there would be such a major distinction between them? So too it is proven from the Tur in the name of the Ramah, who writes that specifically in the case of idol worship the garment is forbidden. This shows that the prohibition is not because the improvement of the forbidden object is present, for then the same would apply to all other prohibitions, just as the Tur wrote earlier concerning a torch opposite the bread, which is forbidden even regarding orlah. Necessarily, the reason is zeh v'zeh gorem, as we explained. Furthermore, the Rosh explicitly writes in the first chapter of Chullin, cited by the Beit Yosef in this siman, concerning a dangerously ill animal slaughtered with a knife of idol worship, that it is unlike a new oven, bread baked in a forbidden oven, or a garment woven with a forbidden shuttle, because in those cases the entire object was made through the prohibition. The Rosh thus expressly rules that the bread and garment are forbidden despite zeh v'zeh gorem, even without a torch opposite the bread. He compares them to a new oven, concerning which the Mishnah states that it must be demolished and that heating it a second time with permitted wood does not suffice, although the issue is only zeh v'zeh gorem. The same applies to bread baked with forbidden wood in an old oven without a torch opposite it, and certainly to the garment. This is surely the Tur's intent when he writes that so too it appears from the words of his father, who cited the Mishnah. The Beit Yosef asks that the Rif also cited it. In my opinion, the Tur is alluding to the Rosh's discussion in the first chapter of Chullin, where he raises a difficulty from this Mishnah, demonstrating that he considers it halachically authoritative. Although in the chapter Kol Shaah the Rosh writes that according to the view permitting zeh v'zeh gorem a new oven is cooled, that refers to orlah, not idol worship, as will be explained shortly. The Perishah writes that the Tur's statement that according to the authority who permits zeh v'zeh gorem a new oven is cooled does not conflict with the Rosh's prohibition of a garment woven with a forbidden shuttle. An oven is different, because it was already an oven initially and was merely completed through forbidden wood, similar to a knife used to slaughter a dangerously ill animal, which merely enables the act. End quote. I am astonished that he wrote this without precision, for the Rosh explicitly writes in the first chapter of Chullin that even a new oven is forbidden, like the bread and the shuttle. It therefore appears to me that according to the Tur's conclusion in the name of the Rosh, following the Ramah, a new oven must likewise be demolished, as the Beit Yosef writes concerning the Tur's statement, “a new one must be demolished.” It is therefore clear that this Mishnah is indeed the halacha. The Beit Yosef's question from a garden fertilized with manure of idol worship presents no difficulty, for there is a major distinction. Whenever the forbidden cause is indispensable, so that the result could not have come about without it, the result is forbidden in the case of idol worship even though a permitted cause also participated. Thus, a new oven completed with forbidden wood is forbidden, as is bread baked with forbidden wood in an old oven, because bread cannot be baked without wood, and a garment cannot be made without a shuttle. In contrast, manure of idol worship merely improves a field, and the field could still produce without it, although not as well. Therefore zeh v'zeh gorem is permitted there. The same applies to planting beneath an asheirah because of its shade, mentioned in seif 11. The Rosh expressly distinguishes in the first chapter of Chullin between the oven, bread, and shuttle on one side, and planting beneath the tree on the other, precisely as I have explained. A similar distinction appears regarding assistance in siman 125, seif 3, whether the assisting force has substance or not. There, too, the Jewish participant must be the stronger one, and the Tur cites the Rashba distinguishing whether the non-Jew could perform the act alone. Although the law is lenient there because the Sages were lenient regarding yayin nesech, we nevertheless see that this distinction concerning assistance is valid. Likewise here, when the forbidden assistance is indispensable and substantial, zeh v'zeh gorem is forbidden. This is the intent of the Rosh's statement that the bread and garment are completed through the prohibition, meaning that they could not exist without it. Although regarding orlah the conclusion in the chapter Kol Shaah is that even bread is permitted because of zeh v'zeh gorem, the Ramah writes that idol worship is different. There is clear proof from Pesachim 27a, where the Gemara says, “Perhaps you heard Rabbi Eliezer say that the bread is forbidden specifically regarding idol worship, whose prohibition is severe.” Although the Gemara later states that Rabbi Eliezer said this concerning all prohibitions, the poskim nevertheless accepted the distinction for halacha. In the chapter Kol HaTzelamim, 49a, this reasoning is also mentioned several times to distinguish idol worship from other prohibitions. The poskim therefore maintain that when zeh v'zeh gorem involves a result essentially completed through the forbidden cause, the Ramah and the Rosh hold that in the case of idol worship it is not permitted. Where the forbidden cause does not essentially complete the object but merely assists and improves it, it is permitted. Regarding orlah and other prohibitions, all forms of zeh v'zeh gorem are permitted without this distinction. The Rambam's words must also be understood this way. In chapter 7 of Hilchos Avodah Zarah he writes the law of one who took a shuttle from an asheirah, mentioned by the Shulchan Aruch in this seif, and afterwards the law of one who took wood from it, etc. However, the Rambam does not write there the law that if one removed all the fire the food is permitted. Moreover, when he writes concerning idol worship that the bread is forbidden, he does not state the reason that the improvement of the wood is present in the bread, whereas in Hilchos Maachalos Asuros he does state this reason. This teaches that regarding idol worship, even without the improvement of the wood being present, and even without a torch opposite the bread, it remains forbidden because of zeh v'zeh gorem. The Beit Yosef writes that in Hilchos Avodah Zarah the Rambam relied on what he wrote in Hilchos Maachalos Asuros, and therefore he combined the laws here in the Shulchan Aruch and ruled in seif 4 that if the fire was removed it is permitted. This is exceedingly forced. Had that been the Rambam's intent, he should have explained in Hilchos Maachalos Asuros that the same law applies to idol worship. Rather, in my humble opinion, regarding idol worship he ruled stringently in accordance with the plain meaning of the Mishnah, forbidding zeh v'zeh gorem even when no coal or flame of the forbidden wood remains. The Rama's addition, “while the torch is opposite it,” follows the Beit Yosef's understanding and does not account for what we have explained. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, sought to explain that the Ramah is stringent regarding bread and a garment because one's body derives benefit from them, unlike planting beneath the tree. However, he did not account for what is written in seif 11 concerning planting beneath it. He further wrote that we do not rule like the Ramah but like the majority of the poskim. This was not stated precisely, for the Rosh expressly rules this way, and as we explained, all agree except that the Beit Yosef understood the Rambam leniently. In truth, that is not the Rambam's view in my humble opinion. The Rif also cites the Mishnah without qualification. Had he held leniently, he should have stated that this Mishnah does not follow the halacha, rather than leaving us to infer this from an external difficulty concerning a fertilized garden and thereby removing the Mishnah from practical halacha. It therefore appears as clear as the sun that one must rule stringently regarding these cases of zeh v'zeh gorem involving idol worship. Who could presume to be lenient against the explicit rulings of the Rosh and the Ramah, together with the proper understanding of the Rambam, on the basis of a forced interpretation of the Rambam?
