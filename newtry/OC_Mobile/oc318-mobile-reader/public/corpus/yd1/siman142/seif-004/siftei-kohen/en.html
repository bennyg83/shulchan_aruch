He heated the oven with them etc. Until end of seif 6: in truth the words of the Tur, mechaber, and other poskim need a craftsman to reconcile them — therefore I am compelled to copy the language of the Gemara and the poskim.
And now I will clarify: aside from this, one should wonder at the Tur who wrote "and if flame opposite it the bread is forbidden" — implying he rules like the one who says even glowing coals permitted, contrary to the Rosh’s position, as proven explicitly in the Rosh that even for the one who says zeh v’zeh gorem permitted, he ruled so — see there — because there is wood benefit in bread when the coals are glowing; and so he wrote in the glosses there: the halacha is zeh v’zeh gorem permitted etc., and the halacha is there is wood benefit in bread when the coals are glowing, to forbid bread baked with forbidden wood — end; and so he wrote in Tur Orach Chaim siman 445 explicitly. And further because here regarding an idol, even with dim coals it is forbidden even for the one who says zeh v’zeh gorem permitted, because the Rosh wrote there that what we permit with dim coals according to all is specifically regarding orlah peels and kilayim stalks since they are subject to burning and we rule all that is burned its ash is permitted; but regarding chametz we rule like the Rabbis who say crumble and scatter to the wind, which is in the category of all that is buried, whose digging is forbidden; and all the more with glowing coals — Rabbenu Yonah — end; and if so regarding an idol too, where we rule grind and scatter to the wind as the Mechaber wrote in siman 147, and its ash too is forbidden as the Tur wrote in seif 1 of this siman — if so even dim coals would be forbidden, for whenever the coals are forbidden there is wood benefit in bread and forbidden even for one who says zeh v’zeh gorem permitted. And so in the glosses there: but with dim coals permitted — this applies only to orlah wood and kilayim stalks; but chametz on Passover even its ash is forbidden — end; and so in Orach Chaim siman 445: if he cooked or baked with it — that bread and dish forbidden; and if it is permissible — for the Rabbis who say ash is permitted — a dish cooked and bread baked are not forbidden unless the bread was baked and dish cooked while the chametz body still existed or the coals were glowing; but if they were extinguished, permitted. And per the Rabbis that its ash is forbidden — in every case forbidden — end.
And what he wrote "and if he did not cool it and he baked bread in it, it is forbidden" — since he was silent it implies even in old he deals with; and therefore necessarily when flame was opposite it, as the Rav explained — for if cooked on coals or in oven heat he wrote nearby that permitted; and he follows his reasoning who explained the Rambam's words so; but per my humble opinion even in oven heat or cooked on coals the Rambam forbade here regarding idol, and specifically regarding orlah where ash and coals are permitted he distinguished so — which is not the case with an idol, as I wrote above.
And what he wrote “it became mixed with others, he shall lead payment for that bread to the Dead Sea” — implies wherever it was not mixed leading benefit to the Dead Sea does not help; however I already wrote in s.k. 8 that even without mixing one may sell the bread to a gentile when he cuts it before the Israelite — see there.
And the rest of all the loaves are permitted — even for eating; and not like yayin nesech which is forbidden to drink since it was forbidden because of a gentile's touch; but bread, because it was baked with something forbidden for eating, is forbidden only for benefit; and since he led benefit to the Dead Sea, he no longer benefits from the gentile's action — thus wrote R. Yonah in the name of the French rabbis; and it is the Rosh's and Tur's opinion — see there.
And what he wrote, "he swept out all the fire," etc., implies that it applies even to a new oven. So too from what he wrote in seif 6, "if one cooked upon coals of idolatrous wood," etc., it implies that it applies also to a new oven. So wrote Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh, that since Rambam did not distinguish, it implies that it speaks also of a new one. I did not get to the bottom of his view. Although Rambam in chapter 16 of Hilchot Ma'achalot Assurot did not distinguish between new and old, that is regarding orlah, whose coals and ashes are permitted; if so, once they become coals their prohibition has gone. Since the oven itself is not the actual prohibition, but was completed with prohibited wood, and the prohibited wood has gone, there is no prohibition here at all, as is explained in Beit Yosef. But with idolatrous wood, whose coals and ashes are forbidden, how can cooking on coals or in a new oven be permitted? The entire cause is prohibited, and the prohibited wood has not gone. In truth, he follows his own approach, for he wrote in Beit Yosef: it is difficult to me, since the coal of an idol is forbidden, why do we permit if one cooked upon coals? One must say that when we say it is forbidden, that is only ab initio, end quote. Bach similarly wrote about the Tur's statement that if a torch is opposite it the bread is forbidden: zeh ve-zeh gorem is permitted only when one cooked upon coals, even glowing ones; so it is in Perek Kol Shaah regarding orlah, and from there we learn for asherah wood. Although it is forbidden to benefit from the coal of an idol, that is ab initio; here it says that if one cooked upon coals it is permitted after the fact, end quote. In my eyes this is astonishing. How can one say so? Since a coal of orlah is forbidden ab initio, and so Tosafot wrote in Perek Kol Shaah, then the coals of all prohibitions of benefit are forbidden ab initio. If so, what does the baraita in the last chapter of Beitzah and the poskim teach by saying that the coal of an idol is forbidden? The coal of every prohibition of benefit is forbidden ab initio. Rather, certainly it teaches that the coal of an idol is forbidden even after the fact. So too the plain matter implies, and so it is proven there explicitly. It says: with a coal of hekdesh one commits meilah through it, but with a flame one may not benefit ab initio and does not commit meilah; a coal of avodah zarah is forbidden, but its flame is permitted. It asks: why is the flame of avodah zarah permitted, and the flame of hekdesh forbidden? It answers: avodah zarah is repulsive and people separate from it, so the Rabbis did not decree about it; hekdesh is not repulsive and people do not separate from it, so the Rabbis decreed about it. If it were true that a coal of an idol is permitted after the fact, it should have asked from the coal taught in the first clause: why with a coal of hekdesh does one commit meilah after the fact and incur an asham, while with a coal of avodah zarah it is permitted after the fact? Furthermore, the answer would not answer anything, for certainly the rule that one commits meilah with a coal of hekdesh is not a rabbinic decree, since he incurs an asham; how could the Rabbis decree that one incur an asham and bring chullin into the Temple court? Rather, certainly one commits meilah by Torah law. If so, if you say a coal of an idol is permitted after the fact because it has no substance, it is difficult: why with hekdesh does one commit meilah? Rather, certainly a coal of an idol is forbidden even after the fact. What the Tur wrote, that if a torch is opposite it the bread is forbidden, was already necessarily explained above as coming only to teach that the improvement of the wood is in the bread; and indeed it is forbidden with coals. Furthermore, even if you say that he specifically means when a torch is opposite it, as Beit Yosef and Bach understood, there is no difficulty: here it speaks where the coals are in the oven, so it is zeh ve-zeh gorem. That is why he wrote that even though we hold zeh ve-zeh gorem is permitted, if a torch is opposite it the bread is forbidden; infer from this that wherever no torch is opposite it, the bread is permitted because it is zeh ve-zeh gorem. The coal of an idol that is forbidden refers to where there is no additional permitted cause. But indeed a coal of an idol is forbidden after the fact. It is further astonishing regarding the Mechaber, who in Orach Chaim siman 445 copied Rambam's language: if one cooked or baked with it, that bread and that cooked food are forbidden in benefit, and its charcoal is forbidden in benefit, end quote. There it implies from the Tur and Beit Yosef in Rambam's explanation that even if one cooked with dim coals and ashes of chametz, the bread and cooked food are forbidden. But here the Mechaber wrote that if one cooked upon coals, even if they are burning, they are permitted. So too one must wonder at Ateret Zekenim, who wrote here like the Mechaber and wrote that a coal is forbidden only ab initio, but after the fact permitted, while in Levush HaChur siman 445 he wrote that there is no difference whether the bread was baked and the cooked food cooked while the body of the chametz still existed or while the coals were glowing, or whether they went out and afterward it was baked or cooked; in every case they are forbidden. It is further astonishing regarding Ateret Zekenim, who in this siman seif 4 wrote that if one baked the bread with that heating in an old oven it is permitted and in a new one forbidden, for there is no permitted cause here at all, and this is when no torch is opposite it, etc.; how then did he write in seif 6 that a coal of an idol is permitted after the fact? This requires study.
