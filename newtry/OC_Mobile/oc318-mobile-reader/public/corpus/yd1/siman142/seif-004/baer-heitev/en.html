He heats it. The result is caused jointly by a permitted and forbidden factor and is permitted. Although joint causation is ordinarily forbidden initially, here the oven has no remedy except demolition, so it is treated as after the fact. With a new oven, permission after reheating applies only where one first places permitted wood and heats the oven before inserting bread. One may not initially place the bread inside and then heat it. Shach.
Forbidden. Shach understands that this applies even to an old oven, where the flame is directly opposite the bread. He argues that even residual oven heat or coals should be forbidden regarding idolatry; the distinction between coals and direct flame applies only to orlah. Taz agrees that with idolatry even residual oven heat or coals forbid.
He shall cast. Shach infers that without mixture, sending the benefit to the Dead Sea does not help. As stated above, one may nevertheless sell it to a non-Jew excluding the forbidden value, provided the non-Jew cuts it. The remaining loaves are permitted even for eating. This differs from yayin nesech, where drinking remains forbidden because of non-Jewish touch. Bread baked through a prohibited source was forbidden only as benefit; once that benefit is discarded, no prohibited benefit remains.
Permitted. Shach notes that the Mechaber appears to apply this even to a new oven and to cooking upon coals. He disagrees. Regarding orlah, whose coals and ashes are permitted, Rambam's ruling may apply. Regarding idolatrous wood, whose coals and ashes are forbidden, cooking in a new oven is entirely caused by prohibition. Shach therefore concludes against the Mechaber that coals of idolatry forbid even after the fact. Taz agrees, and even in an old oven, after all fire is removed, food cooked or baked in the residual heat is forbidden. Shach also questions the Mechaber's differing ruling in Orach Chaim concerning chametz coals.
