Therefore it is permitted to take rings. See Bekhor Shor in his novellae to Avodah Zarah 29b, who wrote that from here it appears that if any ornament of gold, pearls, or the like is tied onto the dead, it is forbidden in benefit; and according to the Bach's view, brought by Be'er Heitev s.k. 1, even if they are braided into the hair. At first glance one should forbid even rings if they are fastened tightly, for the Rosh wrote at the beginning of Chullin that the tying written regarding tefillin means that one tightens them on his arm, etc. However, the root of this ruling is very astonishing, for at the end of the first chapter of Arakhin we heard only that ornaments of the dead are forbidden when they are like part of his body, such as a wig and the like, meaning a false tooth. Other ornaments are not heard of anywhere. Moreover, silver and gold ornaments should be no more stringent than the clothing of the dead, which is obviously not forbidden even if tied to him at the time of death, and we have never heard of anyone who forbids it. He discusses this at length and concludes that since the Rambam and Semag permit even the hair of the dead itself, and so too appears to be Tosafot's view in Bava Kamma 10, s.v. she-ha-shor, and even the Rashba and Ramban forbid only the dead person's own hair and not a wig, therefore one may rely on the permissive authorities at least regarding ornaments of silver, gold, pearls, and the like, which are not part of the body. Even if they were tied or braided on at the time of death, they may be permitted, and one should not destroy Jewish money; see there.
