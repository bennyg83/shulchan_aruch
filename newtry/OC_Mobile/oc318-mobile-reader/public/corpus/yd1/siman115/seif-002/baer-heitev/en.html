Carcass. Rabbenu Yitzchak ibn Migash challenged: why should it not be nullified in sixty? And he resolved that it constitutes a prohibiting agent and forbids in any amount.
In herbs. For the Sages did not distinguish, since there are those who curdle them even now in the skin of a carcass.
The custom. And Shach disagrees with this law and ruled: even if the Jew sees everything, nevertheless it is forbidden as long as the gentile makes the cheeses; and behold proof that even with herbs it is forbidden because they did not distinguish. (If so, the same applies if he saw that gentiles made them; and that is what the Mishnah taught anonymously: cheeses of gentiles are forbidden. And regarding milk of a gentile the Mishnah taught: milk that a gentile milked and a Jew does not see it is forbidden, and if the Jew sees it is permitted — hence in cheeses there is no distinction between whether he saw the making of the cheeses or not. If so, one must warn the supervisors who go with the gentiles when they make cheeses to sell to Jews that the Jew should place the rennet into the milk; and if not, all the cheeses are forbidden.) And what we practice to permit buying from the cheese merchants is because they practiced to rent the animals; and if so the cheese belongs to the Jew; and the same applies if one buys a quantity of cheeses from him — if so the cheese at the time of their making belonged to Jews; but if a gentile makes cheeses for himself, even though a Jew was there from beginning to end it is forbidden (anything in which a Jew did not perform an act) — end of his words.
The milking. Shach wrote: Rama wrote this according to his method; but in my humble opinion the poskim do not deal with a case where gentiles made the cheeses, but rather from the law of milk that is permitted when the gentile's intent is to make cheeses and the Jew must afterward make the cheeses; and nevertheless even according to his view that he permits b'dieved, he wrote on this in Issur VeHeter that one should not permit except in a case of great loss; however where he saw the milking only that he was not there at the beginning of the milking, it appears one should permit b'dieved even without great loss.
That his intent. Shach wrote: meaning that we know with certainty that his intent is to make cheese; and then even if he had something impure in the flock it is permitted b'dieved, for the reason is that the gentile's intent is to curdle; and even if he has not yet made the cheese but only the milk was milked, it is called b'dieved for this matter; and specifically if one buys from him a quantity of cheeses or by weight; but if one buys by measure it is forbidden; and nevertheless it is forbidden to eat the milk thus, since he did not see the milking, even if we know that the gentile's intent is to make cheese — end of Shach's words.
