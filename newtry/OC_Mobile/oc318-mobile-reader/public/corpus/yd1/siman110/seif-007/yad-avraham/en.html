Question and responsum for siman 110. Question: Seven animals were slaughtered in a village a parsa from our city; a man took one tripe called tzaptzucha and found a needle in it — head protruding externally, most embedded diagonally through the cavity; no drop of blood, only redness near the needle externally without disease or pus; cutting the red spot showed no pus or blood; rust test negative, middle black. Before finding the needle all seven were cut into large pieces together, salted in three or four places, and rinsed; afterward the needle was found from one of the seven, unknown which — great loss and time of distress. Answer: The mixtures appear permitted as dry-in-dry (siman 109), since before cooking there is no prohibition from salting or piece worthy of honor. Initially only safek treifah — all issur because the tripe was rinsed before presentation; if not rinsed it would plainly be permitted per Shach siman 48 s.k. 14 and 30 (needle through-and-through without blood drop = after shechitah). Redness alone does not disqualify (Peri Megadim, Nishmas Adam). Rust in needle middle — follow Maharshal; even Bach disagrees, rely on Maharshal in great loss. Rinsing creates safek treifah (Rashba responsum, Beit Yosef, Avodat HaGedolah). Needle commonness does not block double safek (Peri Megadim, Mishbetzot Zahav). Double safek applies: perhaps R. Tam's view (Taz siman 110) or Rashba on large piece not piece worthy of honor (siman 101). Shach rules like R. Tam; acharonim lenient on piece not worthy of honor. Slaughterhouse decree (Shach seif 5) does not apply to individual at home. Peri Megadim permitted safek treifah in store mixture by double safek — all the more here. Dry-in-dry nullified; rabbinic issur — Torah spared Israel's property (Chullin 49). Signed Avraham A.A. R. Yehuda Leib.
Question. Seven animals were slaughtered in a village one parsa from our city. One resident of our city took one intestine, called tzefetzuche, brought it home, and found a needle in it. It was brought before me. The head of the needle protruded on the outer side, while most of it was embedded inward diagonally until the other side, into the cavity of the intestine. There was no drop of blood at all; only outside, near the needle, there was a red appearance, but no illness or decay was recognizable. I also cut the red place and found no moisture of pus or blood. It had no rust, only blackness in the middle. I saw that the intestine's cavity still contained waste, and thought it had not yet been rinsed, until I asked and they said it had been rinsed. Before the needle was found, all seven animals had been cut into large pieces as usual, all together, salted in three or four places, rinsed, and afterward the needle was found in this intestine from one of the seven animals, and it was not known from which. There was great loss and also pressing need.
Answer. It appears that the mixture may be permitted like dry mixed with dry in siman 109, since the doubt became known before cooking. There is no need to prohibit because of the salting or because of chatichah hareuyah lehitchabed, as I will explain. First, there is here only a doubtful treifah. The entire prohibition arises because the intestine was rinsed before being brought to me; had it not been rinsed, it would have been plainly permitted based on the Shach in siman 48 s.k. 14 and 30, who writes that both in the hemeses and beit hakosot, in a case of great loss, even if a needle is embedded through and through, if there is no drop of blood we say it entered after slaughter, since there is substance for it to catch on, and had it been there during life there would have been blood. The simple sugya in Perek Elu Treifot also implies that wherever there is something for it to catch on we say so; what difference is there between hemeses, beit hakosot, or gizzard? The Minchat Yaakov writes similarly. Even if one invokes the view that we are not expert in checking for a drop of blood, the Shach already wrote there that many later authorities omitted this stringency and did not accept it, and that this is how one should rule; the Minchat Yaakov likewise writes so. The red appearance outside does not hurt, for the Peri Megadim, in the name of responsa Nachalat Shiva, explains that redness of flesh alone is not disqualifying even with a needle; a mere change of appearance does not invalidate organs unless there is a lesion on one side and the appearance passes through. Rust in the middle of the needle also does not hurt according to Maharshal, and even though the Bach disagrees, in great loss one may rely on Maharshal, especially since the Kereti UPeleti agrees and explains that because the rust is not on the head of the needle that pierced, we say it was pushed and pierced after slaughter. Rust is not mentioned in the Gemara and is not proof like blood; perhaps the animal swallowed an already-rusted needle. Since the only defect here is that the intestine was washed and the examination was lost, this is only a doubtful treifah, as the Rashba responsum that is the source for this law states. At least it helps for sefek sefeika. Even though Maharam Lublin, cited by the Shach in siman 50, wrote that with a needle prohibition is more common than permission, that was regarding complete permission not based on "once slaughtered, it was permitted"; nevertheless it is still a doubt for sefek sefeika, as the Peri Megadim writes explicitly, because the animal has a chazakah of permissibility from birth, when it had no needle. Even a doubtful derusah, which Tosafot call common toward prohibition, can still combine for sefek sefeika, and similarly with a broken wing whose timing is unknown. Here there is also a basis to attribute permissibly: perhaps the jostling of carrying the intestine a parsa pushed the needle through only afterward, and they told me the intestine had been placed near a wall where needles were kept. Therefore, if the forbidden piece had been identifiable, salting alone would not forbid the rest, for Shevut Yaakov permitted a doubtful treifah salted with kosher meat through a sefek sefeika: perhaps it was not treifah, and perhaps the halachah follows Raavyah that salting forbids only the thickness of a peel. In great loss one may rely even on a lone authority against many, as seen in Choshen Mishpat siman 25 and Shach Yoreh Deah siman 242. Further, here there is doubt whether the prohibited piece was ever salted with the permitted meat in a way that left no majority afterward; perhaps it was salted alone and only later mixed. This resembles the case of doubtful impure fish salted with pure fish, permitted in siman 83 by sefek sefeika. Since taste from salting does not prohibit, only the unrecognized prohibited piece remains, and this is the law of dry mixed with dry, where one in two is nullified and the whole mixture becomes permitted. Nor should it be prohibited as chatichah hareuyah lehitchabed, for there is a full sefek sefeika: perhaps the halachah follows Rabbenu Tam and his school, who permit a doubtful treifah that became mixed before the doubt was known even among important items that do not normally become nullified; and if not, perhaps the halachah follows the Rashba and his school, that a large piece is not chatichah hareuyah lehitchabed unless it is fit to be served as it is. The Mechaber brought this view plainly, and even though the Rama records the stricter custom, the Peri Megadim explains that this can still count as a doubt. Many later authorities are lenient on each side of this doubt, including the Shach, Minchat Yaakov, Peri Megadim, and others. The stringency of meat markets, where after a treifah is found one may not buy even a piece not fit for honor lest people confuse pieces, applies only where many buyers are involved and error is likely, not to an individual in his home. Even the market rule is only a stringency. The case cited from Beit Yaakov concerning a butcher's house involved a definite treifah and a setting frequented by many; our case is only a doubtful treifah and a private home. There is also an additional support from Maharibal, who permitted utensils of a doubtful treifah after twenty-four hours as a rabbinic doubt, and from Peri Megadim, who tends to be lenient in great loss where a Torah doubt became rabbinic before it was known. Although Shaar HaMelech discusses limits to this principle, there are proofs from several sugyot and responsa that where a Torah doubt becomes a rabbinic mixture before awareness, one may treat it leniently. The conclusion is that our case is dry mixed with dry. According to the Rif, Rashi, Rabbenu Tam and their supporters, the Shach, Minchat Yaakov, Peri Megadim, Maharibal, and others, the mixture is permitted. We should not be stringent in a rabbinic prohibition, for the Torah has concern for Jewish money, and all these authorities are worthy to rely on in such a case.
