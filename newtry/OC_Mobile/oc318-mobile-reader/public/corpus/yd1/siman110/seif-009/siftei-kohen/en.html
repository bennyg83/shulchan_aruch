And it appears that he holds that since in the first mixture each item was forbidden, because there is certainly a doubtful prohibition present, the same applies when one piece separates and stands alone. Since it is known that this piece separated from that mixture, its law is like the mixture itself: we decree lest one take from the fixed mixture, and we do not permit by sefek sefeika. Even if it became mixed one with one, wherever there is no majority it is as though it is known that it separated from the prohibited mixture, and it remains forbidden.
And Tosafot wrote on the case of a married Jewish woman that one might ask: there is still a sefek sefeika, perhaps she was coerced and perhaps willingly, and if willingly, perhaps when she was a minor, for seduction of a minor is treated as coercion. They answer that this is one name of coercion. The Maggid Mishneh likewise writes at the beginning of chapter 3 of Hilchot Issurei Biah in the name of the commentators that this is one doubt. From here one learns that a sefek sefeika works only when the two doubts are genuinely distinct; when both turn on one legal name or one root, it is not a proper sefek sefeika. This also explains several cases in which the poskim reject combinations that appear to be two doubts but are actually one substance of uncertainty.
And there is a difficulty from the Rashba in Chullin, cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 57, regarding an animal with a lion's claw lodged in its back, where there is doubt whether the lion entered at all; he permits by sefek sefeika: perhaps the lion did not enter, and if it did, perhaps this claw did not come from the lion but from rubbing against the wall. Yet that sefek sefeika is not reversible. One must say that where the first doubt is whether any prohibited event occurred at all, and the second is whether the visible sign is from that event, this is a valid structure even if not perfectly reversible, or that there the two doubts arise together and the case is different from a sequence that must be reversed.
I later found explicitly in responsa Menachem Azaryah siman 126 in accordance with my words, and I greatly rejoiced. He writes that although some later rabbis relied on a sefek sefeika even when it cannot be reversed, the matter is twofold: wherever it is not clear which doubt arose first and we are merely pursuing doubts, the doubts must be reversible in order to permit; otherwise, one cannot open with the second doubt. But where one doubt is naturally first in the reality of the case, or where the form of the case itself creates the order, it need not be reversible in the same way.
I later saw in Sefer She'erit Yosef, in the rulings of Mar Shmuel, that he too holds that any sefek sefeika that is not reversible is not a sefek sefeika. He left difficult the case at the beginning of Bava Kamma regarding part of a placenta without a fetus, which Rashi and Tosafot explain as a sefek sefeika although it is not reversible. One may answer that there the doubt is whether this place contained the fetus at all, and if it did, whether the fetus was a viable offspring; or that the case is a combined uncertainty in the object itself and not a constructed two-step doubt. In such matters the requirement of reversibility is not the same as where we attempt to build a heter by arranging separate doubts.
And also the words of the Issur VeHeter require explanation, for at first glance they are unclear and appear to have been printed with error. Their meaning is that when meat was found and a non-Jew took it not in our presence, one should not say that since it is forbidden as meat that disappeared from sight, because perhaps it was exchanged for neveilah, it should be treated like a rabbinic prohibition that contains a Torah concern in its own body, such as the cheeses discussed below. Rather, meat that disappeared from sight is a rabbinic decree upon the circumstance, not an intrinsic doubtful Torah prohibition in the piece. Therefore, when additional doubts or mixtures arise, we may sometimes treat it more leniently than an item whose very substance is a doubtful Torah prohibition. The printed wording must be read this way, and with it the later distinctions in the laws of mixtures are understandable.
And one should not ask on the Rav's statement that where the prohibition itself is a doubtful Torah prohibition, and only from another side it becomes rabbinic, we do not say safek derabbanan lekula. For below in siman 111, with a Torah prohibition that fell into two pots, where each pot has a majority in a way that permits by Torah law though rabbinically forbidden, it is treated as a doubt in a rabbinic matter. There it is different: the Torah prohibition has already been nullified by majority in each pot, so the remaining issue is only rabbinic. Here, however, the intrinsic doubt of Torah prohibition remains in the piece itself, and only an external factor made it rabbinic.
And similarly if there is doubt whether a piece is from kosher meat or from a doubtful Torah-level treifah, it is permitted if it first became mixed one with one, as explained in the earlier rules. Likewise, a rabbinic prohibition, even where it was not forbidden because of a Torah concern in its own body but only as a decree lest one confuse it with another matter, such as an egg of a neveilah mixed into a thousand, may still not be nullified if the Sages gave it importance. Nevertheless, where the doubt is only rabbinic or where the matter entered a mixture in a way the Torah permits, the rules of safek derabbanan and sefek sefeika apply according to the distinctions explained above.
And even if it had a chazakah of prohibition, etc. The Rav wrote in Darkei Moshe at the end of siman 57 and in Torat Chattat that the Issur VeHeter says no sefek sefeika permits anything that has a chazakah of prohibition, but his words are not correct, for the Rashba wrote in responsum siman 401 that sefek sefeika helps even for something that had a chazakah of prohibition. At first glance this seems to challenge the Issur VeHeter broadly. But one must distinguish: where the chazakah is still operating directly against the doubts, it weakens the sefek sefeika; where the doubts undermine the basis of the chazakah or concern a later mixture, the sefek sefeika can help. Therefore the Rashba's case and the Issur VeHeter's rule are not necessarily identical. The matter depends on whether the prohibition was established in the very object now before us or only in the status that is now subject to two independent doubts.

A sefek sefeika even though it can be clarified by checking the lung, etc. Meaning that no checking is required at all. So the Darkei Moshe wrote at the end of siman 57 in the name of Piskei Mahari, end of siman 47. However, the Rashba in his Chiddushim to Chullin wrote that even where there is a sefek sefeika, if the matter can be clarified by examination, it requires examination. In my humble opinion this is also proven from the Shas. The rule that a sefek sefeika permits does not mean one may ignore an available clarification when the object itself can be checked. Only where checking is impossible, lost, or not legally required after the mixture has taken effect do we rely on the sefek sefeika without further inquiry. Therefore the matter must be judged according to whether the examination is practically and halachically available.
