(Shulchan Aruch, seif 10, gloss) The father cannot redeem through an agent. There is proof that agency does work from the rule that if the father gives the redemption money as a gift on condition that it be returned, the son is redeemed. The condition must be fulfilled, as appears in Kiddushin 6a. If pidyon haben could not be performed through an agent, how could a condition cancel the act? We rule in Perek HaMadir that any act that cannot be performed through agency cannot be made subject to a condition, and even a condition phrased as "on condition that" does not work in chalitzah. Rivash, siman 175, writes similarly. This indicates that conditions are effective in pidyon haben because the mitzvah can be performed through an agent. Sha'ar HaMelech, Hilchot Ishut, raises the same question. I later saw an explanation that the actual transfer of money can certainly be done through an agent, as is evident in Bechorot 50, while the verbal declaration must be made by the father himself. Verbal acts do not require the ordinary rules of conditions, which is why conditions work with vows, oaths, and nezirus. Nevertheless, if the wife was expressly appointed as his agent to redeem the child, perhaps she may do so, even if redemption performed by her independently would not be effective. A wife is in any event like her husband's hand when she acts expressly as his agent. Birkei Yosef cites Responsa Devar Moshe that if a woman redeemed her firstborn son using her husband's property, the redemption is ineffective, and when the husband returns he should redeem again without a blessing. Perhaps that case involved no explicit appointment. Although in matters of prohibition we do not automatically say that a wife's hand is like her husband's, Menachot 93 requires a verse to exclude a wife from semichah on her husband's korban, implying that absent the verse her hand could represent his even in a mitzvah context. Thus, with explicit appointment, agency should at least be possible.
And if another person redeems his fellow's son with his own money, Ran in Pesachim indicates that this may work, though initially the father's consent is required so as not to deprive him of a mitzvah incumbent upon him.
(There) And beit din also does not redeem him without the father. See Taz and Nekudot HaKesef, who disputes Taz. Nekudot HaKesef proves from Tosafot Ketubot 11 that with acquisition for a minor, zechiyah without the person's presence works only where the matter is a complete benefit. He also brings proof from Kiddushin 43b that through beit din it can work even where the benefit is not complete and contains some disadvantage. Pnei Yehoshua writes similarly. Ketzot HaChoshen, siman 243, rejects that proof, distinguishing division of an estate, where the minor already owns a share and division is unavoidable, from an ordinary mitzvah requiring agency, where beit din cannot create agency for a minor without his consent. He also says that the Sotah case is not comparable because no property is being acquired, only agency is being created, and the owner's satisfaction is insufficient. There is nevertheless support for Nekudot HaKesef from Tosafot and Rambam, Hilchot Terumot chapter 8, that deaf-mutes, mentally incompetent persons, and minors for whom beit din acquired slaves may feed those slaves terumah. This is not a wholly beneficial acquisition, as Gittin 52 shows, yet beit din can act for them. Likewise, beit din can acquire wives for deaf-mutes and mentally incompetent persons even though marriage creates prohibitions and monetary obligations, and all the more so nowadays when it excludes all other women. This demonstrates broader authority of beit din than ordinary zechiyah. Ketzot's distinction regarding an apotropus separating terumah from orphan property can likewise be challenged from Tosafot Shabbos 53 concerning tithing animals by a guardian.
(Seif 12) If he became a treifah before thirty days passed, he is not obligated in redemption. Shach, seif katan 16, explains this as a definite treifah. In a doubtful treifah the father is exempt, but if the child later lives twelve months he becomes obligated because survival for twelve months removes the doubt. This is not universally accepted. Some authorities hold that a human treifah can live beyond twelve months, as Tosafot cite in the name of Rabbenu Tam at the end of Zevachim, and Hagahot Mordechai at the beginning of the third chapter of Chullin cites Tosafot Shantz similarly. In my work on Rambam, Hilchot Issurei Bi'ah chapter 10, halachah 11, I proved that Rambam also holds this. Therefore, even survival for twelve months would not create an obligation to redeem, because the burden of proof in monetary matters lies on the claimant. Minchah Chadashah disagrees with Shach for another reason, namely that only a minority of treifot survive, and we do not follow a majority to extract money. This is incorrect according to Hafla'ah and HaMikneh, who hold that where the same majority has already been accepted for a non-monetary legal consequence, it may then be applied to the monetary consequence as well. Here, the majority that treifot do not survive is relevant to prohibition law and would therefore also affect the monetary issue.
