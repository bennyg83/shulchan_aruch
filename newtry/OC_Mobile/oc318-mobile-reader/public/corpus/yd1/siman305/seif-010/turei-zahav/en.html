On the redemption. The blessing is formulated with "al," "on the redemption," rather than with the lamed form used for some mitzvot, because this mitzvah can ultimately be performed by the son himself if he grows up without having been redeemed. At that point it can be performed only by the son himself. Beit Yosef cites Rivash.
And while giving him the money he recites the blessing, and so forth. Meaning he recites it at the time of giving, not afterward, because a blessing over a mitzvah must precede the performance. This is unlike the practice of some who first place the money before the kohen and only afterward recite the blessing.

The father cannot redeem through an agent. The related statement that beit din also cannot redeem the child without the father appears in the question addressed to Rivash, responsum 131. The questioner asks why Rambam formulated the blessing for pidyon haben as "al pidyon," since the primary obligation rests upon the father. He says only by derivation from "padoh tifdeh" do we learn that the son redeems himself, but redemption through an agent or beit din does not exist. Rivash's answer does not mention this premise at all; he merely gives another reason for the wording "al" rather than the lamed form. Rama inferred that because Rivash did not contradict the questioner's statement, Rivash accepted it. This is astonishing. In Perek HaShoel and Nedarim 72 we find the general rule that throughout the Torah a person's agent is like himself. What excludes agency here when the father appoints an agent to redeem the son and gives him the money due the kohen? A recently printed work called Tzedah LaDerech already raised this question. In Parashat Bo he strongly challenges the ruling here and consequently rules in practice that if a firstborn son's father dies before thirty days, the paternal grandfather or beit din may redeem him. He brings lengthy proofs and reports that R. Leib of Prague ruled this way. Every halachic decisor should of course investigate the Gemara and poskim independently, so I will state what appears correct to me without showing favor to anyone. Regarding the father's ability to redeem through an agent, the new work is correct. Ran at the beginning of Pesachim writes explicitly that some mitzvot, although imposed upon a person, may be discharged through another, such as destroying chametz, milah, pidyon haben, and similar mitzvot. He also cites Maharshal in the name of Sefer Me'il Tzedek: if the father is not in the city, another person can apparently redeem the firstborn because a person's agent is like himself, and one can transfer the redemption money to the kohen through another because one may acquire merit for a person in his absence. Maharshal himself comments that as long as the father is in the city, another person cannot redeem without the father's knowledge. I will return to that point below, with Hashem's help. But the new work's ruling that beit din may redeem an orphaned firstborn by transferring redemption money to the kohen does not appear correct to me. Indeed, the very source he cites proves the opposite. Nedarim, Perek Ein Bein HaMudar, asks whether one who separates terumah from his own produce on behalf of another person's produce requires the owner's knowledge. Perhaps, since it is a benefit, we acquire the benefit for him even in his absence, which Ran explains as meaning the person is presumptively treated as his agent. Or perhaps the mitzvah is personally his and he prefers to perform it himself, so notification is required. The question is unresolved, and Yoreh Deah and Rambam rule that the terumah takes effect and notification is unnecessary. The new work learns from this that another person may similarly use his own money to redeem a minor. In my opinion the opposite follows. Tosafot at the beginning of Ketubot 11, explaining immersion of a minor convert, write that agency can be created for a minor where the matter is overwhelmingly beneficial despite containing some slight disadvantage. Their precise phrase "some slight disadvantage" shows that because we find agency for a minor only in the case of conversion, it applies only to a complete benefit with no meaningful adverse side. If there is even a slight detriment that would not prevent acquisition for an adult because the benefit predominates, we still do not say an agent can act for a minor. Applied to terumah, this shows that Nedarim's rule of separating from one's own produce for another's does not concern a minor. Where one separates from a minor's own produce, there is a slight disadvantage because perhaps the minor would prefer to give more than the agent gives, since the mitzvah is precious to him. Tosafot therefore imply that another person cannot act as the minor's agent in such a case. The same slight disadvantage exists even where the other person uses his own produce for the minor's terumah, because he thereby takes away from the minor the future opportunity to perform the mitzvah personally and with his own property once he grows up. Thus Nedarim must concern an adult, for whom such a slight loss of mitzvah opportunity does not defeat the overwhelmingly beneficial agency. A minor, who cannot appoint an agent and for whom even a slight adverse element prevents acquisition through zechiyah, is different. The exact same logic applies to pidyon haben. Another person redeeming the child now removes from him the opportunity to fulfill the mitzvah personally when he becomes an adult. The new work glanced at Tosafot but did not analyze how their distinction applies here. He interprets the second side of the Nedarim doubt differently, but even though with an adult we ultimately follow the first side and treat the act as beneficial, it still remains a matter containing a slight adverse aspect. The new work also cites many proofs from Kiddushin; one who studies the passages carefully will see they support the opposite. Therefore, it appears to me that while the father is alive he may appoint an agent to redeem his son with the father's money, and the paternal grandfather has no greater inherent status than an agent except where he acts by the father's authority. Likewise, another adult may use his own money to redeem the child on the father's behalf because this is comparable to separating his own terumah for another adult's produce. But if the father dies, the child should not be redeemed until he becomes an adult. Maharil says similarly. The premise asserted by the questioner to Rivash is not binding halachah, and Rivash's silence does not establish it.
