On the pidyon. The reason this blessing is formulated with “on” is that it is not like those mitzvot formulated with “to,” since this mitzvah can be performed by the son when he grows up, and then it cannot be performed except by himself. Beit Yosef in the name of the Rivash.
And as he gives him the money, he blesses, etc. Meaning, not afterward, for we require the blessing to precede the performance of the mitzvah. This is unlike the practice of some, who already place the money before the kohen and afterward recite the blessing.
And if there is wine in the city, etc. In a city where there is no wine, they bless shehakol over beer. It appears that in those places where there is no wine, one must perform the pidyon before the blessing of hamotzi over the meal, for during the meal one does not bless over beer. This is unlike a place where there is wine, where they perform it after hamotzi.
And the father cannot redeem through an agent. Nor does the court redeem him without the father. This is from the question that the Rivash was asked in siman 131, meaning that the questioner asked him as follows: why did the Rambam write in the text of pidyon haben “on pidyon, etc.”, since the mitzvah of pidyon is essentially incumbent upon the father, only from the doubling “padeh tifdeh” we learn that he redeems himself; but through an agent or through the court we do not have this; end quote. In the responsum that the Rivash answered, nothing of this was mentioned; he only answered him with another resolution of the wording “on” and not “to redeem” with a lamed. The Rama learned from this that since the Rivash did not refute this point, this shows that he accepted it. In truth this is astonishing, for we find in Perek HaShoel and in Nedarim 72 that throughout the Torah a person’s agent is like himself. How would an agent whom the father appointed to redeem, giving him the money due to the kohen, be excluded here? A newly printed book, Tzeidah LaDerekh, already awakened to this, and in Parashat Bo it is written how he, of blessed memory, wondered at this ruling forbidding redemption through an agent because of this question. Because of this he descended to rule in practice there that a bekhor whose father died before thirty days may be redeemed by his paternal grandfather or by the court. He elaborated there with proofs and wrote at the end that they testified that Maharar Leib of Prague, of blessed memory, ruled so. Since it is proper for every decisor to search in his own mind through the Gemara and poskim for the root of the matter, I said regarding this that I will not show favoritism, and I will mention here what appears to me after examination. Regarding his ruling that the father can redeem through an agent, he wrote well, for so wrote the Ran in the first chapter of Pesachim: “There is another mitzvah which, although incumbent upon him, he can exempt himself from through another, such as removing chametz, circumcision, pidyon haben, and the like”; end quote. The above book brought this. He further wrote in the name of Maharshal, in the name of Sefer Meil Tzedek: “If the father is not in the city, it appears that another person can also redeem the bekhor, for a person’s agent is like himself, and he can also transfer merit through another to the kohen, for one may confer benefit upon a person in his absence.” Maharshal himself wrote regarding this that it appears to him that as long as the father is in the city, no other person may redeem without the father’s knowledge, etc.; see there. I will place my hand upon this with the help of God at the end. But first I say: what he ruled, that the court can redeem the bekhor when his father died and transfer the pidyon money to the kohen, does not appear to me. The proof the above book brought from there appears to me to prove the opposite, namely from the sugya in Nedarim, Perek Ein Bein HaMudar, Nedarim 36, where they asked: One who separates terumah from his own for his fellow’s produce, does he need his fellow’s consent or not? Do we say that since it is a benefit to him, and the Ran explains that one may confer benefit upon a person in his absence and presumably it is like his agency, or perhaps it is his mitzvah and he prefers to perform it himself, therefore one must inform him? The question was not resolved, and in Yoreh Deah, and likewise the Rambam, rule that his terumah is terumah and he need not inform him. From this the author of that book brought proof that the same applies to pidyon haben, that another can confer the benefit of his money, since it is a benefit for the minor. This does not appear at all. On the contrary, the opposite is proven. Tosafot in the first chapter of Ketubot 11, explaining that we immerse a minor convert because there is agency for a minor where there is some obligation, wrote there; from their use of “some obligation” and not simply “obligation,” it is implied that since we find agency for a minor only in this matter, that we immerse a minor convert, therefore only in a case like a convert, where there is no obligation at all in the world. But if there is even some obligation, which regarding an adult we would not consider because there is a greater side of benefit, nevertheless regarding a minor we do not say that one can make an agent for him except in an absolute benefit with no slight obligation. From this it is clear that what Nedarim says, that one who separates from his own for his fellow’s produce is effective, does not speak of a minor, for Tosafot conclude that wherever there is a side of obligation, there is no agency for a minor at all. This applies even where one separates from the minor’s produce for the minor’s produce, and they mention the concern that perhaps the minor wants to give more, meaning that he might desire to give more than what that other person gives from the minor’s property on his behalf, because the mitzvah is especially beloved to him; therefore the act of the other is nothing. Consequently, where another makes terumah from his own for the minor, there is also this slight obligation to the minor, for he has taken the mitzvah from him. If he had waited until the minor grew up, he would have fulfilled the mitzvah with his own body and money. Rather, it is simple that separating from one’s own for another’s produce is effective only for an adult; then we are not concerned with this matter, that he takes from him the merit of the mitzvah, because it is only a slight obligation. But with a minor, for whom even a slight obligation disqualifies, as Tosafot wrote, he is not his agent by virtue of conferring benefit in his absence, for a minor even in his presence is like not in his presence. If so, the same is true exactly regarding this pidyon of a bekhor: one may say that this other person who redeems him is not like his agent, since there is some obligation in this, as we mentioned. The author of the above book saw the words of Tosafot but was only like one peering through the cracks, and did not examine well how to learn from them for our case, which contains the slight obligation we mentioned. He explains “or perhaps, etc.” even though regarding an adult we follow the first side and consider it a benefit, nevertheless it is not exempt from being called a slight side of obligation. The above book also contains many proofs from the Gemara in the first chapter of Kiddushin; one who examines the Gemara there will see that the opposite can be proven. See there. Therefore it appears to me that as long as the father is alive, he can appoint an agent to redeem with his money in his place. For this reason, the paternal grandfather is like his father. The words of the questioner in Responsa Rivash are not halachah, and we do not live by his mouth; the Rivash did not bother to answer them. Likewise, another person can give from his own in place of the father and redeem the bekhor, for he is like his agent, as I wrote regarding one who separates from his own for his fellow’s produce. But if the father died, he should not be redeemed until he grows up. So it is stated in Maharil; see there.
