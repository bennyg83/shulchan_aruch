This seif is Rambam's language through the words "his son is redeemed," and Shulchan Aruch adds, "provided it is worth," and so forth, in order to answer Ran's question on Rambam from Kiddushin 8a. The baraita says: "This calf is for redemption of my son," and the Gemara asks whether it is worth five sela or not. Beit Yosef explains Rambam as follows: anything worth five sela to some person may be accepted by this kohen for five sela even if it is not worth that amount to him. When Rav Ashi says this applies only to someone like Rav Kahana, the point is that if the item is not worth five sela to anyone, Rav Kahana cannot simply say, "To me it is worth five sela." Rather, a kerchief of the type that might genuinely be worth five sela to Rav Kahana or someone of his stature can be accepted at that value even if it is not generally worth so much. But an item that is not worth five sela to anyone cannot be transformed into five sela merely by the kohen's statement. On this basis Beit Yosef explains Rambam's phrase "anything not worth that amount in the market" as meaning it may nevertheless be worth five sela to some people and the kohen accepted it for five. This is what Shulchan Aruch means here by saying the item is not worth five sela "to anyone." I am astonished that such a holy mouth, the light of our eyes, would say this, and all the more so establish halachah upon it. According to his explanation, why does the Gemara need to say that the kohen "accepted it upon himself"? If the item is already worth five sela to some people, silence should suffice. One cannot answer that the Gemara proves an explicit declaration is required from Rav Kahana's words, "to me it is worth five sela," because on Beit Yosef's interpretation Rav Kahana was not speaking only about himself. The phrase means "to me and people like me, distinguished individuals, it is worth five sela." If it is objectively known that an item is worth five sela to some people, there should be no need for any declaration or acceptance. Rav Kahana's statement would merely reveal the item's market among a class of people. Furthermore, Beit Yosef's explanation of the Gemara's phrase "but for everyone else" as meaning "if it is not worth five sela to anyone" is a strained homiletic reading rather than the straightforward path of the sugya. Why force Rambam away from the simple meaning that it must specifically be worth five sela to the kohen receiving it? Even more difficult, Beit Yosef's explanation may reconcile Rambam's phrase "not worth that amount in the market" by reading it as "not in the general market but worth it to some individuals," but what shall we do with Tur, who writes simply: if he gave the kohen an object in place of five sela and the kohen accepted it at that value, his son is redeemed even if the object is not worth that much? Tur plainly means it is not worth that amount to anyone. My father-in-law explains Rambam and Tur by saying the decisive point is that the father says, "Here is this object for five sela," and so forth. His approach is even more forced than Beit Yosef's. If so, the entire give-and-take of the Gemara would have been unnecessary. When the questioner asked why the son is redeemed if the calf is not worth five sela, the answer should simply have been that the father expressly said, "this calf for five sela." Rav Kahana's case would then be different only because the father said nothing, so Rav Kahana had to say, "to me it is worth five sela." But the Gemara itself brings Rav Kahana as the explanation of the baraita, indicating that the same principle governs both. Nor, in my humble opinion, can one sustain the distinction my father-in-law makes between a kerchief and other objects. It appears to me that Rambam and Tur were troubled by the Gemara's wording. Instead of saying merely, "and it was worth five sela to him," which would directly express the supposed answer as later illustrated by Rav Kahana, the Gemara says, "and the kohen accepted it." This implies that the act of acceptance itself is sufficient even if the item is not actually worth five sela. The kohen expressly says that he accepts it as five sela. The Gemara then cites Rav Kahana as support. Rav Kahana said, "to me it is worth five sela." That case is actually weaker than an explicit acceptance at a stated redemption value, because Rav Kahana justified the acceptance by saying the item was worth that amount to him, and therefore it indeed had to possess that subjective value. But when the kohen directly accepts an item as the five-sela redemption even though it is worth less, that deliberate acceptance itself is effective. The comparison is that in both cases an item objectively worth less can accomplish redemption by virtue of the kohen's acceptance. This appears to me the correct understanding of Rambam and Tur.
