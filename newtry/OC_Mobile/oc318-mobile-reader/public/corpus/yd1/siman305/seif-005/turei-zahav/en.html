If he gave him a vessel, etc. This seif is the language of the Rambam up to “his son is redeemed,” and the Shulchan Aruch added, “provided that it is worth, etc.,” to answer the Ran’s question against the Rambam, which the Beit Yosef brought from the sugya in the first chapter of Kiddushin 8, where it teaches: “This calf is for the redemption of my son,” until “to increase in value with it, if not, etc.” The Beit Yosef wrote that it appears to him that the Rambam holds that anything worth five selaim to any person, even though to this kohen it is not worth five selaim, may be accepted by him as five selaim. What Rav Ashi said, “we said this only in a case like Rav Kahana, etc.,” means that if it is not worth five selaim to anyone, it does not help that Rav Kahana said, “to me it is worth five selaim.” This is its explanation: “we said this only” regarding something like a scarf, which to Rav Kahana and those like him is sometimes worth five selaim, even though to everyone it is not worth so much; Rav Kahana can say that to him it is worth five selaim. But “everyone,” meaning something that to everyone is not worth five selaim, he has no authority to do this. This is what the Rambam wrote, “anything that is not worth in the market,” meaning, but to some people it is worth five selaim, and the kohen accepted it for five; end quote. This is what he wrote here, that it is not worth five selaim to anyone. I am astonished that a holy mouth, the light of our eyes, would say this, and all the more set the halachah so. According to his explanation it is difficult: why did the Talmud need to say that the kohen accepted it upon himself? Even silence should suffice, since in any event it is worth that amount to some person. One cannot say that the Gemara proves that such a statement is needed from Rav Kahana saying, “to me it is worth five selaim,” for according to the Beit Yosef’s explanation, Rav Kahana necessarily did not say this about himself, since it need not be worth five selaim to him; even if it is worth so only to others, that suffices. Rather, according to his explanation, this is what he said: to me and those like me, namely a great person, it is worth five selaim. If so, whenever we know that outside it is worth that amount to other people, no statement or acceptance at all is required. There is no proof from Rav Kahana, for he came only to reveal that this item is worth that amount to other people like him. Beyond this great pressure and forced reading, according to his explanation of the Gemara’s phrase “but everyone,” meaning if to everyone it is not worth, etc., this is a homiletic and forced path, far from the simple paved explanation for all. Who compelled the Rambam to explain the plain sense this way and depart from the simple meaning, that it must be worth that amount specifically to him? What is still more difficult is that according to his explanation he resolved the Rambam’s words, namely that when the Rambam wrote that it is not worth in the market, he meant that outside the market it is worth to some person. But what shall we do with the words of our teacher the Tur, who did not write so, but wrote: “If he gave him an object for five selaim and the kohen accepted it as such, his son is redeemed, even if it is not worth so much”; end quote. This implies that it is not worth so much to anyone. My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, explained the Rambam and Tur as holding that when the father says, “Here is this object, etc.”; see there. His way is more forced than that of the Beit Yosef. If so, the entire give-and-take of the Gemara about this would have been unnecessary. When the questioner asked, if it is not worth five selaim, why is his son redeemed when he says, “This calf is for five selaim,” the Gemara should have said that he is redeemed because the father explicitly said “five selaim.” It is not comparable to Rav Kahana, who needed to say “to me it is worth,” since the father said nothing; unlike where the father says so explicitly. Furthermore, according to his words there is a distinction between this case of a calf for five selaim and the case of Rav Kahana, yet we see that the Gemara brings the case of Rav Kahana regarding it. Also the distinction he wrote between a scarf and other objects, in my humble opinion, should not have been written. It appears to me that the Rambam and Tur were troubled by the Gemara’s phrase “and where the kohen accepted it.” It should have said, “and it was worth five selaim to him,” for that is the essence of the answer, as it concludes from Rav Kahana. Since the answerer wanted to state the permission, he should have stated it as it is. Rather, this must be what it means: the kohen accepted it as five selaim; although it was not worth that amount, he said that he accepts it as five selaim. It brings proof from Rav Kahana, who said, “to me it is worth five selaim.” This is worse than saying that he accepts it even though it is not worth that amount, because when he says he accepts it because it is worth that amount in his eyes, it must certainly be worth that amount to him, as Rav Ashi said. The reason the Gemara brings proof from Rav Kahana is that just as there, although it is not worth five selaim, nevertheless he is redeemed because it is worth that amount to him, so too where he explicitly accepted it, although it is not worth that amount, such acceptance is effective. So it appears to me in their view.
