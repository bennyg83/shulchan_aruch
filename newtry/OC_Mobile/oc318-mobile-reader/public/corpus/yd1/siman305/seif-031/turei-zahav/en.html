Or two males and one female, the kohen has nothing. The Tur brought the Rambam's view, that if they bore two males and one female, the man whose wife had not previously borne gives five selaim to the kohen, since he is exempt only through two doubts, etc. The Tur wrote: I do not understand his words, etc. The Beit Yosef added another difficulty: why is this different from the case of both being his wives, one of whom had previously borne, where he is exempt out of doubt? It appears to me that both difficulties can be answered in one way. With two men, we say to each individually: before you are three children, two males and one female, and we follow the majority; we say to you that you certainly fathered a male. Therefore the man whose wife had not previously borne is treated as having certainly fathered a male. If you wish to say that perhaps I also fathered the female, we answer that even according to your words you do not have a definite exemption, for perhaps the female emerged last. Thus, regarding liability, this is not treated as a double doubt, because the male is considered certain. This also answers the Beit Yosef's question: where both women belong to one man, we cannot say to him by majority that he certainly fathered a male, for this man certainly fathered twins from one woman and one child from the other; it is just as plausible to say that the twins were born from the woman who had previously borne and the woman who had not previously borne gave birth to a female as the reverse. Thus there is no majority regarding this man. This, to my mind, settles the Rambam's view well. But there remains a difficulty from Rav Huna's baraita, which says that even with two males and one female they are exempt. The Kesef Mishneh in the name of Mahari Korkos wrote that the Rambam had a different version in the Gemara, according to which Rav Huna's statement refers to the first clause of the Mishnah, namely two wives of one man. This is very surprising, for immediately before Rav Huna's statement the Talmud explains the latter clause of the Mishnah, two wives of two men; how could the Talmud explain the latter clause before the first? It appears to me that the Rambam explains that Rav Huna's baraita follows Rabbi Meir, who says each can push the obligation to the other, namely according to the first version above; but we rule only like Rabbi Yehudah, that one cannot push it away. So it appears to me.
