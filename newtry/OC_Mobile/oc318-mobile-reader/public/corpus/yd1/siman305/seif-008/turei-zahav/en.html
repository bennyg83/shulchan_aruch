But he should not give to the kohen on condition, etc. This means that this should not be his intent without saying it explicitly, for whenever he states it explicitly the end of the seif explains that it works. Here he says that it should not be in his mind. If the father intends this and the kohen thinks not to return it, their intentions do not match at the time of giving, and there is no pidyon if the kohen later returns it. If the kohen does not return it, the child becomes redeemed afterward, at the moment he keeps it for himself, for then the intentions of giver and kohen match. But if he returns it, there is no pidyon, neither at the time of giving nor later. If, however, the kohen also intended to return it, their intentions match for return, and the child is redeemed even if the kohen later returns it; it is like explicitly saying, “on condition that you return it to me.” This is the everyday custom: the father places a large sum for the pidyon, intending that it be returned, and the kohen also thinks so; afterward he sometimes returns all of it or keeps some small amount. With this the words of the Rama in this seif, “and the kohen's intent was, etc.,” are also explained. In the Perishah he wrote that the Mechaber's view depends on how the kohen thinks to return it, whether by way of condition or otherwise. His words are very astonishing to anyone who sees them, for in truth there is no distinction in this. In every case where both intentions are the same, the child is redeemed; but if the father thinks it will be returned and the kohen thinks not to return it at all, he is not redeemed if the kohen returns it, as I wrote nearby.
Five selaim and you shall return them to me, etc. In this wording it implies a retraction: first he says, “Here are five selaim for you,” which sounds like a complete gift forever, and afterward he says, “and you shall return them to me”; he thus retracts from his first words and cancels them. We find a similar point in chapter Mi She-Achazo, Gittin 72, in Rashi's explanation of the mishnah regarding one who says “from today and after death.” This is unlike saying “on condition that you return,” for there is no retraction there; rather, he explains his words, since one who says “on condition” is like one who says “from now.” Thus the gift from now takes effect only with this condition. But when he says “and you shall return it,” it is not “from now,” and the latter words cancel the former; we say, “take hold of the latter expression,” as at the end of chapter HaShoel. This appears to me the reason. The Perishah wrote that if he said “and you shall return,” it means “return immediately,” and is not a gift, unlike “on condition,” which is as though he says it is acquired to you from now, only that you return it after some time. His words are not understandable to me, for why should “and you shall return them” mean he must return them immediately more than “on condition”? The opposite is more plausible, and as I wrote the matter is clear.
