Even the smallest amount. See Shach above, seif katan 23, who asks that this contradicts what the Mechaber wrote above in seif 7, where he requires an opening large enough to remove the vessel from susceptibility to tumah. He answers with difficulty that there the discussion is only about the preferred procedure. In truth there is a further contradiction, for there the Mechaber cites the Rambam without qualification that even such an opening does not help unless the vessel is also fixed in the ground or incorporated into a structure, whereas here he cites the Rash and Rosh that even the smallest opening alone suffices, even if the vessel was not fixed in the ground, and the implication is that no fixing is required even initially. The Shach's answer does not resolve this. There are additional contradictions as well. I found in Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 45, that he resolves the matter as follows. Above, in seif 7, the issue is immersion inside the vessel, which is a Torah matter, so the Mechaber was concerned for the Rambam's view requiring both an opening that removes the vessel's status and that it be fixed in the ground. Here, however, the issue is whether water flowing from this vessel invalidates a mikveh as drawn water. Since many authorities hold that drawn water invalidates only by rabbinic law, the Mechaber relied on the Rash and Rosh that even the smallest hole helps, though the vessel was not fixed in the ground. Although according to the Rash and Rosh there is a resulting stringency, namely that sealing with both olive refuse and lime constitutes a complete seal, so fixing it into the ground would then be ineffective because it is like fixing a vessel without any hole, nevertheless only the combination of lime and olive refuse is a complete seal. One alone is not. Thus the Mechaber above in seif 7 was precise in mentioning lime or gypsum, but if it was sealed with lime and olive refuse together, fixing it in the ground would indeed not help. This also resolves the Shach's unresolved difficulty in seif katan 80 with the Rama. The sefer Be'er Yaakov likewise distinguishes in this manner. See also Responsa Chatam Sofer, end of siman 215.
At the sides. See Responsa Meil Tzedakah, siman 39.
One may rely on the lenient authorities. See Responsa Mishkenot Yaakov, siman 45, concerning mikvaot formed by digging deeply into the ground until water wells up naturally. When one wishes to clean them, must the vessel used to draw out the water be pierced with an opening the size of the mouth of a wineskin, or may one say that these mikvaot have the law of a spring and are not invalidated by drawn water at all? He argues at great length for stringency for two reasons. First, the principle that a spring is not invalidated by drawn water is not clear or undisputed. Many leading early authorities hold that even a spring is invalidated by drawn water for human immersion whenever it contains less than forty seah. One should not be lenient against them except in a case of great need, and even then only after the fact where three log of drawn water fell in. If the majority of the mikveh was formed in this way, there is certainly no room for leniency even after the fact. Second, there is reason to doubt whether such mikvaot have the status of a spring at all, since the Tosefta, and the Rambam accordingly, rule that one who digs beside the sea or a river has the law of a mikveh. Accordingly, all arguments for leniency regarding drawn water in our mikvaot collapse, because who can determine which mikveh is fed by its own spring rather than by the river, particularly in countries situated among rivers and marshlands where true springs are not found. Although the Shulchan Aruch and Rama below in seif 52 appear to contradict this explicitly, their words there themselves require great study. In practice, he concludes, we have only the words of the Rambam and the Tosefta. The general rule is that in mikvaot near a river, whenever there is room to suspect that the water does not come from an independent spring but is drawn underground from the river, no halachic authority should permit any leniency applicable to a spring, neither regarding three log nor certainly regarding a majority of drawn water, even after the fact, for this concerns a possible prohibition punishable by karet. He later found that Responsa Maharit, part 2, likewise rules stringently in all this.
Because drawn water does not invalidate a spring. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 216, concerning a mikveh into which three log of drawn water fell before it contained forty seah, thereby invalidating it, after which the spring beneath it broke through and the water rose until it contained many times forty seah. The questioner thought to invalidate it, reasoning from the Rama's language that drawn water does not invalidate a spring only when the spring preceded the drawn water, whereas if the drawn water preceded the spring, the spring does not validate it. He sought to distinguish the Mechaber's ruling above in seif 11, where order makes no difference, as referring to a spring flowing in from above, but not to one welling from underneath. The Chatam Sofer rejects this distinction and says the Rama did not intend it. Nevertheless, he concludes that since the Responsa attributed to Ramban, siman 231, cited briefly in Beit Yosef, writes that although it appears more likely that there is no distinction whether spring water came first or drawn water came first, it is proper to be careful initially, and since we must also reckon with the Sheiltot's view that even a spring is invalidated by drawn water, therefore if there is no nullification in forty-one and the public will not incur great expense, but only inconvenience, one who is stringent is praiseworthy.
