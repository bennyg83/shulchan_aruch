With hot water it is prohibited. Tiferet LeMoshe writes that even lukewarm water is prohibited when heated by fire, because the decree made no distinction. People do not normally bathe in excessively hot water, so when the Sages prohibited bathing in hot water they included ordinary warm water.
To prohibit all bathing. See Baer Heitev in the name of the Shach. Dagul MeRevavah writes that the leniency applies only to face, hands, and feet, but bathing the entire body even in cold water is prohibited according to our custom throughout all thirty days. Responsa Teshuat Chen, siman 31, discusses this at length and concludes that a God-fearing person should personally be stringent not to bathe his entire body even in cold water. For others, however, one may rule leniently, specifically on Friday where there is some mitzvah need. Recreational bathing in rivers should be prohibited. Baer Heitev also cites Maharshal that bathing was prohibited because it could lead to haircutting. The Taz answers the obvious question from Chol HaMoed, where haircutting is prohibited but bathing is not, by explaining that on Chol HaMoed the haircut prohibition applies to everyone. According to this, if Pesach or Sukkot occurs during the seven days, so that the thirty-day restriction remains afterward, it may perhaps be permitted to bathe on Chol HaMoed, since the concern of coming to cut hair does not apply because haircutting is already prohibited to everyone. However, the Maharshal's rationale itself is difficult: if bathing is prohibited because of possible haircutting, why is bathing not prohibited for mourning a father or mother even after thirty days for as long as haircutting remains forbidden until friends rebuke him, as below siman 390:4?
