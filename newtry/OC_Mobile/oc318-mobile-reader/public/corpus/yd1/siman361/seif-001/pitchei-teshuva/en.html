If there are burial societies in the city. [See Magen Avraham siman 153 s.k. 41, who wrote: men who took it upon themselves to bury the dead cannot prevent others who come to bury — for in the performance of a mitzvah everyone wishes to merit (Mishpetei Shmuel 56; see Be'er Zehav siman 182). In our country where there are appointed societies for this, it seems one must give to the chevra as is their custom — end quote. See Responsa Chatam Sofer siman 329, who wrote about Magen Avraham: it seems to me he is dealing with men who want to also be among their members and they prevent them — therefore he wrote that they cannot prevent many from performing a mitzvah. But if the men do not want to join the society, it seems it is not within their power to deprive them of their established mitzvah. Regarding what Magen Avraham alluded to from Responsa Be'er Zehav siman 182 — in Choshen Mishpat end of siman 149 Rama ruled contrary to it. He further wrote: nevertheless it seemed to me at first glance that this applies when also the chevra kadisha wants to perform it free of charge as an act of loving-kindness — then they have precedence. But where they want payment that they assessed, while others want to do it for free or at lower cost — it is obvious they cannot thereby deprive others. Even though we hold that members of a society can impose their assessments upon themselves — that is on their own members, with everyone's consent and with the agreement of the town's scholarly leader. But to make a regulation to extract money from someone outside their society — that for every deceased one must give so-and-so, and if one refuses no one may bury him — that is not within their power. Magen Avraham's statement that one must give them as their custom — perhaps his intent is if the deceased belongs to the chevra's members, for upon their own members they can impose as customary; but upon someone outside the chevra it is not within their power. Or perhaps Magen Avraham holds that what was customarily imposed on those outside the chevra was originally established by consent of the entire community and the town leader. And even if so, that applies only when there is a fixed assessment — that everyone outside the chevra pays a fixed amount, or a fixed percentage per hundred — a definite rule equal for all. But as is practiced in small communities — a toll with no fixed rate, like an extortioner rising on his own — let it be silenced and not said. Nor is it pleasing to the deceased of Israel that people such as these should tend to them. In siman 330 he wrote: once it became known to him that a guest came to his town and passed away there, and the gabbaim of the chevra kadisha took from the orphans who were with him eight hundred gold coins for a grave site and burial expenses; when he heard this he summoned the heads of the holy Sephardic community and they answered that they had never buried anyone in their cemetery for free. If they want a place befitting his honor — graves of righteous men shall not be sold for less than a thousand gold coins; and they agreed to give several hundred gold coins. He wrote: if what they say is so, there is no doubt that all their practices follow Torah custom — it is not improper to sell a grave. For there is an explicit source that it is fitting to be buried in one's own grave, in Bava Batra 112a: "a righteous man was found buried in a grave not his own" — implying this is a concern. Therefore since they give him a grave site at fair value, with the heirs seeking to honor the deceased — they may raise the price of the land like property of Nebi Bar Mariyon, and there is no overcharging for land. All the more so for something customarily sold at high prices. Only this I do not know: who gave them permission to bury a stranger of unknown deeds alongside righteous men? Can money answer everything? As for what they said they wanted to honor him in the special place for guests — based on Ramban, parshat Chayei Sarah, on "a stranger and a resident" — that the custom was to have ancestral cemeteries and a special field in which all strangers were buried.
