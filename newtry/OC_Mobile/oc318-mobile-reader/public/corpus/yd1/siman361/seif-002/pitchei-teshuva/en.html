See Magen Avraham, siman 153, seif katan 41, who writes that people who have established a practice of burying the dead cannot prevent others who come to perform burials, because each person wishes to merit participation in the mitzvah. He cites Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 56, and Be'er Sheva, siman 182. In our lands, where designated societies are appointed for this purpose, Magen Avraham writes that one must pay the society according to local custom. Chatam Sofer, siman 329, explains that Magen Avraham appears to speak of people who want to join the society and are being excluded, and therefore they cannot prevent the public from performing a mitzvah. But if those people do not wish to join the society, they cannot simply deprive the society of its established possession of the mitzvah. As for Magen Avraham's citation to Be'er Sheva, Rama in Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 149, rules against that responsum. Chatam Sofer further writes that this priority would appear to apply when the chevra kadisha is also prepared to perform the burial without charge, as an act of kindness. But if they demand their fixed fee and others are willing to do it free of charge or for less, they certainly have no right to cause others a loss. Although members of a society may impose regulations and fines upon their own members, that authority exists only over their own group, with the consent of the members and the approval of the Torah scholar serving the city. They cannot enact a rule extracting money from one who is outside their society, requiring him to pay a fixed amount for every deceased person, and declaring that otherwise no one may bury his dead. Perhaps Magen Avraham's statement that one must pay the society according to custom refers only to a deceased person belonging to members of the chevra kadisha, over whom the society can impose its established rules. Alternatively, perhaps he assumes that the practice of charging outsiders was originally enacted by the entire community and with the approval of the communal Torah scholar. Even then, the charge must be fixed and uniform, such as a set sum for every outsider or a fixed percentage of assets, equally applied to all. But a variable levy in small communities, with no fixed amount and imposed like an arbitrary tax collector, should be abolished. It is not fitting for the dead of Israel to be handled by people who act in such a manner. In siman 330 Chatam Sofer relates that he once learned of a traveler who came to Pressburg and died there. The officers of the chevra kadisha took eight hundred gold coins from the orphans who were with him for the grave site and burial expenses. Upon hearing this he trembled and summoned the society's leaders. They replied that their longstanding custom was to provide everyone a grave for a small fee, on condition that no one demand a particular place among the prestigious graves of renowned people. They told the deceased's two sons that if they did not request an important location but would bury him in the area designated for travelers, a small sum would suffice, aside from purchasing the grave. If, however, they wanted a location befitting his honor among the graves of tzaddikim, they would not sell it for less than one thousand gold coins. After negotiation, the sons agreed to pay eight hundred. Chatam Sofer writes that if this account was accurate, there is no doubt that their custom was a Torah-valid custom. Charging at least some price for a grave has a root in Bava Batra 112a, which speaks of a tzaddik being found buried in a grave that is not his, indicating that there is significance to being buried in one's own grave. Therefore, when they offer a grave at a fair value but the heirs seek a more honorable plot, the society may raise the price of the land, like the property of the house of Bar Marion. There is generally no law of price fraud for land, all the more so for something ordinarily purchased at a high price. What he did not understand was who gave them authority to bury an unknown stranger, whose deeds were not known, among tzaddikim and men of good deeds merely because money was paid. He also notes that their practice of designating a place for travelers has a basis in Ramban to Parashat Chayei Sarah on "a resident alien," who writes that the custom was for each family to have its own cemetery and for there to be a separate burial field in which all outsiders were buried. Consequently, Chatam Sofer writes that he could not recover from the chevra kadisha what they had already collected. See there at length, and also siman 331.
