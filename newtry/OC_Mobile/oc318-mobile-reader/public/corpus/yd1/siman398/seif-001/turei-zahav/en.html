Tur concludes that only aninut on the first day has Torah status, while aninut and mourning are distinct legal categories: what is forbidden in one may be permitted in the other. An onen remains so on the day of death even after burial with respect to eating sacrificial food and ma'aser sheni, as Zevachim teaches. Before burial, Chazal added prohibitions on meat, wine, marital relations, and exemption from mitzvot because the deceased lies before him for burial. At that stage he is still permitted in shoes, bathing, haircutting, and the other ordinary mourning restrictions. Once the grave is closed, he may eat meat and wine and becomes obligated in mitzvot, except that on the first day he does not wear tefillin. Tur's phrase that what is forbidden in one is permitted in the other is not absolute, because marital relations are forbidden in both. It refers only to the other distinctions just described.
