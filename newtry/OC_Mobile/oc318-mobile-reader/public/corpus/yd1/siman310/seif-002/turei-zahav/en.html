It appears that this law applies even if he later shows it to a sage and the sage finds a blemish in it, as mentioned in seif 1; nevertheless he must return the money to him. Although this is forbidden only because of a decree, and above in siman 119 he wrote that whenever the prohibition is rabbinic, what he ate he ate, nevertheless, since it is forbidden for benefit, even rabbinically he returns the money, as explained there.
It should be buried, and he returns, etc. It appears that he returns it to the seller, and the seller buries it. It is not necessary to say that if the seller wants this, he certainly must give it to him, for the seller wants to fulfill the mitzvah, like that case in Perek HaChovel, Bava Kamma 91: “You killed my ox.” “You told me to kill it.” He is exempt, and the Gemara establishes it as an ox standing to be killed, where he claims: “I wanted to perform the mitzvah with my property.” Here too it is so with this meat, which stands for burial. And if he does not want this, nevertheless the trouble is upon him, since he caused it. One should not say that he should not give it to him because he is suspected in this matter, for he is not suspected of eating it or selling it to another after it has been forbidden to him. Although he initially sold it in prohibition, that is not comparable to before they forbade it to him, when he thought there was a permission to slaughter based on a blemish that he had not shown to a sage. If we know that he is intentional, it appears that in this case we certainly announce about him that people should not buy meat from him, as explained in siman 119 regarding one who sold forbidden items, seif 15. As for eating it, it is obvious that he is not suspected of eating it, for it is explained there in seif 2 that one suspected of selling prohibition is permitted for eating with him, since he is not thereby suspected regarding eating. So it appears to me.
