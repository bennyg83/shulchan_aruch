That did not take root, etc. If it took root before the omer, however, even though it was not harvested until after the omer, the omer permits it. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 151, writes that taking root before the omer means three days beforehand. This is explicitly demonstrated in the chapter Makom Shenahagu concerning the teaching about one who weeds on the thirteenth, etc.
Nevertheless, ordinary grain, etc. Meaning ordinary grain whose status is unknown, whether it took root before the omer. If, however, it is known that most grain of that species did not take root before the omer, or that it was sown after the omer, then even if a minority took root before the omer it is forbidden, and this does not constitute a double doubt. This is implied by Tur from Rosh’s responsum and by Terumat HaDeshen, siman 191.
By virtue of a double doubt, etc. Although chadash is an item that will eventually become permitted, and such an item remains forbidden even where there is a double doubt, this can be resolved according to what I wrote above in siman 110, seif katan 25.
After the harvest. Before the harvest there is certainly no concern, since no new grain is yet present, for harvest time has not arrived.
For it is preferable that they remain inadvertent, etc. If most drinking in the country is wine, and barley and oats are needed only occasionally as food, so that there is no public hardship in separating from the prohibition of chadash, then where there is only one doubt, whether the grain is from last year or from this year, it is a mitzvah to protest among the populace and teach them that it is forbidden. There is no double doubt by saying that perhaps it is leftover grain from last year, and if it is from this year, perhaps it came from the minority sown sufficiently before the omer. Since that possibility is only a minority, it does not create a double doubt, for a minority in relation to a majority is regarded as nonexistent, as Tosafot state explicitly in the sugya of the open entrance in the first chapter of Ketubot. In Lechem Mishneh at the end of tractate Terumot, he writes at length to permit beer made from chadash barley on the ground that the liquid extracted from the barley is permitted. The matter requires consideration, for Rosh’s responsum at the beginning of klal 2, the cited Terumat HaDeshen, and the other poskim imply that it is forbidden. Bach writes at length to prove that the prohibition of chadash does not apply to produce belonging to non-Jews. All his words are astonishing. The view of Tosafot and those who agree with them is correct, and their proofs are clear, that chadash applies to produce belonging to non-Jews. In particular, Bach’s proof from the Gemara in the first chapter of Rosh Hashanah 13a is not valid. There, were it not for the inference that they ate from the morrow after Pesach, but did not eat beforehand, one could have said that they ate old grain. Alternatively, this follows the tanna in the first chapter of Kiddushin who holds that “in all your dwelling places” teaches that the prohibition did not begin until after conquest and settlement. The Gemara there states that this tanna does not accept the derivation that they ate after Pesach but not before, whereas Rabbi Eliezer, who holds that “in all your dwelling places” means wherever you dwell, does accept that derivation. Thus, the answer in Rosh Hashanah, “This cannot enter your mind, for it is written, ‘On the morrow after Pesach,’” necessarily follows Rabbi Eliezer. Without that inference, one could have said that they did not bring the offering and that chadash did not apply until after conquest and settlement. On the contrary, this provides a clear proof that chadash applies to produce belonging to non-Jews. Since the Gemara asks from where they brought the offering, it assumes that all Eretz Yisrael had belonged to non-Jews. Nevertheless, when it infers that they ate after Pesach but not beforehand, its only difficulty is from where they brought the offering. The fact that they had not eaten beforehand is fully understandable, demonstrating that chadash applies to produce belonging to non-Jews. I also see a clear proof from the Yerushalmi at the beginning of the second chapter of Challah, concerning moist and dry standing grain. Its meaning appears to be as follows: if they found moist standing grain, is it prohibited because of chadash? Perhaps since it is ultimately destined to be harvested, it is considered chadash belonging to a Jew. Or perhaps, since it came into Jewish possession from non-Jews, it is permitted. He asked him why he did not ask concerning dry standing grain. He replied that even dry grain is included, for there is no distinction, and chadash applies to produce belonging to non-Jews. Rav Shimshon of Sens, in his commentary on the Mishnah, explains moist as new grain and dry as old grain from the previous year, and writes that it is a great wonder why the omer had not permitted last year’s grain. I do not understand his view, for the matter is clear as I have explained.
