One who buys an animal is not concerned lest its mother or offspring was slaughtered that day; but the seller must warn the buyer and inform him whether he slaughtered the mother or offspring that day, or if he sold her to another to slaughter her that day; and if it is one of the four seasons when anyone who buys an animal usually slaughters it immediately—and they are: erev Pesach, erev Shavuot, erev Rosh Hashanah, and erev the last Yom Tov of the festival—he must inform him that he sold today the mother or daughter; and if he did not inform him, he slaughters and is not concerned, whether he bought from a Jew or from a non-Jew; and if it became known to him afterward that its mother or daughter was slaughtered that day, it is a mistaken transaction; and specifically when he sold both in one day; but if he sold the mother or daughter on the day before, he need not inform him; and if he sold one to the groom and the other to the bride, even if he sold on two days, he must inform him that certainly both are slaughtered in one day; if two bought oto v'et beno in one day, the first buyer slaughters and not the second {Rama: in what case is this said—when both bought from one person, for as soon as he sold to the first he could not slaughter what remained in his hand because the buyer took it on condition to slaughter it immediately (and therefore also the buyer from him may not slaughter, for he cannot sell him only the right in his hand) (Tur) (Be'er HaGolah)}; but if they bought from two people, both are equal and whoever slaughters first is alert and successful.
