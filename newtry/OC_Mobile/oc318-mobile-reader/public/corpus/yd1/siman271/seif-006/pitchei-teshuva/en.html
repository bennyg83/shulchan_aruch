For the sake of a Sefer Torah. See Bnei Yonah, who strongly protests the practice of some scribes who purchase hides processed by gentiles before the final scraping and smoothing with a knife and pumice stone, and then perform only that final scraping for the sake of the mitzvah. He considers this a serious prohibition, and even after the fact would invalidate reading from such a Sefer Torah where another Sefer Torah is available. If no other Sefer Torah exists, perhaps one may read from it, preferably without a blessing. See also Responsa Perach Mateh Aharon, part 2, siman 101, regarding parchment recovered from captivity where there was doubt whether it had been processed for the sake of a Sefer Torah. A Sefer Torah written on it is valid after the fact because there is a dispute among the Rishonim whether processing lishmah is required at all, plus an additional doubt whether these particular hides were in fact processed lishmah, creating a double doubt. Moreover, even stringent authorities concede that in pressing circumstances one may write on parchment not processed lishmah; therefore after the fact it may always be read publicly. Responsa Pnei Aryeh, siman 52, disagrees and reports that such a case arose in Rotterdam, where he ordered the sheets not processed lishmah removed and placed in genizah. Responsa Noda BiYehudah, second edition, Yoreh Deah siman 168, discusses a sofer who processed parchment and said he was doing so lishmah, afterward intended to use it for an invalid book, then abandoned that plan and wrote a Sefer Torah on it. If he merely thought of writing an invalid book, the thought has no effect. If he expressly said he wished to write an invalid book, speech can cancel speech. The physical processing itself is not decisive either way because the hide required processing regardless. If the sofer did not personally process it but gave it to a gentile and assisted by placing the lime, there is an affirmative act showing that the processing was for sacred writing. He further suggests that even if the sofer processed it himself and expressly said he wanted to write an invalid Sefer Torah, the parchment may remain valid because he did not say he was changing the purpose of the processing itself to the writing of an invalid Sefer Torah. Thus he may not have revoked his original declaration.
