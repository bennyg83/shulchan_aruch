They are forbidden to set up. This applies even where the courtyard is not subject to division. The Atteres Zahav writes that although partners ordinarily do not object to one another's use, they can object if they choose, as explained in Choshen Mishpat 161:10. Here, where one is forbidden benefit, they certainly object, and the use is forbidden. The glosses to the Perishah say likewise, but their formulation is not precise. Tosafot and the Ran maintain that since the partners could object, even when they do not object the user benefits from their forbearance, and the use is forbidden. This is explicit in their words. Tosafot also cites another reason: when one sets up a mill, many people gather there and occupy both his portion and his partner's portion; similarly, chickens spread throughout the courtyard. According to this explanation, any use that fills the entire courtyard, such as placing or spreading fruit throughout it, is forbidden. Maharshal writes this as well.
He is forbidden to set up, etc. This applies even where the courtyard is not subject to division.
They would compel the person who made the vow, etc. This applies even where the courtyard is not subject to division. Although the person barred by the vow may enter, we are concerned that the partners may use the courtyard in a forbidden manner, such as by setting up an oven. The Rosh and Ran write this, and the Beit Yosef cites them. This also appears to be the view of the Rambam in chapter 7 of Hilchos Nedarim and the Semag, prohibition 242. Rashi and Tosafot, however, maintain that where entry into the courtyard is permitted, they do not compel a sale. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that even where the courtyard is not subject to division they compel a sale, while some say they do not, and he considers the first view primary. It appears that where they wish to divide, or to invoke the rule of 'buy or sell' in a courtyard not subject to division, they are not compelled to choose one course, but may proceed as they wish. The Bach writes similarly regarding a courtyard subject to division, from which the same follows regarding 'buy or sell' where it is not subject to division.
The person who made the vow, etc. The Ran writes that specifically when only one partner made the vow do they compel him, because he is forbidden while the other remains permitted, and we fear that he may become jealous of his partner. But where they forbade benefit to one another, so that both are forbidden, there is no such concern, since each remembers his own prohibition. The Atteres Zahav writes likewise. The Rosh and the Tur in Choshen Mishpat, siman 180, however, write that if they forbade benefit to one another and one of them habitually violates the restriction, they compel that habitual offender to sell. Rabbeinu Yerucham says likewise. The Yerushalmi explains that two occurrences establish him as habitual, as cited by the Ran. It appears that the reason they ordinarily do not compel where both forbade one another is that, since both made vows, neither has a stronger claim than the other.
The person who made the vow, etc. The Mechaber's unqualified wording implies that he rules like the Rosh and Tur: whether one partner forbade himself from the other's share or forbade the other from his own share, they compel a sale. The Atteres Zahav writes likewise.
To sell. The Bach writes that this means selling to a third party, but he may not sell to the partner even though he said only, 'my courtyard.' He cites Responsa Rivash, siman 357, that selling it to the person who made the vow is forbidden, since that person benefits from the house that comes to him through the seller. His words are puzzling, for why may the share not be sold to the partner who did not make the vow? The Rivash says only that when one says, 'Konam from your house,' the owner may not sell the house to the person who made the vow. It therefore appears that the Bach means that if the partner who did not make the vow wishes to sell his share, he may not sell it to the partner who made the vow.
