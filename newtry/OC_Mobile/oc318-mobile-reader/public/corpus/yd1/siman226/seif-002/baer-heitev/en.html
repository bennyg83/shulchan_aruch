And they would compel him. The Shach writes that this applies even where the courtyard cannot be divided. Although the person under the vow is permitted to enter it, we are concerned that the partners may make a prohibited use of the courtyard, such as setting up an oven, etc. Rashi and Tosafos, however, hold that where entry into the courtyard is permitted, they do not compel a sale. It appears that if the partners wish to divide it or proceed by the rule of 'you take it or I will take it,' they may do as they wish. The Ran writes that they compelled a sale specifically where only one of the partners had vowed, but if both had vowed they do not compel them, because each one remembers his own prohibition. The Tur in Choshen Mishpat, siman 180, however, writes that if they vowed not to benefit from one another and one of them is accustomed to prohibited use, they compel the accustomed party to sell. The Jerusalem Talmud explains that two occurrences are sufficient to establish him as accustomed. It also implies that there is no distinction whether he prohibited himself from his fellow's share or prohibited his fellow from his own share; in either case they compel a sale. The Bach writes that he must sell specifically to a third party, but may not sell to his partner, even if he said only 'my courtyard.' His words are astonishing, etc. In Nekudos HaKesef, however, the Shach retracted and wrote that the Bach's words are correct. See there.
