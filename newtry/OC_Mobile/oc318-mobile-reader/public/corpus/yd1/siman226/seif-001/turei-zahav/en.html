he wrote at the end specifically for the need of the partners; and to me the words of our Rabbi the Tur appear as their simple meaning, and they follow the words of his father the Rosh who wrote: and our Rabbi Shimshon wrote in name of Rabbi Yochanan that halachah d'oraisa there is berirah as is proven here, etc.; and according to this ruling: partners who forbade each other from treading of the foot are permitted to enter the courtyard; and if one of the market forbade — forbidden to enter the courtyard, for vaday is forbidden; and because of berirah one should not permit partners when there is a law of division, for Rabbi Eliezer does not permit because of berirah since there is a remedy in division; and all the more an ordinary person — why should he enter the courtyard; and that Rabbi Eliezer permits in the Mishnah for an ordinary person — it appears to me he deals entering for the need of one of the members of the courtyard — end quote. It is clear from his words that specifically when there is a law of division, the one entering must have need for one of the partners, for they wrote specifically on this law; and automatically when there is no law of division it is permitted even for his own need; and what he wrote initially "and if one of the market forbade," etc. — this does not refer to partners but to an ordinary person, and comes to exclude the explanation of Rabbeinu Tam that he brings before this, that the ruling is: if they forbade each other from treading of the foot — forbidden to enter the courtyard, and if the owner of the courtyard forbade one of the market — permitted to enter the courtyard, for vaday is permitted in benefit — end quote; he wrote that two matters are opposite to the ruling of Rabbi Shimshon, and this is clear to one who examines there. And so it is in the hints that the Tur composed: partners who vowed benefit from each other are permitted to enter the courtyard, for we hold there is berirah, and specifically when there is no law of division; but when there is — no; and all the more if one of the market was mudar from them not to enter except for the need of one of the members of the courtyard — end quote; behold he wrote the prohibition for one of the market after the law that there is a law of division, for if not it would also refer to the first part — how is it relevant to say on this "all the more," what kal vachomer is here; rather certainly it refers to the end, and such are his words here in the Tur; and it appears the reason for these laws: whenever there is no law of division, there is no vow at all on this courtyard, even if one of the market forbade, for he can say to him "I am not entering into yours but into your fellow's"; and so the Rambam wrote in chapter 4 of Laws of Nedarim, and these are the words of Rabbi Eliezer in the Mishnah — namely when there is no law of division; and with this we say there is berirah; but when there is a law of division he cannot say "I enter into your fellow's," since there is place to divide — the domains are not mixed; and this is simple; nevertheless when it is for the need of one of the partners he can enter for another reason that the Ran mentioned: the Rashba wrote specifically when he enters for the need of the partner, whether he needs the partner or the partner needs him, because the courtyard is acquired to each one of them and to everyone who needs them; but for one of the market to enter for himself is forbidden, for if they acquired this to each other the courtyard for treading of their feet and for all they need — they did not acquire it for the whole world — end quote. And some of these words the Rosh also brought in name of Rabbi Shimshon bar Avraham the Frenchman, who is the Rashba; from this it is clear that whoever needs the courtyard, even for his own benefit through the partner, there is permission, for the courtyard is acquired to him for this; if so even when there is a law of division there is this permission that he says "I enter into mine," meaning in his fellow's share I have a right, as we say we are not stringent when there is a law of division except because remedy is possible — then we do not say there is berirah; and this is specifically among the partners themselves who vowed from each other, since it is in their power to compel each other, unlike one of the market — berirah helps him also in this, since he is not a partner in division with the noder from him; and if so what difference to me whether there is a law of division or not — and this is clear to every understander. And now it is well what there is to challenge apparently in the words of the Rosh that I brought, that he asks "and that Rabbi Eliezer permits in the Mishnah," etc. — he should have answered that Rabbi Eliezer deals when there is no law of division, and with this he permitted specifically for one of the market like the first part of partners who vowed, dealing when there is no law of division, as I mentioned in siman 224; and indeed it is not difficult, for the Rosh comes to teach us that this sugya in the Mishnah "one of the market was mudar benefit from one of them" that Rabbi Eliezer permits deals in any courtyard, even when there is a law of division, whenever there is need for one of the partners — for with this it is even when there is a law of division like when there is no law of division, for one reason: he has no power to divide and the courtyard is acquired to him for this. It is found the words of the Tur are well aimed: when there is enough for division it is forbidden for one of the market to enter for his own need, for the courtyard is not acquired to him for this and he cannot say "I enter into your fellow's"; for when he enters for his own need each one of them has power to protest from the law upon him — therefore he also has power to forbid upon him, for behold he cannot say "I enter into mine"; unlike when entering for the need of one of the partners — he has no power to protest, therefore he also cannot forbid upon him, for it is like saying "I enter into mine"; therefore when entering for the need of one of the partners and there is a law of division, its law is like there is no law of division and entering for his own need — in both these it is permitted, for he can say "I enter into your fellow's" as we said; and the challenge of Beit Yosef "what is different from entering to visit him" is removed — for here the permission is because he can say "I enter into your fellow's"; but the Rashba certainly holds that also when there is no law of division there is permission only when needed for partners, as is clear in the responsum of the Ramban siman 242, and these are the Rashba's words as Beit Yosef wrote in the introduction of his book; therefore he ruled here properly in Shulchan Aruch that when there is no law of division it is permitted specifically when entering for the need of one of the partners. And still we need to inform regarding this permission that one of the market needs one of the partners — for it appears clear this means he needs one of the members of the courtyard other than the noder; but whoever needs the noder — certainly absolute prohibition; and so is the implication of the Rashba's language that we mentioned, that he wrote "he needs the partner" and meant the second partner; and this is a matter in the Rashba's reason, for the main reason in the Rashba is that the courtyard is acquired to everyone who needs the partner on his account — and this does not apply to the noder, for although it was acquired to him on his account, nevertheless he can forbid his upon his fellow; and I saw Maharshal wrote that if he enters for the need of the madir — it is permitted, for certainly he needs him, etc. — I do not see these words at all, for certainly one who forbids his fellow from his property forbids him even from his needs if he will need him, as is clear in siman 221 seif 4 "my son will not enter at all," etc. — and behold there is no greater need than this; rather specifically from his livelihood we say there he did not forbid him, but not from his other needs; and in the words of the Tur Maharshal also elaborated to explain what he wrote when there is no law of division that it is permitted when he forbade one of the market — namely also specifically when needed for one of the partners; and this is not in the implication of the language at all, for he wrote "the vow did not take effect" — and if per his words the vow did take effect regarding that one of the market is forbidden if he enters not for the need of one of the partners; and even though here in Shulchan Aruch he also wrote "the vow did not take effect" — there is no proof from this, for he was dragged after the Tur's language and added afterward on it; and in my humble opinion it is clear in the words of the Rosh and Tur that I wrote; and in what he wrote initially "when there is a law of division" anonymously for prohibition and it does not help "needed for partners" — one may rely to be lenient regarding needed for one of the partners, since the Rosh and Tur wrote so explicitly. And it is a great wonder on Beit Yosef that he did not see after these words of the Rosh — had he seen them he would not have written that it was switched for our Rabbi between place and place; so it appears in my humble opinion, and so Maharshal ruled for halachah.
