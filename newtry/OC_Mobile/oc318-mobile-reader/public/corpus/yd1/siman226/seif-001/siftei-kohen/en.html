If it is subject to division. This means that each partner can receive an area of four amos by four amos, as stated in Choshen Mishpat 171:3.
The vow takes effect. It is not permitted on the basis of bererah, since the matter can be remedied through division.
That he is forbidden to enter it. This implies that he may not enter even for the needs of one of the partners. This is also implied by the concluding clause, which states that he enters for the partner's need, indicating that in the opening case entry is forbidden under all circumstances. The Atteres Zahav writes likewise. The Tur writes that all the more so, if one partner forbade his property to an outsider, the outsider may not enter for his own needs, though he may enter for the needs of one of the partners. If the courtyard is not subject to division, the vow does not take effect and entry is permitted, whether the partners forbade one another or one partner forbade his property to an outsider. The Beit Yosef questions this distinction, for if the courtyard is subject to division, why should entering for a partner's need make any difference? He writes that Rabbeinu Yerucham also appears to hold this way. The Bach disputes the Beit Yosef, but all his arguments are puzzling to me. His treatment of Rabbeinu Yerucham is especially surprising, for he examined the beginning of Rabbeinu Yerucham's words but not their conclusion. There it is clear that Rabbeinu Yerucham agrees precisely with the Beit Yosef. This is also established by the Rosh, and Maharshal rules like the Mechaber in Perek Shor Shenagach Es HaParah, siman 32. The Rashba and Ran likewise write that Rabbi Eliezer permits an outsider specifically when he enters for the partner's need. It follows that where the courtyard is subject to division, entry is forbidden even for the partner's need, since the Gemara states that in such a courtyard even Rabbi Eliezer agrees. All this is clear, and one must not be lenient or depart from the Mechaber's ruling.
The vow does not take effect. This is because there is bererah. The Rosh and Ran explain in the name of the Rashba that since the entire courtyard is necessarily acquired to each partner for walking through it, one partner has no power to forbid it to the other. Doing so would be like forbidding another person from using that person's own property. This is the meaning of bererah here: whenever a partner enters the courtyard, we view the place on which he treads as acquired to him alone, since the courtyard was originally acquired by them on this condition, as neither can compel the other to divide it. This bererah is part of the original acquisition itself, as though each partner acquired exclusive ownership of the place beneath his feet at the moment he steps there. Maharshal agrees. This also resolves the general rule that bererah is unavailable in Torah law, because the bererah here is unlike ordinary bererah for the reason stated. It appears that any other use, apart from uses such as setting up a mill or oven or raising chickens, is likewise permitted in a courtyard not subject to division. Maharshal writes this as well.
Whether he is needed by the partner, etc. The Bach writes in Choshen Mishpat, siman 180, that the Tur and Rosh imply that entry is permitted only when he enters because the partner needs him. Rabbeinu Yerucham also appears to say this.
A place in the synagogue, etc. This means a large place that is subject to division. If it is not subject to division, the vow does not take effect at all. The Atteres Zahav writes this, and it is also stated in the responsum of the Rashba cited there.
He intended only, etc. Nevertheless, if he now explains what he intended, whether sacred property for the poor or sacred property for Temple maintenance, the matter follows his explanation, as stated above at the beginning of siman 208. This is from the Rashba there. See also the beginning of siman 258.
