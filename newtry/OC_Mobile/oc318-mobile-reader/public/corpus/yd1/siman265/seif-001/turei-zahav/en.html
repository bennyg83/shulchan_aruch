Between cutting the foreskin, etc. The Tur writes that the Rashbam introduced the practice of reciting the blessing, “to bring him into the covenant,” specifically before the circumcision, because the phrase “to bring him” indicates the future. Furthermore, blessings over mitzvos must be recited before their performance. Rabbeinu Tam wrote that the existing custom need not be changed. The Rosh explains his reasoning: The requirement to bless before performing the mitzvah applies specifically when the person performing the mitzvah recites the blessing. Here, however, the circumcision is performed by another person. Rav Sherira Shalom likewise follows Rabbeinu Tam, but was even more stringent and said that it is forbidden to bless until after the circumcision, since it is performed by another. The Beit Yosef wrote that Rabbeinu Tam also accepts Rav Sherira Shalom's stringency, for Tosafos and the Rosh wrote in the chapter R' Eliezer DeMilah that Rabbeinu Tam restored the former custom of blessing after the circumcision. It appears to me that the opposite is proven from there. They wrote that the original custom was to bless after the circumcision, and that the Rashbam later changed it. Rabbeinu Tam objected and said that the custom should not be changed. All his proofs there are directed toward justifying the established custom. This implies that were it not for the change of custom, he would not have objected. The Rosh concludes as written here, that since the blessing is recited before periah, it is also considered before the mitzvah's completion, for one who circumcised but did not perform periah is considered as though he did not circumcise. It appears that this procedure satisfies both opinions when it is possible. Where it is impossible, the blessing should certainly be recited before the performance, as I wrote in siman 1, seif 7. The Rosh's wording also implies this, for he writes that this is called before the performance, indicating that even when the mitzvah is performed by another, one should bless beforehand whenever possible. It appears to me that a father who personally circumcises his son should follow the Rashbam and first recite both blessings, “concerning circumcision” and “to bring him into the covenant,” and then cut. He cannot recite the second blessing between cutting and periah, for he is occupied in the midst of performing the mitzvah. How could he interrupt then with the blessing? Furthermore, pausing between cutting and periah causes pain to the child, the wound fills with blood, and afterward periah becomes difficult. It therefore appears that in this case the blessing certainly should not be recited between cutting and periah. One might suggest reciting “to bring him into the covenant” after periah, but even according to Rabbeinu Tam and Rav Sherira Shalom that is forbidden when the father himself performs the mitzvah, for then the blessing must certainly precede its performance. He should therefore recite both blessings before cutting. This is how I personally acted when I circumcised my son, may he live, and it accords with all opinions. I afterward found that Maharil also ruled this way.
