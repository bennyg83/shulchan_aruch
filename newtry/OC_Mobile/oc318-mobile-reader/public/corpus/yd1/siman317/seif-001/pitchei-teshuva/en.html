And it became mixed. [See the responsum Chatam Sofer siman 307 regarding a firstborn that became mixed up. The case was: a Jew made a mark on the firstborn with its wool so it would not become mixed, and handed it to a non-Jewish shepherd within his flock, informing him that this was a consecrated animal forbidden to be shorn. When the time came to shear his flock, the shepherd sheared all the animals including the firstborn, and thereby the distinguishing mark was gone and the firstborn became mixed among the entire flock. Furthermore, the shepherd castrated many of the flock, and it is doubtful whether the firstborn is among those castrated or among the unblemished ones. Now there are two issues to discuss: First, regarding the unblemished ones — they are all in a state of doubtful firstborn, and all must graze until they become blemished. As for whether to permit via separation (perish), the dispute of Rabbeinu Tam and Ri is already known (see above siman 110 se'if 6 in Taz and Shakh), and here where we are dealing with consecrated animals all opinions are equal — separation does not help, for we decree lest one take from the fixed group. Second, there is discussion regarding those castrated by the shepherd. This act is sufficient to permit the firstborn to be eaten with its blemish (see Shakh siman 313 se'if katan 6). Now that it is a doubtful firstborn, all of them may be eaten by Israelites — for we say to the kohen: bring proof that this is a firstborn, except for one of them which is given to the kohen so as not to steal from the tribe. However, regarding shearing and working — since all of them bear the doubt of firstborn, they are forever forbidden for shearing and working, etc. He concludes: since we are not certain that a firstborn became mixed into the flock — for the Jew did not see this and only the shepherd says so, and his words are nothing but astonishing, to say he changed what the master had commanded him; and furthermore it is known that a non-Jew is not believed either to permit or to forbid (see Shakh siman 127 se'if katan 20) — and even though we see that the number of shorn animals is as it was with the firstborn, nevertheless perhaps another animal replaced it, and even a double doubt does not apply here — to say of each one: perhaps no firstborn became mixed at all, and even if it did become mixed, perhaps this is not it — for it cannot switch; and here there is no need first to determine whether a firstborn was present, since a firstborn certainly was there, but at any rate it does not emerge from doubt. Therefore one may find a leniency for those that were blemished to permit them for shearing and working, in one of two ways. First way: give one of them to the kohen as is proper, and the kohen slaughters and eats it — for he is commanded to eat it and hastening a mitzvah is preferable. Once this one is slaughtered, all the rest become permitted for shearing and working, as per above siman 110 se'if 7 where one of a group is eaten. Here he is not acting explicitly to nullify them — the kohen is simply eating his own as his mitzvah requires — except that according to this the remaining ones may only be shorn two at a time, not one at a time, as stated there. Second way: rely on the Rema there se'if 6 to separate them all together so that the fixed ones are no longer in their place, whereupon it is permitted even if they were separated before us, and one may take the separated ones and they are permitted for shearing even one at a time, even if they returned afterward to their fixed place, as the Shakh writes there — except the last two would remain forbidden. To this may be added the leniency above: slaughter one of the separated ones, and then we say the one slaughtered was the firstborn, thereby permitting even the last two. And regarding the unblemished remaining ones there is also leniency to be found — not only if they are a minority and the majority were castrated can we say the prohibition was among the majority castrated and all the unblemished are permitted; but also particularly since they were castrated before the mixture became known, they are like "separated before the doubt arose," and we do not decree lest one take from the fixed group. Even if they are a majority or half, which does not apply the leniency mentioned above — nonetheless if they are all uprooted from their fixed place one may permit them by the above method as for the blemished ones, etc. Furthermore, from the best, if possible, after they have been separated one by one, it is good to arrange a conspicuous blemish via a non-Jew and they may then be eaten with their blemish. What constitutes a conspicuous blemish — see above siman 309. Every blemish must be seen by three members of the community and not [ruled upon] by one individual alone, as stated there. And what he wrote further regarding the wool that became mixed — I wrote about that above siman 308 se'if 8.]
