And it became mixed. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 307, concerning a firstborn that became mixed with a flock. The Jew had placed a mark in the animal's wool to prevent confusion and entrusted it to a non-Jewish shepherd among the flock, informing him that this was a consecrated animal forbidden in shearing. When the time for shearing arrived, the shepherd sheared the entire flock, including the firstborn, so its distinguishing mark was lost and it became mixed among the whole flock. In addition, the shepherd castrated many animals, and it was uncertain whether the firstborn was among the castrated or the intact animals. There are two issues. First, all the intact animals are doubtful unblemished firstborns and should graze until they develop blemishes. The known dispute between Rabbeinu Tam and Rabbeinu Yitzchak about permitting an item that separated from a fixed mixture does not help here, because when consecrated animals are involved all agree that separation of one does not help, lest one take directly from the fixed mixture. Second, one must consider the animals castrated by the shepherd. Such an act is sufficient to permit a firstborn for eating once blemished. Since here each animal is only a doubtful firstborn, all may be eaten by an Israelite, because the kohen must prove that a particular animal is a firstborn, except that one animal must be given to a kohen because of the tribe's monetary claim. Yet regarding shearing and work, since each remains a possible firstborn, all would seemingly remain prohibited forever. The Chatam Sofer ultimately rules leniently because it was never established with certainty that the firstborn became mixed into the flock. The Jew did not see this; only the shepherd said so, and the shepherd's story itself is surprising, since it assumes he violated the owner's explicit instructions. In any event a non-Jew is not believed either to permit or to prohibit, as Shach writes in siman 127, seif katan 20. Although the number of shorn animals appears to match the number of animals including the firstborn, perhaps another animal was substituted. This does not create a full double doubt for each animal, because the doubts cannot be reversed in order and we already know that a firstborn existed somewhere. Nevertheless, the case remains doubtful. He therefore proposes two ways to permit the blemished animals for shearing and work. First, one animal should be given to a kohen as required. The kohen should slaughter and eat it, since eating the firstborn is his mitzvah and performing the mitzvah promptly is preferable. Once that animal is eaten, all the others become permitted for shearing and work, like the rule in siman 110, seif 7, where one item from the mixture was eaten. Here the act is not done explicitly in order to nullify the prohibition, because the kohen is simply eating his own animal as a mitzvah. According to this route, however, the remaining animals should be shorn only two at a time, not singly, as explained there. Second, one may rely on the Rama in siman 110, seif 6, by moving all the animals together so that none remains in its original fixed location. Then even separation in our presence is effective. The separated animals may be taken and shorn individually, even if they later return to a fixed group, as the Shach explains there, though the final two would remain prohibited. To solve that problem, one of the separated animals can be slaughtered, and we may say that the slaughtered one was the firstborn, thereby permitting the final two as well. A leniency can also be found for the remaining intact animals. If they are a minority and most of the flock was castrated, one may say the prohibition was among the majority that were castrated and the intact animals are permitted. This is especially so because the castration occurred before the mixture became known, which is like separation before the doubt arose, where the decree against taking from the fixed mixture does not apply. Even if the intact animals are the majority or exactly half, so that this argument is unavailable, if all are removed from their fixed location one may permit them by the same method described for the blemished animals. As an added precaution, if possible, after they separate one by one it is proper to have a non-Jew cause an unmistakable blemish in them; they may then be eaten because of that blemish. For the definition of an unmistakable blemish see siman 309. Every blemish must be inspected by three members of the congregation and may not be permitted by one person alone. See there.
