For he is forbidden from benefit of his father-in-law. One might ask: since the son-in-law is forbidden to derive benefit from his father-in-law because [the father-in-law] declared a vow against him — why should it not be permitted for [the father-in-law] to give a gift to his daughter outright, since certainly the son-in-law would derive no benefit from that gift, as he would surely not violate the prohibition? The answer is: since the son-in-law in any case acquires [rights in] the gift through his marriage to his wife, it is as though he received it from his father-in-law and then gives it back to his wife — and for this reason it is clear that even if the son-in-law also declared himself vowed from his father-in-law's property, it would not help for the father-in-law to give a gift to his wife: for what is the difference between the son-in-law declaring himself vowed against benefit from that gift versus his father-in-law declaring a vow against him? In both cases it is forbidden to him; and moreover, with his own vow he could release it and then wish to benefit — so what he accomplished by declaring himself vowed amounts to nothing, as he returns to the prohibition by virtue of his father-in-law's vow. And in Perisha it is written: "it seems certainly that if the husband declared himself vowed against benefit from those fruits, the father-in-law is permitted to give to his daughter — but our master does not deal with this case" — end of quote. But he was not precise in this ruling at all — it is an outright prohibition as long as [the father-in-law] does not say "on condition that your husband has no rights over them, but you shall do such-and-such with it"; and this is what the minority opinion here says, which is stringent.
