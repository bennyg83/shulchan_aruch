And he did not specify that the gift, etc. This is Rambam's view. Only when the giver says, 'and do with them whatever you wish,' does he reveal that even to her he did not give them absolutely, such that the husband would immediately acquire them. Rather, it is as though he said: they are yours for whatever particular use you choose at any time, which is like saying that she may use them for one specified matter. But if he says only, 'on condition that your husband has no rights in them,' without any further specification, it is ineffective, because once she acquires the gift for every purpose the husband also acquires rights in it. This follows Shmuel in the Gemara. The dissenting view is that of the Tur, who rules like Rav. Rav holds that specifically when the giver states that she is to use it for a particular matter does she fail to acquire it absolutely and acquires it only for that purpose; therefore her husband does not acquire it either, and the stipulation that her husband has no rights in it is effective. It is obvious that if the giver did not say, 'on condition that your husband has no rights in it,' but merely said, 'I give this to you as a gift so that you use it for a particular matter,' it is forbidden. This follows a fortiori: if saying explicitly that the husband has no rights is insufficient by itself, all the more so where he does not mention the husband at all, the husband certainly acquires rights when she accepts the gift. The Mishnah and the poskim explicitly state that both formulations are required. The Derisha writes that the law in this case requires study, but I do not know what doubt there could be.
