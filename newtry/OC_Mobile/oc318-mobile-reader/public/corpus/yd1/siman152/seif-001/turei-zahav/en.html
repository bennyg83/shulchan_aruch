Even if he eats of his own. As it says "and he will call you and you will eat of his sacrifice" — shall we say only when he eats of his sacrifice? Rava said: if so, let the verse say "and you will eat of his sacrifice" — what is "and he will call you"? From the time of the invitation. Rashi: from the time you are invited to him I attribute your eating as though it is his sacrifice. It appears this invitation that the Torah forbade means principally that a Jew's eating should not be through the gentiles' invitation; and presumably when he invites one he invites him to his house — therefore they permitted when he sends to a Jew's house as written afterward. According to this it appears there is also prohibition in a Jew's house — when he sends to many some food or drink that they gather and drink or eat there, nevertheless through his invitation they eat there and it is like a gentile's house. They did not permit when sending to a Jew's house unless he does not say they should gather together in this meal; but if he mentions they should gather together it is an actual invitation and there is prohibition if they gather together — rather each takes his portion home. So it appears to me.
Unless he is an important person. From this wording it implies that what an important person must beware is from piety; so Beit Yosef wrote that therefore the Rambam and Rif omitted what the Gemara says "Rav Yitzchak is different as an important person" — since it is a matter of piety they did not mention it. I am astonished — for in many places they mentioned this distinction that an important person has a different law; one who is precise in the Gemara's language will see they did not touch on piety here — for there it challenges: and after twelve months it is permitted, but Rav Yitzchak visited that gentile after twelve months and we heard he confessed, and he explained he did not eat — "Rav Yitzchak is different as an important person." Rashi: the gentile rejoiced in him — it implies the matter does not depend on piety but is an absolute law: whoever knows the gentile rejoices in him — and this is presumably an important person. This is proven in the Gemara's language, which does not say "important person is different" as the Talmud says in chapter 25 (48b) regarding passing under an asherah, and similarly in chapter "What is neshech" (Bava Metzia 73a) this wording appears — and that is where it is a matter of piety; not so here, where it is because the gentile rejoices in him since he is an important person — therefore it said Rav Yitzchak is different as an important person and there is joy in him. That the Rif and Rambam omitted is for another reason: since we see in Rav Yitzchak that before he heard from the gentile that he confessed to his idol he ate and did not suspect he might confess until he heard clearly he confessed — therefore the Talmud said Rav Yitzchak is different as an important person and an impure incident occurred that he confessed; but presumably we are not concerned even for an important person, as Rav Yitzchak was not concerned after twelve months. So appears the view of the Rif and Rambam. But the Tur wrote in truth: whoever knows the gentile rejoices in him is forbidden by law, and each person follows assessment of intent in this; in any case Beit Yosef's words are not settled in what he tied the matter to piety — for necessarily according to the Rif and Rambam we are not concerned for this at all even from piety, and according to the Tur one must be concerned from the law. So it appears to me.
