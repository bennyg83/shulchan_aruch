It is forbidden for a Jew to eat there. In the Derishah, he was uncertain whether there is room for leniency here because of enmity, as in siman 148, where they said that one may rejoice with them because it is as though he is exchanging with them. I am astonished that a great man such as he should have been uncertain about this, for it is explicit in the verse from which they derived this law: "And he will invite you, and you will eat from his sacrifice." At the beginning of that very verse it is explicit: "Lest you make a covenant with the inhabitants of the land, and they stray after their daughters." Thus, the Torah commanded that there be enmity between us and them in order to distance us from their daughters. How, then, could we permit this because of enmity? We find a leniency because of enmity only where the mitzvah is for some other reason, as above in siman 148, where because of the prohibition of idolatry it is proper to distance oneself from them in certain matters, and the Sages permitted some matters because of enmity. This is not so here, where the very essence of the mitzvah prohibiting the meal is so that we should not have affection for them. How could we nullify that very purpose because of enmity? The same is true even regarding a rabbinic enactment, such as the prohibition against eating food cooked by non-Jews, where the purpose is that we should not become close to them because of their daughters. It is obvious that there is no leniency whatsoever because of enmity. All this is clear in my eyes.
Even if he eats food of his own. For it is stated, "And he will invite you, and you will eat from his sacrifice." Perhaps this applies only when he actually eats from his sacrifice? Rava said: If so, the verse should have said, "And you will eat from his sacrifice." What is taught by the words, "And he will invite you"? The prohibition applies from the time of the invitation. Rashi explains: From the time that you are invited by him, I regard your eating as though it were from his sacrifice. It appears that the primary meaning of the invitation forbidden by the Torah is that the Jew's eating should not occur by virtue of the non-Jew's invitation. Presumably, when a person invites someone, he invites him to his own home. Therefore, they permitted it when the non-Jew sends the food to the Jew's home, as stated afterward. Based on this, it appears to me that there can be a prohibition even in a Jew's home, namely, when the non-Jew sends food or drink to many people so that they should gather and eat or drink together. After all, they are eating there because of his invitation, and it is considered like the home of a non-Jew. They permitted sending food to a Jew's home only where he does not tell them to gather together for this meal. But if he specifically mentions that they should gather together, this is an actual invitation, and it is forbidden for them to assemble together. Rather, each person should take his portion home. This appears correct in my humble opinion.
Unless he is an important person. This wording implies that the caution required of an important person is a matter of piety. The Beit Yosef likewise wrote that this is why the Rambam and the Rif omitted the Gemara's statement, "Rav Yitzchak is different, for he is an important person." Since this is merely a pious practice, they did not mention it. I am astonished, for in many places they mention this distinction, that an important person is subject to a different law. One who examines the language of the Gemara carefully will see that this is not treated here as a matter of piety. The Gemara asks there: Is it permitted after twelve months? Rav Yitzchak once visited the home of a certain non-Jew after twelve months, heard that the non-Jew had offered thanks to his idol, withdrew, and did not eat. The Gemara answers: Rav Yitzchak is different, for he was an important person. Rashi explains that the non-Jew rejoiced over him. This implies that the matter does not depend upon piety, but is an absolute law whenever one knows that the non-Jew rejoices over him, which is presumably the case with an important person. This is also demonstrated by the Gemara's wording. It did not say, "An important person is different," as the Talmud says in chapter 25, folio 48, regarding one who passed beneath an asherah, and likewise in the chapter Eizehu Neshech, Bava Metzia 73. That wording is used where the matter is one of piety. Here, however, the reason is that the non-Jew rejoices over him because he is an important person. Therefore, the Gemara said specifically, "Rav Yitzchak is different, for he is an important person," meaning that the non-Jew rejoiced over him. The Rif and the Rambam omitted this law for a different reason. We see that before Rav Yitzchak heard from the non-Jew that he had offered thanks to his idol, he ate and did not suspect that the non-Jew might do so. Only after he clearly heard that the non-Jew had offered thanks did he refrain from eating. The Gemara therefore said that Rav Yitzchak was different because he was an important person, and on account of this an improper incident occurred, namely, that the non-Jew offered thanks. Ordinarily, however, we are not concerned for this possibility even with an important person, just as Rav Yitzchak himself was not concerned after twelve months. This appears to me to be the view of the Rif and the Rambam. The Tur, however, indeed wrote that whenever a person knows that the non-Jew rejoices over him, it is forbidden as a matter of law, and each person must assess the circumstances. In any event, the words of the Beit Yosef, who made the matter dependent upon piety, are not resolved. Whichever way one looks at it, his explanation cannot stand. According to the Rif and the Rambam, there is no concern at all, even as a matter of piety; according to the Tur, one must be concerned as a matter of law. This appears correct in my humble opinion.
