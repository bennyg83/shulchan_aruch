Israel should not eat there. For the sake of Hashem’s people, Hashem’s promise is that Hashem’s people will be blessed with Him because He is a gift of His people, and that He will be blessed with Him, and that He will be blessed with Him, and that He will be blessed with Him, because He will bless us, because He will bless us, and that He will bless us, because He will bless us And this is self-evident: we do not find permission because of enmity except where the mitzvah is because of something else — such as above siman 148, where the prohibition is because of idolatry and they permitted certain things because of enmity with them; not so here, where the principal mitzvah of the prohibition of eating itself is so that we should have no love with them — how can we nullify this very thing because of enmity? And so even regarding a rabbinic prohibition, such as not eating the cooking of gentiles — where the matter is so that we have no proximity with them because of their daughters — it is obvious there is no permission because of enmity at all. All this is self-evident to me.
Even if he eats from his own. For it says "and he called to you and you ate of his sacrifice" — one might say: perhaps only when he eats of his sacrifice; Rava says: if so, the verse should have said "and you ate of his sacrifice"; what does "and he called to you" teach us? From the moment he calls you. Rashi explains: from the moment you are called by him, I account your eating as though it were of his sacrifice. And it seems to me that the essential meaning of this "calling" that the Torah forbids is that the Jewish person's eating not come about through the gentile's invitation — for when he invites someone he invites him to his home; and therefore they permitted when he sends the food to the Jew's home, as stated afterward. And based on this it seems to me that even in the Jew's home there is a prohibition — namely when a gentile sends food or drink to many people to gather and eat or drink together, for in any case they eat through his invitation, and it resembles the gentile's home. And they only permitted when he sends to a Jew's home if he does not say that they should gather together in that eating; but if he mentions that they should gather together, this constitutes an actual calling, and it is forbidden if they gather together — rather each should take his portion to his own home. So it seems to me in this matter. Unless he is a person of importance. From this language it implies that what a person of importance should be careful about is on account of his piety; and so Beit Yosef wrote — that therefore Rambam and Rif omitted what the Talmud says: "R' Yitzchak is different, for he is a person of importance" — for since it is merely a matter of piety they did not mention it; and I wonder — for in many places they mentioned this distinction that a person of importance has a different law; and one who examines the Talmud's language carefully will see that it is not treated as a matter of piety here: for the Talmud challenges there — "and after twelve months it is permitted, and yet R' Yitzchak went to the home of that gentile after twelve months and we heard that the gentile gave thanks and R' Yitzchak withdrew and did not eat — he is different, for he is a person of importance"; and Rashi explains that the gentile was glad to have him — implying the matter does not depend on piety but is an actual law for anyone where it is known that the gentile rejoices in him, and this is naturally the case with a person of importance. And this is proven from the Talmud's language, which did not say "a person of importance is different" as the Talmud does elsewhere in chapter Kol HaTzelamim (48a) and in chapter Eizehu Neshekh (Bava Metzia 73) — in those cases it is a matter of piety; but here, since it is due to the gentile's rejoicing in him because he is a person of importance, therefore it says "R' Yitzchak is different, for he is a person of importance" and the gentile has cause for rejoicing. And the reason Rif and Rambam omitted it is for a different reason — since we see that R' Yitzchak, before he heard from the gentile that he had given thanks to his idol, ate and was not concerned that perhaps he would give thanks — until he heard with certainty that he had given thanks; therefore the Talmud says: R' Yitzchak is different, for he was a person of importance and encountered this troublesome incident of giving thanks through it; but ordinarily we do not suspect this even with a person of importance, just as R' Yitzchak himself was not concerned after twelve months. This, it seems to me, is the view of Rif and Rambam. But the Tur indeed wrote: in truth, whoever knows that the gentile rejoices in him is forbidden by the law, and each person should follow his own judgment in this matter. In any case Beit Yosef's attribution of the matter to piety is not settled — for either way it is not so: for Rif and Rambam, we are not concerned about this at all even as piety; and for the Tur, one is concerned about this by the law. So it seems to me.
