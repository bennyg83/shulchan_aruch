And he completes intercourse. This means while the organ is still erect. Thus wrote Tur. This applies even though she saw blood during that act of intercourse.
Her time to menstruate arrived, but she had not menstruated. Tur writes here, "Even if she is a bogeret whose hymenal blood has ceased," etc. Beit Yosef asks why Tur says "even if she is a bogeret." On the contrary, with a bogeret there is greater reason to attribute the blood to menstruation, since she no longer has hymenal blood. This presents no difficulty. Tur intends to teach us two novel rulings. First, even if the blood is certainly hymenal blood rather than menstrual blood, such as when her time to menstruate has not arrived and she has never menstruated, we nevertheless rule stringently lest people confuse one type of blood with another. Tur then teaches that one should not say: it is appropriate to rule stringently only where it is clear that she saw hymenal blood, lest people confuse the types of blood. If it is not clear that she saw any blood, even hymenal blood, why rule stringently and require him to separate immediately, when it is not yet known that she saw blood? Accordingly, when she is a bogeret whose hymenal blood has ceased, perhaps he should not separate. Tur therefore teaches that he must nevertheless rule stringently, since she may have seen blood without his knowledge. He then adds that even if it is certain that she did not see blood, because she examined herself, we remain concerned that semen may have covered it. These points are clear. Contrary to my revered father-in-law's statement, the lines have not been transposed; the text is correct and in its proper order.
And semen covered it. Why is this explanation needed here, whereas at the beginning of siman 193, concerning blood caused by desire, the explanation is simply that she did not sense it? One may answer that there she walks from place to place, so there is certainly no particular location where one could search to determine whether blood emerged from her. Here, however, she lies in bed, and one can check whether blood is present on the sheet beneath her. It was therefore necessary to explain that the blood may have been covered by semen. Perishah strained unnecessarily to resolve this question.
She should not begin counting until the fifth day, etc. I found it stated in the name of Maharal of Prague that although the prevalent custom is now for a menstruant to begin counting on the sixth day, as explained in siman 196, nevertheless, when she begins counting after her first intercourse involving hymenal blood, she begins counting on the fifth day. He issued this as a practical ruling. See what I wrote concerning this in siman 196, se'if 11.
