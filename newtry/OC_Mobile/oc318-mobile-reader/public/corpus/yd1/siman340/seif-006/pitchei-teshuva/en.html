An upright person. See Tiferet LeMoshe, who writes that it requires study whether the same law applies to an upright woman, since this rule is not dependent upon Torah scholarship, or perhaps the person must be a man of Torah, although not a great scholar, which cannot apply to a woman; see there.
And Torah scholars are exempt. See Shach, seif katan 13, who writes that the words of Raavad and Rosh appear to indicate that even Torah scholars are obligated. See Agudat Ezov, kuntres Alon Bachut, discourse 1, who challenges this and writes that the exemption of Torah scholars from this keriah was said only regarding an upright person who is not as learned as they are, because it is comparable to the rule of an elder for whom such an act is beneath his dignity. The law of keriah here follows the law of standing and showing honor, where a greater person does not stand before a lesser one. If, however, the deceased was a scholar of equal stature, Torah scholars too are obligated. He brings proof that no one can disagree and say that a Torah scholar must rend for an upright person of lesser stature, from Megillah 28a concerning one who taught halachot, see there. In my humble opinion the proof can be rejected. See Rashi there, Sotah 22b, and Tosafot Gittin 31b, and consider carefully.
