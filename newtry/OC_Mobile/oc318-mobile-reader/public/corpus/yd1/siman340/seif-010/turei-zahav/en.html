The Semak likewise rules that one does not rend the outer cloak called a grink. This is the basis for the Rama’s statement that the outer overcoat is not customarily rent, meaning the garment called a rok in German. Masat Binyamin argues that the rok requires rending because it is a full garment, and that the Rama exempted only the garment called a shul-mantel, worn by some elders in Krakow for dignity in the synagogue. He provides no proof. His words are highly astonishing. A shul-mantel is not worn to a cemetery, so how could the Rama allude to a garment not ordinarily present at the time of mourning? The Agudah states explicitly in Moed Katan that some explain the exempt garment as the kapa in France or an overcoat, because it is made for modesty and is not worn while sitting at home. The rok likewise is not ordinarily worn at home. Masat Binyamin cites this as support but says the Agudah could not have meant the rok because that garment exists only in our region. This is no argument, since garments serving the same function have the same law. Both are outer garments not generally worn at home. Occasional exceptions are disregarded. Moreover, one should not even be stringent to rend the rok, because such stringency may produce leniency. For relatives other than parents, only the outer garment must be rent, and it is certainly improper to satisfy the obligation by rending the rok when that garment is exempt. The Beit Yosef cites Hagahot Maimoniyot as raising the same concern regarding the maktorin, which corresponds to our rok. It is therefore clear that the Rama’s exempt outer overcoat is our rok. Although it is an important garment, the law depends not upon importance but upon whether it is ordinarily worn at all times.
