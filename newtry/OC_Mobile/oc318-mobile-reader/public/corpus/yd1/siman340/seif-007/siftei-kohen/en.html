Until they reveal their heart. According to the "some say" in seif 8, all the more so here he tears only a tefach. In siman 242 the Beit Yosef resolved what the Rambam wrote that for one's teacher he tears until he reveals his heart, but what he wrote so regarding a Torah scholar he left requiring study; see there.
And some say. In the Shulchan Aruch it is marked, "so it appears in the Tur," but this is not so. On the contrary, the Tur implies the opposite. Rather, this is the view of the Maharam brought by the Rosh, and Hagahot Maimoniyot in the name of "some say." He brought proof from the Talmud: when Rav Safra died, the rabbis did not tear for him, saying, "we did not learn from him." Abaye said to them: is it taught, "when one's teacher dies"? It is taught, "when a sage dies." And furthermore, his teachings are in our mouths every day in the study hall. The Rosh wrote: this does not appear correct; rather this is the explanation: furthermore, even if it taught "when one's teacher dies," his teachings are in our mouths every day, so he is our teacher. The Beit Yosef wrote that the Rosh's words seem clear in their reasoning and one should not move from them. To me, the Maharam's words are clear in their reasoning. According to the Rosh's explanation, it is difficult: did the rabbis not know that his teachings were in their mouths every day? Rather, they held that this is not called one's teacher, and Abaye would then be saying that it is one's teacher, but the essential point is missing from the text; it should have said: furthermore, since his teachings are in our mouths, he is our teacher. Also, how did Abaye know this? Perhaps the rabbis held that having his teachings in their mouths does not make him their teacher. Moreover, why say "furthermore," since Rav Safra was at least a sage? Rather, the explanation is: is it taught "when one's teacher dies"? It is taught "when a sage dies," meaning one whom we know from his teachings that he innovated. Furthermore, his teachings are in our mouths all day, so we know from his teachings that he innovated. This is clear.
And so the custom is to be lenient. The Bach wrote that this implies he holds that even another sage who is not equal to the deceased sage but lesser than him does not tear. In my humble opinion, no one holds this, for the baraita explicitly says: when a sage dies, all are like his relatives and all tear for him; and as Abaye said in the Talmud, is it taught "when one's teacher dies"? It is taught "when a sage dies," even if he is not his teacher. The Rosh inferred this from Abaye's statement, and it is explicit in the Beit Yosef. These are his words. I have not understood his final intent, for the Maharam indeed says this and explains that "when a sage dies" means one known from his teachings to have innovated. The Rav's words follow this, and I have already written nearby that the Maharam's words are clear. Therefore I am astonished that the custom is not to tear even for a sage known from his teachings to have innovated, even for one greater than oneself, and they have no basis to rely upon. Perhaps they hold that one is not called a Torah scholar unless he knows a halachic matter everywhere, even in tractate Kallah, as stated in the Talmud; and the Agudah already wrote in the first chapter of Chullin that now, because of our sins, there is no Torah scholar, for one does not know even tractate Kallah. Mahari Weil and Mahari Weil bring this in their responsa, as I wrote above in siman 18 s.k. 29, and this requires study.
