They are permitted, etc. For from that which is written "when a man vows a vow to Hashem" we derive "until he vows with something vowed" — and this is the wording of the Rambam at the beginning of (chapter 3) [chapter 1] of Hilchot Nedarim: A vow is divided into two parts. The first part is that one forbids upon himself permitted things — for example if he says "the fruits of such-and-such province are forbidden upon me" for thirty days or forever, or "such-and-such species from the fruits of the world" or "these fruits are forbidden upon me" — in any language of prohibition he is forbidden in them, even though there is no oath at all and no mention of the Name or a substitute. And regarding this it is said in the Torah "to prohibit a prohibition upon his soul" — to forbid upon himself permitted things. And likewise if he said "behold they are upon me as a prohibition," behold they are forbidden. And this part is what I call nedarim of prohibition. The second part is that one obligates himself in a korban that he is not obligated in — for example if he says "behold upon me to bring a burnt offering," etc. One who says "these fruits upon me as a korban" or who said "behold they are like a korban," or who said to his fellow "whatever I eat with you — upon me as a korban or like a korban" or "behold they are upon me as a korban" — behold these are forbidden to him, because it is possible that a person may vow a korban and make an animal that was secular into a korban and it becomes forbidden. But if he said "fruits, etc., like pig meat," etc. — behold these are permitted and there is no vow here, for it is impossible to make something that is not pig meat like pig meat. This is the principle: anything that, because of permitted things like forbidden things — if that forbidden thing one could make by vow, behold these are forbidden; and if one cannot make it by vow, behold these are permitted, end quote.
