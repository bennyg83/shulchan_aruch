They are permitted. All these items are forbidden intrinsically. It is therefore impossible to say that a permitted object should become "like this," because the permitted object cannot itself become intrinsically forbidden in the same manner. Taz. Rambam gives this rule: whenever one compares permitted objects to prohibited objects, if the prohibited object could itself have been created through a vow, the permitted object becomes forbidden; if it could not have been made prohibited through a vow, the permitted objects remain permitted.
"An opening." Ran writes that we do not open for him on the basis of his mother's honor, by saying, "Had you known that this would dishonor your mother, would you have vowed?" One might have thought that because the prohibition is only rabbinic, such an opening could be used, so he teaches otherwise. Beit Yosef cites a responsum of Rashbatz concerning one who was compelled to prohibit a certain woman to himself and associated the vow with an intrinsically prohibited object: it is possible that in a case of duress the Sages did not impose the stringency of requiring release.
"Regarding other vows." Bach rules that nowadays everyone is treated as a layman even regarding other vows. The Rama's words appear primary. Shach.
