One who deliberately takes his own life. See Responsa Even Shoham, siman 44, concerning a man who followed an upright path and afflicted himself with fasting and immersion, and later apparently took his own life. On one day he went to immerse and told the woman who lived there, "If they look for me, they will find me at the immersion house." He remained there for a time and returned home. The next day they found him in his house lying in blood with a knife in his hand and his throat cut. Several days earlier he had sharpened the knife and said that he intended to make it into a shechitah knife. On the wall was found a note in his handwriting asking his students to recite Kaddish after him. The question was whether he was deemed one who deliberately took his own life. The responsum rules that no mourning practice should be withheld from him. First, this is not classified as deliberate suicide because no one saw him kill himself, as stated in Baer Heitev, seif katan 2, and we do not rely here upon circumstantial indications. His instruction that Kaddish be said can be explained as stemming from weakness and fear that he might die. Furthermore, perhaps a disturbed spirit confused him and he acted while insane; the halachic category applies only where one acts with a clear mind. It is also possible that he did so out of a mistaken notion of repentance. See there for further reasons. Chatam Sofer, siman 326, discusses at length the wording of the baraita in the second chapter of Semachot. He concludes that even if one says, "I will go up to the roof and throw myself down," and we see him go up, but later find him below dead, we assume it is possible that he fell for another reason. If, however, we actually saw him fall, then even if we cannot prove that he cast himself down, since we saw him fall from the very height from which he said he would throw himself, there is a presumption that he acted intentionally. But if the fall itself was not seen, even though he was found dead in that place, it is obvious that he is not classified as a deliberate suicide. Accordingly, Maharshal's statement, cited in Baer Heitev, seif katan 2, that if they did not see him go up, is unnecessary, for even if they saw him go up, he is not so classified unless they saw him fall. Chatam Sofer further explains that the Shulchan Aruch's case, "if they saw him strangled," means circumstances from which it appears certain that he himself performed the act, such as finding him hanging in a room locked from within, or lying fallen upon his own sword in circumstances indicating self-infliction. Even then, although it is certain that he killed himself, we assume that he may not have acted with settled intent, but that a disturbed spirit terrified him, or he was frightened by some matter, or there was another sudden cause, unless he was heard to say with a settled mind that he intended to die because of anger or the like. Yet even without an explicit statement, if his actions, anger, anguish, and preparations clearly showed that he was preparing for death, and afterward he is found strangled in circumstances indicating that he did it himself, that is a powerful and evident presumption equivalent to an explicit statement. See his proof from Rosh, end of the chapter Eilu Metziot, siman 141. See also Responsa Besamim Rosh, siman 345, which states that one who was destitute, went before two witnesses, said, "I am weary of my life," and killed himself, is not classified as a deliberate suicide if he acted because of overwhelming troubles, worries, suffering, or extreme poverty. Har Avel, branch 11, questions this ruling and writes that it does not bear the authentic seal of the Rosh. Chatam Sofer likewise writes that the case of Rabbi Chanina ben Teradyon in the first chapter of Avodah Zarah proves that even one in extreme distress still falls under the category of deliberate suicide, and on this basis rejects Besamim Rosh, siman 345. See also Responsa Beit Ephraim, Yoreh Deah, siman 76.
