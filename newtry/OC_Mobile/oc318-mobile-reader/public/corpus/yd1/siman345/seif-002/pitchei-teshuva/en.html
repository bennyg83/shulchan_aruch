One who took his own life. See Responsa Even Shoham, siman 44, concerning a man who followed a righteous path and afflicted himself through fasting and immersion, but later apparently took his own life. One day he went to immerse and told the woman who lived there, 'If they seek me, they will find me in the bathhouse.' He remained there for a time and returned home. The next day he was found in his house lying covered in blood, with a knife in his hand, having cut himself during twilight. Several days earlier he had sharpened the knife and said that he intended to make it into a slaughtering knife. On the wall was found a handwritten instruction that his students should recite Kaddish for him. He was asked whether this person is deemed one who took his own life, and he ruled that no funeral honor should be withheld. First, he is not deemed one who took his own life because no one saw him do so, and in this matter circumstantial evidence is insufficient. His instruction that Kaddish be recited can be explained as fear that he might die after feeling weakness. Further, it is possible that an evil spirit confused him and that he acted in a state of insanity; one is classified as taking his own life only when acting with a settled mind. It is also possible that he did so out of an act of repentance. See there for additional reasons. Chasam Sofer, siman 326, explains at length the language of the Baraisa in Semachos, chapter 2. His conclusion is that even if a person said, 'I will ascend to the roof and cast myself down,' and he was seen ascending, but was later found fallen and dead, we attribute the fall to another cause unless he was actually seen falling. If he was seen falling from the very height from which he had announced that he would cast himself, we presume that he did so intentionally. But if the fall itself was not witnessed, even though he was found dead in that place, he is plainly not classified as one who took his own life. Accordingly, Maharshal's statement, cited in Baer Heitev, seif katan 2, that if he was not seen ascending, etc., is unnecessary, for even if he was seen ascending, he is not so classified unless he was seen falling. Chasam Sofer further explains that the Shulchan Aruch's case, 'If they saw him strangled,' means that circumstances establish with certainty that he hanged himself, such as where he is found hanging in a room locked from within. Likewise, 'fallen upon his sword' means that the circumstances show that he acted upon himself. Even when it is certain that he caused his own death, we presume that he did not act with settled intent, but that an evil spirit terrified him, or that he was frightened by some circumstance, unless he was heard state with composure that he was acting because of anger or the like. However, even without an explicit statement, if his actions, anger, anguish, and preparations clearly indicated that he was preparing for death, and he was then found strangled in circumstances showing that he did it himself, this is compelling evidence and is equivalent to an explicit statement. Chasam Sofer proves this from Rosh at the end of chapter Eilu Metziyos, siman 141. Responsa Besamim Rosh, siman 345, writes concerning one destitute and unclothed who said before two people, 'I despise my life,' and then killed himself, that he is not classified as one who took his own life where he acted because of overwhelming troubles, worries, suffering, or utter poverty. Har Avel, branch 11, questions this ruling and writes that it does not bear the authentic seal of the Rosh. Chasam Sofer likewise proves from Rabbi Chanina ben Teradyon in Avodah Zarah that even one who is in extreme distress is nevertheless classified as taking his own life, and he therefore rejects Besamim Rosh, siman 345. See also Responsa Beis Ephraim, Yoreh Deah, siman 76. [Source citations 83, 96].
