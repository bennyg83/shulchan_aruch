Regarding its statement that Ramban and Tur hold that the mourners do rend, it follows that they likewise observe mourning for him, except that they do not eulogize him; see Beit Yosef. Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 326, presents grounds that Ramban's view is the halachah and rejects the proof of Lechem Mishneh at the end of the first chapter of Hilchot Avel. Nevertheless, for practical halachah, who can raise his head against the consensus of Beit Yosef in Shulchan Aruch, who rules without qualification like Rambam, especially since the rule in mourning is to follow the lenient opinion? Still, Chatam Sofer writes that where the honor of a distinguished family is at stake, and the family would otherwise suffer lasting shame because one member corrupted his conduct, whereas if the rabbi permits them to mourn people will say that the rabbis investigated and determined that he did not have the halachic status of one who deliberately took his own life, then the rabbi may initially instruct them to observe mourning even if it is actually clear to him that the deceased met all the criteria of deliberate suicide. He adduces proof from Sanhedrin 47 and adds that the essential view is that of Ramban. Although the law of mourning follows the lenient opinion, regarding injury to the dignity of a Jewish family we do not apply leniency to the honor of the children of Avraham, Yitzchak, and Yaakov. See Orach Chaim 139:3 in the gloss. See also Responsa Lachmei Todah of Mahari Bassan, siman 18. Chatam Sofer further writes that although it is customary not to recite Kaddish for one who deliberately took his own life, he knows no sound reason for this. Merely because the deceased did not act as one of his people, should we therefore not save him from descending to destruction? Avshalom, for example, was certainly not one who acted as his people should. The custom developed because the other mourners will not allow his son to share their Kaddish opportunities, arguing that a person cannot take his own life and then impose the burden of rectifying his soul upon them, for perhaps he would otherwise have lived many years and died at another time or in another place. Therefore, if the son gathers ten men in his own home, in a manner that causes no loss to other mourners, he may certainly recite Kaddish, and should do so for the full twelve months. The reason Kaddish is normally said only eleven months is not to designate one's father as wicked, but this man rendered himself wicked and would prefer to have atonement. In the synagogue, however, where there are other mourners, if the sons themselves know with certainty that their father deliberately took his own life, then even if the other mourners do not know this, taking Kaddish opportunities from them is theft from the worthy deceased. This is all the more so in light of Magen Avraham, siman 152, regarding two mourners under compulsion. In such a case one should not invoke family honor at the expense of other deceased persons. If, however, there is any genuine uncertainty whether the deceased had the status of deliberate suicide, then even if they were stringent in his burial and treated him with some degradation, regarding Kaddish his sons have the right to recite it. The inhabitants of the city are partners with one another in this matter, and upon the father's death the sons acquired their share of the Kaddish rights. Those partners who seek to displace them by claiming that their father died in a manner that forfeited his share must bring proof. See there.
