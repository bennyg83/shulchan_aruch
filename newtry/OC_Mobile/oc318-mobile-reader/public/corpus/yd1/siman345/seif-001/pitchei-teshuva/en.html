Regarding what he wrote, that the Ramban and Tur hold that the mourners do tear, etc., and according to this they would likewise mourn for him, except that they do not eulogize him, see the Beit Yosef. See Chatam Sofer responsa siman 326, who showed grounds that the Ramban's view is the halachah and rejected the proof of the Lechem Mishneh at the end of the first chapter of Hilkhot Avel. Yet in practical halachah, who can raise his head against the agreement of the Beit Yosef in the Shulchan Aruch, who stated anonymously like the Rambam, especially since the halachah follows the lenient view in mourning? Nevertheless, I say that where there is the honor of a distinguished family, for whom it would be eternal disgrace and shame that one of them corrupted his deeds, but if the decisor permits them to mourn then people will say that the rabbis examined the matter and it became clear that he did not have the law of one who destroyed himself, the decisor may rule ab initio that they should mourn, even if in truth it became clear to him that the person had the full law of one who destroys himself knowingly. There is proof from Sanhedrin 47, etc. Moreover, the primary view is like the Ramban. Although regarding mourning the halachah follows the lenient view, regarding family disgrace the halachah is not like the lenient view when it concerns the honor of the children of Avraham, Yitzchak, and Yaakov; see Orach Chayim siman 139 seif 3 in the gloss. These are his words; see there. See Lechem Todah responsa of Rabbi Yitzchak Bassan z"l, siman 18, regarding this. Chatam Sofer further wrote there that although it is customary that kaddish is not recited after one who destroyed himself knowingly, I do not know a sound reason for this. Because he did not act as one of his people, shall we not save him from descending to destruction? Who was less one who acts as his people than Avshalom, etc.? Rather, the custom spread because other mourners will not allow him to share kaddish with them, arguing: he cannot destroy himself and cast the repair of his soul upon us; perhaps he would have lived many years and died at another time or place. According to this, it seems obvious that if the son gathers ten people in his home in a way that causes no loss to other mourners, he may recite kaddish, and he should say it all twelve months. Why do we not say twelve months ordinarily? Only because doing so would make his father appear wicked. But this man made himself wicked, and it is better for him to have atonement. However, to say kaddish in the synagogue where there are other mourners, if the sons know truthfully that their father destroyed himself knowingly, even if the other mourners do not know this, by saying kaddish they rob the proper dead, all the more so from what the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 152 regarding two compelled persons, etc. There is no concern here for family honor in the face of robbing other dead. But if there is any doubt whether he had the law of one who destroyed himself knowingly, even if they were stringent with him in burial and disgraced him, regarding saying kaddish the law is with his sons that they may say kaddish, for the townspeople are partners in this matter, and as soon as their father died they acquired their share. If the other partners come to push them away, claiming that their father died in a way that caused them to lose their share, they must bring proof. This is clear; see there.
