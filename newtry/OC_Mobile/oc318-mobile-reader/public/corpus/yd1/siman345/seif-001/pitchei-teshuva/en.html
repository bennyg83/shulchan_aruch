See Baer Heitev, which notes Ramban and Tur's view that mourners do rend for one who took his own life, and according to them they also observe mourning, though no eulogy is given. Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 326, argues that Ramban's position is fundamentally correct and rejects Lechem Mishneh's proof at the end of Hilchot Avel chapter 1. Nevertheless, for practical halachah he will not oppose Beit Yosef and Shulchan Aruch, who rule like Rambam, especially because we follow the lenient view in mourning. He adds, however, that where the honor of a distinguished family is at stake and they would suffer enduring shame because one member acted improperly, while permitting them to mourn would lead people to assume the rabbis investigated and concluded that he did not have the formal halachic status of one who intentionally took his own life, a rav may permit mourning even if he personally knows the strict status actually applied. Ramban is in any case the primary position, and although mourning itself follows the lenient view, regarding family disgrace we do not minimize the honor of descendants of Avraham, Yitzchak, and Yaakov. See Orach Chayyim siman 139, seif 3 in the gloss. See also Responsa Lachmei Todah of Mahari Basan, siman 18. Chatam Sofer further writes that although the common practice is not to recite Kaddish for one who intentionally took his own life, he knows no compelling reason for the practice. Why should such a sinner be denied every effort to rescue his soul from destruction? Avshalom also failed to act as a proper member of Israel, yet spiritual rescue was still sought for him. The custom developed because other mourners refuse to surrender their share of Kaddish, arguing that the deceased brought this situation upon himself and should not impose the cost of his soul's rectification upon others. He might otherwise have lived for years and died at another time or place. Therefore, if the son assembles a private minyan at home, causing no loss to other mourners, he may recite Kaddish and should do so for the full twelve months. The usual reason for stopping after eleven months is to avoid classifying one's father as wicked, but this person already rendered himself wicked and would prefer the atonement. In a synagogue where there are other mourners, however, if the sons know with certainty that their father deliberately took his own life, reciting Kaddish at the expense of other mourners would wrong the deceased righteous people represented by those mourners. Family honor does not justify taking another deceased person's right. If there is any genuine doubt whether the strict status applies, then even if burial was conducted stringently and the deceased was treated with disgrace, the sons retain the right to recite Kaddish. Members of a community share these rights, and the sons acquired their share immediately upon the father's death. Those who seek to exclude them must prove that the death occurred in a manner that forfeited it.
