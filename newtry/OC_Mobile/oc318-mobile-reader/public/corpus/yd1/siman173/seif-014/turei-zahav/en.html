The Levush explains the permission by saying that the higher price is not charged because payment is delayed, but because if the barrel is lost or broken, the buyer pays nothing; if he cannot sell it, he returns it; and if he sells it at a loss, he caused his own loss, for he should have returned it. End quote. This is difficult, for according to that reasoning, in the law at the beginning of this siman, where selling at a higher deferred price is forbidden, we could also say that the buyer caused his own loss, since no one forced him to purchase in that manner. It appears to me that the Rambam wrote these words to explain Rav Nachman's deferred-price sale, as the Maggid Mishneh states. The Rambam permits merchandise to be given for sale at a price above its present value, meaning at the value it is expected to have later. To distinguish this from Rav Pappa's forbidden deferred-price sale, the Rambam makes a different distinction. Rav Pappa's arrangement is forbidden when a complete and irrevocable sale is made immediately. Here, however, the recipient may return the merchandise whenever he wishes. Until he actually sells it, it remains like a deposit or loaned object in his hands, and when he sells it on his own initiative he then accepts upon himself to pay the amount the owner could have received later. This is permitted, for the owner performs no forbidden act; the recipient acts on his own with the owner's property in his possession. The Beit Yosef expresses this principle below, writing that he took the goods on his own initiative and used them. In a sale arrangement of this kind, all agree that it is permitted. Responsibility must therefore remain with the owner until the recipient sells the merchandise, for otherwise it would become an immediate sale. The Rambam gives two forms. In the first, the owner bears all responsibility until it is sold. In the second, the recipient accepts responsibility immediately, yet it is still not treated as a sale because the owner promises that any amount above the fixed sum will belong to the recipient. The recipient waits until the higher-price season and may receive even more; in exchange for that potential gain he accepts responsibility. Nevertheless, he may return the merchandise whenever he wishes. Thus, there is no sale from the owner to him; rather, he independently sells another person's property that is in his possession and undertakes to pay the future higher value. This is permitted. The two forms of the seif are thereby explained. In the first form, although the text states, 'If he cannot sell it, he returns it,' as part of the rationale rather than as an explicit condition, it is obvious that because the owner bears responsibility and no sale has yet occurred, the recipient may return it whenever he wishes. This appears correct to me.
