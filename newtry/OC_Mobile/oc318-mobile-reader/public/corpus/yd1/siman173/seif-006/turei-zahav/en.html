And what is written in the gloss: "and it seems to me from the words of Asher and Tosafot, etc." Levush, my father-in-law of blessed memory, and Derisha raised a difficulty — for in siman 175 regarding pricing for produce, one only prices up to the quantity one has. And I am greatly puzzled by what Rema wrote: "and it seems to me from the words of Tosafot" — for Tosafot's language regarding what is stated in the Talmud about this law, "since he has produce, this buyer acquires from now," is: just as borrowing a se'ah-for-a-se'ah is permitted with the formula "until my son comes," since se'ah-for-se'ah is only rabbinically prohibited — so too via sale, even a se'ah for two se'ahs is permitted through "until my son comes" — end of quote. From this Rema deduced here and in Darkei Moshe, writing: since they compare this matter to a se'ah-for-se'ah loan and there one need not have all the se'ah — end of quote. I say: there is no proof here at all — for they cannot be completely analogous. For having produce does not help when one wishes to borrow a se'ah for two se'ahs; they only said this for a se'ah-for-se'ah loan, where the concern is lest it appreciate; and having produce helps for that, as explained above; but for a se'ah-for-two-se'ahs loan — which is explicit interest — having produce does not help, and the Rosh wrote this explicitly in chapter HaZahav: "and se'ah for two se'ahs, we do not find permitted even when one has it" — end of quote. But here having produce helps even if one gives him more than a dinar in exchange for one dinar, since it is by way of sale — but the comparison is only in that both have a leniency on account of having; yet each follows its own rule: for this permitted arrangement of sale, one must have what one sells — as I wrote at the beginning of siman 163 in the name of Nimukei Yosef — and they are compared to each other only in that both have a leniency when one has; but each case follows its own rule. And I have further seen fit to mention a practice: some make a permitted arrangement where the borrower writes that he is selling the lender a certain sum of money, while it is known that he does not have that sum — the lender relying on the borrower's admission that he has it. This seems to me an outright biblical prohibition — for what does a false admission accomplish to remove a biblical prohibition? Even though an admission has the power to obligate a person financially for something he does not owe, who can release the Heavenly portion? As I wrote in siman 169 seif 11. And a further proof: what Rema wrote in siman 169 seif 11 — that one who is not established as having non-Jewish collateral cannot help himself with a false admission — so too here.
