Unminted money. This means, as the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Ramban, money without an image stamped upon it. It is regarded like produce, and when he possesses it, the transaction is permitted. This is unlike Tosafos, who maintain that even stamped money is permitted, provided it is of another type.
And what he wrote in the gloss, 'It appears to me from the words of the Rosh and Tosafos, etc.' The Levush, my father-in-law of blessed memory, and the Derishah challenged this, for in siman 175, when one fixes a price for produce, he may fix only up to the quantity he possesses. I am greatly astonished by what the Rama wrote, 'It appears to me from Tosafos.' Tosafos explain the Gemara's statement that when he possesses it, it is regarded as though he says, 'Lend me until my son arrives.' Just as a se'ah for a se'ah loan is permitted when he says, 'Until my son arrives,' because se'ah for se'ah is only rabbinically forbidden, so too, by way of purchase and sale, even a se'ah for two se'ah is permitted when he says, 'Until my son arrives.' End quote. From this the Rama learned, here and in Darkei Moshe, that since they compare this case to a se'ah for a se'ah loan, and there he need not possess the entire se'ah, the same should apply here. I say that there is no proof. The two cases are clearly not entirely comparable, for possession does not help when one wishes to borrow a se'ah for two se'ah. The allowance was stated only for a se'ah for a se'ah loan, where the concern is that the price may rise; possession removes that concern. An explicit loan of a se'ah for two se'ah is fixed interest, and possession does not help. The Rosh wrote this explicitly in the chapter HaZahav: 'We have not found that a se'ah for two se'ah is permitted even when he possesses it.' End quote. Here, however, possession helps even if he gives him more than one dinar's worth for a dinar, because it is by way of purchase and sale. Tosafos mean only that just as a se'ah for a se'ah loan has an allowance when he possesses some of it, according to the law of that case, so too this purchase and sale has an allowance when he possesses the subject matter, according to the law of this case. Here he must possess everything he sells, as I wrote at the beginning of siman 163 in the name of the Nimukei Yosef. The comparison is only that in both cases possession creates an allowance, but each follows its own law. This appears clear. I have also seen fit to mention a practice whereby a borrower writes that he sells the lender a certain quantity of silver, though it is known that he does not possess that quantity, and the lender relies upon his admission that he does. This appears to me to involve a Torah prohibition. How can a false admission uproot a Torah prohibition? An admission can obligate a person monetarily for something he did not owe, but who can waive Hashem's portion? This is similar to what I wrote in siman 169, seif 11. There is further proof from the Rama there, who rules that when a person is not established as possessing collateral belonging to a non-Jew, his false admission does not help. The same applies here.
