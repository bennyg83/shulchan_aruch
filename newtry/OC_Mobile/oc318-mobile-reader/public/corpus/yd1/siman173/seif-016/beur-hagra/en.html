Provided that they are, etc. Tosafot there, s.v. 'the donkey drivers,' etc., and as explained above in the preceding seif.
And some permit, etc. Rabbeinu Hananel explains it this way, as cited by Nimukei Yosef. This resolves Tosafot's question on 73a, s.v. 'what is the reason': 'It could not have said,' etc. Since here the goods are under the buyer's responsibility, payment for the agent's labor alone does not suffice, unlike Tosafot's understanding of Rabbeinu Hananel, according to which the goods remain under the seller's responsibility. They were therefore forced into a strained interpretation of his words.
(Supplement) And some permit, etc. This is also indicated by the Yerushalmi, whose concluding clause speaks even where the merchant bears responsibility. It states there: If one transports a package from place to place and another says to him, 'Give it to me, and I will pay you according to the price at which you sell it in such-and-such place,' then if responsibility remains with the giver it is prohibited, but for the buyer it is permitted. 'But donkey drivers who receive produce from householders may provide them with produce at the expensive location according to the price at the inexpensive location.' The word 'but' implies that here it is permitted even while the donkey drivers bear responsibility. The Yerushalmi asks: What is the reason? Rav Huna says: He becomes his agent. They challenged Rav Huna: Is an agent who suffers unavoidable loss liable for the loss? This is rhetorical, for an agent is exempt from unavoidable loss, whereas here he is liable. It answers: Is it not possible that at times an unpaid custodian stipulates to have the status of a borrower? Thus, responsibility rests upon the merchant. Tosafot's question there, s.v. 'the donkey drivers,' nevertheless remains difficult. Therefore Rabbeinu Hananel explains that the householder pays him for his labor and sustenance. Although the Yerushalmi implies that no payment is required at all, because he is the householder's agent, as noted below in siman 177, seif 1, in the gloss, the Yerushalmi does not accept the Babylonian Gemara's rationale that 'they are pleased,' etc., and instead gives other reasons there. According to our Gemara, which says that they are pleased, etc., and thus that the donkey drivers receive a benefit, the Yerushalmi's rationale no longer applies. Nevertheless, we learn from the Yerushalmi that responsibility rests upon the agent, from its use of 'but,' etc., and from its statement, 'he becomes,' etc. Even though he receives payment for his labor, he must still accept responsibility, as in the package case. This is why the Gemara asks, 'Nevertheless,' etc. According to Tosafot's explanation, however, this is simply the law of the package case. What then is novel here, and why does it say 'but'? Furthermore, why does the Gemara ask, 'What is the reason?' Payment was not mentioned there. See Tosafot, s.v. 'what is the reason,' etc. Their explanation is strained, for according to it the main novelty, that no payment is required, is absent from the text and is mentioned neither in the first clause nor in the second. According to Rabbeinu Hananel, however, everything is clear. (End of supplement.)
