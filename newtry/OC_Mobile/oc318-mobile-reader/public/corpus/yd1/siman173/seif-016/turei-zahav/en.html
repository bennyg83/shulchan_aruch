That they be under the buyer's responsibility. This means the merchant who receives the money. The Gemara says that the merchant benefits because the marketplace is opened to him, meaning that he becomes known as a merchant, people recognize him, and later give him goods on credit. Alternatively, they lower prices for him, for when they hear that he is not earning, they discount their goods further so that he will profit and continue doing business with them. Tosafos write that although responsibility for the return journey rests upon the one who supplied the money, without these reasons the arrangement would be forbidden, since responsibility on the outward journey rests upon the merchants, who benefit from the money by purchasing merchandise there. Their effort on the return journey would then be compensation for the loan created during the outward journey. According to this, if he pays them compensation for their effort on the return journey, it is permitted. This appears obvious to me.
But with other types of merchandise it is forbidden. These two reasons apply specifically to grain, which is common and whose merchants constantly come and go. Other merchandise is different, for one who sells today may not be selling tomorrow.
And some permit produce even if he accepts responsibility. So write the commentators in the name of Rabbeinu Tam and the Ramban. The reason is explained in the Nimukei Yosef and Rivash, siman 306. Although there would seem to be grounds to prohibit, since once he receives the money he may spend it, and therefore he accepts responsibility for the return journey with the produce and supplies it at the lower price, it is nevertheless permitted. The benefit is not compensation for a loan. Although he used the money during the outward journey, he is like a moneychanger with whom money was deposited, who repays it at the destination. They make this arrangement because of the two benefits stated above, that the marketplace is opened to them and prices are lowered for them. They are merely the sender's agents, except that a guardian may accept the liability of a borrower; therefore they accept responsibility for the merchandise on the return journey. End quote. One should not challenge this from seif 15, where even compensation for effort does not permit the arrangement unless responsibility rests upon the seller. Although one might also say there that the marketplace is opened to him, as the Tur writes that he says, 'When I sell it, I will repay you immediately,' meaning that he wishes to establish his credit, that case concerns ordinary merchandise, where there is no genuine opening of the marketplace. Furthermore, these two reasons apply specifically to one who purchases produce, because the seller benefits when the buyer purchases merchandise for him. When others see money in the buyer's hands, many sellers approach him, and that benefit compensates him for accepting responsibility. In the earlier case, however, the seller receives no comparable benefit from potential buyers. Anyone who comes to buy has no concern, for he can purchase in many places and need not ingratiate himself with anyone. The Gemara at the beginning of Eizehu Neshech says that people's money itself serves as a broker, meaning that no broker is needed because the donkey drivers bring the grain directly to the owner of the money. Thus, the seller receives no benefit of this kind, and the buyer must therefore accept responsibility.
