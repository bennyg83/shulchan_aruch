(Supplement) In what case, etc. This is the deferred-price arrangement of Rav Nahman. Rif omitted it, and Milchamot likewise demonstrated that the halachah does not follow Rav Nahman, discussing the matter at length. He further wrote there that Rav Nahman's arrangement, according to the Geonim, is like that of Rav Pappa: he sells to him in Tishrei at the price current in Nisan and waits until Nisan. The Gemara challenged this, etc., and answered, etc. There, in the Mishnah, he fixed the price for payment at the threshing season at twelve maneh. Here, however, the price is merely that which will prevail in Nisan and is not fixed, for the price may not rise. This is why it is called tarsha, as the Arukh writes that tarash in Arabic means deaf, comparable to the expression in Tanhuma at the end of Behukotai, 'your deaf dance, strike upon it,' etc.; it denotes something not fixed. The Yerushalmi explicitly rules that Rav Nahman's arrangement is prohibited. It states there: Rabbi Hiyya the Elder had flax. Wine merchants came to buy it. He said to them, 'I do not intend to sell it now, but only at Purim.' They said to him, 'Sell it to us at the price for which you expect to sell it at Purim.' He came and asked Rabbi, who told him that it was prohibited. His words are persuasive. (End of supplement.)

An item, etc. As Baal HaTerumot writes in the name of Ramban, the Gemara's challenge there from the Mishnah, 'but if payment is at the threshing season,' etc., applies even though he raises the price considerably, because land is expensive in Nisan, as stated in the first chapter of Bava Kamma, 7a.
(Supplement) An item, etc. This is difficult in my eyes. Since its assessed value is known, it is as though the price were fixed. Even if in most cases it is customary, etc., it is nevertheless near to profit and remote from loss, etc. Hagahot Mordekhai, however, writes differently: 'The ancient custom of delivering cloaks after a long period for a set amount at the high market price is because their price depends upon fortune. If a nobleman comes to the city and desires them, their price will rise beyond their value. Had the nobleman come immediately, their price would have doubled, and there is no payment for waiting,' etc. One must say that in most years noblemen would come to the city, for otherwise it would appear as payment for waiting. It is clear that there, if the nobleman came, the price would double beyond what the buyer had paid. This is what he means by saying that their price depends upon fortune. It is therefore also near to profit, as with all merchandise whose value depends upon fortune. Nevertheless, he requires that this occur in most cases, etc., because although it is legally permitted, it would otherwise appear, etc. This requires study. (End of supplement.)
