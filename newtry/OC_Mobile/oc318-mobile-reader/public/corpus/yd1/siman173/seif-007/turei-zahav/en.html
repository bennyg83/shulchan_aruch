If they are in the seller’s possession. The Beit Yosef explained in the name of the Rivash that the reason is: since by Torah law money effects acquisition, and if one retracts he receives the curse of “mi shepara,” with respect to ribit it is as though he acquired them as a complete acquisition from now, since the prohibition of selling produce in this way is only rabbinic. But when he does not have them, we are concerned that perhaps the price will rise and the buyer will profit by advancing the money, and the seller will also have to buy them at a higher price; end quote.
But if he specifies, etc. In the Tur it is written here that some say that even if he specifies, it is permitted when he has them. It appears that the reason is that although at the beginning of this siman specifying is forbidden according to all, there it is different, because the buyer requests time to wait for the money, and delay is relevant there. This is not so here, where the item being purchased is ready in the seller’s possession, except that he waits until his son comes; this is not called delay. The Tur disagrees and holds that this too is called delay. What the Rama wrote here, “and if after time,” with a yod, means after my son comes, and even so it is forbidden.
Even if he does not have them, it is permitted. There are astonishing words here, and we will copy the source. Rav Nachman said: one who gives money to a wax-seller, when four blocks go for a zuz, and says to him, “I will give you five,” if the seller has them it is permitted, and if he does not have them it is forbidden. We ask: it is obvious that if he does not have them it is forbidden. We answer: even though he has credit in others’ hands, as the Tur wrote here. Tosafot and the Rosh asked from what Rav Nachman himself said, that tarsha is permitted, namely the law mentioned at the beginning of this siman, that one may sell for more than its value because of waiting for the money, provided he does not specify: “If now, then for less.” Here too, it should be permitted to sell cheaply because of advancing the money, since he does not specify. They answered: since here four go for a zuz, it is like specifying, “if now, then for less.” Rav Nachman permitted tarsha only with something that has no fixed market price and no known valuation. This is their language. The Beit Yosef wrote regarding this: it appears from their words that if it is something whose valuation is not known, even if he does not have it, one may advance money in order to buy from him cheaply, similar to Rav Nachman’s tarsha mentioned at the beginning of this siman; end quote. The Darkhei Moshe copied this as follows: even if he does not have it, it is permitted to advance the money in order to buy cheaply, just as one may sell expensively where its valuation is not known, as mentioned at the beginning of the siman; end quote. From this came this gloss permitting even when he does not have it, so long as he does not specify. I was stunned for a time at these two great lions above me over this ruling. First, how could it enter our minds to permit fixing a price for anything in the world when he does not have it and the market price has not emerged? How is this different from the Mishnah, “one may not fix a price for produce until the market price emerges”? So too even with something whose valuation is not known, such as a cow or cloak, how can we permit it? It can come to outright ribit: this person received one gold coin to provide him a cow at such-and-such time, and when that time arrives the cow becomes more expensive and he must give two gold coins’ worth. This is complete neshekh, for we see that he pays two for one gold coin. What they wanted to learn from Rav Nachman’s tarsha, where he permits regarding a cow, is not comparable at all. There, we do not see that he pays more money than he received. Although at the time of sale he bought at a high price, nevertheless with this buyer we do not see that he pays more, and one can say the cow was worth that much, since its valuation is not public and known. This is unlike this seller, who discounts by giving a cow and receives for it less than he will have to spend to provide a cow; this is ribit visible to the eyes. All the more is there wonder at what the Beit Yosef and Rama wrote explicitly, that he gives cheaply in order to buy cheaply in advance. Who permitted this to us? This is complete ribit, for he takes what he did not give him. A further difficulty I have is from the above Tosafot, for according to them the entire prohibition in Rav Nachman’s wax case is because, since the market price had emerged, it is like specifying. Consequently, if the market price had not emerged, it would be permitted to fix a price. Tosafot explicitly wrote afterward that regarding produce there is prohibition only specifically when the market price has emerged. This is against the Mishnah, “one may not fix a price for produce until the market price emerges.” If so, fixing produce against produce is difficult. All this is difficult according to the Beit Yosef and Rama’s understanding of Tosafot, that they wrote to permit when its valuation is not known and he does not have it. But when we look with the help of Heaven, this is not the path. Heaven forbid to permit, where he does not have it, any fixing in the world without a market price having emerged. The understanding of Tosafot is as follows. There are two categories before us. One category is ribit visible to the eyes, where everyone sees that the borrower gives more money than he received. This is the case of “one may not fix a price for produce,” and the same applies to anything, lest it rise in price and our eyes see the seller giving more. The second category is inferred ribit, where we do not see that he gives more than he received in the sum of money, but from the nature of the sale we infer that he does this because of the advancing of money, and the seller loses. This inference is where we see that he gives him cheaper than the market price that emerged, or buys from him more expensively. With this everything is correct. We must ask further in Tosafot’s words: why did they ask Rav Nachman against Rav Nachman? They should have asked Rav Nachman’s tarsha against the Mishnah, “one may not fix a price for produce,” asking why Rav Nachman permitted tarsha, where the buyer takes at more than the market price; how is this different from “one may not fix,” where there is prohibition because of the seller’s loss? Certainly according to Tosafot’s answer one can resolve this, but they should have proven their answer from that question and not from a contradiction between Rav Nachman’s statements, since in the contradiction within Rav Nachman one could say he retracted; but if the question is from the Mishnah, you can answer only as Tosafot answered, distinguishing known valuation. Therefore it appears to say that from “one may not fix” there is no difficulty, because there it is forbidden due to the possibility of visible prohibition if the price rises, as I mentioned. This is unlike Rav Nachman’s tarsha, where there is no concern that it may rise, for he has already bought it at the high price. Rather, the concern is an inferred prohibition, because we infer why this person buys at a high price: certainly because of waiting for the money. Therefore one could distinguish that there is prohibition only where it is visible to the eyes, since the prohibition here is rabbinic and they did not decree inferred prohibition at all. Therefore Tosafot asked from Rav Nachman against Rav Nachman, from Rav Nachman’s wax case, which also deals only with inferred prohibition. You cannot say that he forbids where four go for a zuz and he gives him five because it will come to visible prohibition if he must buy for him five, for if so, why would Rav Nachman forbid only where the market price had emerged at four and he fixes at five? There is prohibition in all fixing in the world, even where the market price has not emerged at all, as in the Mishnah, “one may not fix.” Rather, certainly Rav Nachman’s wax case speaks even where there is no prohibition from “one may not fix,” namely no concern that perhaps it will become more expensive; it speaks because of another prohibition, where there is no prohibition from fear of price increase, such as where the time he obligated himself to give five blocks has already arrived and at that time the market too became cheap at that rate. In that case it is permitted from the perspective of “one may not fix for produce,” yet there is prohibition because at the time he made the sale it was inferred ribit, for then the price was four, and why did he take money to give five? Certainly because of the advancing of money. Rav Nachman’s wax case teaches that even inferred ribit is prohibited. Therefore Tosafot ask inferred ribit against inferred ribit: in Rav Nachman’s wax case we say it is forbidden even without specifying, while in Rav Nachman’s tarsha we say it is permitted, as we mentioned. Tosafot answered well that there is a distinction in inferred ribit: specifically with produce and wax, if the market price emerged, because it is public; and likewise even if the market price did not emerge but there is publicity because its valuation is known, so that everyone knows its value, since all produce and wax are equal in this, we forbid inferred ribit. But Rav Nachman’s tarsha has neither of these, for their valuation is not known, such as a cow or cloak, where each cow has its own price and market price does not apply because not all cows are equal. The same applies to some fruits that are not all equal; they are like a cow or cloak. In the Asheri he did not mention two matters here, but only mentioned that their market price is known; automatically, with anything whose market price is not known at the time of fixing, there is permission regarding inferred ribit, for there is no strong inference, even though he is truly discounting for him, because it is not public to all, since not everyone knows the value of this cow. All this distinction between known valuation and unknown valuation applies where there is no concern that perhaps it will become more expensive, as we said, for we already saw that it did not become more expensive. But where there is concern that perhaps it will become more expensive, Rav Nachman did not speak at all; it is mentioned in the Mishnah, “one may not fix a price for produce,” and the same applies to everything, for it is one reason, since our eyes see that he pays more than he took. But in a place where there is no ribit visible to the eyes, because there is no concern that it may become more expensive, then there is a distinction: if its valuation is known or the market price emerged, there is prohibition, whether from the seller’s side, such as where he discounts for him, or from the buyer’s side, where he buys at a high price, for this is like specifying. But where its valuation is not known, there is no distinction between whether the loss is on the buyer’s side, where he buys expensively, which is Rav Nachman’s tarsha, or on the seller’s side, where he sells cheaply; in both cases it is permitted, since there is no concern in either that it may become more expensive. There is proof for the above explanation from Tosafot’s language. They wrote there: “I will give you five,” meaning at such-and-such later time; if he has them, it is permitted, and if he does not have them, it is forbidden. But four for a zuz is permitted to fix, since that is the market price, for one may fix according to the market price; end quote. It is difficult: why did they need to write that it speaks of a later time? Rather, they were troubled that if it speaks where he says, “I will give you five for a zuz immediately,” it is obvious that it is forbidden if he does not have them, since he will certainly have to give for the price of four what he received for the price of five. Rather, it speaks of a later time, meaning it is possible that they will then be cheap, and Rav Nachman teaches that although they will be cheap, it is forbidden if he fixed with him at five, since at the time he received the money there was a prohibition. Therefore it is forbidden when he does not have them. Afterward they wrote, “but four for a zuz, etc.,” meaning: since it speaks of after time, it should have been forbidden even at four, because there is also a doubt that perhaps it will rise in price. To this they answered that this is permitted because such is the market price, etc. Thus he came to teach us the opposite, that there is novelty in saying five, that although at that later time there will be a time of cheapness, and if he had fixed already for the produce it would be permitted, even so here it is forbidden for the reason we mentioned. So it appears clear in my humble opinion: wherever there is concern that perhaps it will become more expensive, one must forbid where he does not have it. No permission may be learned from Tosafot and the Asheri except where there is no concern that it may become more expensive, as in Rav Nachman’s tarsha, where that does not apply at all; and likewise with produce if we saw that it did not become more expensive, in that case we permit inferred ribit.
