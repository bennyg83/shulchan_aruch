If they are in the seller's possession. The Beit Yosef explains in the name of the Rivash that since, according to the Torah, money effects acquisition, and if he retracts he is subject to the curse of 'He Who exacted punishment,' with respect to interest it is regarded as though the buyer fully acquired them immediately. The prohibition against selling produce in this manner is only rabbinic. When the seller does not possess the produce, however, we are concerned that the price may rise and the buyer profits because he advanced the money, while the seller will also need to purchase it at the higher price. End quote.
But if he specifies, etc. The Tur cites an opinion that even if he specifies the lower cash price, it is permitted when the seller possesses the item. The reason appears to be that although at the beginning of this siman all agree that specification is forbidden, there the buyer seeks additional time to pay the money, and therefore delayed payment applies. Here, however, the merchandise is ready in the seller's possession, and he merely waits until his son arrives; this is not called delayed payment. The Tur disagrees and holds that even this is called delayed payment. What the Rama writes here, 'and if it is after time,' means after the son arrives, and even so it is forbidden.
Even if he does not possess it, it is permitted. There are astonishing matters here. Rav Nachman taught that if one gives money to a wax seller when 4 cakes sell for a zuz, and the seller says, 'I will give you 5,' it is permitted if he possesses them and forbidden if he does not. The Gemara asks that the prohibition when he does not possess them is obvious, and answers that even credit owed to him by others is insufficient, as the Tur writes here. Tosafos and the Rosh ask from Rav Nachman's own ruling that a deferred-price sale is permitted, meaning that one may sell for more than the present value because payment is delayed, provided he does not expressly state a lower cash price. Why then should it not likewise be permitted to sell cheaply because money is advanced, when he does not expressly state the discount? They answer that since 4 cakes presently sell for a zuz, selling 5 is equivalent to expressly stating a lower cash price. Rav Nachman's deferred-price allowance applies only to an item without a fixed market price or known appraisal. End quote. The Beit Yosef understood from this that when an item's appraisal is unknown, one may advance money to buy it cheaply even if the seller does not possess it, like Rav Nachman's deferred-price sale. Darkei Moshe copied this, and from it arose the gloss permitting the case whenever the discount is not expressly stated. I was astonished for a long time at these two great authorities. How could one permit fixing a future delivery for any item when the seller does not possess it and no market price has been established? How is this different from the Mishnah, 'One may not fix a price for produce until the market price has been established'? Even for an item without a known appraisal, such as a cow or cloak, the price may rise. If he received 1 gold coin to supply a cow at a later date, and then must spend 2 gold coins to supply it, this is visible interest, for our eyes see that he pays 2 for the 1 he received. Rav Nachman's deferred-price sale is entirely different. There the buyer does not visibly pay more money than he received, and since the cow has no published appraisal, it may have been worth the deferred price. Here the seller gives a cow cheaply and receives less than he must later spend to supply it. This is visible interest. All the more so is it astonishing that the Beit Yosef and Rama expressly permit advancing money in order to buy cheaply. Who permitted this? It is full interest, for he receives what he did not give. The proper explanation, with Hashem's help, is that there are two categories. The first is visible interest, where all see that the borrower gives more than he received. This is the law that one may not fix a price for produce before the market price is established, and the same applies to every item that may rise in price. The second is interest proven by the circumstances. We do not see a greater amount being paid, but the nature of the transaction proves that the difference was given because money was advanced or payment delayed, such as selling below the known market price or buying above it. Rav Nachman's deferred-price sale concerns only this second category. Since the item has neither a fixed market price nor a known appraisal, the excess is not publicly evident, and the Sages did not prohibit it. Rav Nachman's wax case also concerns circumstantial interest, in a setting where the concern of a later price rise has already been removed, such as when the delivery date arrived and the market then fell to 5 cakes per zuz. Even so, if at the time of the agreement the market was 4 and he promised 5, the transaction was evidently made because of the advance payment and is forbidden when he did not possess the wax. Tosafos therefore compare Rav Nachman's two rulings and distinguish between an item with a known price or appraisal, where the circumstantial interest is evident, and a cow, cloak, or similar item without a known appraisal, where it is not. All this distinction applies only where there is no concern that the item may rise in price. Whenever such a concern exists, the Mishnah's law applies and it is forbidden when the seller does not possess the item. This is the clear meaning of Tosafos and the Rosh. No permission may be derived from them for a forward purchase before the market price is established when the seller lacks the goods.
