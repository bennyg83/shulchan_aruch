Provided that he gives him compensation for his effort. So write Tosafos and the Rosh, for otherwise he exerts himself on the journey because of the loan that will follow. The Rosh concludes that the teaching is that we are not concerned that they may undervalue the compensation for his labor and sustenance. End quote. Tosafos write that the final clause was needed to teach that if the outward responsibility rests upon the buyer it is forbidden, for one might have thought it does not appear like interest since he sells it in the more expensive place. The Beit Yosef cites the Maggid Mishneh and Mordechai in the name of the Or Zarua that no compensation for effort is required. He explains that the recipient did not stipulate that the owner lend him money; rather, he independently takes the proceeds and uses them, so there is no interest prohibition, and his effort is an act of kindness. In my opinion, this explanation does not yet remove the interest concern. Rather, the reason is that the buyer exerts himself for his own benefit, so that the proceeds will reach his hands. His effort is not compensation for the loan; he works for himself by converting the produce into money. Although the seller also benefits, that does not create a prohibition when the buyer benefits as well. This is clear from siman 177, where a shared benefit is not problematic. Consider a person who lends another an object to sell, fixing an amount he must pay if he does not return it intact. The recipient sells it and later pays the fixed amount. Surely his effort in selling it is not interest; he exerted himself for his own benefit. The Beit Yosef rules like Tosafos and the Rosh that compensation must be given. I am not worthy to decide between them, but it appears obvious that the required compensation need be no greater than that mentioned in siman 177, where even a small amount suffices. Although the Beit Yosef inferred from the Rosh that full compensation is required, practical halacha does not require that, since other great authorities require none at all and their reasoning is persuasive. At the very least, we may rely upon them to accept a small amount. The Shulchan Aruch's unqualified phrase, 'compensation for his effort,' also implies this.
