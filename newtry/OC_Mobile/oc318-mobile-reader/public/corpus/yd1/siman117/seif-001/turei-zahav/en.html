It is forbidden to engage in commerce with it. In the Beit Yosef, in the name of the Rashba, the reason is a decree lest he come to eat from them; excluded are a donkey and a camel, for a person raises them for labor. And I wondered, for in the chapter Kol Sha'ah (Pesachim daf 23) it is stated that it is a d'oraisa prohibition, for we learned: if tamei species came into his hands, it is permitted to sell them to a non-Jew; and it explains the reason, for it is written “to you” , it shall be yours; and we ask: if so, even l'chatchila as well. And it answers: this case is different, for the verse says “they shall be,” meaning, they shall remain in their state; and Tosafos there wrote that it is by Torah law. And it appears that the Rashba wrote this decree: that the Torah forbade it for this reason, since we see a permission because of “to you,” and a prohibition because of “they shall be,” and thus the matter is given over to the Sages, that they choose what will be forbidden and what will be permitted. An example of the matter is the prohibition of melacha on Chol HaMoed in the second chapter of Chagigah. Therefore the Sages judged that in any matter that is food one must withhold it lest he come to eat it, but an item designated for labor is permitted. Afterwards I saw in Terumat HaDeshen siman 200, who wrote in the name of the Tosafos gilyon that this derivation is an asmachta. And this is written in the gilyon of Tosafos in the chapter Kol Sha'ah that I mentioned, where they wrote that the prohibition of commerce with these is by Torah law; on this the gilyon wrote that they did not mean a full d'oraisa, for in truth it is only d'rabbanan, and it is merely an asmachta. But it is difficult for me from what Tosafos wrote in the chapter Lulav HaGazul (Sukkah daf 39), s.v. “and let him give it,” etc., regarding Shevi'is, and this is their language: and it is possible that one who purchases from his fellow in order to profit and sell for a higher price, this too is commerce; and in the fourth chapter of Shevi'is we learned that one does not engage in commerce with fruits of Shevi'is, and not with firstborns, and not with terumos, and not with neveilos and treifos, etc.; you do not find that they are all equal regarding commerce except in such a case where the Torah explicitly permitted selling, as it is written: “or sell to a non-Jew.” End of their words. And if it is so that the prohibition of commerce with tamei items is only d'rabbanan, what do Tosafos prove from neveilos and treifos from the verse “or sell to a non-Jew,” for there it is certainly permitted by Torah law, and the Sages forbade it; and the Mishnah states that by rabbinic law they are equal. And it appears to answer that the Sages do not have power to forbid a matter that the Torah explicitly explained as permitted; for the Sages have power to be stringent only in a place where there is neither an explicit prohibition nor an explicit permission by Torah law. This is not so in a place where there is an explicit permission by Torah law. And this is included in their statement: I heard that a court would administer lashes and punishments not according to the law, and not to violate the words of Torah, but to make a fence for the Torah. For it is difficult: is it not obvious that no person has power to violate the words of Torah? And in Choshen Mishpat siman 2 we wrote about this. And according to the premise I mentioned, it is explained better: that here they meant that even to be stringent they have no power where they are overriding the Torah. Therefore Tosafos prove well: since we find a permission in the Torah, certainly the Sages did not forbid it at all for sale; rather, it must be that even by rabbinic law it is permitted, and that is where he did not purchase in order to profit.
Or to lend on it. In Terumat HaDeshen siman 200 he inclined regarding the matter and concludes that it is a disgraceful thing to lend on pig hides; and according to what the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Rashba that one must be concerned lest he come to eat it, there is also a complete prohibition in lending. There: to feed it to his non-Jewish workers. In the Beit Yosef no reason for this is found, and on the contrary, he wrote in the name of the Jerusalem Talmud: one who purchased pigs for the needs of non-Jewish workers, when he sells them he sells them only for their value. And one must say that there it speaks regarding one who transgressed and purchased. And regarding the reason for the prohibition here, it is according to the Gemara in Avodah Zarah (daf 62): for it was taught: the donkey-drivers who were doing work with fruits of Shevi'is, their wage is Shevi'is. What does it mean “their wage is Shevi'is”? Shall we say that we give them from fruits of Shevi'is? It turns out this one is paying his debt from fruits of Shevi'is, and the Torah said “for eating” and not for commerce. It is clear from here that giving to one’s workers is called commerce; consequently, it is also forbidden to purchase for his workers, because he is paying his debt, which is like commerce. And even though Tosafos there wrote that the reason that it is paying his debt is because he profits with fruits of Shevi'is and we do not call it “for eating,” etc., here he is not profiting with tamei items, for he purchases them with money; nevertheless, presumably they sell him these items cheaply in contrast to other tahor species; therefore we call it commerce. And it would seem that also when neveilos and treifos came into his house, another Jew should not buy them cheaply and sell them for a higher price to a non-Jew, as Tosafos wrote there in Avodah Zarah: Rabbeinu Yitzchak says that the commerce that is forbidden regarding fruits of Shevi'is is to purchase many at once and bring them from a cheap place to an expensive place, etc. And so they wrote further in the chapter Lulav HaGazul (Sukkah daf 39): and it is possible that one who purchases from his fellow in order to profit and sell for a higher price, that too is commerce; and in the fourth chapter of Shevi'is we learned: one does not engage in commerce with fruits of Shevi'is, etc., and not with neveilos and treifos, etc., and you do not find that they are all equal, etc., end of their words, as I copied above. And another proof is from what the Tur wrote in Yoreh De'ah siman 151 regarding selling an animal, and in Sefer HaTerumah he wrote: even according to this, one should permit only when it came into his hands, but to purchase in order to profit, no. End of his words. But upon precise analysis one finds a permission here: for by Torah law the sale of treifos remains even for us, as the Beit Yosef wrote regarding this, for it is an explicit verse: “or sell to a non-Jew.” And this sale has no difference whether he sells by himself or by another, for we rule at the beginning of the chapter HaIsh Mekadesh: a person’s agent is like himself. And the language of the verse also implies so, for it says “or sell” and it did not say “you shall sell” as it said first “to the convert you shall give it,” to teach that even if the sale is not by your hand but by another, it is permitted. And this is not comparable to Shevi'is and the sale of an animal that we mentioned, for there the buyer is not called the agent of the Jew, since the seller could sell it to the buyer so that he would eat it himself; why should we say that he is his agent to sell to a non-Jew? In contrast, here, regarding the sale of a treifah, where it is impossible to sell it to him except to a non-Jew, it follows that the seller who sells to the buyer is selling to him specifically this matter: that he will be in his place to sell to a non-Jew. And consider: if you do not say so, there will be a loss for one to whom a neveilah came into his hands, and he is not capable of selling to a non-Jew, shall he lose it and not sell it to another who can sell to a non-Jew? That is certainly not so. And there is no difference whether the agent profits or not, for every broker who buys and sells is called an agent. And for this reason, even one who purchases from his fellow Jew a treifah to feed it to his non-Jewish workers, there is no prohibition, for there is no prohibition here regarding this treifah, for he stands in the place of the seller and is his agent; and so is the simple practice, as I have seen. And even though my father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote to forbid purchasing in order to profit thereby, what appears to me I have written.
There, to feed them to his non-Jewish workers. In Beit Yosef no reason is found for this, and on the contrary he wrote in the name of the Yerushalmi that one who purchased pigs for the purpose of non-Jewish workers, when he sells them may only sell them at cost. One must say the Yerushalmi speaks of one who already transgressed and purchased them. As for the reason for the prohibition, [it derives] from the Talmud in Avodah Zarah (62a): donkey-drivers who did their work with shevi'it produce receive their wages in shevi'it produce; what does "wages in shevi'it produce" mean? If we give them from shevi'it produce, this one is paying his debt with shevi'it produce , and the Torah said "for eating" and not "for commerce." Behold, giving [produce] to one's workers is considered commerce; likewise it is forbidden to purchase [tamei animals] for one's workers on account of paying one's debt, which constitutes commerce. Even though Tosafot wrote there that the reason for "paying one's debt" is that he profits from shevi'it produce and it cannot be called "for eating" , and here one does not profit from tamei items since he purchases them with money , nevertheless presumably these items are sold to him cheaper than other pure species, and we therefore call it "commerce." And at first glance it appears that even in the case of neveilot and tereifot that came into a Jew's home by chance, another Jew may not buy them cheaply and sell them to a non-Jew at a higher price , as Tosafot wrote in Avodah Zarah: R' Yitzchak says the commerce forbidden with shevi'it produce means to buy a large quantity together and transport from a cheap place to an expensive place, etc. And similarly in chapter Lulav HaGazul (Sukkah 39a): perhaps one who buys from another in order to profit and sell at a higher price , that too is commerce; and in the fourth chapter of Shevi'it we learn one may not engage in commerce with shevi'it produce... or with neveilot and tereifot... end of their words, as I copied nearby. Further proof from what Tur wrote in YD siman 151 regarding selling an animal, and Sefer HaTerumot wrote: even according to this, we only permit when it came to him [unexpectedly]; but to buy in order to profit , no. End of his words. But upon careful examination you will find a case where it is permitted: for the Torah law on selling tereifot applies even nowadays, as Beit Yosef wrote , as the verse explicitly says "or sell to a non-Jew"; and this selling makes no distinction between selling oneself and selling through another, for we rule at the beginning of chapter HaIsh Mekadesh that throughout the Torah an agent is like the sender. The wording of the verse also supports this , it says "or sell" and not "you shall sell" as it said initially "to the convert you shall give it" , to indicate that even if the sale is not done through you directly but through another, it is permitted. This is not comparable to shevi'it and the animal sale we mentioned, for there the buyer is not considered the Israelite's agent, since the seller could sell them to the buyer to eat himself , why say he is his agent to sell to a non-Jew? But here, in the sale of a tereifah which can only be sold to a non-Jew , the seller to the buyer sells him specifically this role: to stand in his place and sell to a non-Jew. Think about it: if you do not say so, one who has a neveilah come to him and cannot sell it to a non-Jew himself , would he suffer a loss and not sell it to another who can? Certainly not. Nor does it matter whether the agent profits, for any middleman who buys and sells is called an agent. Therefore a Jew who buys a tereifah from another to feed to his non-Jewish workers incurs no prohibition, for there is no prohibition on this tereifah , he stands in the seller's place and is his agent. And so the practice is established as I have observed. Even though my father-in-law z"l wrote it is prohibited to buy in order to profit , in this I have written what appears to my humble opinion.
And if tamei [species] came into a hunter’s hands. It is explained in the Beit Yosef that the halacha follows the Sages: that it is specifically a hunter whose livelihood is that that they permitted, but not another person.
Provided that he did not intend for that. Here the gloss printed nearby, “and he must sell it immediately,” etc., belongs, and it was printed there in error, for regarding a rabbinic prohibition it is permitted to engage in commerce in any manner that he wishes; and so it is in the Beit Yosef, in the name of Orchos Chayyim: this was written regarding the first clause. I was asked regarding the large goats in this district; it is the practice of craftsmen not to buy the hides except when they are killed by severing the throat, but not slaughtered, because they become somewhat damaged; therefore one must be in doubt whether a Jew may kill the goats in this manner for this. It appears to me that it is a complete permission. For if we will be concerned for bal tashchis, and like what I wrote at the end of the previous siman in the name of Tosafos that one should not give uncovered water even to a tamei animal, that is not so, for he has benefit from this and there is no bal tashchis, as written in the Smag, negative commandments siman 229, that whenever he has benefit that is greater than the destruction, there is no prohibition of bal tashchis. And so it is in Tosafos, Avodah Zarah (daf 11), s.v. “the main,” etc., that it is permitted to render the animal a treifah. And likewise we find in the chapter HaShochet (Chullin daf 28): one who slaughters and needs the blood may kill by severing the throat or by uprooting; and it is explained there regarding a wild animal, and he needs the blood to dye wool with it. This is exactly like our case. And likewise in the chapter Kisuy HaDam (daf 85), Rabbi permitted R' Chiyya to render the animal a treifah and to slaughter it and to remove its blood and dye wool with it. And if because one does not engage in commerce with neveilos and treifos, that also does not apply here, since in a permitted manner he makes it a neveilah; and it is afterwards as if a neveilah came into his hands in his house, for since he acts first in a permitted manner for his own benefit, afterwards there is no prohibition at all. For the Sages forbade only where he intended from the outset to act in a forbidden manner, such as to trap tamei species; which is not so here. And similarly, in the chapter HaYah Korei (Berachos daf 14), Rav said: one should not begin, and if he began he finishes; Abaye said: we begin, since they begin in the west; and since we began, we also finish. That is, since he began in a permitted manner, even though at the time of beginning he knows that he will finish, and here as well it is so. And I saw in a responsum newly printed, of the Rav R' Binyamin of blessed memory: he too permitted killing the goats by severing the throat, but in the gloss there it is written that that which the Gemara permitted killing by severing the throat or uprooting in order to dye wool is on condition that he afterwards throw the meat away, because one does not engage in commerce with neveilos, and that is a mistake in my view. For if so, it should have said in the Gemara that he should throw the meat to dogs. And furthermore, if so it should have forbidden the killing by severing the throat in the first place, lest he not throw the meat afterwards and he will eat it; for according to the Rashba, whom the Beit Yosef brings, this is the reason that it is forbidden to be involved with neveilos and treifos. Rather, it is simple: anything that he does first in a permitted manner, such as that he needs it for the blood or for the hide, the meat afterwards is as if a neveilah came into his hands, and it is permitted to sell it to a non-Jew. But one who does not know the laws of shechitah and wants to sell the meat to a non-Jew, and wants to slaughter in a forbidden manner, that is certainly forbidden, for from the outset he makes it into a neveilah solely for selling the neveilah, and not for any permitted matter. And all of this appears to me clear and simple. The Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Orchos Chayyim that it is forbidden to smear one’s flesh with pig fat, for smearing is included in drinking; and it appears that we do not rule so, for Tosafos explicitly wrote in the last chapter of Yoma (daf 77) that it is permitted for a person to smear with cheilev, for we do not find ‘smearing is like drinking’ except regarding Yom Kippur, and prohibitions of benefit, since there is an asmachta; but cheilev, which is permitted for benefit, it is permitted. And so wrote in the chapter Bnos Kusim in the name of Rabbeinu Tam. And so wrote the Beit Yosef in siman 123 in the name of the Rashba in a responsum, that it is permitted to smear with pig fat even not in a place of danger. Except that in Orchos Chayyim, end of klal 39, I saw that he wrote that there is no permission for smearing with cheilev or with pig [fat] except where there is pain; but for a healthy person, for pleasure, no; and therefore one should not smear a baby with pig fat, for that is only for pleasure. But one may allow a non-Jew to smear them and to feed them, for we do not say that one must warn adults regarding minors except with respect to smearing them with one’s own hands. End of his words; and so is the primary ruling. And in the Levush he distinguished of his own reasoning to forbid smearing with pig fat but not with other cheilev, and he was not precise in the source of these matters, for there is no distinction between them at all. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that those who lease towns and villages from the minister, meaning, all the income of the minister, and included in this are the pigs in the courtyard, and during the period of the lease, a Jew raises the pigs and feeds them to his workers, they are not doing properly. End of his words. Nevertheless, it appears that one can find a permission for this, since their primary intention at the time of the lease is to lease other things, and this is secondary; there is no prohibition in it. For the Tur wrote at the beginning of this siman: or if he trapped tamei together with tahor, it implies that he intentionally trapped them together, and nevertheless it is permitted. That is because his intention is only on tahor alone, but it is impossible for the tahor to be trapped alone, for they are mixed and tamei are trapped with them. Therefore it is permitted even though he knows initially that it will be so; nevertheless we follow his intention in what he wants, and it is similar to that which is stated in the chapter HaSho'el: what is permitted he wants to acquire, what is forbidden he does not want to acquire. It is like what the Tur wrote in the laws of Pesach regarding a non-Jew who brings chametz to a Jew on the last festival day. Here too is similar. And that which is raised during the period of the lease is like “came into his hands,” and it is permitted to feed them to his non-Jewish workers; and they already practiced so in the lands of Russia and Volhynia, and many, and the Sages did not protest, because they are items belonging to the minister, and the Jew’s name is not upon them and he has no involvement with them; and the non-Jewish workers are the workers of the minister, and they eat from that which belongs to him, so it appears to me to find merit for them in this. It is forbidden to sell a neveilah under the presumption that it is kosher. In the Tur the reason is because of deception, which is forbidden even toward a non-Jew. And in what the Tur wrote: ‘and it appears that even according to those who explain,’ etc., one can ask: behold Tosafos wrote in the chapter Gid HaNasheh (daf 94), s.v. ‘treifah,’ etc., that there is no concern lest a Jew buy from him, for he was concerned that for deception of the non-Jew he said so. End of their words. So too here, why should it be forbidden before a Jew, for the Jew will not buy from the non-Jew, since now he holds that deception is permitted toward him? And why should we not say that those “explainers” also hold this reasoning, and we create a new dispute for no reason between this interpretation and the earlier interpretation regarding this reasoning? And even if you will say that the Tur has proof from the language “there are those who explain” who said “we do not decree lest he give it,” etc., which implies that they do not have that decree, if so, it is difficult upon the explainers themselves: why do they need this, and why do we not say that they were not concerned at all that a Jew would buy from him, for he was concerned to say it because of deception of the non-Jew, since it is permitted? I was asked about those large rams in this region of Galilee , it is the practice of craftsmen not to purchase the hides except when the animals are slaughtered by nechira [cutting the windpipe without proper shechitah], but not when properly slaughtered, because they become somewhat damaged [by proper shechitah]. The question therefore arises: may a Jew slaughter the rams by nechira for this purpose, or not?
It appears to me that it is entirely permitted. For if we were to worry about bal tashchit [wanton destruction] , as I wrote at the end of the previous siman in the name of Tosafot, that one should not give exposed water even to a tamei animal , that does not apply here, for he derives benefit from this and there is no bal tashchit, as Semag (Lav 229) wrote that wherever one has great benefit from the destruction there is no prohibition of bal tashchit. And so too it states in Tosafot in Avodah Zarah (11a) that it is permitted to render an animal a tereifah [when there is benefit]. And similarly we find in chapter HaShochet (Chullin 28b) that one who slaughters and needs the blood may perform nechira or tear out [me'aker]; and it is explained there regarding a wild animal where one needs the blood to dye wool , here too the case is exactly analogous. And similarly in chapter Kisui HaDam (Chullin 85b) where Rabbi permitted Rabbi Chiyya to render the animal a tereifah, slaughter it, and draw its blood to dye wool. And regarding the prohibition of engaging in commerce with neveilot and tereifot , that too does not apply here, since one renders it a neveilah in a permitted way; thereafter it is as if a neveilah came into his possession. For since he first acted in a permitted way for his own benefit, there is afterwards no prohibition , for the Sages only prohibited this when one originally intended to act in a prohibited manner, such as trapping tamei species [specifically for commerce] , not so here. And similarly in chapter HaYah Korei (Berakhot 14a), Rav said: he shall not begin; but if he began he completes; Abaye said: we do begin, since they begin in the West; and since we begin, we also complete , meaning, since he began in a permitted way, even though at the moment of beginning he knows he will finish [in a particular manner], here too it is the same. I also saw in a recently published responsum of the Rav R' Binyamin z"l [Nekudot HaKesef: he too permitted nechira of the rams]; except that in the gloss there it is written that the reason the Talmud permitted nechira and tearing-out to dye wool is that the person must afterwards discard the meat, on account of the prohibition of engaging in commerce with neveilot , and that is an error in my humble opinion. For if so, the Talmud should have stated that he must throw the meat to the dogs; and furthermore, in that case it should have prohibited the nechira from the outset too, lest he not discard the meat afterwards and eat it , for that is the very reason, according to Rashba cited by Beit Yosef, for which it is prohibited to deal in neveilot and tereifot. But it is a plain matter: anything one does first in a permitted way , such as when one needs the blood or the hide , the meat afterwards is as if a neveilah came to him, and he may sell it to a non-Jew. But one who does not know the laws of shechitah and wants to sell the meat to a non-Jew and wishes to slaughter improperly , this is certainly forbidden, for from the outset he makes it a neveilah solely for the purpose of selling the neveilah, not for any permitted reason. All this seems to me clear and obvious.
