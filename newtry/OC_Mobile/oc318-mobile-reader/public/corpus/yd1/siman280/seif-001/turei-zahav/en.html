If the tear is within two lines, he should sew it, etc. My father-in-law writes that until it is sewn, even a tear in one line makes the scroll unfit for reading. This does not appear correct. A tear of fewer than three lines does not entirely invalidate the scroll; the law merely teaches that sewing such a tear is permitted and is not considered aesthetically degrading. The reason sewing is not used for a tear of three lines is the requirement of 'This is my God and I will beautify Him.' Maharik's responsum regarding adhesive supports this: he argues that if a tear did not invalidate the scroll even before sealing, there would be no need to seal it. This implies that where no invalidation exists, sealing is not required. The same applies to sewing. There must be some minimum tear that does not invalidate, and the more compelling reading is that fewer than three lines is not an invalidation. The Beit Yosef's alternate explanation of Rambam, treating the matter as an unresolved question, is not primary.
He should not sew it. The reason is that the repair is unattractive and violates the principle of beautifying a mitzvah. Therefore the sheet should be replaced. An external parchment patch likewise does not cure an already invalid tear. Nevertheless, after the fact, if it was repaired by sewing or adhesive and this is discovered during the Torah reading, another scroll need not be taken out merely for this, because the defect is one of beautification rather than an absolute invalidation. Certainly, where no other Torah scroll is available, this one may be read.
And between one word and another, etc. According to Rambam this is the unresolved question in the Gemara, as the Beit Yosef explains. The Tur mentions only a tear between lines, apparently because his version of the Gemara did not contain the case between words. For Rambam, the earlier law may concern a tear between letters. Kesef Mishneh likewise explains it this way. Rambam rules leniently because there is no Torah-level prohibition here. A tear in the margin plainly does not invalidate a Torah scroll, as the Rosh writes in a responsum cited by the Beit Yosef. Nevertheless, practical halacha should be stringent regarding a tear between words just as between letters: within three lines it should not be left unrepaired.
And likewise, if something is missing, one writes it on a parchment patch, etc. This is unlike a case where the body of a letter was split by the tear, where gluing a patch does not help because the letter's valid writing itself depends upon the later adhesion and it was not surrounded by parchment at the time of writing. Where the parchment is merely missing, however, one may first attach a sound patch, thereby restoring the writing surface, and then write upon it. The attached patch becomes part of the sheet before the writing. Therefore even one letter may be written partly on the original sheet and partly on the properly attached patch. This is the intent of Maharik and follows from the Jerusalem Talmud's statement that scrolls may be glued with adhesive.
