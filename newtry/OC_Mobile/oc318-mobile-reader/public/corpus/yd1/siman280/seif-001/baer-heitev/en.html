He should sew. The Bach wrote: as long as they have not sewn it, even if it was torn only in one line, it is forbidden to read from it until he sews it. But the Taz wrote that this does not appear to him, for there is no complete invalidation here when it is less than three lines. Rather, he teaches that there is no prohibition in sewing, so that you should not say it is unbecoming thereby, which is the reason one should not sew when it was torn up to three lines, because it is unbecoming and we require "this is my God and I will beautify Him." Therefore he teaches that with less than three, it is not unbecoming. But in truth, even if he did not sew it, it is valid if it is not within three lines (and in Nekudat HaKesef he wrote that his words do not appear correct, for from the poskim it implies that one must sew; see there). If it is within three, the Taz wrote that one must remove the sheet, and it is simple that even attaching parchment from outside with glue does not help in this. It appears that after the fact, if he repaired it by sewing or glue and this was found during the reading, one should not take out another because of this, and all the more so if there is no other Sefer Torah, one should read from this Torah; end quote. The Bach wrote that this law of being torn applies specifically where the tear began in the sheet, in the margin above or below, and came within three lines; in this case he should not sew. But if the margin was not torn and it was torn only within the lines, even four or five lines may also be sewn (and Ra'em I siman 89 did not write so. We practice to glue parchment over it from outside, and Maharam HaLevi wrote: it appears that wherever the body of the sheet was not torn or split through and through, but only scratched upon the surface of the parchment and the parchment remains intact, there is no concern). If only the margin alone was torn above or below, they may read from it at the outset; end quote. This is correct. Nevertheless, it appears that at the outset one should repair whatever possible. Shach.
More. In Nekudat HaKesef he wrote that he found in many responsa that they ruled that even more than three lines may be sewn, and there is no doubt that had the Rav seen the words of those poskim, he would not have written so; end quote. The Bach wrote that with our Sifrei Torah, according to everyone one should sew, for the sinews are white and our parchments are also white; if so, it is known that the stitches are not noticeable in the Sefer Torah. Even with three lines one should sew, and likewise one may glue parchment over the tears with glue.
To a word. The Shach wrote: it appears to me that its explanation is that the first clause speaks between one letter and another, but the letter itself was nevertheless not ruined; here it speaks between one word and another, and not between one letter and another, and therefore he should sew. The Taz wrote: as a matter of halachah one should be stringent between one word and another as between letters, like the Tur's view in this, that within three lines one should not sew.
To glue. The Shach wrote: meaning, if it is a tear that comes within two lines or within three, and it is treated in such a way that sewing is permitted, gluing is also permitted. But if sewing is forbidden, gluing is also forbidden. From the Bach's words, however, it implies that even where sewing is forbidden, gluing is permitted, for with a tear within three lines where sewing is forbidden, the Rosh permits gluing. It appears to me that the Rosh is worthy to rely upon in pressing circumstances. However, all this is where the letter was not split; but if the letter was split, it is forbidden to glue in every case; end quote.
The glued piece. The Bach wrote that Maharik permitted the practice of patching with a patch over holes in a sheet before beginning to write a Sefer Torah, and afterward they write on the patch, unlike some sages who brought proofs to forbid; he rejected their proofs. But the primary view does not appear so to me, but rather like the forbidding authorities. Nevertheless, since the early authorities practiced reading from such a Sefer Torah before great authorities of the world and they did not protest, no great authority should protest and rule to forbid others. But one who is stringent for himself with his own Sefer Torah is praiseworthy, and blessing will come upon him. However, it appears that if he wrote part of a letter on the sheet and part on the patch, it is invalid according to all, for when the patch is separated there will be half a letter here and half a letter there. Moreover, even if the whole letter was written on the sheet, but some of the ink on one side of that letter also passes onto the patch, the parchment surrounding the writing on the patch does not help for the letter written on the sheet; rather, the letter on the sheet must be surrounded by parchment on the sheet itself without the patch, and likewise the letter on the patch must be surrounded by parchment on the patch itself without the sheet; end quote. The Taz disagrees with this and wrote that since glued parchment helps for what is written on it, so that it is as though it were written on the sheet, why should it not also help to write half on this and half on that? Was split. This is not comparable to what is written in Orach Chayim siman 32 seif 16, that if one of the simple letters was broken it is valid, for there it speaks where the form of the letter did not change, even though it is divided and not attached with glue; see there. So wrote the Levush.
