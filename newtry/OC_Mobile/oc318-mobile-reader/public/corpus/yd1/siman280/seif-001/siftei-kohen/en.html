If a sheet was torn, etc. This means that it was torn in the upper or lower margin and the tear extended into as many as two lines, as stated by the Tur. The Bach writes that the Tur appears to have explained the matter precisely in this manner. Only where the tear began in the margin of the sheet and extended into two lines does it follow that if it reached three lines it may not be sewn when the parchment was not treated with gall. If, however, the margin was not torn and the tear occurred only within the written lines, it may be sewn even if it crossed three, four, or five lines.
It may be sewn. As long as it has not been sewn, one may not read from the scroll. If the tear did not extend into a written line, but only the upper or lower margin was torn, one may read from it initially even before it has been sewn. The Bach states this, and it is correct. Nevertheless, it appears that initially one should repair everything that can be repaired.
If it is evident that the parchment was treated with gall, etc. Our Torah scrolls may be sewn according to all opinions, for the sinews are white and our parchment is also white, so it is evident that the stitches are not noticeable in the Torah scroll. It may therefore be sewn even across three lines. Likewise, one may affix parchment over the tears with adhesive. The Bach states this at length; see there.
Likewise, between one column and another it may be sewn, etc. This implies that in such a case it may be sewn under all circumstances. The Tur, however, maintains that if it was torn between one line and another, or between one column and another, away from the writing, but the tear was long enough that, had it been opposite the writing, it would have extended through three lines, it may not be sewn. See there.
And between one word and another. It appears to me that the first clause discusses a tear between one letter and another, where the letter itself was nevertheless not damaged. Here it discusses a tear between one word and another, rather than between letters, and it may therefore be sewn. The Tur, however, appears not to distinguish between a tear between letters and one between words. See there.
Whenever it is permissible to sew them. Meaning, if the tear extends into two lines, or into three lines on parchment treated with gall, in a manner in which sewing is permitted, adhesion is likewise permitted. If sewing is forbidden, adhesion is also forbidden. The Bach writes in seif 2 that the custom is to be lenient in accordance with the Rosh. His language implies that even where sewing is forbidden, adhesion is permitted, for the Rosh permits adhesion where a tear extends through three lines, although sewing is forbidden. An examination of the Rosh's words indicates even more strongly that adhesion is permitted in every case. It is possible, however, that the Bach meant only that the custom follows the Rosh rather than Maharam, who disagrees and maintains that adhesion is forbidden even where sewing is permitted, but not that the custom follows every leniency of the Rosh. Nevertheless, it appears to me that the Rosh is sufficiently authoritative to rely upon in pressing circumstances. All this applies where the letter itself was not split. If a letter was split, adhesion is forbidden in every case, as will be explained.
Likewise, if something is missing, one writes, etc. The Bach writes as follows: Maharik, in responsum 122, discussed the custom of placing a patch over holes in a sheet before beginning to write the Torah scroll and then writing upon the patch. Some scholars brought proofs to forbid this, and he rejected their proofs. In my humble opinion, the position of those who forbid it is correct. Nevertheless, since earlier generations read from such Torah scrolls before the great authorities of their times and those authorities did not protest, no later authority should protest or rule that others may not rely upon them. One who is stringent for himself regarding his own Torah scroll is praiseworthy, and blessing shall come upon him. It nevertheless appears that if part of a letter was written on the sheet and part on the patch, the scroll is invalid according to all opinions. Even when a letter is torn through its middle, although the adhesive holds it together, it is invalid because it is not surrounded by blank parchment, despite the entire letter having been written on the sheet. All the more so where part of the letter is written on the sheet and part on the patch, for if the patch were separated, half the letter would remain here and half there, as two separate pieces. Moreover, even if the entire letter was written on the sheet, but some of the ink on one side of the letter also extends onto the patch, the blank parchment surrounding the letter on the patch does not serve the letter written on the sheet. A letter written on the sheet must be surrounded by blank parchment on the sheet itself, without reliance upon the patch, and a letter written on the patch must likewise be surrounded by blank parchment on the patch itself, without reliance upon the sheet. I discussed this at length in my responsa. See the laws concerning these matters in Orach Chaim, siman 32, and what I wrote there.
If any letter was split, etc., it is invalid. This does not contradict the ruling in Orach Chaim, siman 32, seif 16, that if one of the simple letters was broken it may remain valid. There the letter's form was not altered, even though its parts were separated and not attached. See there. The Ateret Zahav states this, and it is cited in Maadanei Melech, folio 82a.
