Because of the concern that her daughter may imitate her. Her daughter may assume that her mother immersed on the night following the seventh day and may do the same herself. Immersion during the seventh day itself, however, is absolutely forbidden, as the Tur writes, lest she see blood later that day and thereby invalidate her clean days retroactively. See Taz, seif katan 8, in his explanation of the Tur, although his words are somewhat difficult and require study. This is also implied and clearly stated by the Gemara and all the poskim. I nevertheless saw that Maharil, at the beginning of Hilchos Niddah, writes that even on the seventh day the reason is the concern that her daughter may imitate her. He also writes that in our times, when our wives are not treated as possible zavos, and so forth. His words are difficult and require study; perhaps a textual error entered the books. The Shach writes that when a woman immerses on the eighth day, where the prohibition is only because of the concern that her daughter may imitate her, the custom is to be lenient and permit her to immerse before nightfall, provided that she does not return home until after dark. By contrast, when she immerses on the seventh day, no leniency is permitted, and she may immerse only after nightfall. One should protest against those who do not follow this practice. The Shach further writes that there is no distinction between a woman who has a daughter and one who does not, which is obvious.
And brides. The Shach writes that this leniency applies specifically in our communities, where brides do not immerse on the seventh day. In a place where brides immerse on the seventh day from the onset of their sighting, or even in our communities where a bride's immersion happens to fall on the seventh day, for example, where her count was interrupted and she must immerse immediately after completing seven clean days, she may not immerse during the seventh day. The prohibition is not merely the concern that her daughter may imitate her, but also the concern that she may see blood later that day and thereby invalidate the entire count retroactively, leaving her a zavah retroactively, as stated in the Gemara and the poskim. His words appear difficult at first glance. Since the reason brides are permitted to immerse by day is that they do not join the groom until nightfall, even if her count were invalidated retroactively there would seemingly be no concern of prohibited relations. It may be that the Shach means that on the seventh day we nevertheless decree lest she go to the groom during the day. On the eighth day, however, the prohibition is itself only a decree because of the concern that her daughter may imitate her, and we do not impose an additional decree upon that decree. This is readily understood.
