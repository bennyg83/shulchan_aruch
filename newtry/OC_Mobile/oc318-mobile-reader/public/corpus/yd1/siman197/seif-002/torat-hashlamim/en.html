So as not to neglect the mitzvah of being fruitful and multiplying. Without this consideration, however, immersion at its proper time is not itself a mitzvah. For this reason it is forbidden to immerse on Tishah BeAv and Yom Kippur even when the proper time for immersion has arrived, as explained in Orach Chaim, simanim 554 and 613. The immersion itself is not a mitzvah; it serves only to enable fulfillment of the mitzvah of marital relations, and relations are forbidden on those days. Accordingly, when Tishah BeAv falls on Shabbos, regarding which the Rama rules in Orach Chaim, siman 554, that the custom is to prohibit marital relations, she may not immerse. This is also stated by Olas Shabbos in the name of a responsum of Rabbi Yisrael of Bruna, contrary to what the Shelah writes on page 101a, permitting it on the ground that it is a mitzvah immersion. In truth, that reasoning is incorrect, for there is no independent mitzvah in the immersion itself. This is readily understood. I likewise heard in the name of my grandfather, the renowned gaon Rabbi Shimon, of blessed memory, that he was unwilling to permit it in such a case.
If she was unable to immerse earlier. If she could have immersed earlier but did not do so, she may not immerse on Shabbos, even though there is a mitzvah of marital relations, since she could have immersed before Shabbos. Where she was not negligent, however, she may immerse. Maharam of Lublin therefore ruled in responsum 71 that if a woman immersed on Thursday night and afterward discovered that the immersion had not been valid, for example, because she found dirt beneath a fingernail, she may immerse on Friday night. Similarly, if she immersed on Shabbos but had forgotten to cut a fingernail and therefore requires another immersion, then if there is dirt beneath it, or even if there is no dirt beneath it but the nail can be cut by a non-Jew, she must immerse again. This applies only if the non-Jew removes it without an implement, using his hand or teeth, as the Shach writes in Nekudos HaKesef, siman 198; see the Taz there. Even if she had relations with her husband and afterward noticed that she had forgotten the fingernail, the Shach writes that she may immerse again on Friday night. The Taz disagrees, maintaining that once she had relations she requires no further immersion, as is evident from his comments in siman 198. Maharam of Lublin also writes this in a responsum, and this appears to be the primary ruling. See there.
After childbirth. If she transgressed and immersed, Chut HaShani writes in responsum 70 that if she acted inadvertently she is permitted in every case; if she acted intentionally, she is forbidden to her husband for that Shabbos but is permitted afterward. On Yom Tov she is permitted in every case, even if she acted intentionally. See his lengthy discussion there. The proof he brings from Responsa Rashba, siman 785, cited by the Beis Yosef on Tur, Orach Chaim, at the end of siman 503, that on Yom Tov no prohibition is imposed after the fact when one transgressed and performed the act, is no proof. The Rashba's position is not accepted by most poskim, as is clear from the Rosh and the other poskim concerning the question of one who transgressed and baked, cited by the Beis Yosef in siman 527. Rambam likewise rules in chapter 23 of Hilchos Shabbos that one who intentionally separates terumah and maaser on Yom Tov may not eat the produce until after Yom Tov. Olas Shabbos writes similarly in siman 503, and this is also the view of Mordechai. As for the Beis Yosef's ruling in siman 503 permitting food that was cooked in violation of Yom Tov, one may explain that he refers to an inadvertent act, or even to an intentional act on Shabbos where the person has nothing else to eat, in honor of Shabbos, following the Rosh and those who agree with him, as cited by the Beis Yosef in siman 527, rather than relying upon the Rashba's reasoning. Nevertheless, Chut HaShani's practical ruling is correct. Even if she intentionally immersed on Shabbos, she is permitted after Shabbos, since the prohibition here is merely a stringency, and even its underlying concern is only rabbinic, because the immersion appears to repair her status. We therefore do not penalize an intentional offender by forbidding her permanently, as Chut HaShani writes there. This is unlike intentional cooking on Shabbos, where the food is forbidden to the offender permanently, since cooking entails a Torah prohibition. Furthermore, where a person acts with the intention of rectifying something, the penalty is not imposed so severely, as stated in Gittin 54. Although the third mishnah of the second chapter of Terumos appears to equate tithing and cooking in this respect by grouping them together, while one who tithes likewise intends to rectify the produce just as this woman intends to rectify her status, that comparison is not conclusive, as Chut HaShani writes there. This also appears clearly from Rambam. In chapter 2 of Hilchos Shabbos, regarding cooking, he rules in accordance with Rabbi Yehudah that if one cooked intentionally, the food is forbidden to him permanently, while if he cooked inadvertently, it is forbidden to him until after Shabbos. Yet in chapter 23 he rules that one who tithes on Shabbos inadvertently may eat immediately, while if he acted intentionally he may eat after Shabbos. The distinction must therefore be as stated. These words of Rambam escaped the notice of Chut HaShani and of the Tosafos Yom Tov in his commentary to that mishnah; see there. All the more so here, where the prohibition is only a stringency and a custom. Certainly on Yom Tov, which is not mentioned at all by Terumas HaDeshen, the Rama, or the other acharonim, who discuss only Shabbos, it appears that one should not be stringent at all. We should not extend the stringency beyond its stated scope; see Orach Chaim, siman 511. I do not know the basis for Chut HaShani's ruling that immersion is forbidden ab initio on Yom Tov. He attributes this to Terumas HaDeshen, yet Terumas HaDeshen makes no mention of Yom Tov. Moreover, according to the reason given by Terumas HaDeshen, it appears that the ruling applies specifically to Shabbos. Consider this carefully, and see Responsa Maharam of Lublin, siman 53.
After childbirth. If she saw impure blood, meaning after thirty-three days following the birth of a boy or sixty-six days following the birth of a girl, and the time for her immersion falls on Friday night, she may immerse, since this is the proper time for her immersion. Shach, in the name of Maharil.
But in a place where there is no established custom, one should not be stringent. The Bach rules stringently regarding these laws, and the Taz follows him. The Shach, however, disputes the Bach, and in Nekudos HaKesef he writes at great length to reject all the arguments of the Taz and to uphold the Rama's words, which are sound and clear. See there, for his reasoning is correct.
A widow. This applies specifically to a widow. Since a first act of relations with her is forbidden on Shabbos, the husband is separated from her with respect to every form of intimacy. This differs from a virgin whose immersion falls on Friday night. Although the custom is not to consummate the marriage for the first time on Shabbos, there is still a mitzvah to gladden the groom and bride through other forms of closeness, embracing, and kissing. Nevertheless, it is preferable for her to immerse before Shabbos begins. Thus concludes Maharam of Lublin in responsum 93. The Shach writes that this is the accepted practice.
