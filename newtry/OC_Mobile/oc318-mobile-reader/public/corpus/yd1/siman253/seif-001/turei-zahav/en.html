One who has fourteen meals should not take from the charity fund. In the chapter Kol Kisvei, the Gemara asks whose opinion this follows. According to the Sages, there should be fifteen meals, since three meals are required on Shabbos. According to R' Chidka, who holds that four meals are required on Shabbos, there should be sixteen. The Gemara answers that this follows R' Akiva, who said: Make your Shabbos like a weekday and do not become dependent upon others. Rashi explains that regarding Shabbos, one who is able should prepare Shabbos meals either according to the Sages or according to R' Chidka. However, one who must depend upon others should make his Shabbos like a weekday and should not impose the honor of his Shabbosos upon others, end quote. The Perishah cited this in the name of the Rash's explanation of the statement, “Make your Shabbos like a weekday,” and wrote that this applies specifically according to R' Chidka, whereas according to the Sages there is no difficulty. He did not examine the sugya that I cited, for even according to the Sages we say this. One should not challenge this from the end of siman 250, where it is stated that on Shabbos he is given food for three meals. Tosafos answers that he should not begin taking charity merely because of the Shabbos meals, but if he already takes charity, he also receives enough for the Shabbos meals.

The Tur writes that one must sell a silver menorah and a silver table. The Beit Yosef asks that the Gemara states that Rav Zevid gave this answer and was refuted, since one may say, “I will not accept anything that is not silver.” My teachers answered that the Gemara does not refer to an actual table, but to the cloth spread upon the table. This does not appear correct to me. Rather, it seems that there is a distinction between their tables and ours. In Talmudic times, each person had a small individual table, as Tosafos writes in the chapter Arvei Pesachim, 100b, s.v. “The table is not brought.” The table was therefore like an eating utensil designated specifically for him. Even regarding eating utensils, it appears that the exemption applies only to what is designated for him personally. A large utensil that he uses together with his household certainly should not be retained in silver for the sake of his household. Even regarding arranging the needs of a debtor, we say that provision is made for him, but not for his wife and children. The reference must therefore be to what is designated for him personally, and the same applies to his individual table. Nowadays, however, when one table serves all those dining, he must certainly sell it if it is silver. For this reason, the Rambam also did not list a table among the eating utensils that are retained for him. This appears clear and correct to me.
