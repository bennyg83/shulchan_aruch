He may not take, etc. Their practice was to distribute food from the tamchui every day and from the communal charity fund each week. Therefore, one who has food for one day may not take from the tamchui, and one who has food for fourteen meals may not take from the communal fund. The Semak, at the end of siman 267, writes that the laws of the communal fund and tamchui are not practiced in our times. It appears that he means the detailed regulations of the communal fund described below in siman 256. Certainly, however, every Jewish community must maintain a charity fund, as the Rambam and the Mechaber write at the beginning of siman 256.

For his wife's kesubah. Rabbeinu Shimshon explains that this applies even to a married woman. The Bach writes that the husband designated utensils and jewelry for his wife before the wedding, appraised them at a stated value, and recorded that value in her kesubah.
But a scraper, etc. The Tur writes that he must likewise sell a silver lamp and table. See the Derishah and the Bach, who explain the Tur's reasoning. The Bach rules that this is the primary view: a table, lamp, and similar items, from which his body does not directly benefit, must be sold. See there. The Rosh, Tur, and other poskim further write in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that if these utensils came into his possession only after he had already received charity, such as through inheritance or finding them, he is not required to sell them. Rashi further writes, as cited by the Rosh and Rabbeinu Yerucham, that if it later became known that he had actually been wealthy and possessed two hundred zuz, beit din collects from him what he received. If he lacks sufficient funds to repay, he must sell his expensive household utensils and use less costly ones. Although charity funds have no individual claimant, the money is collected from him as a penalty. The Bach writes that one who rules in accordance with Rashi should not be criticized.
