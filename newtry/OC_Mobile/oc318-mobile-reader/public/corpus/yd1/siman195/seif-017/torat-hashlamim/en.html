If her husband is a physician. The Beit Yosef follows his own approach in what he wrote concerning the Ramban's responsum cited in siman 127. He states that although the Ramban's words imply that if her illness is dangerous and no other physicians are available, her husband may treat her because of pikuach nefesh, this may be because the Ramban holds that touching a niddah is prohibited only by rabbinic law. According to the Rambam, however, who holds that intimate contact with a forbidden relation is prohibited by Torah law, perhaps even in a case of pikuach nefesh it is forbidden as an accessory to forbidden relations, and the matter requires study. The Shach challenges the Beit Yosef and writes that even according to the Rambam there is certainly no Torah prohibition unless the contact is performed in a manner of desire and intimate affection, which is not the case here. He adds that the accepted practice is for Jewish physicians to feel the pulse of a woman, whether Jewish or non-Jewish, even when non-Jewish physicians are available. His words, however, are not compelling. Since according to the Rambam any pleasurable bodily contact is prohibited by Torah law, it is possible that feeling her pulse is forbidden because the matter may readily lead to a Torah prohibition. This also appears from the Ramban's responsum itself, which implies that according to the opinion that every affectionate approach is prohibited by Torah law, namely the Rambam's view, one should be stringent here. The Shach's appeal to common practice is likewise no proof, because there is greater concern regarding one's own wife while she is a niddah, since he is familiar and comfortable with her, than regarding another woman, as stated in the responsum of the Rashba, siman 1188, and in the gloss to the Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer, siman 21. The Rama, who rules contrary to the Beit Yosef, relies on the glosses to the Shaarei Dura and on Hagahot Maimoniyot, which permit it, as he writes in the Darkei Moshe; see there.
