To put it away in an enclosed courtyard. The Beit Yosef permits this in the name of Rabbeinu Yerucham. Maharil, however, records that Mahari Segal instructed that immediately after the circumcision all the instruments should be removed from the mohel's hands, for they become muktzeh, and that he should not return to take the knife from the sand where he placed the foreskin while occupied with periah. The Maharshal also permits carrying it afterward, as the Rama rules here, because it was fit for use at twilight and there is no muktzeh for only part of Shabbos. Although I am not worthy to decide between these great authorities, it appears clear to me that carrying it is forbidden. The Gemara in Beitzah teaches that a pestle, though permitted for cutting meat on Yom Tov, may not be carried after its permitted use has ended. The same applies to a roasting spit after the meat was roasted. A circumcision knife is comparable: once the mitzvah has been completed, it returns to its muktzeh status. The principle that there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos applies where an item was fit at twilight, later became unfit, and then became fit again. Here it was fit at twilight only for the mitzvah itself, not for later carrying. Rabbeinu Yerucham's permission should therefore be understood narrowly: the knife may be placed safely in the same room so that it will not be lost, since otherwise a mohel might refrain from performing the circumcision. It may not be carried elsewhere. The same reasoning applies to a shofar after the blasts have been completed. Even before the circumcision, the knife may not be carried unnecessarily, but only for the needs of the mitzvah.
Through roofs and courtyards. This refers to utensils that were resting in those areas when Shabbos began. All roofs and courtyards are regarded as one domain for such utensils, even when they belong to different owners. The prohibition applies only to utensils that were inside a house when Shabbos began. Such utensils may not be carried from the house into the courtyard unless the houses joined in an eruv, and even after such an eruv they may not be carried from that courtyard to another courtyard that did not join with it.
Even if the courtyards joined with the houses. This means that each house made an eruv with its own courtyard. We do not decree that permitting utensils that rested in one courtyard to be carried into another courtyard will lead people to carry utensils that rested in a house from one courtyard to another.
