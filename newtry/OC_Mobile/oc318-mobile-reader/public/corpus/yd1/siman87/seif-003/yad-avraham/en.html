(There) That it is not forbidden from the Torah except through cooking. The Acharonim wrote that iruy cooks at least kedei kelipah, and if so it is the manner of cooking and forbidden from the Torah. And in my humble opinion it is not forbidden from the Torah. And proof from the Talmud in the beginning of chapter 25 (daf 104), where they say there that it is forbidden to raise meat, even of fowl, on a table where one eats cheese — a decree lest domestic animal meat, which is forbidden d'oraisa. And it challenges: this is cold with cold, and if so it would be a decree upon a decree, for from the Torah it is not forbidden except through cooking. And it concludes: a decree lest one raise it in a boiling pot, which is cooking. And now, why do they strain to establish it thus — for behold there is no way to raise the pot in which they cook on the table, as Tosafot Chadashim wrote in the margin, and they did not establish it in a second vessel. And even though a kli sheini does not cook, nevertheless when one pours from the pot to a kli sheini it becomes forbidden kedei kelipah. Rather, learn from this that there is no d'oraisa prohibition here, and if so it is permitted in benefit. And likewise siman 91 seif 4 — see there. And another proof for this: in the Gemara in Pesachim (daf 76) regarding meat that fell into milk, Rav Ila's view prevails: if it is cold, kedei kelipah suffices. And challenge Rav: why is the milk permitted — it is nevelah milk, for kedei kelipah from the milk becomes forbidden, and min b'mino is not nullified according to his view, as we challenge according to him in chapter Kol HaBasar (daf 108) regarding an olive-size of meat that fell into a cauldron of milk. Rather, it is necessary that from the Torah this is not the manner of cooking where it does not cook but only kedei kelipah; therefore we did not forbid where peeling is impossible, such as a moist thing like milk. And study this carefully. And so I found explicitly in Ran at the end of chapter Kol HaBasar that hot into cold is not the manner of cooking. And Beit Yosef brings it siman 92 — and if so, everything is permitted in benefit. And what is written there: only through cooking. And if roasting is included in cooking — the Acharonim disagree, and the view of Peri Chadash is that roasting is included in cooking in basar b'chalav. And he brought proof from that which we find regarding Pesach, where it is only roasted and it is written "and you shall cook and eat" — apparently it calls roasting cooking. And likewise in Ma'adanei Yom Tov. And in Chavat Da'at he wrote it is not proof, for in Bava Metzia the Talmud challenges: wherever "his hand" is written it is literal, and it is written "and he took all his land from his hand," and it answers: that case is different, for there one cannot say his hand literally; but where one can say it is literal, an amplification is needed to say it is not literal. And if so, one can say here too: although the verse "and you shall cook" means roasting, it is because there one cannot say anything but roasting; but where it says actual cooking, an amplification is needed for roasting — end of his words. And it appears the sugya in Nedarim (daf 54) was hidden from him, where they teach: R' Yoshiah forbids roasted to one who vowed from cooked, and even though there is no proof for the matter, a remembrance — as stated "and they cooked the Pesach in fire as its manner." And it says: let us say R' Yoshiah holds in vows one follows Torah language, and it concludes that he holds one follows the language of people, and in the place of R' Yoshiah even roasting is called cooked, and the verse he cited is merely an asmachta. It implies that according to the view that in vows one follows Torah language, he holds that one who vows from cooked is forbidden in roasting everywhere. And likewise in Yerushalmi they derive from this that regarding Pesach the verse calls roasting cooking. And likewise there explicitly in the explanation of Rosh and Ran. Apparently one can derive from Pesach generally, as Peri Chadash and Ma'adanei Yom Tov wrote — only regarding vows one does not derive for one who holds one does not follow Torah language in them; but generally he agrees we derive from Pesach that roasting is included in cooking of the verse. And likewise Mordechai in law 92, and these are his words: every roasted thing is called a dish, as written "and they cooked the Pesach." And likewise in Rosh chapter 2 of Beitzah. And see Tosafot in chapter 96 (daf 96). And what Chavat Da'at challenged — from Pesach one cannot learn generally, for it is different because one cannot say that "and they cooked" is actual cooking, just as one cannot learn from "and he took all his land from his hand" that it is not literal — it appears they are not comparable at all. For there certainly one cannot err at all and say his hand literally took his land, and therefore the verse need not be expounded thus even though generally "his hand" is literal. But here regarding Pesach, if in general roasting is not called cooking, the verse should not have called it here cooking, and it should not have relied on Pesach whose law is roasting; nevertheless one might err and say there is cooking that is permitted, such as after roasting — and in this the verse "and they cooked the Pesach" deals. Rather, learn from this that roasting is called cooked. And greater than this is clear from the words of Rambam in parashat Bo that even without a verse we say roasting is included in cooking. And according to this, since roasting is included in cooking in basar b'chalav, it is forbidden in benefit. And it appears that if a drop of milk fell on a roasted piece and there is no sixty against it, the entire piece is forbidden in benefit — not like what is written in Chagurat Shmuel that it is not forbidden in benefit in roasting except kedei kelipah, for many poskim hold that milk is like oil and spreads through the whole piece. And one can bring support for this from that which is stated at the end of Ketubot (daf 110) on the verse "a land flowing with milk and honey" — oils from milk and sweets from honey. And also that it is also stated in Sifri parashat Ekev: oils like milk, etc. — it implies milk is oil. And even from aggadah we learn what was not explained in the Talmud; and that which we do not learn from aggadah is to decide halacha where they argue in the Talmud, as Peri Chadash wrote at the beginning of Mayim Chaim. Or specifically if it contradicts the transmitted general rule, as Radbaz wrote siman 567, and in Kenesset HaGedolah brought in Be'er Yaakov at the beginning of Hilchot Gittin — and the author of Be'er Heitev disagreed with Peri Chadash there. And it appears Peri Chadash follows his reason as stated. And it appears the law is with him, for in Yerushalmi beginning of Pe'ah they compare aggadot to Tosefta, and certainly from Tosefta we learn when there is nothing against the general rule — and likewise from aggadah. And again I found this reasoning in Noda BiYehuda Even HaEzer siman 161 in the name of Chayei Adam; and what Noda BiYehuda rejected. (Here deleted from the manuscript.) And further: on a piece where milk fell, all agree it spreads through the whole, as proven in Yoreh Deah siman 92 and siman 105. And likewise he proved in Torat Yekutiel siman 91 that for this matter it is for all like oil that spreads through the whole. But again I saw in Raavan siman 17, who challenged the commentators regarding kutach, that roasting is called cooking and they derive from Pesach. And he wrote: I do not hold that there it is written "as its manner" and teaches about "and they cooked" that it is roasting, which is its manner; but here it is not proven, and the tanna need not teach that one might err in it — end. And this is like Chavat Da'at. And one can also prove like Raavan from the sugya in Nedarim that I brought, where R' Yoshiah says "and even though there is no proof for the matter, a remembrance." And Ran wrote: however, it is not a full proof, as stated in the conclusion that in vows one follows the language of people. And it is difficult: if so, according to the view that R' Yoshiah holds one follows Torah language, why is it not a full proof? But according to Raavan it works out well that it is not proof even according to that view. And on this basis one can also say the matter stated in Mekhilta in Mishpatim on the verse "and it is found in his hand" — his hand is only his dominion, and even though there is no proof for the matter, a remembrance — as stated "and he took all his land from his hand." And at first glance it is a full proof. But because it is different that one cannot say his hand literally, as stated in the Gemara in Bava Metzia (daf 56), therefore Mekhilta intended this. Only in Yerushalmi chapter HaZorek it states that R' Yishmael derives from the verse "and he took" that his hand is his dominion.
