(Ibid.) "Exempt, and one does not receive lashes for eating them, etc." See in siman 88, where he wishes to prove from the words of Tosafot, chapter 25, daf 114, s.v. HaMevashel b'Mei Chalav, that "exempt" means exempt and it is permitted to cook l'chatchila. For Tosafot wrote: "One who cooks in milk whey is exempt, but there is indeed a prohibition, for kuttach is made from milk whey, and we said above that it is forbidden to eat meat with kuttach" — implying that only in that case is it forbidden l'chatchila, but other things where we do not find explicitly that they are forbidden, it is permitted to cook them l'chatchila. But his words have no basis whatsoever. On the contrary, from there it is proven that "exempt" here means exempt but forbidden. For one could ask: how does Tosafot bring proof from kuttach, which is forbidden to eat, to the matter of cooking — perhaps it is only forbidden to eat, but to cook is permitted even l'chatchila? It must therefore be that Tosafot holds that anything that is forbidden to eat is likewise forbidden to cook l'chatchila. If so, the same applies to all other prohibitions that our Sages forbade to eat on account of basar b'chalav — cooking is likewise forbidden.

They further wrote that the vessel in which water is prepared for washing, etc., which is made from the ash upon the stove, etc. The Tzemach Tzedek raised the question: is it not established law that the ash of a stove impairs flavor? If so, what is the concern, since its taste has already been impaired? One can answer: the Tosafot in the chapter Gid HaNasheh, daf 100b, wrote as follows: According to the interpretation of R' Eliezer, it is well understood why the Torah commanded to kasher all utensils, etc. — for do not the initial waters produced during kashering, etc., carry the absorbed taste and return and render things forbidden, etc. And one might say: when a utensil is not ben yomo, one koshers it, since the Torah only prohibited a pot that is ben yomo. But commentators raise the objection: how would this have helped if one were to kasher those that are not ben yomo? — for then one would have to say that the Torah also prohibited noten taam lifgam; if so, the question still returns to its original place, that the waters give taste and return and render things forbidden. And one can answer: the Rambam wrote that the reason noten taam lifgam is permitted is because it is used not in the normal manner of deriving benefit from the forbidden substance. But regarding basar b'chalav, the Bach holds that using it not in the normal manner of deriving benefit is also subject to liability, as stated in Pesachim daf 26. If so, a new ruling emerges according to the Rambam's approach: if the taste of meat or milk was absorbed into a pot and it impairs flavor, it is permitted, since it is not considered basar b'chalav at all — for there is no taste of meat here to render the milk forbidden, since the taste of the meat impairs flavor. But this is not so if the pot was already rendered forbidden through cooking basar b'chalav — even if it is not ben yomo and its taste has also been impaired, it is nonetheless forbidden, since it has already been rendered nevelah through basar b'chalav. And even though the taste has now been impaired, even so it is forbidden, for in basar b'chalav even using it not in the normal manner of deriving benefit is also forbidden. According to this, there is no difficulty with the Rama. For here we are dealing with the prohibition of basar b'chalav: even though it is impaired, it is nevertheless forbidden. And with this, the aforementioned words of the Tosafot are also resolved. For what it says — that when a vessel is not ben yomo one koshers it — is not because of concern for other prohibitions, regarding which it is correctly established that noten taam lifgam is permitted. Rather, what it writes — that the Torah required commanding the kashering of a vessel that is not ben yomo — is because of the concern that perhaps the gentile had been cooking basar b'chalav in it, in which case even noten taam lifgam is also forbidden. And if so, one cannot raise the objection: but the waters themselves give taste and thus render other things forbidden! This is not a valid objection, for it is established law that regarding a prohibition of mashehu, one does not apply chatichah naaseit nevelah. For if we were to say that in all Torah prohibitions noten taam lifgam is forbidden, then the established law that taam k'ikar d'oraisa applies even to noten taam lifgam would truly be d'oraisa, and one could correctly apply chatichah naaseit nevelah to it. But now that we say that in all Torah prohibitions noten taam lifgam is permitted, therefore the ruling that basar b'chalav is forbidden even when it impairs flavor is only like a prohibition of mashehu, and regarding mashehu one does not apply chatichah naaseit nevelah. Deliberate on this carefully.
