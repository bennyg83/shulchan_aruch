He may demand. Beit Yosef writes that this applies not only where one lent one hundred for one hundred fifty, but even where monthly interest was stipulated.
To defer him. Shach writes that with an arev shaluf dotz, even interest already paid may be recovered from the Jewish guarantor, unlike Rama. Bach is still more lenient, but the authorities disagree. Shach understands the leniency of Rabbi Yosef Korkos as applying only to an arev kablan, not shaluf dotz.
To give. Taz writes that a borrower who agreed to reimburse all damages from late payment need not repay profits the lender pays to a third party, even through a permitted loan. Reimbursement of such financing costs is still interest. Taz also discusses partnership borrowing: if one active partner borrows from a non-Jew for the business, the interest may be treated as a partnership expense; if a passive investor borrows personally and contributes the funds, he may not shift that interest to the partnership.
Already. Taz disagrees with Rama and would recover interest already paid. Shach and Nekudot HaKesef uphold Rama. Taz permits paying a fee to an arev kablan because the fee compensates assumption of risk, not a loan. He forbids paying one who becomes the sole promissory-note obligor, though Nekudot HaKesef doubts this and notes lenient custom.
