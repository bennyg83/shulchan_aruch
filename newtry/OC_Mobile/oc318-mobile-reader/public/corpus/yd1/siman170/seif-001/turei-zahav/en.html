Question: Reuven paid Shimon a fee to serve as an arev kablan for him to Levi, who would lend Reuven money. Is there any concern of ribbis in this arrangement? Perhaps Shimon will ultimately have to pay the amount that Levi lent Reuven, in which case Reuven will become Shimon's borrower, and the fee Reuven paid Shimon will then constitute ribbis.
Answer: It appears that according to Rashba and the authorities who follow him, cited by the Beit Yosef at the beginning of siman 170, the prohibition where a Jew becomes guarantor to a non-Jew for another Jew for principal and ribbis applies specifically to an arev shlof dotz, meaning that the non-Jewish lender has no right at all to demand payment from the borrower and can compel him to go directly to the guarantor. Where the non-Jewish lender may demand payment from whichever of them he chooses, the arrangement is permitted. Accordingly, our case is certainly permitted, for here too the lender may demand payment from whichever party he chooses. Shimon therefore does not have the status of Reuven's lender; rather, both are liable as borrowers toward Levi. One might argue that after Shimon is actually forced to pay Levi, Shimon then becomes Reuven's lender. That does not create a problem. The same possibility exists in the permitted case of a Jew who guarantees a Jewish borrower's debt to a non-Jew where the non-Jew may collect from either one. The guarantor might ultimately pay first. We necessarily follow the status at the time of the original loan: at that time the guarantor was not the borrower's lender. When afterward he seeks reimbursement from the borrower, it is as though at that later point he lent him the amount that he paid to the non-Jew on his behalf. The borrower then owes him reimbursement because the original arrangement was made permissibly. The same applies here. The fee paid initially is not considered in relation to a later loan, because at the time the fee was given Shimon was not Reuven's lender; both were borrowers or obligors toward Levi. Only afterward, if Shimon pays, a new debtor-creditor relationship arises between Reuven and Shimon, and that is a new circumstance. Nor should one say that this case is worse because in the ordinary guarantor case the guarantor receives no ribbis from the borrower at all: he merely receives reimbursement of exactly what he paid the non-Jew, whereas here Reuven paid Shimon a fee in advance, and perhaps that fee was paid because Shimon might later have to pay and become Reuven's lender, making it analogous to advance ribbis. That cannot be correct. If so, it would be forbidden to pay someone any fee even to serve as an ordinary guarantor, since perhaps the guarantor will ultimately have to pay and the borrower will then owe him reimbursement. But this is plainly not so. The Rosh writes explicitly in a responsum, cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of Choshen Mishpat, siman 129, that where one promised his fellow compensation for becoming his guarantor, the obligation to pay that fee requires a kinyan. This clearly shows that receiving compensation for serving as a guarantor is fully permitted. The reason is that the fee is not paid for any later loan. It is paid for the service the guarantor performs now by inducing the lender to advance the funds to the borrower and by accepting responsibility so that the lender is more secure. At the outset the guarantor does not intend or expect that he will actually have to pay; if he anticipated that result, he would not have agreed to guarantee the debt. Therefore the compensation cannot be characterized as ribbis for a future loan. This is comparable to what I wrote at the end of siman 173 concerning one who promises one hundred litrin, etc., according to the explanation I gave there. See there. On this basis, it appears to me that even Rashi, whose view the Beit Yosef cites at the beginning of siman 170 regarding a Jew who guarantees a Jewish borrower's debt to a non-Jew, would agree here. Rashi permits that guaranty only when the non-Jew accepts that he will not demand payment from the guarantor first, but only after first demanding payment from the borrower and finding that he cannot pay. If the non-Jew may demand payment first from whichever party he chooses, Rashi forbids the arrangement. Nevertheless, in our case, where the Jew receives compensation merely for becoming guarantor for another Jew, Rashi would agree that it is permitted, because the compensation is not ribbis paid for a loan that may arise later, as explained. However, if one pays another person a fee so that the latter alone will issue his own note to the lender, that is certainly forbidden, because the second person immediately becomes the sole borrower from the lender and then lends the proceeds onward to the first person. Where one pays another person a fee to issue his own note to a seller for merchandise that the buyer wishes to purchase, because the seller does not trust the buyer, there is a permissible arrangement. The guarantor should say to the seller: "I give you my note for the money, and I am the purchaser." The guarantor then becomes the purchaser from the seller, and afterward he may make a new sale of the merchandise to the original buyer. This is plainly fully permitted. So it appears to me.
