Behold it is permitted. Not only if he lent one hundred for one hundred fifty; but even if he stipulated with him to give him such-and-such interest per month — Beit Yosef in the name of Beit HaTalmud; and Darkei Moshe brought it.
And some say that he is not, etc. Beit Yosef wrote that ab initio we rule that whenever he can claim first from the guarantor it is forbidden, until he accepts upon himself not to claim from him until he first claims from the borrower and cannot collect from him, or where the borrower is violent and the court cannot extract from him, or he does not come to court, such as if he fled and the like. Any guarantor who is like an ordinary guarantor is permitted. But after the fact we rely on the "some say" view, and where the money stands, we leave it there, like doubts where the burden of proof is on the claimant, end quote. It is clear from his words that with an arev sheluf dotz, even if he already paid him the ribbit, it is extracted from him, unlike the Rav's view. In Darkei Moshe and Bach he is even more lenient and rules like R. Yosef Korkos, that it is forbidden only ab initio, but after the fact he may eat all the loss, since it is because of him that he loses, and there is no ribbit in this, even with an arev sheluf dotz. The view of all the poskim is not so. In my humble opinion, R. Yosef Korkos himself was lenient in this only with an arev kablan, but not with an arev sheluf dotz; see there. With this is settled what responsa Masat Binyamin at the end of siman 67 left as a difficulty on R. Yosef Korkos and the Rav, that the Gemara implies that with a Jewish guarantor it is Torah-level ribbit. One can say the Gemara speaks of sheluf dotz. In any case there is no difficulty, for one can say he holds like Baal HaItur, brought by Beit Yosef, that the Gemara speaks specifically of a non-Jew who borrowed from a Jew, etc.; see there.
