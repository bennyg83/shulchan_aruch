And if the Jew is guarantor for the principal, etc. The Rama's position requires study. It appears to permit even a form of guaranty in which the lender may proceed directly against the guarantor, yet such an arrangement should be forbidden. Terumas HaDeshen discusses a Jew who purchases half of a non-Jew's interest-bearing debt from another Jew, with the seller guaranteeing the purchaser's principal. He rules stringently because, although the interest comes from the non-Jew, the purchaser gives his money to a Jew while relying upon that Jew's guaranty, so the transaction appears as ribbis between Jews. The Rama learned from that responsum both a permission where the non-Jew himself receives the money and a prohibition where a Jew receives it. But the cited sources do not establish a general permission for a Jewish guarantor of principal where the guaranty makes him directly liable. Hagahos Ashri records Avi HaEzri's ruling only after the fact: where a Jew improperly became guarantor for a non-Jew for principal and interest, the guarantor must at least repay the principal, because the money was advanced on his undertaking, while the interest may be collected only from the non-Jew. That source therefore proves the existence of the prohibition rather than a permission.
Or in any case, it is only as a guarantor for the interest, etc. Levush wrote: and some say that even without collateral — even if the non-Jew took the money directly from the lender — and even if the Jew became guarantor only for the interest — it is forbidden to be an arev kablan for the non-Jew, since when the Jew says to him "as long as my money is with the non-Jew I will give you a dinar per month," it follows that as long as the non-Jew does not pay, he is obligated to give the interest — and thus responsibility for the principal is also upon him until the non-Jew repays. It is as if he borrowed money from a Jew and said "as long as I give you a dinar per month you cannot compel me to give you the principal" — end quote Levush. But if he gives him the non-Jew's collateral for the principal, etc. Darkei Moshe explains that a loan made upon a non-Jew's collateral is not regarded as a loan to the Jew, because the lender relies upon the collateral, as explained in siman 169. He further compares it to one who says, 'Here is a zuz; lend to so-and-so.' In my view, however, if the Jew bears responsibility in addition to the non-Jew's collateral, the arrangement remains problematic. The Rama himself permits only a guaranty for the interest while not guaranteeing the principal; if the Jew truly guarantees the principal, the responsibility is his and the collateral does not alter that. The Bach distinguishes a broker who merely brings the non-Jew's collateral from one who already owns a debt secured by such collateral and asks another Jew to lend him upon it, but his distinction conflicts with his own earlier analysis. In my humble opinion, the essential rule follows the substantive allocation of liability.
