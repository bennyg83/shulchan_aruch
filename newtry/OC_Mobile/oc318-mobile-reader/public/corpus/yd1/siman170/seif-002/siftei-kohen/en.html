And if a Jew became guarantor, etc. The law turns upon whether the guaranty makes him the effective borrower under non-Jewish law or merely provides secondary security for the non-Jew's debt.
That he has the means to pay. And he wishes to pay — to exclude one who is mute, or fled, and such — as stated in s.k. 1 in the name of Beit Yosef.
If he guaranteed only the principal, etc. Even then one must determine whether the lender may proceed first against the Jewish guarantor. If so, the arrangement may have the character of a loan from Jew to Jew.
But if the non-Jew remains the primary debtor, etc. Where the lender must first seek payment from the non-Jew and the Jew is only a secondary guarantor, the principal may be permitted, though the interest requires separate analysis.
And if collateral of the non-Jew is in his possession, etc. Collateral securing the non-Jew's own debt strengthens the characterization that the loan is truly to the non-Jew rather than to the Jewish intermediary.
If the collateral was returned at the Jew's direction, etc. The Jew who caused the lender to relinquish security may become liable like a guarantor at the time the money was advanced, because the lender relied upon his undertaking.
He must pay the entire principal and interest. He need not first sue either the non-Jewish borrower or guarantor. With collateral, even without a formal kinyan, he must pay because the collateral was returned at his direction, making him analogous to a guarantor at the time of the loan. Whether releasing merely a guarantor, rather than the borrower himself, requires a kinyan is less clear. If the Jew is an arev kablan, however, his status is like the borrower himself.
But not the interest etc. Even if they performed kinyan from his hand — Beit Yosef.
