A guarantor. For in their laws the borrower directs the lender to the guarantor, from whom he collects the debt. If so, it is as though the gentile lent to the guarantor and the guarantor lent to the borrower. Even if he is not a shlof dotz guarantor, it is forbidden, because it emerges that the lender's money increases with the contractor, and it turns out that a Jew is taking interest from a Jew. But above in seif 1, where a Jew borrowed from a gentile, although the lender's money increases with the contractor, we are not concerned, for the gentile is the one taking the interest, and it is permitted according to the "some say" view that it is forbidden only with shlof dotz. Shach.
The principal. The Taz wrote that Rama's view here requires study, for it implies even in a prohibited case, such as shlof dotz, and as it appears this is forbidden; he discussed proofs and demonstrations at length, see there. (In Nekudat HaKesef he challenged him and wrote: what prohibition is there for a Jew to be a guarantor for the interest, even with shlof dotz? Certainly there is no prohibition at all. Is it forbidden to say, "I will give you interest," etc.? It is only forbidden to fulfill his guarantee, namely to pay the interest, but in mere guarantorship there is no prohibition at all. If so, it is obvious that where he guaranteed only the principal, it is permitted to fulfill his guarantee; and even if he is a guarantor for both the principal and the interest, it is permitted when he pays only the principal and not the interest, for mere guarantorship has no prohibition; end quote.) From his hand. The Shach wrote that in this case, even if he did not become a guarantor at all, it is forbidden, for since there is no agency for a gentile, the Jew who takes the money is only a borrower; and since he takes the money from the hand of a Jew, it is forbidden. Nevertheless, it appears that this prohibition when the Jew takes the money applies even if he is guarantor only for the principal specifically when the Jew later gives the interest; but if the gentile gives the interest, it is permitted. Collateral. The Levush wrote: and some say that without collateral, even if the gentile took the money from the lender's hand, and even if the Jew guaranteed only the interest alone, it is forbidden for the Jew to be a contractor-guarantor for the gentile. Since the Jew says to him, "as long as my money is in the gentile's hand, you give me a dinar per month," then as long as the gentile does not repay, he is obligated to give him the interest. It turns out that responsibility for the principal is also upon him until he repays it, and this is like one who borrows money from a Jew and says to him, "as long as I give you a dinar of interest per month, you have no right to compel me to repay you the principal"; end quote. The Bach challenged him that these words are from Ba'al HaTerumot, and he speaks where the Jew takes the money from the lender. The Shach wrote that this is no challenge, for since the reason depends on the fact that if the gentile does not repay he is obligated to pay, it follows that responsibility for the principal is upon him, etc.; this implies that there is no distinction. But it appears that the words of Ba'al HaTerumot are not relevant here, for there he speaks where he explicitly says, "as long as my money is in the gentile's hand, you give me a dinar per month," etc.; therefore, since he cannot force him to repay as long as he gives him the interest, responsibility for the principal is upon him, and he is guarantor for both principal and interest. But where the Jew has no responsibility for the principal at all, it is permitted, as written in Darkei Moshe, for it is like "take a zuz and lend to so-and-so," which is permitted; end quote, Shach. The Bach wrote that if he has in his hand a gentile's collateral upon which he already lent, and he wants his fellow to lend to him upon it, and he wants to be guarantor even only for the interest, it is forbidden, for it is close to profit and far from loss. But Rama speaks of where he is a broker and brings a gentile's collateral so that a Jew lend upon it; then it is permitted to be guarantor for the interest and not for the principal. The Taz challenged him and wrote that he contradicts his own words that he brought from Ba'al HaTerumot: whenever the Jew takes the money from the Jew for the gentile and he is guarantor for the interest, it is as though he is guarantor for the principal. What does the collateral help here, since there too he relies for the principal only on the gentile, and nevertheless it is forbidden? (According to what I wrote in the name of the Shach, that Ba'al HaTerumot does not speak of such a case, this is not difficult at all. So too in Nekudat HaKesef he wrote that the plain custom is that a Jew gives a gentile's collateral for the principal and is guarantor for the interest.) The Shach wrote about these words of the Bach, that if a Jew already lent upon the collateral, etc., this is specifically where he explicitly sold it, and in a case that is permitted only because of sale. But in the unspecified case that I wrote above in siman 169, where it is not permitted because of sale but because it is the gentile's collateral and has not been definitively acquired by the Jew, then even if the Jew gives the interest it is permitted. Likewise, where he sells it to him, the prohibition is only to become a guarantor; but where he did not become a guarantor and the lender later agrees to return it to him, it is permitted to give him the interest. See above in siman 169 seif 18 in the gloss regarding these laws. To pay. The Shach wrote that he need not sue either the borrower or the gentile guarantor. With collateral, even if he did not make a kinyan with him, he must pay, since he returned the collateral at his word; he is like a guarantor at the time the money is given, as written in Choshen Mishpat siman 101 seif 5. However, where he exempted the guarantor, it requires study whether a kinyan is required or not. Although Choshen Mishpat siman 129 seif 3 states that if he exempted the borrower at the guarantor's instruction it is like the time the money is given, one may say that this is specifically where he exempted the borrower and no further dealing remained with him, only with the guarantor alone. But here, where he exempted only the guarantor and still has dealings with the borrower, he was not completely released. However, all this is with an ordinary guarantor; but with a contractor-guarantor, his law is like the borrower himself, and it is as though he exempted the borrower himself. And onward. The Taz wrote that if this Jew made a kinyan for this, he would be obligated to pay everything, even the interest that accrues from then onward, for a guarantor not at the time the money is given becomes obligated through a kinyan. But the Shach wrote in the name of the Beit Yosef that in this case, even if they made a kinyan from him, he is exempt from paying the interest from then onward.
