Guarantor. Under their law the borrower may direct the lender to the guarantor, so it is as though the non-Jew lent to the guarantor and the guarantor lent to the borrower. Even without shaluf dotz this may create Jewish interest because the lender's money increases with the Jewish guarantor. Shach.
The principal. Taz questions Rama's implication that even an otherwise prohibited guarantor arrangement may be fulfilled as to principal. Nekudot HaKesef answers that merely becoming guarantor is not forbidden; only paying the interest is. Therefore principal may always be paid.
From his hand. Even without formal guarantee, if the Jew receives the money from another Jew he becomes the borrower, because a non-Jew has no halachic agency. If the Jew later pays interest, it is forbidden; if the non-Jew pays the interest directly, it is permitted. Shach.
Collateral. Levush discusses whether a Jew may guarantee only the interest where the non-Jew receives the funds directly. Shach distinguishes an explicit arrangement in which ongoing interest makes the Jewish guarantor effectively responsible for principal, which is forbidden, from a case with no principal liability, which is permitted. With adequate non-Jewish collateral, common practice permits a Jew to guarantee interest alone. See siman 169.
To pay. Shach writes that the lender need not first sue the borrower or non-Jewish guarantor. Where collateral was returned based on the guarantor's instruction, the guarantor must pay even without a kinyan, like a guarantor at the time of the loan. Whether release of a guarantor requires a kinyan remains uncertain, except an arev kablan is treated like the borrower himself.
From then onward. Taz writes that if the Jew made a kinyan, he must pay even future interest because a guarantor after the loan becomes obligated through kinyan. Shach cites Beit Yosef that even with kinyan he is exempt from interest accruing thereafter.
