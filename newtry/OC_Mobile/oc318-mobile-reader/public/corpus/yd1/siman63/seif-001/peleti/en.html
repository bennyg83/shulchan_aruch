And here I say: were it not for the Maggid Mishneh and those who came after him, I would say that the Rambam's intent does not disagree with our Gemara; rather they said in the Gemara that Israelite slaughterers must announce if there is treifah, and the authors gave the reason that the norm of the law among Israelite slaughterers is that one may buy from a gentile, for certainly most are kosher and from the majority it separates — only that if treifot happen to an Israelite slaughterer they would not be permitted to sell to a gentile lest he sell it to an Israelite; therefore they enacted announcement. And in a place where they do not announce it is forbidden to buy from a gentile; and the Rambam holds that certainly by law one should not forbid found in a gentile's hand because of meat that disappeared from sight, for he did not remove his eyes from it; but the Sages saw fit to forbid any meat not supervised by an Israelite — what did they do? They thought to nullify the announcement so they would not announce, so that automatically there would be no permission to buy from a gentile; therefore the Gemara says for the law where they would announce: found in a gentile's hand is permitted and there is no concern of meat that disappeared from sight. But in the Jerusalem Talmud Rav forbids — meaning: a pretext was found so they would not announce, and meat fell to the butchers; and if so, automatically it is forbidden by law; and this is what the Rambam wrote, that the Sages forbade all meat because it has no announcement at all, as he never mentioned announcement anywhere in the Rambam. And it must be said: since it is forbidden because it disappeared from sight, the announcement is nullified — this is what I would say. But the Maggid Mishneh does not appear from his words to hold thus; rather he holds simply: since meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden, how does announcement apply — therefore the Rambam omitted announcement, for it is not in reality; and in such a case where one stands and sees and buys from butchers and does not remove his eyes — this is not a case for which they would enact announcement; and if so, the challenge returns to its place, that in the Gemara we say that in a gentile's hand meat that disappeared from sight does not apply; and likewise in the Gemara in many places they say 'they announced' and 'they did not announce' — this is not in reality; and see below s.k. 10 what is written there. And what I would say briefly on this is that there are two aspects in the matter: certainly according to Rashi's explanation, regarding found in a gentile's hand removal of sight does not apply, for presumably the gentile did not remove his eyes; however according to what the Maggid Mishneh wrote explaining the Jerusalem Talmud, there is more concern for a raven regarding found in a gentile's hand, since otherwise there is a side of doubt perhaps he took treifah from the shops — see there. And even though the Rambam wrote 'even if all sellers are Israelites' — nevertheless it deals in a place where they do not announce, as written; and if so, what difference does it make that the Israelite slaughterer perhaps slaughtered treifah and sold to the gentile — for it is permitted to sell from this; one may say: if there is no treifah at all in the shops and all are Israelites — then even the Rambam agrees it is permitted, as appears from the Maggid Mishneh's words; and they did not forbid only because of concern for a raven. And with this the Rambam's words in Mishnah Shekalim are understood, that he explained: pieces are permitted — meaning in a city that is entirely Israel; but if there is a gentile in it — forbidden because of meat that disappeared from sight. Taz wondered: in an Israelite city too; and according to what I said it is settled: in a place where there is a gentile we are concerned lest Israelites sold him treifot, not so when entirely Israel — there is no concern, and they were not concerned for meat that disappeared from sight — requires further study, for it appears that in his composition the Rambam retracted — requires further study. And it appears that this depends on two views among the authors in that which we say: everything that separates from the majority is presumed to have separated from the majority — whether this is definite and decisive with no measure of doubt at all, and we are witnesses that from the majority it separates, as Tosafot imply in the first chapter of Ketubot and in many places; and accordingly the Maggid Mishneh's view in what he wrote that for doubt of a raven we also combine doubt that perhaps he took from treifah shops — for behold most shops are kosher and from the majority it separates — if so, this is certainly from kosher shops and there is no doubt at all. However according to what I said in Asifas Zekenim in the first chapter of Bava Metzia regarding 'one of the appointed leaped into it,' etc., and he wrote in the name of the Rosh: even though we say that from the majority it separates — nevertheless it is doubt, only the Torah permitted this doubt — see there. And accordingly the Maggid Mishneh's words are clear: even though most shops are kosher — nevertheless there is doubt perhaps treifah separated. And it appears that in these views Rav and Shmuel disagreed at the end of the first chapter of Ketubot and chapter 9 of Yoma, whether we follow the majority for pikuach nefesh or not — for Rav explains from the majority idolatry, and one does not desecrate Shabbat for it, for from the majority it separates and an idolater is certainly present; and this is because he holds like the aforementioned Tosafot view that there is no doubt at all here. But Shmuel holds we do not follow, etc., and we desecrate Shabbat for him — it implies he holds that nevertheless it is doubt, and doubt of pikuach nefesh overrides Shabbat; for if there were no doubt here, from where in the Torah would we desecrate Shabbat for him. And it is not difficult that our Gemara's sugya follows Rav, who holds there is no remnant of place for doubt at all, as I wrote — therefore it answers well regarding found in a gentile's hand we are not concerned for a raven, for he did not remove his eyes, as Rashi explained; and the Maggid Mishneh's view that there is also doubt perhaps because of treifah — is not so, for according to Rav there is no doubt at all. But the Jerusalem Talmud follows Shmuel's conclusions above, from which it is proven that he holds that nevertheless there is doubt in it; and if so, the Maggid Mishneh's view is correct from the outset to be lenient regarding a gentile's matter, where there is also doubt perhaps from treifah shops; and we who rule like Shmuel rule like this Jerusalem Talmud, and the Rambam's words are clear — requires further study. In the second case it appears to me from ancient times what they challenged greatly from that which we say there in the Gemara, and this that Rav said meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden was not stated explicitly, but from his inference — for this man was a tanna to the first part: he threw one and brought up two; Rav said to him: such things happen here and we forbid. They said in the Gemara: if from his inference — what? And they answered: there were gentile hides — know that his prohibitions are more common, end of his words. Be that as it may, Rav did not say explicitly that meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden, but inferred from the inference of the aforementioned incident; and the Gemara challenged him that one cannot learn from it; and see Tosafot who challenged that perhaps we do not learn from this because he did not remove his eyes, and Rav pushed it off. If so, according to the Gemara everywhere one cannot rule like Rav, since we do not find it stated explicitly from him, only by inference — and it is difficult on Rif and Rambam and other authors who ruled meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden — from where is this, since Rav never said it; and it requires further study at first glance, for I did not find in any author who addressed this. And what I would say must be thus: certainly in the essence of the law that meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden — it is implied explicitly from Rav, who said limbs found in the borders are permitted, and Rav explained because of nevelot, but prohibitions stand — meaning Rav. And it must be said: if so, what is he answering — if he said from inference or explicitly; and it appears that with this he answers: whether meat that disappeared from sight is a definite and decisive prohibition, or only a concern and doubt of prohibition. And see below in my general rules s.k. 15 that Issur VeHeter's view is that meat that disappeared from sight is not a definite prohibition like gentile cheeses, only a doubt of d'rabbanan prohibition; and if so, from that which is found in the borders, etc., it is proven there is concern of prohibition, but we did not hear of definite prohibition; and Rav said meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden — it implies clear and decisive prohibition. And on this the Gemara says Rav did not say explicitly, only from inference in that incident where Rav forbade both heads; and see in Tosafot that it deals in a place where most slaughterers are Israelites, and they forbade because of concern for ravens — and it is difficult; and if so, one piece should be permitted from double doubt, lest this is the one that fell, and you will say: no, it is not the one that fell, lest it did not come from ravens but fell there from Israelite slaughterers. (Especially Rambam in Laws of Forbidden Foods implies one doubt in itself and one doubt in mixture is double doubt; and see siman 29 what is written in Hagahot Rosh on mixture one in one.) And see in my general rules s.k. 15 that it implies one in one does not suffice; but see there what is written on this, that we are not clear on this law; and therefore it must be said that Rav makes it definite and forbidden and there is no doubt here, and therefore we forbid; and on this the Gemara pushes off that one cannot learn from there, for there most were gentiles, and if so there is no doubt that certainly treifah mixed with kosher one in one; and if so, such is truly the conclusion that Rav does not make it a definite prohibition, only a doubtful prohibition; and if so, it is not difficult why the Gemara challenges 'found,' etc., permitted — what is the difficulty; perhaps this is Torah law, but afterward the Sages forbade it because it disappeared from sight — and this is exactly the Rambam's wording: this is Torah law, but the Sages forbade it. And he wrote: and it must be said — what difference does it make; nevertheless there is great difference: if kosher piece mixed with this piece, if treifah because of majority of treifah shops — obviously forbidden; but if kosher only because it disappeared from sight — forbidden, it has double doubt as I wrote and is kosher. Only our Gemara's sugya follows the view that it is definite prohibition, and also one in one treifah; and if so, it challenges well — what difference if forbidden for this reason or that; therefore the Gemara had to say that regarding found in a gentile's hand there is no meat that disappeared from sight at all. But in the conclusion the Gemara says that from inference nothing is implied, and Rav never said it; and in truth in mixtures one in one it is permitted; and if so, one may well say that which we teach 'regarding found one follows the majority' is Torah law, and nevertheless now the Sages forbade it, as I wrote; and there is no difficulty 'what difference' — there is difference whether one in one is forbidden or not, as stated.
