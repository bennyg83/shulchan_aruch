A person is obligated to divide his learning: a third in Written Torah — that is, the twenty-four books; a third in Mishnah — that is, Oral Torah; and explaining Written Torah is included in this third in Talmud — that is, that he understand and comprehend the end of a matter from its beginning, and extract a matter from within a matter, and compare matter to matter, and judge with the measures by which Torah is expounded until he know how the roots of mitzvot are and how to derive forbidden and permitted and the like — matters learned from oral tradition. How so? Like one who has a craft and engages in his work three hours a day and nine in Torah — three in Written Torah, three in Oral Torah, three understanding matter from matter. When do we say this? At the beginning of a person's learning; but when he grows in Torah and need not always learn Written Torah nor always engage in Oral Torah — he reads at fixed times Written Torah and words of Oral Torah so he not forget matters of Torah law, and spends all his days in Talmud alone according to the breadth of his heart and settles his mind: {Rama: And some say that in Talmud Bavli, which mixes Scripture, Mishnah, and Gemara, a person fulfills his obligation at once (Tur in the name of Rabbenu Tam, and see above siman 245 seif 6); and a person should learn only Scripture, Mishnah, Gemara, and decisors following them — and by this he acquires this world and the World to Come, but not other wisdoms (Rivash siman 45, students of Rashba); nevertheless it is permitted to learn other wisdoms casually, provided they are not books of heretics — and this is called among the sages strolling in the orchard; and a person should not stroll in the orchard until he has filled his belly with meat and wine — that is, knowing prohibition and permission and laws of mitzvot (Rambam end of Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah).}
