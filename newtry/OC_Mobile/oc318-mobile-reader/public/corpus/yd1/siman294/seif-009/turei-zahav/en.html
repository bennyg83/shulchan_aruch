The language of this seif follows the Tur; and Derisha explained it as referring to the formulation preceding it — namely buying grapes sold outside [the vineyard]; but we cannot say that in any situation it is permitted, for then it would follow that even a Jew picking [himself] is permitted — but that is forbidden. And I proved from what the Tur wrote in siman 296 at its beginning — regarding doubt whether vegetables were sown there in prohibition, a Jew may not pick with his own hands — a fortiori regarding orla which is a law from Moses at Sinai, that a Jew may not take from the doubtful produce himself. And the Tur holds that in orla — which is a law from Moses at Sinai in the Diaspora — it is forbidden to buy if one sees the non-Jew picking from there; but in kilayei hakerem, which is only rabbinic in the Diaspora, they were lenient — it is not forbidden even if one sees the non-Jew picking; only a Jew may not pick from it himself. But this is not clear to me, for the Tur's beginning contradicts his end: at the beginning he only wrote that one must not see [the grapes] picked from orla plantings — implying the only concern is not to see the non-Jew picking from them in one's presence; but seeing him from a distance picking from a vineyard with a mixture of permitted and forbidden is permitted — and as he explicitly states: even knowing they came from that vineyard; and knowledge and sight are equivalent. Yet at the end he requires specifically that [the non-Jew] sell outside the doubtful vineyard, and specifically without one seeing him pick from there. But this is a fortiori: it is clearly more serious to have certain forbidden [produce] present — unknown which grapes he picked — than to be uncertain whether any forbidden grapes exist at all, as in the principle of double doubt [safek safeka] explained in siman 110. And my father-in-law of blessed memory wrote that even at the beginning it is forbidden if one sees the non-Jew picking from that vineyard, even without seeing him pick specifically from the orla; and what is written "that one should not see [the grapes] having been picked from orla plantings" refers to plantings with doubtful orla; and the reason one may buy even knowing they came from that vineyard is because knowledge and sight are equivalent. But this is not at all the plain meaning — would the Tur write so obscurely? And he wrote explicitly: "even knowing they came from that vineyard" — which implies sight and knowledge are the same. Furthermore, why should it truly be forbidden for a Jew to pick himself from a vineyard with doubtful orla, given the Torah explicitly states that doubtful orla is permitted? It seems to me these things make good sense, and the explanation is: the law from Moses at Sinai regarding orla says all doubtful cases are permitted — as certainly permitted, as if we know for certain there is no orla; and it is therefore no more serious than kilayim in the Diaspora, where in a doubtful case there is a rabbinic element of prohibition and one takes extra precaution. But in orla the doubtful case is explicitly ruled as certain leniency — once there is doubt we act as if certainly permitted. This explains the Tur's statement about doubtful orla where there is a mixture: the non-Jew's picking is uncertain — from which did he take? We may rely on this, since the Torah declared the doubt as certainly permitted. And you will thereby understand Rema's language: "and all the more so a vineyard that is doubtful" — he changed slightly from the Tur, who wrote "and needless to say a vineyard, etc." without the letter shin; Rema wrote "she-karem" [that a vineyard] with a shin — to indicate he refers to the vineyard itself being permitted without the need for a non-Jew to sell outside; and so it is in Mordechai chapter 1 of Kiddushin in a gloss: "they asked about a vineyard that had four or five orla plantings whose locations were unknown, and my teacher forbade the entire vineyard; but it seems to me in practice that everything is permitted, even eating from clusters while still attached to the ground, since doubtful orla is permitted as a law from Moses at Sinai." And regarding doubtful kilayim, by all Talmudic opinions a Jew may not pick by hand — from this it is proven as I wrote that here the doubt makes it superior to kilayim. And Rambam has a different approach from the Tur and is lenient even in the certain case; and Rashi approaches similarly; and for practical halacha the Shulchan Arukh ruled like the Tur, which is stringent — and all is correct through divine assistance, without stumbling.
