If they did not stipulate. This requires study in Orach Chaim, siman 244, seif 3.
It is forbidden. The non-Jew may not later take two years of orlah while the Jew takes two corresponding permitted years. Even if the non-Jew already took the orlah years and the Jew took the permitted years, and they afterward divide without an express arrangement, it remains forbidden. Whenever they did not stipulate initially, the Jew may not subsequently benefit from the orlah fruit at all. This is evident from the unresolved question at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah concerning an unstipulated arrangement.
Provided they do not come to account, etc., against those fruits that are forbidden, etc., ostensibly implies he means: even though they stipulated initially in general that the gentile eats two of orlah and the Jew two permitted, forbidden afterward to come to account after time; but Rambam's language chapter 10 law 14 that the author copied is thus: provided they do not come to account, etc., against those fruits, if they stipulated on this it is forbidden, for it is like exchanging, etc.; and in chapter 6 of Hilchot Shabbat law 17: a partner, etc., if they stipulated initially that Shabbat wages go to the gentile alone, little or much, and one day's wage against Shabbat day to the Jew alone, permitted; and if they did not stipulate initially, when they come to divide the gentile takes all Shabbat wages alone and they divide the rest, end quote. And the Rav Hagahot brought braita end of chapter "work of a gentile": Israel and gentile who leased a field in partnership, the Jew may not say to the gentile: take your share on Shabbat and my share on weekday; if they stipulated initially, permitted; and if they came to account, forbidden perforce; and our Rabbi did not mention "they came to account"; and it seems he explains like Rashi's explanation: this is if they did not stipulate initially and also did not say "take your share on Shabbat and I on weekday," rather this one stood and took his share himself on Shabbat and at the end they came to account, forbidden, end quote Hagahot; and Derishah and acharonim followed his view in Orach Chayim siman 245; and I did not stand on the end of their view: how can one say Rambam's view is thus, behold regarding orlah he wrote if they came to account, forbidden even if they stipulated; and so Tur siman 7 in Rambam's name; and Shabbat and orlah are one law, as proven in the Talmud per Rambam's explanation and as Ran wrote end of chapter work of gentile. But to me it seems clear he explains that which the Talmud says "and if they came to account, forbidden", not like Rashi, rather like Raavad that Ran brought: it refers to when they stipulated; and he holds: therefore what we said "and if they stipulated, permitted" means specifically that they stipulated the gentile eats two orlah and Jew two permitted, little or much, and they did not analyze the account; but if they came to account, meaning they stipulated thus that the gentile eats days of prohibition and afterward they calculate how much fruit the gentile ate on prohibition days until Israel eats against those fruits, if they stipulated thus, forbidden; for only when they stipulated in general that the gentile takes on Shabbat and Israel on weekday, little or much, then the field on prohibition days belongs to the gentile and Israel has no share, and the field on permitted days belongs entirely to Israel; but when they stipulated to calculate afterward, then it is like exchanging orlah fruits and is not considered a stipulation. Per this is also settled what Tosafot and Ran challenged: why does the Talmud say there "if they came to account, forbidden", ostensibly permitted, lest we teach that even if they came to account, even if stipulated, forbidden; certainly per their explanation that "came to account" means after time, difficult; but per Rambam no difficulty, for one cannot say we teach that even if stipulated, forbidden, for since they stipulated to come to account, the stipulation is void from the start, investigate. (And the question what is hinted regarding plain stipulation, Rambam in what he wrote that if they did not stipulate initially, forbidden implies even if the Jew does not afterward say "take two orlah and I two permitted", even in general forbidden.) And if so in Hilchot Shabbat he mentioned "came to account," for he wrote if they stipulated initially that Shabbat wages to gentiles alone, little or much, meaning only such a stipulation that forever belongs to the gentile, little or much, and they will not come to account, helps (also from Bach's words siman 7 it seems he understood Rambam explains "came to account" after time after the stipulation and they also stipulated at time of stipulation; and the language "if they stipulated thus" is strained and not so, rather as I explained; and one could say also Taz who omitted in Rambam's words "if they stipulated thus," etc., nevertheless their intent is thus: "provided they do not come to account", meaning they should not be precise at time of stipulation in the account; but Taz seems he understood "provided they do not come to account" means after time.) Investigate, for all this seems clear to me.
And some permit, etc. This is unlike Shabbat, where all agree that a Jew may not tell a non-Jew, ‘You take your share on Shabbat and I will take mine during the week,’ unless they stipulated initially, as explained in Orach Chaim, siman 245. There, the prohibition concerns agency for labor performed on Shabbat. Here there is no prohibited agency, and the Jew's receipt of corresponding produce is not considered benefit from orlah, because the arrangement is that one partner works and therefore receives the produce. This is the explanation of Rashi, the Rosh, and the poskim.
Only that the Jew says to the non-Jew, etc. They must not later make an exact accounting. If they do so, all agree that it is forbidden where they did not stipulate initially. Rabbeinu Yerucham, Netiv 28, part 2, states likewise.
