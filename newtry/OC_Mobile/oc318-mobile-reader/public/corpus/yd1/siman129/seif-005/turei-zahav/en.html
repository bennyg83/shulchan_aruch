One who gives over a key, etc. The Beit Yosef wrote that the language of the poskim implies that even if he gave him the key without qualification, we say that he gave him only custody of the key. This is unlike the implication of the Rambam's language, that he must explicitly say that he is giving him only custody of the key.
And he is afraid to enter it. This is not comparable to one who leaves his wine in a city of non-Jews without a key or seal. Here, the other non-Jews are afraid of the non-Jew who holds the key, and he in turn is afraid of them, lest they tell the Jew that he entered. This is from the Beit Yosef in the name of the Ran.
Regarding what is written in seif 6, that the Jew heard the sound of the shofar, the Rashba in Toras HaBayis HaAruch asked: If so, why did he forbid above when the Jew informed the non-Jew that he was going far away? Let us say that the Jew may remember his wine and return. When he goes to observe Shabbos or to pray, it is as though he informed him that he was going away. He answered that we say he may remember his wine only where he went away for matters that can be done here, such as prayer and observing Shabbos. When he remembers his wine, he can return and pray here or observe Shabbos here. But where he goes away for something that cannot be done here, such as bathing in a bathhouse and the like, it is forbidden. This still requires further study, end quote. The Tur disagrees and writes that since the reason is that he may remember his wine, this applies in every case. Even though the matter cannot be done here, he will not allow his wine to be lost because of it, end quote. The Rashba's difficulty must therefore be answered according to the Tur. It appears that the Tur resolves it differently, and the matter is entirely clear in my humble opinion. In the two incidents in the Gemara, namely where he heard the sound announcing Shabbos and where he heard the call to prayer in the synagogue, it was not stated without reason that he departed because another event suddenly prompted him. The Gemara did not merely say that he left on his own for these matters. Rather, only in such a case, where he did not leave on his own but departed suddenly, is his wine forgotten. The non-Jew sees this and is therefore afraid that perhaps the Jew will return upon remembering his wine. This is unlike the cases at the beginning of the siman, where the Jew informed him that he was going far away. There, the Jew certainly considered the matter before departing, and the non-Jew thinks that the Jew intentionally left his wine with him and trusts him. He is therefore not afraid. I later found this answer in the Ra'ah's Bedek HaBayis. The Rashba in Mishmeres HaBayis rejected it with a dismissal that has no substance to anyone who examines his words. A proof to our explanation is what is written below in seif 9, that they both departed in alarm. The essential ruling there depends on this circumstance, as will be explained, and it was derived from the fact that the Gemara records the incident in that manner. If so, the same applies here. Since the Gemara records that the incident occurred in this manner, the matter certainly depends on it. This also resolves another difficulty. In seif 1 it states that if the Jew said to the non-Jew who was transporting the wine, Go, and I will come after you, it is forbidden. Why do we not say that the non-Jew is afraid that the Jew may remember his wine and quickly follow him? According to the Rashba's answer, I do not know how this can be resolved. According to what I wrote, however, it is clear. The beginning of this siman certainly speaks where the Jew intentionally left him. The central issue is whether the non-Jew thinks that the Jew entrusted him with the wine. Therefore, when the Jew informs him that he is going far away it is forbidden. Likewise, if he says, Go, and I will come after you, the non-Jew sees that the Jew trusts him with the wine, since he tells him to go with it and does not accompany him to guard it. Why should he think that the Jew will later change his mind, since he did not leave in alarm? A change of mind is relevant only when he leaves because of others or departs unintentionally, so that he may later remember and regret it. This appears entirely clear in my humble opinion. I have seen other answers to this difficulty that are altogether unreasonable and require no response.
