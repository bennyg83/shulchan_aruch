Even if they are stoppered. This means with a wooden stopper, for only when sealed with clay is it permitted. The soldiers are not sufficiently afraid of people to trouble themselves to reseal it with clay if they opened it. But replacing a wooden stopper is not done out of fear; it is simply the normal thing to do, since it involves no effort. Tosafos wrote that although the barrels are stoppered, we do not say that the soldiers came for money, as above, because an army comes only to eat and drink, end quote. One cannot derive from this a leniency regarding present-day armies that come to plunder money, because they also come to drink. This applies specifically at their encampment. But while they are actively plundering, God forbid, which is the latter case, that during war there is no time to pour libations, it is different. I am astonished, however, by the conclusion here that we are concerned about sealed barrels that were opened. These are his own words, as he wrote in the Beit Yosef in the name of the Ran. Why do we not say that they came for money, since it is wartime? One must say that this speaks in a place where people do not ordinarily hide money in barrels. Tosafos could also have answered this way, but preferred an answer that applies even where money is hidden in barrels. The Shulchan Aruch does not discuss that circumstance. We therefore learn that nowadays, when soldiers search for hidden valuables even in barrels, one may be lenient even regarding sealed barrels that were opened. This is exactly the law of thieves stated in seif 11. It applies specifically during the emergency, God forbid, while they are plundering, when they are occupied only with monetary spoil and not with drinking. But if they remained there after the emergency had passed, they certainly opened the barrels to drink. Those that had been sealed and were then opened are therefore forbidden. This appears entirely clear in my humble opinion.
