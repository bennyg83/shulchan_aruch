A barrel of wine that floated in a river and was found opposite a city whose majority are Jews: if there are obstacles in the river and a dam (meaning a blockage) of ponds of water that would have held it in place had it come from elsewhere, it is permitted, for we attribute it only to the city opposite which it was found; but if there is no dam of ponds of water in the river, it is forbidden, for we attribute it to the majority of the surroundings, which are non-Jews. And if it was found opposite a city whose majority are non-Jews: if most of the surroundings are Jews, and it can come there by a straight route without sinking, it is permitted — even if it is quite close to the city of non-Jews — unless its closeness proves that it belongs to a non-Jew; we set aside the “closeness” and follow the majority. And if not, the wine is forbidden in benefit, and the jug belongs to the finder. And if a Jew came and gave a distinguishing mark on it, even the wine is permitted, and it belongs to the finder, for since the majority are non-Jews, the owners despaired. {Rama: And this is specifically when it is sealed with a stopper, but if it is completely open, the wine is forbidden, for we are concerned that perhaps they touched it.} (So it appears from Ran, perek Ein Ma’amidin, and so it is in Tur, netiv 17).
