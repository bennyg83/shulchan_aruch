Specifically when they departed in alarm. This is according to the Rambam. He derived it from the incident in the chapter HaSocher, where they heard the sound of a quarrel and went out. Since the Gemara mentioned that the incident occurred in this manner, it follows that the ruling depends on it. I already wrote that according to the Tur, whose view is primary, the incidents of hearing the shofar and hearing the call to the synagogue likewise depend specifically on the sudden departure. The Beit Yosef explains why they must depart in alarm here. Without alarm, we are concerned that the non-Jew saw the Jew in the marketplace, left him there, and returned home to pour a libation. He is not afraid, because he left the Jew in the marketplace. But when they leave in alarm, a person sometimes leaves his companion in the marketplace, yet when the latter returns home he discovers that his companion preceded him, for in the confusion he passed before him unnoticed. The non-Jew therefore fears that perhaps the Jew preceded him, end quote. What he wrote, that without alarm we are concerned that the non-Jew saw him in the marketplace, is astonishing. Do we forbid wine merely out of doubt? How is this different from siman 128, seif 3, where out of doubt we do not say that the non-Jew locked the door, even though there is an interior key? This is also evident at the beginning of siman 129, as cited there. It is clear that wine is not forbidden until the basis for prohibition is established. This is also evident from the Mishnah concerning one who did not inform the non-Jew that he was going far away. We do not say that perhaps the non-Jew assumed that the Jew was going far away, though that was in fact the case. Likewise, in seif 10 we are lenient out of doubt, and so in many places. Furthermore, what the Beit Yosef wrote, that when they left in alarm, even if the non-Jew saw him in the marketplace he might not have noticed him, is an excessive leniency. The non-Jew's entry was not in alarm; only the departure was. Frequently a person leaves in alarm because of a disturbance outside, but once the disturbance subsides he regains composure. All the more so here, where the alarm was because of a wedding or funeral. Upon returning home there is no alarm. The Gemara and poskim should therefore have stated that the entry too was in alarm. It therefore appears to me that the reason the case specifies that they departed in alarm is to prevent us from forbidding the wine on the ground that the non-Jew is not afraid that the Jew is guarding it. For if the Jew did not trust the non-Jew, why did he go outside and leave his wine, without fearing that the non-Jew might lock the door, as he actually did, and pour a libation, since he has a connection to the house? Therefore, it was necessary that he leave in alarm, so that he did not remember his wine at that time. The non-Jew is then concerned that perhaps the Jew has already remembered his wine and returned home. According to this, if the non-Jew saw the Jew in the marketplace while returning home, it is clearly forbidden whenever the non-Jew locked the door from inside, even though the departure was in alarm. We do not say that the confusion causes him to fear that he did not see properly, for we have no proof for such a leniency, even if he was still confused upon entering. Conversely, where there was no alarm, there is no basis to be stringent merely because perhaps he saw the Jew in the marketplace, for we are not stringent out of doubt. Rather, the reason is that the non-Jew thinks that the Jew trusts him, for otherwise why did he leave his wine? Although at the beginning of this siman, when he leaves him in a shop without informing him that he is going far away, it is permitted, there we do not know that the non-Jew closed the door, so we say that he did not close it and is afraid that the Jew may return. Here, however, there is a definite adverse circumstance before us, for the door is certainly closed. The only ground for permission is that perhaps the Jew already preceded him upstairs. If the Jew did not depart in alarm, the non-Jew reasons that since he left deliberately he certainly remembered his wine. Why, then, would he have hurried so greatly to precede him? Therefore, they required that his departure be in alarm, so that he did not remember his wine at the time, but may subsequently have remembered it and hurried ahead. This appears entirely clear in my humble opinion.
