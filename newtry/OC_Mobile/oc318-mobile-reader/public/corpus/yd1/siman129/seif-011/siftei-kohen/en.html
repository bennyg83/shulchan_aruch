Ishmaelites, etc. For they are not idolaters, as above siman 124 s.k. 12 and siman 128 s.k. 1; and the same applies to other gentiles nowadays.
That there is no gentile path separating them, etc. Bach wrote: in a case of great loss one may rely on the Rosh and Tur to permit even when a gentile path separates, if they are locked at night — so it appears to me — end. And in my humble opinion, Rashba and the Mechaber also agree with this, and they only require that no gentile path separate when the neighborhood doors are not locked at night; and the Rosh (and Rabbeinu Yerucham), who requires that neighborhood doors be locked at night, deals when the public path separates it by day; and therefore the Rosh and Rashba do not disagree — unlike Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe who wrote that they disagree; this appears clear to me.
We follow the majority of the neighborhood residents, etc. Even though majority and proximity — we follow the majority even in evident proximity, as immediately below; here it is different since it was found within the neighborhood itself. Perishah; and likewise Rashba in Mishnat HaRashba, Beit 5, at the end of Gate 4, explicitly.
Even if the majority of the city's thieves, etc. So wrote the Tur. This requires study, for the Gemara permits only because of a double doubt. Rashi explains: perhaps they were non-Jews and perhaps they were Jews; and even if they were non-Jews, perhaps they opened the barrel only for money, and when they saw that it contained wine they withdrew. This shows that the case concerns uncertainty whether the thieves were non-Jews or Jews, meaning that non-Jewish and Jewish thieves were equally common, or alternatively that it was unknown whether most thieves were non-Jews or most were Jews. But where most thieves are non-Jews, there is no double doubt, since the majority of thieves are non-Jews. This also appears from Rabbeinu Yerucham. The matter can be resolved only with difficulty, and I have been brief because this law is not common among us, since people do not ordinarily hide money in wine barrels, as the Rosh wrote. Olat Tamid further writes that where thieves pierced a barrel with a knife while holding a pot and drew wine into it, some permit the wine remaining in the barrel by virtue of a double doubt: perhaps, because the thief was preoccupied with the pot, the knife did not touch the wine and the wine flowed out as soon as the knife passed through the thickness of the barrel; and even if the knife did touch the wine, since the thief intended only to pierce the barrel so that the wine would flow and did not intend contact with the wine, it is the force of a non-Jew without intent and is permitted. He concludes that one may be lenient nowadays, when non-Jews do not pour wine for idolatrous libation. His words imply that if it is known that the knife touched the wine, it is forbidden because there is no double doubt. This is astonishing, for one never needs such a double doubt here. The single reason that he intended only to pierce the barrel is sufficient to permit it. In truth, he understood the Mordechai's wording cited by the Beit Yosef as permitting by means of a double doubt, but that is incorrect. The Mordechai's conclusion permits on this reason alone, even in the era of the Gemara. The Rama likewise ruled above in siman 124, seif 24, that it is permitted for this reason alone, because since he intended only to pierce the barrel it is contact by a non-Jew through another object without intent, which is permitted. It was already explained there that unintentional contact by a non-Jew through another object is permitted even according to the law of the Gemara. See there.
