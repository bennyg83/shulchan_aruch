And when they saw that it was wine, they did not touch it. See Baer Heitev. In Responsa Tashbetz, volume 3, siman 148, he discusses thieves who entered a storehouse and found two sealed barrels and one vat containing raisins that had been pressed in order to extract wine from them. They stole one barrel, and opened the second and left it there. If it is clear that no Jews were involved and that the thieves were only Ishmaelites, the opened barrel should be sold to a non-Jew, and the vessel requires kashering. As for the vat, since no act was done to it there is no concern at all. Even if non-Jews touched its contents, they may be permitted, as stated in the name of the Rashbam regarding non-Jews who touched a cluster of grapes, and all the more so with regard to contact by Ishmaelites; see there.
And the entrance to the storehouse was closed. See Baer Heitev. In Shevut Yaakov, volume 2, siman 72, he discusses a Jew who rented a cellar from a non-Jew and stored wine there secured with a key and seal as required. The non-Jew’s house was broken into, as was the cellar. In the morning a young maidservant became aware of this and ran in alarm to the Jew to inform him. He went there and found the barrels sealed and full, with no moisture around them. There is no concern at all. See also the Beit Yosef, siman 129, and Responsa Rosh regarding one who forgot to secure the key, and so forth; see there. In the case of a non-Jew found in a cellar containing sealed barrels, where one of the barrels was found opened, and when the Jew arrived the non-Jew remained among the barrels and the Jew saw him there, the wine must be prohibited. This is because the barrel had been sealed and was now found open, as explained in siman 129, seif 11, in the gloss. There was also the further adverse circumstance that a nursing woman was found bent over the barrel. We do not simply attribute such circumstances to an ordinary and innocent occurrence, and it is proper to be stringent even though the contact would have been through another object and by means of a flow, as explained in siman 124; see there. Responsa Beit Yehudah, siman 12, discusses a Jew who had a locked room containing several barrels of wine. A non-Jew who served him came and told the owner that he had found the entrance open. The owner went there and found the barrel covers open, with reeds inserted into the spigots and stained with red wine. He struck the servant in order to make him confess, and the servant admitted that he and another non-Jew had done this. A search of his belongings uncovered a bent nail resembling a key. Beit Yehudah writes, on the basis of the responsum of the Rivash cited by the Beit Yosef at the beginning of siman 127, that the Rivash required proof that the non-Jew had touched the wine, such as where the barrels had been open and were later found closed. Nevertheless, the Rivash prohibited only where the non-Jew was found standing beside the wine. In truth, the responsum of the Rivash appears not to be discussing such a case, and this requires study. Otherwise, there remains a doubt whether the non-Jew entered at all, and one may say that Jews entered and stole, as stated in siman 128. As for the non-Jew’s admission that he opened the lock and drew wine, we hold that a non-Jew is not believed to render something prohibited. Even a Jew is not believed to prohibit something when it is not under his control, as stated in siman 128; see also the Rama in siman 110, seif 8. Although here it is certain that the wine was opened and tampered with, nevertheless, since without the non-Jew’s statement it would have been permitted, because he was not found standing beside the wine, even when this adverse circumstance is taken into account one should not prohibit the wine on the basis of the non-Jew’s statement. See there. In my opinion this requires study, for there are numerous strong grounds for suspicion. I have therefore been brief.
