And if the Jews have a neighborhood, etc. The Ra'ah in Bedek HaBayis challenged the Rashba, for we follow the majority rather than proximity. The Rashba answered in Mishmeres HaBayis that this principle applies only when the minority is not separated as an independent area. But when it is separated, as here, where there is a distinct Jewish neighborhood, the majority of the city is irrelevant to that neighborhood, and we follow only the majority within the neighborhood itself, because the residents of the city do not ordinarily pass through there.
Where a road used by non-Jews does not divide it. The Rosh was stringent and wrote that it must specifically be locked at night, as the Beit Yosef records. It appears obvious that the Rashba agrees that in such a case it is permitted. My father-in-law of blessed memory wrote that in a case of substantial loss one may rely on the Ra'ah, who permits where a road used by non-Jews divides it but it is locked at night. This is imprecise, for in that case everyone agrees that it is permitted, even without substantial loss. Whenever it is locked at night, that is more protective than where no road used by non-Jews divides it. This is obvious.
They did not touch it, knowing that it was wine. Tosafos wrote: Perhaps when they search everywhere for hidden Jewish valuables, the wine is also forbidden, because they insert a long reed into the barrel to search within it, end quote. It appears to me that nowadays, when we are lenient regarding ordinary non-Jewish wine where a non-Jew touched it through another object, it is permitted even if they touched it with a reed. Since his intent was not to touch the wine but to search for money, it is unintentional contact and is permitted, as stated above in siman 124, seif 24. Even if he touched it with his hand, there would be grounds to permit it, but one should not be so lenient, since this is not stated explicitly. The glosses of the Ashri in the chapter HaSocher state that nowadays one should not forbid it even if the non-Jew closed the door and would not be apprehended as a thief, unless there is reason to suspect drinking or some personal benefit, end quote.
There is an adverse indication in some of them, etc. Accordingly, the Mordechai at the end of the chapter Rabbi Yishmael ruled in the name of Maharam regarding thieves who entered a cellar and made a spigot hole in one barrel that had not previously been fitted with a spigot, while other barrels there already had spigots. He answered that we do not establish a prohibition concerning those barrels in which there is no adverse indication, as stated in the chapter Gid HaNasheh: Because of this fool, shall all the butcher shops be forbidden? All the more so here, for one may say that they took from the best barrel, namely the full one in which they made a spigot hole. The Darkei Moshe likewise wrote in the name of the Rivash, siman 424, that only the barrel definitely opened by the thieves is forbidden. This is unlike the responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 128, which forbids all the wine lest the thief inspected all of them to determine which was best.
When there are known thieves. With this distinction, the Rama resolves the words of the Mordechai cited by the Beit Yosef. The Mordechai holds that even if it is known that one of the thieves was Jewish, the wine is permitted, whereas in the Gemara Rava permitted only because the majority of thieves were Jewish. This was the Beit Yosef's difficulty, and the Rama's distinction resolves it. See what I wrote in siman 114, seif 10, where the Rama contradicts what he wrote here. In truth, the primary ruling is as he wrote here.
