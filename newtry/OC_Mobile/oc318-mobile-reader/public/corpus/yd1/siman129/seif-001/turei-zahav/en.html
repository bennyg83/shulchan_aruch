One who leaves a non-Jew, etc. The Gemara establishes the necessity of each case. Had it taught only one who transports his wine, I might have said that only there it is permitted, because the non-Jew thinks, perhaps he will come and see me; but with a wagon or ship, if the ship travels far away, he can do as he wishes. Meaning, I might have thought that we should be concerned for this; therefore it teaches us that we are not concerned. Had it taught only a wagon and ship, I might have said that the reason is because he thinks, perhaps he will come by another route, stand by the wall, and see me; but in a shop he can close the door and do as he wishes. Therefore it teaches us that we are not concerned for this. All the cases above in siman 128 in which it is forbidden if he closed the door refer to where we know clearly that he closed it in a manner that prevents the Jew from entering without the non-Jew's knowledge. But where we do not know this, it is permitted, even though there is an interior lock which he could have locked had he wished. Nevertheless, we learn from here that if the non-Jew clearly closed the door, it is forbidden, since he has a connection to the house and can excuse himself, for he is standing there with permission, since the Jew left him there. Likewise, regarding the permission involving a wagon or ship, where we say that the reason is that he thinks the Jew may come by another route, stand by the wall, and see him, this applies specifically where there is another route. But if there is only one route, once the Jew has gone beyond sight it is forbidden, for he cannot return without the non-Jew seeing him. Likewise, if the Jew clearly traveled away by ship or wagon to a place from which he cannot see, it is certainly forbidden. But out of doubt alone we are not concerned for this. All this is from the Rashba in Toras HaBayis HaAruch, and this is why the Mechaber concludes here, provided it was not known, etc.
When is this said? When there is a circuitous route. In the Gemara this was stated regarding one who transports his wine, which is the third case mentioned by the Tur and the Shulchan Aruch at the beginning of the siman. The Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Ran that only regarding one who transports the wine do we say this. But where one leaves a non-Jew in a shop and the Jew goes in and out, a circuitous route is not required, because at every moment the non-Jew is afraid that the Jew may now come and see him approaching the barrel. The Rashba wrote regarding a wagon and ship that a circuitous route is required, as I cited above. The Beit Yosef wrote that the Ran also agrees to this, and excluded only the case of leaving a non-Jew in a shop, because ordinarily the Jew can come there suddenly. However, if the Jew cannot come suddenly to the shop, such as where there is a window at the entrance through which the non-Jew can see whether the Jew is coming, it appears that it is forbidden, because he is no longer afraid, end quote. The Rav's words that if there is a window it is forbidden are astonishing to me. On the contrary, because there is a window, the non-Jew is afraid that the Jew may come and see him through that window, as stated in Shulchan Aruch, siman 128, seif 3. Even were you to say that the wine is not situated opposite the window, it remains difficult, for above, in siman 128, seif 3, he wrote that if there are holes in the door and the Jew cannot see through them, it is permitted. The prohibition applies only if the non-Jew locked the door from inside. But if the interior lock is not closed, all the wine in the house is permitted, because he is afraid, and there is no prohibition merely because the non-Jew can see through the holes. Why, then, should it be forbidden here when there is a window, unless it is established that the non-Jew closed the shop? This is exactly the same law as above. It appears possible to explain why they required a circuitous route specifically regarding one who transports wine and regarding a ship. They are in a field, an open place. Therefore, whenever there is only one route, the non-Jew can take precautions when he sees the Jew traveling far away, since the Jew cannot return so quickly. But in a house or shop, it is possible that the Jew went only a very short distance to one of the nearby houses, and in the blink of an eye he may return and see him. The non-Jew is therefore afraid. Accordingly, a circuitous route is required only in those two cases, namely a wagon or ship and one who transports his wine, but not in the first case of leaving him in a shop. It also appears obvious to me that if one transports wine between houses, the same law applies, and a circuitous route is irrelevant there. The non-Jew is constantly afraid that the Jew may suddenly come upon him. They were stringent only in an open area without houses, where there is only one route. This appears entirely clear in my humble opinion. Although the Perishah wrote that the requirement applies only to the third case, one who transports the wine, nevertheless it appears as I wrote, that it also applies to the middle case, since the Rashba wrote so explicitly and the same reason applies to both.
To enter the bathhouse. See what I wrote regarding seif 6. Likewise, the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch, that if he said, Go, and I will come after you, it is forbidden even where there is a circuitous route, will be explained there.
Or he informed him that he was going far away. This refers to the three cases mentioned above: first, one who leaves a non-Jew in a shop; second, one whose wine was on a ship, etc.; and third, one who was transporting a barrel from place to place, etc. Regarding informing him that he is going far away, there is a dispute. The Ra'avad and Rashba hold that it is forbidden only when he explicitly informs him that he will be away for the amount of time necessary to perform the forbidden act. The Rosh forbids even when he merely says in general that he is going far away. One may be lenient in accordance with the Ra'avad and Rashba, for they are the majority against the Rosh. Furthermore, we rule leniently where he says that he will be away only long enough to open the mouth of the barrel, even though he actually remains away long enough to open and reseal it, unlike the other authorities cited by the Beit Yosef in the name of the Ran, who forbid in this case. Toras HaBayis HaAruch wrote in the name of the Ra'avad that even if he informed him that he was going far away, it is permitted when there is a circuitous route by which he could return within the time required to open and reseal it, provided he did not say, Go, and I will come after you.
Enough time for him to remove, etc. The Tur concludes here: But if he said that he would be away for less than this period, even if that period is long enough to bore a hole in the barrel or its stopper, it is permitted, because we are not concerned that he opened a hole, nor are we concerned that he forged the seal. The Beit Yosef explains that we are not concerned that he removed the entire stopper, since the Jew said that he would be away for less than that period, end quote. The meaning is that although the Jew actually remained away for a long time, and it was possible for the non-Jew to remove the entire stopper, nevertheless, since the Jew did not reveal that he would be away so long, we are not concerned for this. My father-in-law of blessed memory found these words of the Beit Yosef difficult, because the Tur should simply have said that it was impossible for the non-Jew to forge the entire seal. He therefore forced himself into explaining the Tur as speaking of two seals, but this is unnecessary.
And some say that if they are sealed, etc. The Tur gives the reason here, because the sealing of the barrel serves as an identifying sign, etc. In siman 130, seif 4, I wrote that specifically dried plaster is required. Therefore, even in a case of substantial loss there is no permission unless it dried.
