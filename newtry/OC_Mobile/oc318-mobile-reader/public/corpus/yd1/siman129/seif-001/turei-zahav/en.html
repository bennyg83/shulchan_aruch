One who leaves a gentile, etc. The Gemara explains why each case is needed. Had it taught only one who transports his wine, I would have said that only in this case it is permitted, because the gentile thinks perhaps he will come and see me; but in a wagon or ship, if he sends the ship away, the gentile can do whatever he wants. That is, I would have thought we should be concerned for this, so it teaches that we are not concerned. And had it taught only wagon and ship, I would have said that this is because the gentile thinks perhaps he will come by another route and stand by the wall and see me; but in a shop, he closes the door and does whatever he wants. Therefore it teaches that we are not concerned for this. All those cases above in siman 128 where if he closed the door it is forbidden refer to where we know clearly that he closed the door in such a way that the Jew cannot enter there without the gentile knowing. But where we do not know this, it is permitted, even though there is a lock inside and he could have locked it if he wished. Nevertheless, we learn that if in this case the gentile certainly closed the door, it is forbidden, since he has some connection to the house and has an excuse, for he is standing there with permission, since the Jew left him there. Likewise regarding the permission of wagon and ship, where we say that the reason for permission is that he thinks the Jew may come by another route and stand at the wall and see him, this shows that it is specifically where there is another route. But if there is only one route, once the Jew turned his eyes away from him it is forbidden, for he cannot come unless the gentile sees him. So too, if we know clearly that he went far away in a ship or wagon to a place where he cannot see, it is certainly forbidden; only out of doubt we are not concerned for this. All this is from the words of the Rashba in Torat HaBayit, and this is why he concluded here, “provided that it is not known, etc.” When are these words said? When there is a winding path. In the Gemara this is said regarding one transporting his wine, which is the third law mentioned by the Tur and Shulchan Aruch at the beginning of the siman. The Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Ran that we say this specifically with one transporting, but with one who leaves a gentile in a shop while a Jew goes out and comes in, we do not require a winding path, because at every moment the gentile fears him, perhaps the Jew will now come and see that he is approaching the barrel. The Rashba wrote regarding wagon and ship that we require a winding path, as I copied above. The Beit Yosef wrote that the Ran also agrees to this, and excludes only one who leaves a gentile in a shop, where ordinarily a Jew can come there suddenly. However, if the Jew cannot come suddenly to the shop, such as where there is a window by the entrance through which the gentile can see if the Jew comes, it implies that it is forbidden, because he no longer fears; end quote. The master’s words astonish me, regarding what he wrote that if there is a window, etc., it is forbidden. On the contrary, since there is a window the gentile fears that the Jew will come and see through that window, as written in Shulchan Aruch siman 128 seif 3. Even if you say that here the wine is not placed opposite the window, it is still difficult, for above in siman 128 seif 3 he wrote that if there are holes in the door and the Jew cannot see, it is permitted; the prohibition is only if he closed the door with an inside lock. But if no lock is closed, all the wine in the house is permitted because he fears, and there is no prohibition there because the gentile can see through the holes. Why should it be forbidden here where there is a window, if it is not clear that the gentile closed the shop? This is certainly exactly the law above. It seems to give a reason why they specifically said regarding one transporting and regarding a ship that a winding path is needed: they are in the field, an open place, and therefore whenever there is only one route the gentile can be careful, since he sees the Jew going from afar and it is impossible for him to come so soon. But in a house or shop, perhaps he went by a very nearby path to one of the houses and can come and see him in the blink of an eye, and the gentile fears. Therefore here no winding path is needed, only in those two cases: wagon or ship, and one transporting his wine. It appears obvious to me that one who transports wine among houses has the same law; a winding path does not apply there, and the gentile always fears that he may suddenly come upon him. They were stringent only in a place open from houses, where there is only one route. So it appears obvious in my humble opinion. Although the Perishah wrote that this refers only to the third clause, one who transports, nevertheless it appears as I wrote, that it refers also to the middle clause, since the Rashba wrote so explicitly, and the same reason applies to both. To enter the bathhouse. See what I wrote in seif 6. Likewise, what the Shulchan Aruch wrote, “Go, and I will come after you,” that it is forbidden even where there is a winding path, will be explained there.
Or he informed him that he was going far away. This refers to the three matters mentioned above: first, leaving a gentile in a shop; second, where he had wine in a ship, etc.; third, where he was transporting a barrel from place to place, etc. Regarding this notice that he is going far away, there is a dispute. The Raavad and Rashba hold that it is only where he explicitly informs him that he will be away for the amount of time needed for the prohibition. The Rosh forbids even if he says generally that he will be away. One may be lenient in this like the view of the Raavad and Rashba, for they are many against the Rosh. Furthermore, we rule leniently if he says he will be away only long enough to open the barrel’s opening, even though he remains away long enough to open and seal it, unlike the others whom the Beit Yosef brings in the name of the Ran, who forbid this. Torat HaBayit wrote in the name of the Raavad that even if he informed him he was going far away, it is permitted if there is a winding path by which he could come within the time needed to open and seal, and he did not say, “Go, and I will come after you.”
So that he can remove, etc. The Tur concludes here: but if he said he was going away for less than this measure, even if there is enough time for him to pierce a hole in the barrel or in the stopper, it is permitted, because we are not concerned for opening a hole, and we are not concerned for forgery. The Beit Yosef explained: we are not concerned that perhaps he removed the entire stopper, since he said he was going away for less than that amount; end quote. The explanation of his words is that although in truth the Jew stayed away a long time, and it is possible that he removed the whole stopper, nevertheless, since he did not reveal this to him, we are not concerned. My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, found these words of the Beit Yosef difficult, for the Tur should have said that it is impossible for him to have forged the whole seal. Because of this he forced himself in explaining the Tur’s words as speaking of two seals, but this is for nothing.
And some say that if they are sealed, etc. The Tur brings the reason here: since the sealing of the barrel is a sign, etc. In siman 130 seif 4 I wrote that specifically dry plastering is required. Therefore even in a case of great loss there is no permission unless it has dried.
