One who leaves a gentile, etc. In the Gemara he makes a distinction: if it taught "one who passes his wine" one would say specifically in this it is permitted, for the gentile thinks perhaps he will come and see me; but in a wagon or ship, if he sails he does as he wishes — meaning one would say we should concern for this; it teaches us we do not concern for this. And if it taught wagon and ship alone, one would say because he thinks perhaps he will come another way and stand by the wall and see me; but in a shop he closes the door and does as he wishes — it teaches us we do not concern for this. And all those above siman 128 — if he closed the door it is forbidden — that is when we know clearly he closed the door in a matter the Jew cannot enter there without the gentile's knowledge; but when we do not know — permitted; and even though there is a lock inside he could lock if he wished; nevertheless we learn: if here the gentile clearly closed the door it is forbidden, since he has connection in the house and has where to rely — standing there with permission, for Israel left it there; and likewise in the permit of wagon and ship that we say the reason of the permit is he thinks he will come another way and stand by the wall and see — evidently specifically when there is another way; but if there is only one way, since he sailed his eyes are from him it is forbidden, for he cannot come unless he sees him; and likewise if he clearly sailed on a ship or wagon in a place impossible to see — certainly forbidden; but when uncertain we do not concern for this. All this from Rashba in Tosafot HaRosh; and this is what he concludes here — and that it was not known, etc.
In another matter — when there is a roundabout way. In the Gemara they say: this is on one who passes his wine; and the same is the third case the Tur and Shulchan Aruch mentioned at the beginning of the siman; and Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Ran that specifically regarding one who passes they say so; but regarding one who leaves a gentile in a shop and Israel goes in and out — we do not require a roundabout way, for every hour he fears from him lest now he come and see Israel approaching the barrel; and Rashba wrote regarding wagon and ship that we require a roundabout way as I copied above; and Beit Yosef wrote that Ran also agrees to this, and he only limits one who leaves a gentile in a shop — for presumably Israel can come there suddenly; however if Israel cannot come suddenly to the shop, such as when there is a window on the opening that the gentile can see from it if Israel comes — it implies it is forbidden, for he no longer fears — end; and I wonder at the Rav's words where he wrote that if there is a window, etc., it is forbidden — on the contrary, since there is a window the gentile trembles lest Israel come and be seen through that window — as written in Shulchan Aruch siman 128 seif 3; and even if you say the wine is not placed opposite the window here — nevertheless it is difficult, for above siman 128 seif 3 he wrote: if there are holes in the door and Israel cannot see — permitted, for prohibition is only if he closed the door with a lock from inside; but if there is no closed lock, all wine in the house is permitted and trembles, and there is no prohibition because the gentile can see through the holes; and why should it be forbidden here when there is a window if not clearly that he closed the shop — behold this is exactly the law above; and it appears to give a reason why in truth they said specifically regarding one who passes and on a ship that requires a roundabout way — because they are in the field, an open place, and therefore wherever there is only one way the gentile can be careful whenever he sees him going from afar and he cannot come quickly; but in a house or shop it is possible he went a very close way to one of the houses and quickly as in the blink of an eye he will come and see us and trembles — therefore here we do not need a roundabout way, only in those two — wagon and ship and one who passes his wine; but at the beginning regarding one who leaves in a shop — no. And it appears straightforward that also regarding one who passes wine between houses the law is the same, and a roundabout way does not apply there; and always the gentile trembles lest he suddenly come upon him; and they were stringent only in a place open from houses and there is only one way — so it appears in my humble opinion straightforward, even though in Perishah he wrote it applies only to the third gate alone where he passes; nevertheless it appears as I wrote that it applies also to the middle one, since Rashba wrote thus in explanation; and one reason fits both.
