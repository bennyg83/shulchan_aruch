The entrance to the storehouse was closed. See Baer Heitev and Shevut Yaakov, part 2, siman 72. A Jew rented a cellar from a non-Jew and stored wine there under a proper lock and seal. The non-Jew's house and the cellar were broken open. In the morning, a young maidservant noticed this, ran in alarm to a Jew, and informed him. He went and found the barrels sealed and full, with no moisture around them. There is no concern at all. See Beis Yosef, siman 129, and responsa of the Rosh concerning one who forgot to lock the door. There, a non-Jew was found in a cellar containing sealed barrels and had opened one of them. When the Jew arrived, the non-Jew remained between the barrels and the Jew saw him. The wine must be forbidden because the barrel had been sealed and was found open, as explained in siman 129, se'if 11, in the Rama. In another incident, a siphon was found bent over the barrel. We do not attribute such a circumstance to an ordinary occurrence, and it is proper to rule stringently even though the contact was through another object and through a poured stream, as explained in siman 124. See there. Responsa Beis Yehudah, siman 12, discusses a Jew who had a locked room containing several wine barrels. A non-Jewish servant came and told his master that he had found the entrance open. The master went there and found the barrel stoppers open and reeds inserted into the spigots, stained with red wine. He struck the servant to make him confess, and the servant admitted that he and another non-Jew had done this. A search revealed a bent nail resembling a key among his tools. Beis Yehudah based his ruling upon the responsum of the Rivash cited by the Beis Yosef at the beginning of siman 127. The Rivash writes that wine is not forbidden unless there is evidence that the non-Jew touched it, even where barrels had been opened and then closed. Beis Yehudah understood that the Rivash forbade the wine only when the non-Jew was found standing beside it. In truth, however, the Rivash's responsum does not appear to address that circumstance, and the matter requires study. Otherwise, there remains a doubt whether the non-Jew entered at all, and perhaps Jews entered and stole, as stated in siman 128. The non-Jew's confession that he opened the lock and drew wine is insufficient, because a non-Jew is not believed to render wine forbidden. Even a Jew is not believed when the matter is not under his control, as stated in siman 128. See also the Rama in siman 110, se'if 8. Although it is certain here that the wine was opened and tampered with, nevertheless, absent the non-Jew's statement, it would have been permitted because he was not found standing beside it. Even combined with the suspicious circumstances, his statement cannot forbid it. See there. In my humble opinion, this ruling requires study, because there are many strong indications supporting the accusation. I have written briefly.
