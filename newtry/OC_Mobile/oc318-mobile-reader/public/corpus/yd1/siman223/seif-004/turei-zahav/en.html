To his brother or his son. In Choshen Mishpat, siman 366, Tur writes a similar law regarding robbery and explains that "his brother" means his father's brother and "his sons" means his brothers. Rambam does not explain it this way, and the Mishnah does not imply Tur's interpretation.
"Likewise, if he paid them," etc. Rambam writes this. Tur comments: "I do not understand his words. How can he be permitted to pay his debt from property that is forbidden to him? He is benefiting from it," etc. Beit Yosef writes that Tur overlooked the Mishnah in the first chapter of HaGozel, which explicitly states the law as Rambam rules. In my humble opinion, Tur's question is valid. His principal difficulty is with Rambam's formulation, which implies that the person who is prohibited from benefit himself pays his debt with the forbidden property. The Mishnah says only that the person who imposed the prohibition may pay the other's debt without the prohibited person's knowledge or consent. And when the Mishnah says, "creditors come and collect from it," it means against his will, as the wording "they collect" implies. Rambam's phrase "if he paid them," however, implies that he himself pays. Ran, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 216, likewise interprets Rambam so that the prohibited person does not personally pay his creditor, but merely shows the creditor where the assets are. Tur was unwilling to explain Rambam this way. Tur further asks why Rambam writes here that "he must inform them," for what practical difference does that make? Ran answers that this notification makes the assets comparable to hefker, as though he says, "These are my father's assets; I do not know what to do with them. Take them for yourselves." This answer too follows Ran's approach that the person does not himself make payment. Tur, who understands Rambam to mean that he personally pays, therefore asks properly. Thus there is no practical dispute: whenever he personally makes the payment, it is forbidden, and merely informing the recipients does not help. Regarding giving his father's inheritance to his sons, Ran likewise holds that if he personally gives it to them it is forbidden because he receives the benefit of deciding to whom to give. Rather, he shows them the place and says, "These assets of my father are forbidden to me; I do not know what to do with them. Take them for yourselves and do what you wish." Beit Yosef writes that because he tells them that the money is forbidden to him and for that reason gives it to them, and they are his sons or brothers who have a relationship to this property, he receives no significant benefit from giving it to them and it is permitted. Nevertheless, the language implies that this works only after the fact if he already gave or paid; initially, even if he informs them, it should not be done.
