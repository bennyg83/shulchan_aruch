She told her husband, ‘I am impure.’ It appears that the phrase ‘her husband’ is not exact. Rather, the usual case is stated, since modesty ordinarily dictates that a woman disclose her impurity and purity only to her husband. Indeed, in the second chapter of Ketubot, the Gemara does not mention her husband at all. The same follows from the case there of a woman who said, ‘I am betrothed,’ and afterward said, ‘I am unmarried,’ which implies that the same law applies even when she established her status before others, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 47. This is also implied by the Tur's objection to Rambam. Although the Tur himself writes, ‘she told her husband,’ he does not mean it exclusively. This is likewise Rashba's view, as Beit Yosef writes, and it is also implied by the Rav's responsum, siman 2. All this appears obvious, contrary to the Taz, who cites a great authority as saying that the rule applies only when she spoke to her husband, but if she said so to others she is not believed even with a valid explanation. That is incorrect. The objections raised against Rambam have already been resolved differently by the poskim. See below, seif katan 6.
Rather, she comes and lies beside him. The Bach writes that the husband must nevertheless ask her why she has come to him, since she previously told him, ‘I am impure.’ Her explanation must be heard from her own mouth. The Shach writes that this is also the Rama's view. In truth, however, the Rama's wording does not imply this.
Even if she gives an explanation for her earlier statement, she is not believed. If she revealed to her husband from the outset that she intended to act this way for a valid reason, it is obvious that she is believed. Taz. This is also the Shach's conclusion in Nekudot HaKesef.
She is not believed. Beit Yosef, citing Rashba, explains the reason: because of embarrassment or coercion she might say, ‘I am impure,’ but she would not go so far as to perform an act and wear the garments of a niddah. The Bach writes likewise and concludes that it would have sufficed for her merely to tell him, ‘I am impure,’ without wearing niddah garments. This is unlike the case above in siman 1, concerning the butcher who made a sign on the sheep's head, because there he had no other option, whereas here she could simply have said, ‘I am impure.’ The Perishah writes similarly. Their words imply that where she had no other option she is believed, and the Rav writes likewise in responsum 2. These are the Shach's words. However, Maharik's responsum, siman 87, indicates that even where her explanation is that she had no possible alternative but to wear those garments, she is still not believed. See there. It therefore appears that the distinction is as follows: specifically niddah garments are something she would not wear falsely, because by doing so she gives her husband a bad reputation among the neighbors. The Levush writes similarly in Even HaEzer siman 115, seif 2. This is clear.
The sage says that she is lying. The Taz writes that this applies specifically when the sage himself says so, because she relied upon him. By contrast, a single witness who testifies that the sage prohibited her is not believed. His words are straightforward, and this is also clear from the Rosh in Perek Shevuat HaEdut. See siman 126, where it is explained that a single witness is not believed concerning prohibitions in such a case.
The sage is believed. The Taz writes that this applies specifically with respect to the prohibition itself. With respect to monetary loss, such as depriving her of her ketubah, the sage is no more credible than any other single witness. This is clear from the Rosh in Perek HaMadir. The Rav writes likewise in Darkei Moshe. This distinction is necessary so that the rulings of the Rama and the Mechaber do not contradict one another. Here the Rama rules that the sage is believed, and the Mechaber rules similarly in Even HaEzer siman 16, seif 1, following the Rosh in the name of Rambam in Perek Shevuat HaEdut. Yet in Even HaEzer siman 116, seif 1, the Mechaber explicitly rules against Rambam and states that the woman is believed unless witnesses contradict her. The Rosh's own statements would likewise appear contradictory, since in Shevuot he follows Rambam, while in Perek HaMadir he writes that the woman is believed. Rather, the sage alone is not believed specifically to deprive her of her ketubah, as the Rosh writes explicitly in Perek HaMadir: ‘It is reasonable, for how can money be removed on the testimony of one witness? Two witnesses are required.’ With respect to the prohibition itself, however, the Rosh certainly agrees that the sage is believed. Although I saw that the Rav, in responsum 66, expresses some uncertainty whether according to the Rosh in Perek HaMadir the woman is believed even regarding the prohibition, in my humble opinion the Rosh's view is clearly that she is not believed regarding the prohibition. Otherwise, the Rosh's rulings would contradict one another. This conclusion is also necessary in the words of the Mechaber and the Rama themselves, as explained. Chelkat Mechokek in Even HaEzer siman 115, seif katan 4, did not note this distinction, and therefore questioned the apparent contradiction in the Rosh and expressed surprise at the Mechaber. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. According to his approach, he should also have questioned the Mechaber's own rulings from siman 17, seif 8. In truth, the words of the Rosh and the Mechaber are clear as I have explained. The Rav writes in responsum 66 that with respect to a rabbinic prohibition one might say that the first sage is not believed. This conflicts with the implication of the Rama here, since stains are rabbinic, as explained in siman 190. See what I wrote in klal and siman 78, seif katan 4.
