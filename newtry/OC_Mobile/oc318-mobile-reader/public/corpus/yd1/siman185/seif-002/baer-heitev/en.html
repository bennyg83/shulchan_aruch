Impure. A subsequent explanation does not help, as stated in the following seif. There she performed no act and merely said, "I am impure," so it may be said that she spoke that way out of embarrassment. Here, however, she performed an act by wearing the garments of a niddah, and she is therefore not believed even if she later offers an explanation. This is the Rashba's ruling. Rabbi Leib of Prague answered that establishing herself publicly as a niddah is different, because not everyone knows her explanation. On what, then, did she rely when she presented herself that way before everyone? By contrast, when she tells only her husband, "I am impure," she may have relied on subsequently explaining the truth to him. The Taz writes: I learned from this that even regarding a verbal declaration there is a distinction. If she said before many people that she was impure, a later explanation does not help. This is why the authorities formulate the law as, "She said to her husband, I am impure," for the ruling applies only when she said it to her husband alone. A case occurred involving a woman who had given birth three times in the eighth month and feared that the evil eye was the cause. She wished to conceal her pregnancy from members of her household and her neighbors, and her husband agreed that at the beginning of the pregnancy she should say, "I am impure," in order to hide it. The Rama was asked whether her husband might have relations with her privately, and he permitted it. See the Taz and Responsa Rama, siman 2. A woman who claims that her husband had relations with her while she was a niddah, while he denies it entirely, is not believed. This is Mahari Weil, siman 202. If one witness testifies that she was impure, or asked her, "Are you not impure?" and she answered yes, a later explanation does not help. This is the Kenesset HaGedolah in the name of his teacher.
