Melting of the liver, etc. In this he followed Rokeach whom Beit Yosef brought, who learned it from chapter "One who seized him"; and the honor of Rama rests in its place, but he was not precise in this ruling; and I will set before you the Gemara's words that are stated there: for blood that comes from the mouth we examine it with a wheat stalk whether it absorbs; if the blood sticks to the stalk — lung disease comes and it has a remedy; if not — liver disease comes and it has no remedy. We challenge: but we learned the opposite — liver removed and nothing remained of it; lung punctured or lacking — treifah. We answer: since it comes from the mouth, say perhaps it melted and was removed; and Rashi explains: since blood of the liver, which hangs below the lung, rises upward and exits via the mouth — say perhaps it melted and was removed entirely and nothing remained of it, and it fell to the lung via the reed of the liver connected to the reed of the lung — end; it appears clearly the main ruin that the animal or person dies is that the entire liver melted, not part of it; for if there is a flaw also in part of it — the difficulty returns to its place, for a lung that melted and spoiled inside is kosher as long as the simfonot did not melt; if so, liver would be stricter than lung when lacking in part — but we learned the opposite as the questioner cited. Rather, simple: whoever did not lack the two olive-volumes is kosher even if the liver melted. And Rokeach wrote: reed of liver like liver in removal — all of it and nothing remained like an olive — treifah; liver that melted — treifah, as we say in "One who seized him": blood from the mouth — if from liver disease it has no remedy; liver that dried or is hard as stone — treifah — end. And in this Beit Yosef understood Rokeach forbids in melting even if the two olive-volumes remained intact, and he disagreed; and indeed Rokeach did not come to forbid except in melting entirely even the two olive-volumes; and a great novelty he teaches us: if melted — it is as removed; and so we find in Beit Yosef siman 50 in name of Rashba: any limb whose puncture or lack disqualifies — when melted also disqualifies — end; and so Mordechai whom Beit Yosef brought siman 50: Rabbenu Tam said to Ra'am mouth to mouth that wherever punctured forbids — likewise if the flesh around it is bad and rotted; and it itself teaches also Rokeach: even in liver that is all blood — nevertheless if it melted and became blood it forbids if the two olive-volumes did not remain; this is proven clearly from his words, for behold adjacent to this he wrote: liver that dried or is hard as stone — treifah, meaning even the two olive-volumes; and so Rama wrote here: if not so — kosher, as written in this seif; and why not explain the first clause thus too, namely what he wrote "liver that melted" — what difference to me liver that melted or dried? Rather it is clear and simple that Rokeach who forbids in melting means also in melting entirely; and this matter is a great wonder on Rama: in dried he required dryness in all of it, and likewise hard as stone, and in melting he forbids even if the two olive-volumes remained; and Rokeach wrote them adjacent one to another in one language; rather it is simple he refers to all of it in melting too; and Moharach came to strengthen Rama's ruling and said Rokeach disagrees with Rashi and explains another meaning: namely since it exits via the mouth certainly there is rot there and "throw the rot in all of it"; and it is difficult: if so, that which is stated Piskei Avodah Zarah (daf 56): Rav Yosef son of Rav Yehudah asked Rav Yehudah: liver became yellow opposite the intestines — what is the law? He said: treifah. We challenge: let it be only removed — kosher. Rava said: since liver became yellow opposite the intestines, it is known the intestines fell into fire and constricted; and if we say "throw rot in all of it" and in the end it will rot — he would not challenge at all; and also Rava would not need to make treifah on account of the intestines — let it be forbidden because we throw rot in all of the liver. Rather, certainly we do not say in liver "throw rot in all of it," aside from that it is already hidden in our words; for if so it would be stricter than lung — why not say also in lung we throw rot in all of it? Perforce we have no basis to be stringent in a matter Rashi explicitly permits; and to learn from Rokeach's words what he did not intend at all. And again I saw he wrote as our words in Masat Binyamin newly printed: we decide practical law and so we judge a long time practically every day that one does not forbid rotted and became blood if the two olive-volumes remained whole; and one should not be stringent at all in this except if it is known the animal fell from a height of ten handbreadths and crushing and loss of form in the liver is found — forbid, as written siman 58 seif 3.
