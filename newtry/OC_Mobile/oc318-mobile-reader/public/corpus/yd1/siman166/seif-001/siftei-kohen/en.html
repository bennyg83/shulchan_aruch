He shall not perform work etc. Specifically those matters that are public — forbidden; but lending him vessels or his animal and similar things — permitted if he had lent them even without the loan, since it is done with the borrower’s knowledge, as above siman 160 seif 7; and so the poskim write; and the Rosh further wrote that some explain: where it is known that before the loan they loved each other so much that they lent courtyard to each other when needed — it is permitted to lend even after the loan; and it is forbidden only with ordinary people; and so the Mordechai and other poskim; and from Maharshal’s words chapter HaGozel Etzim siman 9 it appears that only matters that are not public — such as lending animals and vessels — are permitted in such a case — see there; and Maharshal further wrote there siman 11: one who borrowed from his fellow may not honor him with a mitzvah — such as calling him up to Torah or buying him the hagbahah — even if he himself knows that without the loan he would do it for him too; nevertheless since the matter becomes publicized it resembles house and slave which is forbidden — unless it is known to all that he does it not on account of the loan — see there.
And if he already dwelt in it etc. Meaning: even though lechatchilah it is forbidden in every case — nevertheless if in practice he already dwelt in it, since the courtyard does not stand for rent and the case is such that if he had not lent to him he would not have been obligated to pay rent on account of his dwelling — in the manner explained in CM siman 363 seif 6, 7, 8 — even now that he lent to him it does not appear as ribbit.
And according to the Rambam even. A courtyard that does not stand for rent and a person who does not normally rent — it is dust of interest. And see in responsum Mabit siman 123 p. 155.
And in a courtyard etc. This refers to the first reasoning — which concedes that here it is dust of interest.
It is avak ribbit, etc. Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the students of Rashba that although it is avak ribbit and is not collected in court as ribbit, nevertheless he must pay him rent; since it is normally rented, even without specification he must pay rent, for he caused him monetary loss, and because there is a prohibition involved his power is not weakened. Even if the courtyard owner knew he was living there and was silent, it does not imply that he intended him to live there for free. Similarly, if he said to him at the time of the loan, "live in my courtyard," he must pay him rent. However, if after lending him he said, "live in my courtyard for free," and did not mention that it was in exchange for his loan, in such a case certainly, even though it is normally rented, it is only avak ribbit, since he did not stipulate with him; if he lived there, he is exempt from paying rent, end quote. Maggid Mishneh likewise wrote in the name of Ramban and Rashba that where it is normally rented, if he lived there without the owners' knowledge, it is obvious that he must pay him full rent and it is extracted from him, for it is no worse than if he had not lent him at all, where there is no prohibition; Beit Yosef brings this. Meaning: this is unlike other avak ribbit, which is with the borrower's knowledge. It appears to me that the same applies where it is not normally rented if the circumstances are such that he would have had to pay rent had he not lent him, such as where he told him to leave, or where he caused the borrower a small loss, such as blackening it, or where he revealed intent to pay, as written there in Choshen Mishpat seifim 6, 7, and 8; in all similar cases it is extracted from him. Maggid Mishneh further wrote that even if he paid him and did not claim it, he can always claim it in court, unlike Raavad who wrote that it is like later ribbit. Beit Yosef wrote the reason: he did not waive it since he did not specify; or even if he specified, it is a mistaken waiver. This requires study where he specified. What the students of Rashba wrote, "and so if he said to him when he lent him, live in my courtyard, he must pay rent," means that even if "live in my courtyard" implies for free, he must pay rent, because since he said so at the time of the loan it is like "lend me and live in my courtyard for free," which is fixed ribbit. What they wrote, however, that if after he lent him, etc., and did not mention that it was for his loan, implies that if he said, "live for the sake of your loan in my courtyard for free," even after lending, it is fixed ribbit. The Tur's view implies that after lending him, even if he said, "live in my courtyard for free because of your money," it is only avak ribbit, as below in s.k. 9. However, the Tur speaks where he already extended the time. It appears to me that the students of Rashba speak where he did not extend the time, and every day is its time; therefore where he says, "for the sake of your loan," it is fixed ribbit. Even if he does not explicitly extend the time, but can claim whenever he wishes, when he says, "live in my courtyard for free for the sake of your loan," it is on the understanding that he extends the time for him in this way, and it is fixed ribbit when he extends the time; the Tur also concedes this. However, where he says at the time he extends the time, "live in my courtyard for free," even if he did not say "for the sake of your loan," it is fixed ribbit according to the view that we do not require stipulation at the time of giving the money; examine carefully. The Maggid Mishneh further wrote that if he lived there without the owners' knowledge, and when they learned they left him the rent because of the loan, this is avak ribbit, end quote. It appears to me that this is where he had already extended the time, as explained, unlike Beit Yosef who wrote that this follows those who say a stipulation at the time of giving the money is required.
