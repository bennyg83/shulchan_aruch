He shall not perform work etc. Specifically those matters that are public — forbidden; but lending him vessels or his animal and similar things — permitted if he had lent them even without the loan, since it is done with the borrower’s knowledge, as above siman 160 seif 7; and so the poskim write; and the Rosh further wrote that some explain: where it is known that before the loan they loved each other so much that they lent courtyard to each other when needed — it is permitted to lend even after the loan; and it is forbidden only with ordinary people; and so the Mordechai and other poskim; and from Maharshal’s words chapter HaGozel Etzim siman 9 it appears that only matters that are not public — such as lending animals and vessels — are permitted in such a case — see there; and Maharshal further wrote there siman 11: one who borrowed from his fellow may not honor him with a mitzvah — such as calling him up to Torah or buying him the hagbahah — even if he himself knows that without the loan he would do it for him too; nevertheless since the matter becomes publicized it resembles house and slave which is forbidden — unless it is known to all that he does it not on account of the loan — see there.
And if he already dwelt in it etc. Meaning: even though lechatchilah it is forbidden in every case — nevertheless if in practice he already dwelt in it, since the courtyard does not stand for rent and the case is such that if he had not lent to him he would not have been obligated to pay rent on account of his dwelling — in the manner explained in CM siman 363 seif 6, 7, 8 — even now that he lent to him it does not appear as ribbit.
And according to the Rambam even. A courtyard that does not stand for rent and a person who does not normally rent — it is dust of interest. And see in responsum Mabit siman 123 p. 155.
And in a courtyard etc. This refers to the first reasoning — which concedes that here it is dust of interest.
It is avak ribbis, etc. Beit Yosef writes in the name of the students of Rashba that although this is avak ribbis and therefore is not recoverable through beit din as ribbis, nevertheless the borrower must pay the owner rent for use of the courtyard. Since the courtyard ordinarily stands for rent, even without an express arrangement one who lives there must pay because he caused the owner a monetary loss. The fact that the arrangement also involved a prohibition does not weaken the owner's monetary claim. Even if the owner knew that he was living there and remained silent, this does not imply that he intended to allow him to live there free of charge. Likewise, if at the time of the loan he said to him, "Live in my courtyard," the borrower must pay rent. However, if after the loan was already made he said to him, "Live in my courtyard for free," and did not say that the free use was in consideration of the loan, then even though the courtyard ordinarily stands for rent, this is only avak ribbis because no fixed ribbis was stipulated. If he already lived there, he is exempt from paying rent. Maggid Mishneh likewise writes in the name of Ramban and Rashba that where a courtyard stands for rent and the borrower lived there without the owner's knowledge, it is obvious that he must pay the full rent, which may be recovered from him. He is no worse than one who would have used the courtyard without any loan at all, where no prohibition would be involved. Beit Yosef cites this. In other words, this is unlike ordinary avak ribbis, which is given with the borrower's consent. It appears to me that the same rule applies even where the courtyard does not ordinarily stand for rent if the circumstances are such that, absent the loan, he would have been obligated to pay. Examples include where the owner told him to leave, where the occupant caused even a small loss such as blackening the walls, or where he demonstrated an intention to pay, as explained in Choshen Mishpat there, seif 6, 7, and 8. In all such cases the payment may be recovered from him. Maggid Mishneh further writes that even if the borrower left and the owner did not demand payment at the time, the owner may later sue him in beit din indefinitely. This is unlike Raavad, who writes that such a later demand would be treated as ribbis paid after the loan. Beit Yosef explains that the owner did not waive the rent because he did not expressly say so. Alternatively, even if he did expressly waive it, the waiver was made in error, etc. The case of an express waiver requires study. When the students of Rashba write that if the lender said at the time of the loan, "Live in my courtyard," the borrower must pay rent, the meaning is that even if the expression could be understood as "live in my courtyard for free," he must nevertheless pay. Since this was said at the time of the loan, it is equivalent to saying, "Lend me money and live in my courtyard for free," which constitutes fixed ribbis. Their continuation, however, says that if after the loan he told him to live there and did not mention that it was in exchange for the loan, it is only avak ribbis. This implies that if after the loan he explicitly said, "Live in my courtyard for free in exchange for your loan," it would be fixed ribbis. The Tur appears to hold that after the loan, even if he says, "Live in my courtyard for free because of your money," it is only avak ribbis, as explained below in seif katan 9. The Tur, however, speaks of a case where the lender had already extended the term of the loan. It therefore appears to me that the students of Rashba discuss a case where he did not yet extend the term, so every day remained a possible due date. Consequently, if he says, "Live in my courtyard for free in exchange for your loan," this is fixed ribbis even if he does not expressly grant an extension, because the understanding is that he will refrain from demanding payment during that time. When he in fact allows additional time, it is fixed ribbis. The Tur would agree to this. Likewise, where at the very moment he extends the term he says, "Live in my courtyard for free," even without expressly saying "in exchange for the loan," this is fixed ribbis according to the view that a stipulation need not have been made at the original disbursement of the money. Consider this carefully. Maggid Mishneh further writes that if the borrower lived there without the owner's knowledge and, once the owner discovered it, the owner waived the rent because of the loan, that waiver is avak ribbis. It appears to me that this assumes the lender had already extended the term, as explained above. This is unlike Beit Yosef, who writes that the ruling follows only those authorities who require the ribbis stipulation to have been fixed at the original time the money was given.
