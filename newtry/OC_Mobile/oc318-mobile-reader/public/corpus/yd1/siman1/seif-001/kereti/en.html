All may slaughter—shechitah is a mitzvah from the Torah, both in an animal and in a bird; see Peleti from where we derive this.
l'chatchila—meaning even alone and others do not see.
Even women—to exclude the laws of Eretz Yisrael called the laws of Eldad HaDani, who brought Sefer Shechitot accepted among the ten tribes; he wrote that women should not slaughter; see Peleti, where I wrote to resolve his words, for Tosafot's question from the Gemara is not difficult for him.
And likewise the custom; Beit Yosef wondered—behold "we have not seen" is not proof; Shach wrote that regarding custom it is proof, as Maharik wrote, and in Choshen Mishpat at the end of siman 37 Shach expanded to resolve his words—for here they challenged him; see what Urim VeTumim wrote there, that the correct view is as Beit Yosef wrote.
And slaves—in Tur it is written "freed," implying slaves cannot slaughter; Taz sought to support this law, but it was already attained from Magen Avraham and Peri Chadash; the essence is as Shach wrote, that Tur holds an unspecified slave is in presumption of being reckless and light-minded, and one does not believe him regarding slaughter; the Mishnah in Zevachim deals with one established in kashrut; see Peleti, where I clarified that Tur's words are only a general stringency, and the law of the matter is that slaves are kosher for slaughter without doubt.
It is permitted to give him to slaughter on the basis that he will be examined after slaughter; we are not concerned lest he forget—for b'dieved, even if they did not examine him, it is kosher; likewise the poskim.
Before us—and Bach's view: even if we can pursue after him, it is not required; see Peleti, for it does not make sense—see there.
Three times to be stringent—we say three times, for a Torah matter twice establishes presumption.
A sage must be wise—not those who take payment from slaughterers—Taz.
Fluent in his mouth—they were stringent in this because it was transmitted to every person literally; woe—that they are not versed in Torah, and if they are not fluent in their mouths, error comes very easily.
For it is a great prohibition—and regarding pressing and the wing membranes, all slaughterers need this always; when two or three years pass, they should return for trial before a member of the city at that time, because perhaps he forgot, and the like; this is a proper custom; I myself practiced thus when I gave kabbalah to a slaughterer—I wrote in it that it helps only until after two or three years, and then the kabbalah is void until he returns to stand for trial; because of the wrath of zealots I did not hold to my custom—may the one God favors, who sits securely, receive reward if he practices so.
A great prohibition—and they wrote that even one accustomed to slaughter chickens is not established for chicks and young doves; a careful person is careful not to let such ones slaughter except with an expert slaughterer; in truth the teacher investigates and inquires into all this; Ran taught until one is expert for an hour; but the main thing—to know and to feel a knife defect—they do not investigate so much; woe, a great stumbling block, for great feeling is needed; sometimes the teacher himself has no feeling, especially an old man who is ashamed to say so; after he checks, meanwhile the defect is common, and many corpses they cast down, and mighty are all the slain; I testify regarding myself—even though they say I have feeling, nevertheless since I have aged I do not rely on my examination until expert slaughterers agree with me.
"Now he became defective"—Rabbi Taz disagrees and forbids retroactively; Peri Chadash agreed with him, and Bach in a responsum; likewise Shach and Nekudat HaKesef ruled like Rama; see Peleti, where I wrote that if he forgot laws of shechitah there is room to be lenient and say: just as he is accustomed to slaughter without delay and pressing, so he always acted from habit; but if he forgot to feel a knife defect—then his knife was not flawless—in this one must rule like Taz and forbid retroactively, even if he took kabbalah; also if there is something to hang it on, such as that he was ill and therefore came to forgetfulness, or that he did not engage for a long time in slaughter and was distracted—and therefore forgot—invalid until that time, but before, kosher.
If he did not take kabbalah—see Peleti, where I raised that specifically he does not know laws of shechitah—all the more examination of the knife; but if he does not know laws of examination—even though l'chatchila they are equal in their law, as Rama wrote, b'dieved it is not treifah even if he did not take kabbalah, and he claimed he knew and now forgot—see Peleti.
All that, etc.—but if he converted his religion, and all the more if he transgressed another sin—whatever happens—we do not forbid his slaughter retroactively, only from when he converted, and the like; for we say a foreign spirit passed over him, and initially he was kosher.
Also utensils—see Peleti, where I wrote that utensils over which Yom Kippur passed—absorbed taste—there is room to be lenient; we are not concerned lest something sharp be cooked in them, for it is uncommon; we are not concerned for an uncommon matter; food cooked in utensils that were then still from Yom Kippur is also forbidden.
