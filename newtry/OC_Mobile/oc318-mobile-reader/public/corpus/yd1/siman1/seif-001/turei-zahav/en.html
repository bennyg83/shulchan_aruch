**In the Tur he brought a braita: “And you shall slaughter as I commanded you”—teaching that Moses was commanded orally, etc.** One can question from that which is stated in chapter four deaths (96a) that Shabbos and honoring parents were commanded at Marah, as written “as the Lord your God commanded you,” and R' Yehuda said “as He commanded you” at Marah—if so, why is this “as I commanded you” different from “as He commanded you”? One can answer that Rashi explained there that from the second Tablets onward Moses did not teach on his own in the plains of Moab, but as he received he repeated and told them; and all that is written in the latter statements was written on the Tablets and likewise heard at Sinai—end of his words. If so, no difficulty: certainly what God told Moses in the plains of Moab in the repetition of the Torah, “and you shall slaughter as I commanded you,” is properly explained on what he taught him in the forty days after Sinai regarding oral Torah; not so regarding “as He commanded you” for honoring parents, which is in the Ten Commandments and likewise heard at Sinai in this wording—therefore one cannot say it refers to what he taught orally in forty days, for those forty days were after that; therefore we had to say it refers to Marah.
**Even women.** So Tosafot proved at the beginning of Chullin from the Mishnah in chapter Kol HaPesulim (Zevachim 31a): all disqualifed who slaughtered—their slaughter is valid—and it lists women, slaves, and tamei regarding kodshim; and there they say that even l'chatchila they slaughter; and it uses deyavad language because of tamei in kodshim, not because of women—hence women even l'chatchila. I have a difficulty from what Tosafot write in Kiddushin (77a) regarding service: “although shechitah is valid with women and slaves, that is b'dieved; l'chatchila only attributed Israelites perform it”—if so, perhaps here too it speaks b'dieved? One can answer that there “b'dieved” is not regarding issur—for certainly it is permitted by law even l'chatchila; rather there it speaks of the reverse of what they concluded there—that l'chatchila only attributed ones do so, or due to extra stringency they do not allow l'chatchila except for attributed ones.
**And slaves.** The Tur wrote “freed slaves,” and the Beit Yosef wondered—for a freed slave is a full Israelite, and both earlier and later authorities cried out on this matter (♯). It appears to me that even though he is freed he is not a full Israelite from the side of reason, for we find in Yalkut Shimoni on parashas Acharei Mos siman 799: “Among you”—to include women and freed slaves; a freed slave needs amplification like a woman. Therefore even in the Mishnah that lists women and slaves here, one can say it speaks of freed ones, and learn the unstated from the explicit elsewhere.
**And some say one may not rely on presumption, etc.** This is Or Zarua’s view; so too Ittur in the Tur’s words: in a city alley we are not concerned at beginning or end; regarding an expert one does not rely except b'dieved—and per them what the Gemara says “most found at shechitah are experts” means specifically b'dieved; and they hold b'dieved even examination is not required, as the Tur writes explicitly in the name of Ittur. Difficulty: Ravina in the first version said l'chatchila we require experts and other amoraim did not agree, because “most found are experts”—per Or Zarua and Ittur they should have agreed, for that majority helps only b'dieved. One can answer: Ravina requires examination b'dieved and they hold even b'dieved no examination. So Beit Yosef wrote: they disagree only at the end of Ravina’s words, not at the beginning. If so, establish the Mishnah thus: “All slaughter—l'chatchila when we know they are experts; their slaughter is valid” refers to when we do not know—valid without examination. The Mishnah’s meaning for b'dieved validity requires an additional factor: for Ravina, examination; for other amoraim each his factor—one cuts an olive of meat, one checks the knife, etc.—as in the sugya. This is required by the Mishnah’s wording “their slaughter is valid,” which refers to those already mentioned; how could one person be l'chatchila and b'dieved? Rather it refers to the quality required in that person: l'chatchila we need that quality known; afterward, if not known l'chatchila it can become known—Ravina’s first version speaks when expert quality was known l'chatchila, then says even if known only at the end it is valid; likewise each resolution (e.g., tamei in kodshim needs long knife not to touch; b'dieved if he says he did not touch, short knife suffices; Abaye: all slaughter even a gentile; l'chatchila needs correction—Israelite stands over him; b'dieved also corrected by cutting olive and giving, etc.). Since for those amoraim b'dieved is valid without examination, the Mishnah must be established as we wrote—clear in the sugya; so we rule.
**Before a sage and experts, etc.** In Rambam and Tur it is not written “and expert”; it appears the Rama comes to teach that two things are required—not enough to take kabbalah from an expert in shechitot alone, but from a sage as well, for he knows how to test him well; and those who take kabbalah from slaughterers who are not sages in any case do not act properly.
**That they be fluent in his mouth.** Although for other rulings the teacher need not know all rulings by heart, nevertheless for shechitah—which is delegated to everyone—they were stringent; proof from the Talmud’s wording in establishing Ravina’s view in this language: “they know about him that he knows to say the laws of shechitah”—this “to say” is seemingly superfluous, but it teaches that he must know the laws by heart. We see many who are unrestrained do not always pay attention to review the laws; therefore great ones are accustomed to investigate slaughterers even after kabbalah—and rebuke will help them.
**And if they inspected a slaughterer, etc.** (♯) First I will cite the source of this law, then our difficulty. Agudah learned it from the dispute of Rav Huna and Rav Chisda in the first chapter of Chullin (10a): one slaughtered with a checked knife before shechitah and they did not check immediately after; a defect was found—Rav Huna disqualifies even if he broke a bone on it between shechitah and knife-check, lest the skin was nicked; Rav Chisda holds it valid, lest the windpipe was nicked. We rule like Rav Chisda when he broke bones, as below siman 18; they challenge Rav Chisda there from one who immersed and a chatzitzah was found—even though he dealt with that type afterward, immersion did not count; a safek chatzitzah removes certainty of immersion. They conclude: here is different—the knife became compromised, the animal did not. Agudah derives: a slaughterer examined and found he does not know shechitah laws—we do not declare utensils treifah retroactively, for a person became compromised, the animal did not; we establish the slaughterer on presumption he knew until now. I question Tosafot there who challenged from Niddah 3a—a mikveh measured and found lacking: all purifications done with it are retroactively impure, “the lack is before you—establish the tamei on its presumption”; we do not say “the mikveh became compromised, the person did not,” and likewise establish the animal on its presumption. They gave three answers: (1) mikveh is different—it began lacking gradually, unlike a knife; (2) here bone certainly damages; for mikveh one can say he certainly immersed in a lacking place; (3) no difference between knife compromise and animal compromise—here we permit due to double doubt: perhaps bone damage, perhaps skin damage and he did not slaughter at the nick. If so, difficulty on Agudah’s law for this slaughterer—why not be stringent like mikveh? Those three answers do not apply: (1) forgetting laws also comes gradually—cannot say he knew everything and forgot all at once; sense contradicts—one forgets study bit by bit; we expound “if you forget, you will forget”; when he slaughters now you cannot say he knew until now and forgot all at once—prohibit retroactively like mikveh; so Mordechai on cheeses from a cow found treifah (R' Yosef Kol Tzof)—if we can say treifah came later, forbid all retroactively; here too he forgot and it came. (2) Tosafot’s second answer does not apply—no certainty for leniency; there is definite disqualification that he does not know, only time of forgetting unknown—Agudah relied on Tosafot’s third answer, as Agudah at the beginning of Niddah. Therefore this slaughterer too: double doubt—perhaps he knew laws then; if you say he did not know, perhaps he slaughtered properly without disqualification—cannot say so, for we rule like Rav Chisda only with bone-breaking; we do not rely on double doubt that perhaps windpipe was damaged; if skin, perhaps he did not slaughter at the nick—we permit only with bone-breaking, and only proper breaking as below siman 18. This slaughterer has no superior reason to permit double doubt—answer: here too there is reason to permit by combining doubts, attributing forgetting to distant time as much as possible. Not like knife—there too one could attribute defect to the last cut (windpipe), yet we require bone-breaking; siman 18 forbids bird even if windpipe touched. (3) Cannot permit utensils on presumption of initial permissibility—Gittin 31, Rashi on measured mikveh: terumot and purifications used with vessels and people who immersed are impure despite presumption of purity for those purifications, because of mikveh/tevilah flaw—so here. Cannot bring proof from Kiddushin 66—R' Akiva learns blemished kohen from ben gerushah whose disqualification is only after two years—this slaughterer is not disqualified by a single act as below siman 7; there he can disqualify by saying “remove your garment and I will show you are blemished”—here too “come, I will show you do not know shechitah laws”; Tosafot’s “two disputes” does not resemble here. Further proof for Rama’s ruling: Rashba responsum 218—they asked about one who did not know laws, wanted to validate past slaughter via “most at shechitah are experts,” perhaps he forgot now but knew until now—validate meat and utensils. He answered: do not eat from his slaughter; we do not say for one examined and ignorant “most are experts,” nor “establish on presumption,” except one initially presumed reliable—like a knife checked and used then found defective, or measured mikveh—and even there there is dispute; but here what presumption exists that he learned and knew? Therefore I see no validation for what he slaughtered or utensils cooked therein—only one doubt: perhaps a flaw occurred, perhaps not—end of his words. From this Rama learned: if he took kabbalah we do not declare retroactively treifah. Question on Rashba’s intent that where presumption exists we rely—what does knife case teach? We do not rely on presumption there except Rav Chisda, but halachah requires bone-breaking. Rashba himself in long Terumat HaDeshen: even with bone-breaking, say animal stands alive in presumption of issur per R' Hillel whom we follow—answer: here since knife was checked first and slaughtered animal is before you, say establish knife on presumption and this flaw came via bone-breaking—knife presumption overrides animal presumption; permit only with bone-breaking. If we find leniency via double doubt here, difficulty—for Rashba also has doubt he knew and forgot, doubt no flaw occurred; for mikveh we rule to forbid even with presumption to permit. Rashba may have written expansively showing disputes (Rav Huna/Rav Chisda, mikveh, Gittin, some say presumption of heter helps)—here all forbid, no presumption at all; Mechaber wrote “and even so they dispute”—Beit Yosef omitted “and even so they dispute” unintentionally; Rashba intended even with presumption there is dispute, here all forbid. Per halachah we rule like Rav Chisda only with bone-breaking; without that double doubt does not help—animal stands in presumption of issur until known properly slaughtered—this slaughterer is retroactively invalid; so Rashba in another responsum. Proof from Mordechai chapter Mi sheMet: teacher tested if student knows learning—if now he knows, he knew initially; here since now he does not know, initially he did not know. Not comparable—we know he knew once when he took kabbalah; there teacher plainly did not know from birth. Mordechai rules like R' Nasan “follow the present” for deathbed gift—we rule like rabbis who disagree only that “the burden of proof is on the extractor”—all the more here safek issur, be stringent. Therefore although Agudah ruled leniently and Rama brought it, I conclude to be stringent: forbid remaining meat from this slaughterer; forbid known utensils cooked therein; one who wishes to be lenient should first refute our structure, then eat—so it appears to me.
