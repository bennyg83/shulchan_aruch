**All slaughter, etc.** Taz brought the words of Tur, who cited the baraita that Rabbi taught: “And you shall slaughter,” etc., “as I commanded you”—teaching that Moses was commanded regarding two [simanim] in an animal and one in a bird, and most of one like him. Taz challenged: generally we derive on what is stated in the verse “as He commanded you”—commanded at Marah; why is this “as I commanded you” different from “as He commanded”? Answer: to distinguish Torah from Mishnah—see there; and in any case there is room to say that at Marah they were commanded only regarding mitzvot that would be practiced immediately from then on; and how many mitzvot the Holy One blessed be He preceded giving them at Marah until giving the entire Torah; but a mitzvah that would not apply to them immediately, only after some time from receiving the Torah—He gave only at giving the Torah, when all of them came—whether practiced immediately or after time. And in the desert we hold like R' Akiva that they did not require shechitah in Chullin—only nehirah meat was permitted them; and likewise Rambam explicitly. If so, how could it enter one's mind that they were commanded at Marah regarding a mitzvah that would not apply to them immediately until entering the Land—and what was its nature at Marah? Perforce “as I commanded you” refers to Moses, who was commanded regarding entering the Land—and straightforward. With this one may resolve the words of Rambam in chapter 1 of Hilchot Shechitah, who wrote: learning bird shechitah from Torah—for regarding bird it says “whoever hunts animal or bird and pours its blood,” teaching that pouring blood of a bird is like blood of an animal—end quote. Maggid Mishneh wondered: in the Gemara we derive from this verse that there is no shechitah for a bird min haTorah—for it is written “and pour its blood”—mere pouring; and it challenges “say animal”; and answers it is equated to disqualifications of kodshim, etc.; and we say “who is Chanina who disagrees?” and we say “it is Rabbi,” who taught “and you shall slaughter,” etc., “as I commanded you,” and we learn Moses was commanded regarding two in animal and one in bird—end quote the Gemara. If so, on the contrary, from this verse “and pour” it should enter one's mind to declare bird kosher without shechitah—and Rambam learns from it that shechitah is required. Maggid Mishneh answered: now that we hold like Rabbi—from “as I commanded you,” etc., that he was commanded regarding bird—the verse “and pour” goes for pouring. And still explanation is needed—for nevertheless the Gemara did not mention we derive from it; on the contrary it should be inferred explicitly the opposite; and many certainly infer from “and you shall slaughter from your cattle and flock as I commanded you” the command regarding animal—but regarding bird we did not hear from where is an allusion (see Tosafot who expounded Rabbeinu Tam, see there). And it appears that certainly in Scripture it is written “for whoever hunts animal or bird”—it opens in the plural and concludes in the singular “and he shall pour its blood,” not “their blood”—plural language (see Chullin 87 s.v. lechalek, see there); and it must be said “and pour” means mere pouring without shechitah—and this is only regarding bird; therefore it wrote “its blood.” Or, conversely, Scripture spoke in singular language to teach that animal and bird are as one species for shechitah, and both require shechitah. And if we say like R' Akiva that in the desert nehirah was permitted—why the first explanation that it comes to teach mere pouring suffices, for then in the desert obviously everything was by pouring and pouring is not a precise matter at all; perforce the second sign is needed to teach they are equal forever, whether in the desert or afterward—either both by pouring or both by shechitah without difference. And this is the intent of the Gemara of Rabbi, who said “as I commanded you” teaches Moses was commanded, etc.; for at Marah, where he holds nehirah was permitted in the desert, there is no need for “and pour” to teach bird suffices by pouring; perforce it must teach one law with animal, and it is proven that also for bird shechitah is required like animal—and these are the words of Rambam; think.
**Even women.** Tosafot and Rosh wrote this—to exclude the laws of Eretz Yisrael, which wrote women do not slaughter l'chatchila because their minds are light-minded; Tosafot challenged him from the beginning of chapter Kol HaPesulim: the Gemara challenges “all invalid persons who slaughtered”—b'dieved yes, l'chatchila no; yet slaughter by a zar is kosher l'chatchila, and it answers because it teaches “slaughter is kosher with women, slaves,” etc., and tamei—l'chatchila no, lest he touch meat—and it does not answer “because of women,” etc.; rather learn that women are kosher l'chatchila. In Tosafot, Ran, and Rosh it mentions anonymously “because their minds are light”; Hagahot Maimoniyot chapter 4 of Hilchot Shechitah wrote their minds are light lest they faint—therefore one may reject their proof, or certainly if one stands al gavav and sees she did not faint it is kosher. In kodshim slaughter it is uncommon for a woman to slaughter without a grown man al gavav, for a kohen must receive the blood—from reception onward it is a mitzvah of kehunah. It is strained to say she slaughtered with a long knife outside the azarah—for in any case a woman's way is not in the azarah, as Tosafot wrote in several places, and the kohen stands in the azarah and receives blood—like tamei in kodshim, which also deals with kehunah—and if so he does not see the slaughter; this is strained. And he holds regarding slaughter in Eretz Yisrael that with one al gavav a woman is permitted l'chatchila even though some say we are concerned for fainting—we do not permit l'chatchila with one al gavav for a woman, but certainly we permit—it is only a general stringency. Perhaps Tosafot go according to their view in Chullin 12b s.v. katan, etc., that a minor with one al gavav has da'at—and if so in kodshim too one may say his slaughter helps; if so those in the Mishnah deal with one al gavav—why does it teach “slaughter is kosher with women,” etc.? It should teach a greater novelty that it is kosher with minors when one stands al gavav—but this is not enough to attack the laws of Eretz Yisrael. Perhaps Tosafot come against the author of laws of Eretz Yisrael in both ways: according to his view those Amoraim who are not concerned for the question “and all who slaughtered,” etc., should explain the Mishnah with that resolution—“all slaughter” means all men slaughter, but women do not slaughter l'chatchila, and if they slaughtered their slaughter is kosher—tzarich iyun; and one must say the Gemara holds “and if they slaughtered” is redundant, and if to permit b'dieved—it already teaches in Zevachim that slaughter is kosher with women, as the Gemara challenged regarding tamei in kodshim; and the Gemara answers there the main topic is kodshim, etc.—this does not apply to disqualification of women, which is mainly in chullin, and one should not make a distinction between kodshim and chullin—two unnecessary teachings fit well. However per this one must say that which does not deal with one al gavav—for otherwise it teaches too much that even without one al gavav it is kosher b'dieved—and Tosafot's question stands well in both ways. In the body of the question itself it should have established the Mishnah thus: “all slaughter”—men, etc.—this question can be asked in several ways; the correct view is as Taz wrote, but not for his reasons—for one may only say that certainly by way of parable: if it teaches “women do not slaughter l'chatchila,” it implies l'chatchila forbidden and b'dieved permitted—and then it should be difficult that it further teaches “and if he slaughtered his slaughter is kosher b'dieved, permitted,” for this is already implied from the precision of the reisha. However for every resolution in the Gemara this question applies: Rabah bar Avuha establishes it regarding tamei in kodshim—do not slaughter l'chatchila; this is if he did not say “it is clear to me I did not touch,”—even b'dieved invalid; only if he says “it is clear to me” then b'dieved permitted—and behold here is another mode not heard from the precision of the reisha. Likewise Abaye and Rava establish Kuti—do not slaughter unless he cuts an olive-size of meat and eats; even b'dieved invalid unless he cuts and eats—then b'dieved permitted and his slaughter is kosher. Likewise R' Ashi regarding apostate—certainly if he does not check his knife afterward b'dieved forbidden; only kosher b'dieved when he checked the knife. And likewise the two versions of Ravina: if we did not ask him hilchos shechitah; and in the final version if he did not say “it is clear to me I did not faint,” and treifah issued b'dieved—only one who knows hilchos shechitah and says “it is clear to me” is then kosher b'dieved. If so the Mishnah deals with three modes—l'chatchila and b'dieved wholly invalid—unlike what is written here, only l'chatchila and b'dieved, which the Mishnah cannot bear at all. In truth Maharshal does not hold so—for he challenged the poskim that l'chatchila one may not rely on “most found at shechitah are experts” only if he slaughtered—no examination needed, we rely on majority; Maharshal challenged: establish the Mishnah “all expert slaughterers slaughter”—when they know he knows; but if they do not know he does not slaughter, and b'dieved his slaughter is kosher—see there; per what is written there is no room for three modes, only l'chatchila and b'dieved, and one cannot say so in the Mishnah as written. But regarding the challenge to the Geonim at the beginning—it is not resolved with the answer I wrote, that there is no case at all of l'chatchila do not slaughter until examined—for if he slaughtered it is permitted; if so the siyafa is redundant, for it explains “all slaughter” on the basis of examination afterward, and if he did not examine b'dieved permitted—and there is no redundant precision at all; think. Nevertheless there is a resolution for laws of Eretz Yisrael—for they certainly stand according to what we hold we are not concerned for fainting, but a woman stands in presumption of fainting; however for one version in the Gemara in the words of Ravina—in every person we are concerned for fainting and require presumption he will not faint; if so for women too—if no presumption, obviously forbidden; if there is presumption, from Torah to forbid. If so there in Zevachim the Gemara sought to answer for all, even for that resolution of Ravina—if so unnecessary “because of women” that women differ from men—even kohanim may not slaughter l'chatchila without presumption, and likewise women; there is no difference between men and women, and therefore it had to say “because of tamei.” According to the conclusion that we are not concerned for fainting—so much the more laws of Eretz Yisrael. The upshot: the words of the author of laws of Eretz Yisrael may not be pushed aside; Rama wrote well that women should not be left to slaughter—do not heed Peri Chadash's testimony that he saw women slaughtering, river by river, and it spread.
**Slaves.** Tur wrote “freed slaves.” Our master Beit Yosef wondered: the Mishnah teaches slaughter is kosher with women and slaves, and unspecified slaves means not freed—for freed ones are in the category of Israel and the term “slaves” does not apply to them; Taz in s.k. 2 holds his view that the term “slaves” applies even to freed ones and they are not in the category of unspecified Israel; Shach in Nekudat HaKesef expanded to challenge and spoke well. See and know Rambam in Hilchot Megillah chapter 1 law 1: “all are obligated in reading the Megillah—men, women, converts, and freed slaves”—he did not write unspecified slaves, only freed slaves. But in truth one may say he holds that the rule “whatever a woman is obligated, slaves are obligated” applies to her through a gezeirah shavah of lamed lamed. In Megillah women themselves are obligated because they were in that miracle—this does not apply to slaves; Haman decreed only on members of the covenant, not on slaves—therefore slaves are exempt; therefore Rambam wrote “freed,” and this is truly against Tosafot, who challenged why a verse is needed that women are obligated in four cups—derive from “they were in that miracle”; one may say that because they were in that miracle slaves were exempt, unlike now that we interpreted the verse “they too” are obligated through gezeirah shavah of lamed lamed. In any case truth guides his way that unspecified slaves means not freed, especially what the Mishnah says. Shach in s.k. 2 raised that unspecified slaves are presumed reckless and light-minded and unfit for slaughter—therefore Tur wrote “freed slaves,” and the Mishnah that teaches slaughter is kosher with slaves deals with one established in kashrut whom they recognize. I did not fully understand his words—for why strain the Gemara in Zevachim to say the slaves in the Mishnah means recognized ones, and establish unspecified slaves as unrecognized, and l'chatchila truly forbidden, only if he slaughtered and they examined that he is in presumption of kashrut it is kosher—therefore the Mishnah uses the language “slave”; if so it is possible to answer thereby that it specifies “slave” because of slaves, and one need not say because of tamei in kodshim. It is also difficult for me to establish the Mishnah in such a case that all Israelites slaughter and not slaves—and if he slaughtered and it is found he is in presumption of kashrut their slaughter is kosher. Also “and all of them”—if his presumption of kashrut was not clarified and he slaughtered and others see—their slaughter is kosher. Therefore it appears even Tur did not forbid except as a general stringency—not to lighten and be reckless with reckless light-minded slaves; but by law they are permitted, and we establish presumption of kashrut; see below siman 126 seif 2 that all disqualifications for testimony are kosher regarding issurim—therefore by law they are kosher, and the Mishnah that deals with law includes them among those kosher l'chatchila.
**But if he is before us**, etc. This method is the method of the ancients and Geonim when Ra'avad testified; they thought that l'chatchila they can give each one to slaughter on the basis that he will be examined after slaughter, and if he slaughtered before us he is not known to be an expert—examination is required. And it appears simply: since it teaches "all slaughter" even when it is not known he is an expert, l'chatchila on the basis he will examine afterward—this case in the siyafa "if it went without examination his slaughter is kosher" is redundant, for it is obvious—otherwise how could one rely and give him to slaughter on the basis they will examine after slaughter, lest he forget and not examine; rather learn examination does not impede b'dieved and therefore we are not concerned he will forget—and likewise all authors; perforce one must say the Mishnah does not deal with this. However nevertheless Rosh's answer was needed—for later on 17a we say "all slaughter"—the Mishnah teaches twice, what does it teach us; Rava answers: one to include Kuti and one apostate; and it is asked why Rava did not say this final Mishnah should teach "all expert slaughterers slaughter" l'chatchila on the basis he will examine as Ravina's text—and we do not establish from Ravina's resolution except because of "most found at slaughter" and in this Mishnah there is no place for this question—and Rosh's question stands in its place that it should teach "all slaughter" l'chatchila even when it is not known he is expert—but in the final words of Rosh it is resolved that it is not called l'chatchila since in any case examination is needed afterward. And now Maharshal's question stands—he challenged Beit Yosef and Ba'al HaItur who ruled l'chatchila they need to know he is expert, only if he slaughtered no examination is needed, we rely on majority; Maharshal challenged and established the Mishnah thus: "all experts slaughter" l'chatchila, and if he slaughtered b'dieved his slaughter is kosher without examination—there is resolution for this: one may understand why Rava did not establish this "all slaughter" in the second Mishnah as "all slaughter" l'chatchila even without presumption he will not faint when we are not concerned for fainting at all as Maharshal wrote there is no concern at all—if so this "all" is not to include we are not concerned for fainting—for this is obvious and not included in the inclusion at all; per this it appears Maharshal should not have said "slaughter" when they know he is expert—for "all" comes to include, and here "all" has no inclusion for there must be experts; perforce regarding l'chatchila there is no inclusion and one must say thus: "all" even without presumption he will not faint—but must be expert certainly, and if he slaughtered b'dieved even when it was not known he is expert it is kosher—and this is Maharshal's intent in his question; but per what is written there is no conclusion we are not concerned for fainting—this is not an inclusion at all; to "we are not concerned for fainting" etc. it is asked well what does "all" include; and there is room for question. One should not be precise with Rosh's words that since examination is still needed, asking is not called l'chatchila—if so why does the sugya challenge in this topic going out and entering l'chatchila—did we not teach "the watchman does not go out," etc.—why challenge, certainly it is not called l'chatchila that he needs to cut an olive-size of meat and give to him—if so it is also not called l'chatchila. One may say the issue itself is difficult here that we should teach "the watchman does not go out" etc.—learn from going out and entering it is like one al gavav; if so why need a remedy to cut olive-size in going out and entering and with one al gavav not needed—and think. **Before us and if we can pursue them.** Bach learned from Ra'avad's words that even Ravina holds "most found at slaughter are experts"—only he holds if they are before us and we can pursue we do not rely on majority and examination is needed. **That we say “now he became defective.”** Agudah learned thus from that which we say: he slaughtered with a knife and it was found defective—R' Chanina declares kosher, and the Gemara explains: what is different from tevel on which something interposing was found and immersion did not take effect—the Gemara answers: a knife became defective but an animal did not become defective; Agudah wrote: likewise here—the slaughterer became defective but the animal did not become defective. Taz wondered: this is for R' Chanina, but we hold like Rav Huna that even if he broke bones in it every day it is forbidden—learn we do not say “the knife became defective”; if so, likewise regarding a slaughterer one should forbid retroactively. Tosafot challenged: what is different from a mikveh that was measured and found lacking—they render impure retroactively and we do not say “the place became defective but the person did not”; Tosafot answered several answers, and one answer that there was a safek the defect was afterward and we thought it was beforehand—perhaps he did not slaughter with that defect. If so, in this case too the safek applies: perhaps now he forgot and we thought beforehand that perhaps nevertheless he slaughtered properly—this is all for R' Chanina; but since we hold like Rav Huna, learn we do not say so—and likewise regarding a slaughterer there is room to forbid. Peri Chadash agreed and brought proof from Rashba who holds regarding cheeses: if you must say it did not become treifah now but only before, we say establish retroactively. If so here too one cannot say that at this moment he forgot—establish retroactively. These words do not help and do not avail—for when Rashba spoke he certainly did not say except for a safek where we say “perhaps it was before”; we too concede in this case there is a safek—only it is a safek as Taz wrote. Rashba derives his reasoning from a found stain and from “I saw it yesterday evening”—all is safek, not vadai. Shach in Nekudat HaKesef argued here there is a majority assisting—even R' Huna admits “most found at slaughter are experts.” I did not understand—for regarding cheeses Tosafot forbid when an animal found treifah has no clear presumption and we do not follow majority that most animals are kosher; one must say here it became defective—it is treifah before you and left the category of majority, therefore majority does not help. Likewise Maharshal explicitly in Chullin: therefore they require presumption of kashrut from the force of majority, not from majority alone—for when it became treifah it left the category of majority and majority does not assist it; and likewise here—“most found at slaughter are experts” means as written: no one stands to slaughter unless it is clear to him in his eyes that they are experts and know how to slaughter—and here before us this one wants to slaughter and cannot slaughter—therefore this majority follows him as with a treifah animal. In truth Rosh at the beginning of Chullin permits cheeses from majority alone even when they saw treifah—but Rashba and other authors do not hold so; they gave only the reason of presumption from the force of majority—see Yeshuot Malko at the beginning of Chullin. What advocates for Agudah: one must understand R' Huna who disagrees with R' Chanina and disqualifies when a defect is found—what is the reason? This is a safek, and in all of Torah we rely on safek; see Ketubot at the beginning—Tosafot wrote even R' Yosef who disagrees on absolute safek concedes; here too it is a safek, and one must say it is in the category of shem oness echad—and even when we judge whether the knife is defective or not, we judge whether the simanim were slaughtered with the defect or not—what do we care if the defect was beforehand on the knife and the simanim were not slaughtered with it, or defective after slaughter and the defect never reached the simanim—two doubts apply: whether he slaughtered with the defective simanim or not, for whatever reason—and therefore it is not a great safek safeka; in truth Tosafot at the beginning of Niddah wrote only sfeika tuva, not safek safeka—therefore R' Huna forbids; but here regarding safek of the slaughterer it is a real safek safeka and shem oness echad does not apply, for it is known whether one safek permits more than its fellow—it is not called shem oness echad; see general rules of safek safeka; and here in the safek “perhaps now, an hour before he slaughtered a quarter-hour ago, certainly treifah”—in such a short time it is impossible to forget without doubt that he also forgot then and it was treifah; but in this safek “perhaps nevertheless he slaughtered properly” you permit all his slaughterings—and therefore this safek does not fail the other and shem oness echad does not apply, and behold it is an absolute safek, and all permit—and therefore Agudah relied on this variant in Tosafot to be a safek. However it may be: for Rambam's words Agudah may rely together to be lenient, as written; and there is a reasoning to distinguish a slaughterer with a defective knife from one who forgot hilchos shechitah: one might say he did not make the defect in the simanim—who prevents him? He did not know from the defect; unlike one who forgot hilchos shechitah—initially he knew and was expert and accustomed to slaughter properly, and habit becomes nature to slaughter properly without delay and pressing, etc.; even after forgetting hilchos shechitah, habit brings that he will slaughter properly; from Torah there is the change of habit and he may slaughter not according to law, etc. Therefore it appears to distinguish one who forgets laws of shechitah and had kabbalah—there is room to be lenient for the reason I wrote; but one who forgets feeling the knife to sense a defect and says about a knife that is not defective—in this one must be stringent, for without doubt he slaughtered with a defective knife since he does not sense to check the knife; in this one must be stringent from Torah as Taz and Peri Chadash ruled. See Kereti as I wrote if there is something to hang it on, such as that he was ill and therefore accustomed to forget, or he was distracted from slaughter and did not engage in it—we attribute the forgetting to that, for we always follow what is common and habitual; and in particular he already took kabbalah—he is forbidden only until that time, but beforehand permitted—for we say he certainly remembered and it is correct and clear.
**If he did not take kabbalah.** Shach in s.k. 8 challenged from the parallel in cheeses regarding an animal found treifah—for we say "now it became defective," and the answer is there majority of animals are kosher, but here there is no majority of all experts, only "most found at slaughter are experts"; and further there it left presumption from life—for one may say "now it became treifah," unlike here laws of slaughter. Shach answered it is impossible he was born knowing—one must say he learned and forgot, and we do not say so—see there; I did not understand—for certainly if we say majority of Israel are learned it is not a complete majority dependent on action, and we hold in Bekhorot a majority dependent on action is not a majority; but here we say thus: majority who prepare themselves to slaughter do not act unless they are experts—and if so behold this is a complete majority like majority of kosher animals—so we say majority of people are kosher and do not come to slaughter unless they know laws of slaughter, and behold this is a complete majority; on the contrary Ra'ah's view in bedikat haBayit: majority is not precise, rather one must say all found at slaughter are experts—for otherwise rely on minority against presumption of issur of the animal and the majority became defective; and even Rashba who disagrees in mishmeret haBayit beginning of gate 1 concedes it is a complete majority until the matter of relying on the aforementioned minority applies. And if so from Ramban's Milchamot it appears there are minorities that are common who are not experts—he compares it to lungs and when treifah is found in the lung nevertheless he declared cheeses kosher retroactively. Also I did not understand the second answer—for he ruled below siman 81 not like Rashba that even if the mouth was wounded it was certainly also days before slaughter, and nevertheless we do not establish retroactively; Peri Chadash wrote the majority became defective—"most found" we see he is not expert and he slaughtered—and his words do not avail, for also in treifah behold treifah is before you and certainly the majority became defective majority of kosher animals as written—if so with wounded mouth how declare kosher, for there is no "after slaughter it became treifah" here, and his words give no support; and to me there is room to say regarding treifot the animal stands in presumption of heter, therefore we say establish the animal on its presumption, and now that it became treifah and the lung was lost it is kosher—unlike here where there is safek in slaughter and no presumption of heter at all; Rav Huna who disqualifies with a defective knife—R' Huna goes according to his reasoning: the animal stands in presumption of issur, and likewise this slaughterer is like one who slaughters with a defective knife—forbidden and stands in presumption of issur, and majority of "most found" cannot assist, etc., for his majority became defective since we see he does not know and left the category of majority as written before—and this is clear: there is no majority and no presumption at all—for the animal is in presumption of issur regarding slaughter, and one should not compare safek in slaughter to safek in treifah—and the practical difference in law if the slaughterer knows laws of slaughter only does not know laws of treifot in other types of treifot transmitted to slaughterers—kosher retroactively since there is presumption of heter and assisting majority, obviously we declare kosher, all the more cheeses; see below siman 50 and straightforward. It should not be only like lost lung which is also kosher—which is not so if simanim were lost that it is treifah, and this is correct. Behold Shach wrote regarding temurah and the like we do not establish presumption to forbid slaughter retroactively—and think, for truth is there is room to distinguish temurah—for even if he sinned he is Israel and we attribute at that hour he was kosher and was slaughtering properly; but regarding laws of Eretz Yisrael it does not suffice that he did not err—and nevertheless think, since we did not find proof for this explicitly in words of ancients—and think.
