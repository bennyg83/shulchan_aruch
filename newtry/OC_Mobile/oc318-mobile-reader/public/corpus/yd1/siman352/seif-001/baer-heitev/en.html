Expensive. Darkei Moshe writes that one who makes fine shrouds for a deceased person demonstrates belief in the resurrection of the dead and is praiseworthy. This refers to making the linen itself fine and attractive, and therefore some seek good-quality linen. Excessively costly shrouds, however, are certainly forbidden. Shach. Nevertheless, everything depends on the person's status. For one of substantial wealth, there is no prohibition in dressing him in more valuable linen, since R. Yochanan called clothing one's honor. What honors the deceased is certainly permitted. In particular, the kabbalists write that the soul is clothed in spiritual garments corresponding to the garments placed on the body, and bring support from the episode of the two spirits in Berachot and other sources. One should therefore be careful not to change customary burial clothing, for Sefer Chassidim and Sefer HaChaim indicate that the dead can be particular about this. Ma'avar Yabbok writes that even after the physical shrouds decay, the soul remains clothed in their spiritual counterpart. Some people leave instructions regarding their shrouds, and it is a mitzvah to fulfill the wishes of the deceased when possible and not contrary to halachah. A case occurred where graves in our cemetery were opened and the deceased were stripped naked; the practice followed the ruling in Shevut Yaakov, part 2, question 103. If one garment was accidentally omitted, it should be placed on top of the coffin. See Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 47, and Knesset Yechezkel. Sefer Chassidim writes that during an epidemic, may Hashem protect us, graves should be examined lest someone have been buried in worn-out clothing or with hands improperly extended, for such matters can be dangerous. Anything required for saving life may be done to a deceased person.
