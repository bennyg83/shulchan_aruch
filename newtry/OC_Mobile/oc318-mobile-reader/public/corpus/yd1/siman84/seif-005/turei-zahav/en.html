Worms found in flour, etc. The Beit Yosef cites an opinion permitting them even if they moved onto the walls of the jar and returned to the flour, like creatures in liquids. This is mistaken. The special inclusion permitting creatures in cisterns does not apply to insects in a vessel, and whether they remain permitted after partially emerging into the air is an unresolved question. They are therefore prohibited. I heard of a ruling that infested wheat should be thrown into a river rather than sold to a non-Jew, lest he bake bread and sell it to a Jew. Maharshal permitted feeding it to one's servants if it was not retained long enough to create a mishap. I do not accept a distinction between a short and long period, for even during a short period the bread may become mixed with other bread in the household. The earlier leniency for a short period concerns an item from which the owner remains separate, unlike bread prepared for household consumption.
Lest they have separated. Those who are lenient above even where a worm's hole opens outward would certainly be lenient here. The Shulchan Aruch's lenient ruling there and stringent ruling here are therefore difficult. The Rama properly rules stringently in both places. The Rokeach's permission of worms found in flour rests on the view that we do not suspect separation. Since we rule that separation is a concern, there is no distinction between larger and smaller flour mites. My revered father-in-law's proposed distinction is unsupported.
It is forbidden to sell them to a non-Jew, etc. My revered father-in-law limited the prohibition to flour, lest the non-Jew bake bread and sell it to a Jew, and permitted sale of infested salt, legumes, or dried fruit because such foods are normally inspected. I disagree. In practice, Jews purchase these products from non-Jews without inspection unless a defect is visible. The Ran's leniency concerned sprouted wheat whose defect was publicly known and visibly recognizable. Therefore, infested salt, legumes, and dried fruit may be sold only in small quantities, as with wheat damaged by water before Pesach. I was also asked about producing distilled liquor from infested flour. Although the intent is to extract vapor rather than nullify the insects, I prohibited it because permitting such use would lead people to eat the flour itself.
