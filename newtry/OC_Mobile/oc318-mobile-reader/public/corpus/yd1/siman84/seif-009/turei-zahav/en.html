Within twelve months without inspection. Maharshal infers that a thorough individual examination is effective even within twelve months, namely, inspecting every fruit for an external defect. The Tur's statement that there is no remedy within twelve months refers only to the hot-water test. The custom is to sort chickpeas upon a table and remove those with holes. Maharshal was reluctant to rely upon women for such laborious inspection, while Toras Chatas records the custom to rely upon them because several grounds for leniency exist.
For there is no fruit, etc. Terumas HaDeshen records a tradition that only in large apples or other large fruit is the worm nullified in sixty. In small fruit the entire fruit may become prohibited and require sixty against the whole item. Maharshal rules stringently. Toras Chatas permits when the dish contains other ingredients besides the fruit. In my opinion, there is an additional basis for leniency from siman 104: repulsive creatures such as flies and mosquitoes do not prohibit when the permitted food exceeds them, provided the worm itself is no longer present.
But three or four, etc. Why mention four once three already establish infestation? The Ran teaches that even when four are found, the liquid may still be permitted through straining, as explained below. Merely discarding the visible insects is insufficient, but straining is effective. The Shulchan Aruch follows the Ran's wording even though it did not state this conclusion explicitly.
Once it has been established as infested, etc. Maharshal avoided dried mushrooms after repeatedly finding worms in the pot even after inspection. This presumption applies specifically to a food in which infestation is common and the inspection was inadequate. If infestation is not ordinarily found, we attribute the discovered worms to an outside source and do not presume more. An incident occurred on Shavuos when women throughout a city baked bread and pastries with raisins from one seller, and worms were later found in the seller's stock. Products purchased before the discovery were permitted through a double doubt: perhaps the worms were not yet present, and even if present perhaps they dissolved in the oven. Products purchased after the discovery were forbidden, for only one doubt remained, whether they dissolved. There is no basis to distinguish flour mites from other worms by claiming they dissolve more readily, especially where Torah prohibition is involved and the mites certainly separated from the raisins during kneading.
