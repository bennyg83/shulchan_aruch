And it is left ownerless in another person's possession. The Beit Yosef cites the Mordechai concerning a woman who died. Rabbeinu Eliezer of Metz said that she had vowed two dinars to charity. After her death, Rabbeinu Eliezer of Metz took possession of her money because he was a charity collector, while the heirs said, “We knew nothing of this charity.” Rabbeinu Yonah ruled that since she had not authorized him concerning this matter, there is no mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. Rather, he should give the money to the heirs and inform them that she had made such a vow, end quote. Accordingly, the Rama's phrase here, “in another person's possession,” means that after the donor's death the property came into that person's possession, or that during her lifetime it had been entrusted to him merely as a deposit. If, however, he says that it came into his possession specifically so that he should give it to charity, he is certainly believed, and there is a mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased, as written in Choshen Mishpat, siman 252.
And one of them died, etc. This comes from a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 259. A person appointed two guardians over his children and instructed them to distribute charity every Chanukah, and afterward his children were to continue, etc. One of the guardians died, and the community wished to appoint another in his place. The Rashba answered that if the testator intended that distribution occur only when there were specifically two guardians, then responsibility for the distribution returns to the children and not to the community. Nevertheless, he wrote, I see in every place that whenever an individual appoints guardians, or even when the community appoints treasurers, charity collectors, or trustees, if one of them dies or travels overseas, the appointment is not void. Rather, those remaining continue to act under their appointment. The law itself requires this, for otherwise, if one traveled for several days on business and was absent at Chanukah, the entire appointment would become void. This is not the intent of the community or of the individual who established the arrangement, end quote. This is astonishing. Since the testator appointed two people, perhaps he trusted neither one without the participation of the other. Why should one now be trusted alone? The appointment should be void. It appears that the Rashba did not conclusively rule that no one should be appointed alongside the survivor. Rather, he said that even if one assumes that the survivor alone is not trusted, the community has no authority over the matter, and it remains with the children as the testator instructed. He then wrote that the appointment is nevertheless never completely void so that no charity should be distributed merely because only one guardian remains. He did not conclusively rule that the community may not appoint another alongside him and that the survivor should remain alone forever. In any event, it appears that the money should not be left under the sole control of one guardian after his colleague dies. The Rashba wrote that the same rule applies even to appointments made by the community, yet we see that when communities appoint two charity collectors for a period and one dies, they appoint another in his place, and the same is done with other positions. All the more so regarding money should one person not be trusted alone. This is especially true in light of what we see over the years, that trustworthy people are few and that the assets of many orphans have collapsed through guardians who had previously been presumed honest. The community should certainly supervise this. Holy communities have already adopted the practice of appointing a guardian of orphans to supervise the conduct of guardians. Here too, they should supervise and ensure that the money is not left in the hands of only one of them. So it appears to me.
