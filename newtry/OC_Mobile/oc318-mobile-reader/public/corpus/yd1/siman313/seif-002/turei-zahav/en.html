Even if he asked, etc. In the Mishnah it is stated as follows: an incident occurred with an old ram, whose hair was dangling, and a certain officer saw it and said, “What is the nature of this animal, that they have left it to age so much?” They said to him, “It is a bekhor and cannot be slaughtered unless it has a blemish.” He took a spear and cut its ear. The incident came before the Sages and they permitted it. Rashi explained that one cannot say this gentile intended to permit it, since he does not know that through a blemish made in it, it may be slaughtered, unless it fell on its own. After they permitted it, he went and cut the ears of other bekhorot, and they forbade them. I copied this Mishnah to examine what difference there is between it and the law written in seif 3, “if they said to a gentile, etc.,” which is from Responsa HaRosh, kelal 20, where he said that it is not comparable to the Mishnah, because in the Mishnah he did not intend to permit, while here she intended to permit. This requires study: how did this incident differ from that one? At first glance it appears from the language of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch that the case of the Rosh is different because the gentile woman was a maidservant and certainly intended the benefit of this master. But this is not so. First, in the responsum of the Rosh there it is not mentioned at all that she was a maidservant, but only an ordinary gentile woman; if her being a maidservant were essential, he certainly would not have omitted it. Second, if we say that the Rosh explained the Mishnah like Rashi, that the officer did not know that it would be slaughtered through this blemish unless it fell on its own, why should we not say the same regarding a maidservant? Does she know more because she is a maidservant? And if we say that he did not explain like Rashi, it is difficult: what was the permission in the officer’s case, since from appearances he intended to permit it, having acted because of this question? One also cannot say that in the Rosh’s case they spoke to the gentile woman without her first asking, for in the Gemara it says explicitly that unknowingly is permitted, to include one speaking innocently. Rashi explains: the officer did not ask them, “What is its nature?” but rather they themselves spoke innocently and said, “Look, this will never be slaughtered except through a blemish,” and they did not intend that he impose a blemish on it. Thus we see that no question is required for this permission. If so, why did the Rosh forbid in his case? What appears to me is that this depends on what we find in Perek HaNizakin, in the aggadah of the destruction, where it says there: a place that for us was a blemish but for them was not a blemish. Rashi explains that for them it was not a blemish unless it was missing a limb. This is the permission in the incident of the officer according to the Rosh, not like Rashi’s explanation. Rather, the reason is that since the officer did not remove any limb, but only pierced the ear with a spear, and he did not know that through this they would permit it, they forbade only after he saw that they permitted through this. This is unlike the Rosh’s case, where the gentile woman cut the ear and it was missing a limb; this is clear to them as a blemish, so she certainly intended to permit it. The Tur’s words can also be explained this way. This is what I wrote: “but he intended to permit, such as after he sees that it is permitted through this,” which is the text, and so it is in Shitah Mekubetzet. This distinction is correct. The Tur was brief where he should have explained further, but truth shows its path as I wrote. So it appears to me.
Until a blemish falls in it. In the Gemara it is stated that even if he said, “until a blemish is made in it,” it is permitted, and we do not say that its implication is that you should make a blemish in it. There we follow leniency, since it is only a rabbinic penalty, even in a certainty.
That the Jew commanded him, etc. This implies even if the Jew denies it and says, “I did not command you,” and this is astonishing, for a gentile speaking innocently is no better than a Jew, who is not believed to forbid his fellow’s property in a case like this, as stated in siman 281 regarding one who says, “The Torah scroll that I wrote, I did not write the Divine Names for their sake,” where he is not believed. In Piskei Maharai siman 169, which is the source of this law, the case is that a gentile woman said that a certain Jew commanded her mother to cut the ear of a bekhor, and it would thereby be permitted for slaughter, and she did so; it is forbidden. There, there is no difficulty even if the Jew denies it, for nevertheless, since she knows that the bekhor is permitted through this and did it for that purpose, there is prohibition because she acted to permit it in any case, even if the command was false. Therefore a gentile speaking innocently is effective for this. This is unlike where it is not known that she knows it is permitted through this, and the prohibition is only because of the command, making it forbidden as though the Jew had done it himself. In that case it is certainly difficult: why should the Jew’s denial not help? Therefore one must say that here the Jew is not before us to deny it. So it appears to me.
