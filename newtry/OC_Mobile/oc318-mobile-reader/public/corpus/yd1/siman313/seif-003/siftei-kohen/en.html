"Because this non-Jewish woman servant intended the same." The Bach explains that since the non-Jewish woman servant lives in the household, her role is to serve the master of the house in whatever is for his benefit and good. Therefore, she presumably intended only to permit [the animal], and likewise those who spoke before her intended for this — for otherwise, since they know she would certainly act this way, they should have been careful not to say these things before the non-Jewish servant. Since they were not careful, evidently their entire intention in speaking before the non-Jewish servant was specifically to cause her to inflict a blemish on it.
[And we do not say that he intended. For he certainly did not intend to permit it — on the contrary, he wanted it not to be slaughtered, since he intended to raise it and would receive his wages if it grew up. It is written in the Darkei Moshe that he found in the responsum of the Or Zaru’a regarding a firstborn animal that an Israelite gave to a non-Jewish watchman and informed him that this was a consecrated animal forbidden to be shorn, and that it was its right upon death to be buried whole in its skin. The non-Jews investigated the matter and informed him that with a blemish it would be permissible. The end of the story shows that the non-Jew came deceptively to the owner of the firstborn and said to him: "Know that I castrated the calf." The Jew was silent and the non-Jew understood that this was true. Moreover, the Jew came and saw that it was not castrated and was still silent. And when the non-Jew saw this, he went and castrated it — whereupon that firstborn animal became fully permitted. And so it is implied by the Mishnah in Bekhorot. The Me’adanei Melekh cites this as well.]
