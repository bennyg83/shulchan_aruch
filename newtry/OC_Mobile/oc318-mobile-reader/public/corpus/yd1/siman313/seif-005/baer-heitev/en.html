The Taz writes that this implies even a kohen is permitted in this case, which requires examination — why should he be believed here, since he is suspected of inflicting a blemish himself; all the more so he might tell a non-Jew to inflict a blemish on it. And in the Piskei Maharai this law is written regarding an Israelite who has a doubtful firstborn — and it still requires examination regarding a kohen. The Darkei Moshe writes in the name of the Or Zaru’a regarding a firstborn that an Israelite gave to a non-Jewish watchman and informed him that this was a consecrated animal and that it had a right upon death to be buried whole in its skin. The non-Jews investigated the matter and informed him that with a blemish it would be permissible. The end of the story shows that the non-Jew came deceptively to the owner of the firstborn and said to him: "Know that I castrated the calf." The Jew was silent and the non-Jew understood that this was true. Moreover, the Jew came and saw it was not castrated and was still silent. When the non-Jew saw this, he went and castrated it — whereupon that firstborn animal became fully permitted. And so it is implied in the Mishnah in Bekhorot. End of quote.
