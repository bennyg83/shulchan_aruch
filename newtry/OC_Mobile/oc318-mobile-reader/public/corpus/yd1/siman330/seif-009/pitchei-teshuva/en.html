It is obligated. The reason is appearance. Although here there would seem to be no actual appearance concern, since who can see whether challah was separated at kneading, baking, or afterward, nevertheless we rule that wherever the Sages prohibited something because of appearance, they prohibited it even in private. Still, this requires study. Perhaps, as Baer Heitev writes above in seif 4, seif katan 6, that principle applies only to challah of Eretz Yisrael, which is Torah law, but not to challah outside Eretz Yisrael. Tosafot in Ketubot 60, s.v. "he presses," and Piskei Tosafot, chapter 8 of Avodah Zarah, siman 14, cited above in siman 87, state that the rule prohibiting even in private applies only where the suspicious appearance is that one is violating a Torah prohibition, not where the underlying prohibition itself is rabbinic. I later found that Responsa Pri Tevuah, siman 66, raises this point and writes that we do not rule in practice like those Tosafot. He concludes that challah should be separated without a blessing.
