They come and take, etc. The Gemara in Nedarim 85 explains that the concluding case is forbidden because the right to choose the recipient has monetary value. In the opening case, however, the kohanim come and take the terumah against his will. Since terumah is fit only for kohanim, by attempting to forbid it to them he renders it like mere dust, and they acquire it as ownerless property. The Ran writes there that, for the same reason, when a person forbids his property to himself, others may take it against his will. Maharshal cites this in Perek HaGozel Etzim, siman 61, but concludes that ordinary property is unlike terumah, and others may not take it from him against his will. Rather, he may give it to charity or another mitzvah purpose. Indeed, even if he locked it away, it is removed from his possession. Nevertheless, if others already consumed it, they are exempt from payment because it had no monetary status. If he later obtains annulment of the vow, however, and some of the forbidden item still remains, for otherwise annulment would not apply, they must pay for what they consumed.
The same law applies to gifts designated for the poor. For example, gleanings, forgotten produce, peah, and the poor tithe may be taken from him against his will. Alternatively, if he has already set aside charity, or even if he has not yet set it aside, beit din may send agents, seize collateral from him for the charity obligation, and give it to the poor. This is from the Bach and Beit Yosef. See Tosafos Yom Tov at the beginning of the final chapter of Nedarim.
