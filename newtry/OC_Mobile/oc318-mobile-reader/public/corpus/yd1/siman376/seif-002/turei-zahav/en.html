For it implies that if it were possible to change, etc. In Perek Shor Shenagach Arba’ah VaChamishah this appears, for Ulla did not want to go comfort Rabbi Shmuel bar Yehudah, saying: “What do I have to do with the consolations of the Babylonians, who say, ‘What can you do?’” which implies that if something could be done, they would do it, and this is blasphemy. Maharshal rejected this from halachah, saying: did not David, king of Israel, peace be upon him, also say this, “Can I bring him back again?” which implies that if it were possible to bring him back, he would do so. I am astonished that a holy mouth would say this, for David’s case is different. He said, “Why should I fast? Can I bring him back again?” Certainly a person must do all efforts of prayers and fasts to seek that his dead relative not die. Regarding this David said that once he had already died, the fasts would not help. He then concluded, “I am going to him, but he will not return to me.” At first glance this is not relevant to his answer to his servants there. Rather, this is what he said: perhaps you will say that the fasts will help me merit to see him alive again when this child rises in the resurrection of the dead. To this he said: I know that the resurrection of the dead will not be in my days, and regarding this he said, “I am going to him, etc.” But those consolers who say, “Do not distress yourself, for what can you do?” are not saying this about prayer, but about distress, that he should not distress himself since it is impossible otherwise. This implies that if it were possible otherwise, he would do something, meaning he would distress himself that it should not be according to human will. That is blasphemy, for it is proper that matters be according to the will of God, blessed be He, not according to the will of man. This is clear in my humble opinion.
