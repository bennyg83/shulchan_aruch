In the responsum of Masa Binyamin, siman 73, it was raised that the statements of the customs contradict each other: first it writes that the day a father or mother died — for a resident — pushes aside the thirty-day mourner, all the more so a twelve-month mourner; for a resident that entire fast day, namely the evening, morning, and afternoon prayers, and the Kaddishim of that day recited before the lectern — implying that he holds the yahrzeit observer takes precedence. Then again it writes that a twelve-month mourner and a yahrzeit guest each get one Kaddish, but a thirty-day guest divides all Kaddishim and prayers with him — implying the thirty-day mourner takes precedence. He resolved: the first case speaks of many yahrzeit observers — if they do not say [Kaddish] at all, their time passes and their "sacrifice" is voided. Accordingly, what the customs say — that if the seven-day mourner is a minor or one whose yahrzeit occurred within those seven days, he says orphan's Kaddish once — applies specifically to a yahrzeit observer, on account of the principle that if his time passes his sacrifice is voided; but a thirty-day mourner need not be given even one Kaddish — end quote. But this is not implied by the earlier statement [about many yahrzeit observers]; and furthermore, from the fact that it writes "pushes aside the thirty-day mourner, all the more so a twelve-month mourner" — it implies that in the case discussed of twelve months, thirty-day mourners are also discussed, and in twelve-month cases certainly even a single yahrzeit observer is included. Furthermore, if so, what is the force of "all the more so" — perhaps where there is only one yahrzeit observer he does not even push aside a twelve-month mourner, and the reason thirty-day mourners are pushed aside is because there are many yahrzeit observers! Rather, it seems that the words are to be taken at face value: it speaks of a single yahrzeit observer, and even so he pushes aside the thirty-day mourner — for the customs hold that yahrzeit takes precedence over thirty days. So too it is implied by the Ateret Zekenim I cite below, that the view of the customs is this. And there is no difficulty from what is said at the end — that a guest yahrzeit observer has only one Kaddish and the thirty-day [resident] divides with the resident twelve-month mourner — for one can say: certainly regarding a guest versus a resident thirty-day mourner, the latter takes precedence, because the resident can say: by right I should give you nothing at all — for where both are thirty-day or both yahrzeit or both twelve-month, the guest gets nothing; but since you are thirty-day and I am twelve-month, you divide with me. But a yahrzeit observer whose time is fixed and who knew a full year in advance that you would come into my domain — one Kaddish is sufficient for you; whereas this cannot be said of a thirty-day mourner. Accordingly, by our ruling that a thirty-day mourner takes precedence — if a thirty-day resident mourner happened [to be in mourning] within the seven days of a minor's mourning, the minor too gets one Kaddish — and so is the practice:

This is in accordance with the view of Maharil and the [Shulhan Arukh] author; and so too it was ruled in the responsum of Masa Binyamin, siman 73; and this is the essential ruling. And he writes further there that this applies specifically where there is only one yahrzeit observer and one thirty-day mourner; but where there are many thirty-day mourners and yahrzeit observers and it is impossible to give a Kaddish to each and every one, and some must inevitably be pushed aside — in such a case the yahrzeit observer takes precedence and the thirty-day mourner is pushed aside, since if the yahrzeit observer does not say Kaddish today, tomorrow his time passes and his "sacrifice" is voided — not so the thirty-day mourner — end quote. Non-member, etc. It is written in the customs: if the deceased has children in another city and they have come there, they are not called residents on account of their father who is buried there — only those who live within the congregation. And all the residents of the congregation are called residents: those who pay taxes, servants of the congregation, and even the poor of the city who have a fixed dwelling there — all are counted as residents. And if a person establishes his residence within the congregation, from the time he is obligated to share the burden with the congregation's residents he is considered a resident — but not from the time he completed fixing the tax with the secular authority, even though he must pay the tax to the authority. Conversely, one who uproots his residence from the congregation — from the time he is no longer obligated to share the burden with the congregation's residents he is considered like a guest, even though he is still obligated to pay the tax to the authority. And if the householder has a teacher or attendant hired for a fixed period and he is single with no wife and children, then he too is called a resident; but if he has a wife and children elsewhere, his status is like a non-member. And if the householder raises an orphan in his home — even for pay — and [the orphan] has no father or mother elsewhere, he is called a resident; but if he has a father or mother, he is not called a resident, even if he is raised as charity, and certainly if he is there for pay — end quote.
