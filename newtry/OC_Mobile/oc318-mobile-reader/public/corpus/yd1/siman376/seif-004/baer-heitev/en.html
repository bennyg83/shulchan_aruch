They take precedence. It is written in the customs that even the seven-day mourner — though he is a non-member — pushes aside the resident, even a thirty-day mourner or a yahrzeit observer; and all this applies only on the Shabbat that falls within the seven days, but on a weekday he does not go to the synagogue. But if the seven-day mourner is a minor who is not yet of age for commandments and goes to the synagogue all seven days to say Kaddish, or one who experienced a parent's death during those seven days — he says orphan's Kaddish once — end quote. And it appears that one whose mourning was annulled by a festival — such as one hour before the festival, whereby the decree of seven or thirty was annulled for him — nonetheless has the law of seven and thirty even on a weekday just as on Shabbat, since legally he goes to the synagogue. In the responsum of Masa Binyamin it was raised that the statements of the customs contradict each other: first it writes that the day a father or mother died — for a resident — pushes aside the thirty-day mourner, all the more so a twelve-month mourner; for a resident that entire fast day, that is, the evening, morning, and afternoon prayers, as well as the Kaddishim of that day said before the lectern — implying he holds that the yahrzeit observer takes precedence. Then it writes that a twelve-month mourner and a yahrzeit guest each get one Kaddish, but a thirty-day guest divides all the Kaddishim and prayers with him — implying that the thirty-day mourner takes precedence. He resolved this: the first case speaks of where there are many yahrzeit observers and the Kaddishim are in short supply and not every person can receive a Kaddish — then the thirty-day mourner is pushed aside in favor of the yahrzeit observer, because if the yahrzeit observer does not say Kaddish on that day, the day passes and his "sacrifice" is voided. Not so the thirty-day mourner, who can say it tomorrow. But where there is ample room for Kaddishim, such as on Shabbat, then the thirty-day mourner takes precedence and says two Kaddishim while the yahrzeit observer says one — end quote. The Tur agreed with him and wrote that the distinction is correct. But the Siftei Kohen challenged him and wrote that the words are to be taken at face value: the customs hold that the yahrzeit observer takes precedence over a thirty-day mourner. And there is no difficulty from what is said at the end, that a guest yahrzeit observer gets only one Kaddish while a thirty-day mourner divides with a resident twelve-month mourner — for one can say that certainly regarding a guest versus a resident thirty-day mourner, the latter takes precedence, because the resident can tell the yahrzeit guest: since your time is fixed and known a full year in advance and you have come into my domain, one Kaddish is sufficient for you — but this cannot be said of a thirty-day mourner. Accordingly, by our ruling that a thirty-day mourner takes precedence, if a thirty-day resident mourner happened [to be in mourning] within the seven days [of a minor's mourning], the minor too gets one Kaddish — and so is the practice — end quote:
And the custom. There are congregations where it is customary for the resident to push aside the guest; there are places where they are equal; and there are places where it is customary for the resident to push aside the non-member. Nevertheless, the first time [the guest mourns] he has permission to pray and say orphan's Kaddish once — so it is written in the customs. And it seems to me that the rule that the guest has one Kaddish means that he is like a resident twelve-month mourner; accordingly, where the resident does not get even one Kaddish — such as when a thirty-day mourner is present and the like — the guest too gets nothing. Siftei Kohen. And it is written further in the customs: if the deceased has children in another city and they have come there, they are not called residents on account of their father who is buried there — only those who live within the congregation. And all the residents of the congregation are called residents: those who pay taxes, servants of the congregation, and even the poor of the city who have a fixed dwelling there — all are counted as residents. And if a person establishes his residence within the congregation, then from the time he is obligated to share the burden with the congregation's residents he is considered a resident — but not from the time he completed fixing the tax with the secular authority, even though he must pay the tax to the authority. Conversely, one who uproots his residence from the congregation — from the time he is no longer obligated to share the burden with the congregation's residents he is considered like a guest, even though he is still obligated to pay the tax to the authority. And if the householder has a teacher or attendant hired for a fixed period and he is single, with no wife and children, then he too is called a resident; but if he has a wife and children elsewhere, his status is like a non-member. And if the householder raises an orphan in his home — even for pay — and [the orphan] has no father or mother elsewhere, he is called a resident; but if he has a father or mother, he is not called a resident, even if he is being raised as charity, and certainly if he is there for pay — end quote. And it seems that young men and students studying in a yeshivah — even if they have a father or mother elsewhere — have the status of a resident; and even a householder who studies in a yeshivah, even if he has a wife and children elsewhere and pays taxes where his wife and children are — nonetheless has the status of a resident where he studies, and so is the practice.
