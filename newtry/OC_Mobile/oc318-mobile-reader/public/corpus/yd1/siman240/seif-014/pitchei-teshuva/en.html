He leaves his mother. If both parents ask him to perform a task that a wife is not obligated to perform for her husband, they may be equal. The classic case uses bringing water because a wife always owes that service even if she brought many maidservants. Pitchei Teshuva leaves the distinction uncertain.
And attends to his father's honor. Rabbi Akiva Eiger discusses conflicting parental wills where the father's request entails a severe transgression. He rejects the claim that the son's lack of obligation to obey automatically gives priority to the mother. The underlying mitzvah of honoring the father still exists, though it does not override the prohibition. He also discusses testamentary instructions to give inherited property to a third party.
If she is divorced from his father, they are equal. Noda BiYehudah holds that where the father is deceased and the widowed mother gives a contrary instruction, the living parent takes precedence. Rabbi Akiva Eiger questions this from the law of saying Kaddish for a deceased mother when the living father objects, which suggests that living status alone does not always control.
Whichever he wishes he may give precedence. Maharshal rules that he should not prefer either parent. For redemption from captivity, however, the mother comes first. Pitchei Teshuva extends this to requests for food and clothing, where a woman precedes a man.
