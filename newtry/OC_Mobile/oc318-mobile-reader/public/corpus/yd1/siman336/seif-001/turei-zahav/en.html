The Torah gave permission, and it is a mitzvah, and so forth. One may ask: if healing is truly a mitzvah, why is it first described merely as permission? The matter appears as follows. The ideal and ultimate healing comes through mercy from Heaven, as Hashem says, "I have wounded and I will heal." A person, however, is generally not worthy to rely on miraculous healing and must pursue treatment through the natural order. Hashem consented to this and allowed healing to operate through natural medical means. That is the "permission" granted to the physician. Once the human condition is such that life depends on natural treatment, the physician is obligated to heal, and it becomes a mitzvah. Berachot teaches that one who enters for bloodletting should pray that the procedure be healing, because true healing is from Hashem. Abaye says one should not imply that human healing is improper, because the school of R. Yishmael derives from "he shall surely heal" that permission was given to the physician to heal. This does not mean medicine is the highest ideal for a person worthy of direct heavenly healing. Rather, the Torah recognized ordinary human spiritual standing and authorized natural treatment. Therefore, in our actual human condition, the physician has a duty to practice medicine. Ramban in Parashat Bechukotai discusses this at length. He also says that medical treatment was not the way of the houses of the perfectly righteous. The statement that Rabbah did not call a physician to his home for twenty-two years may mean, as one gaonic explanation says, that his humility and merit protected him so that he did not need a physician at all.
