For it forbids its mixture up to one hundred and one. At first glance it appears that this means if it is present but not recognizable. But if they removed the challah from the dish into which it fell, although the dish is of the same type as the challah, one hundred and one is not needed, and it is nullified in sixty like other prohibitions. But in Tosafot, Perek Gid HaNasheh 99, s.v. ein bahem, it is explained that according to Rashi, with the same type, even if the prohibition was removed, one hundred and one is required to nullify it. Although from what they wrote afterward, that one may explain another way, this law is not necessary, nevertheless, since according to Rashi it is necessary, we should not be lenient. Furthermore, Rabbeinu Shimshon in tractate Orlah, p. 81, also wrote like Rashi. It is written in the Darkhei Moshe in the name of the Rashba that after the fact it is nullified in the majority.
He should ask a sage, etc. So Teshuvot Maimoniyyot, Hilkhot Zeraim, brings in the name of Sefer HaMitzvot, that through she’eilah the sanctity of the challah is uprooted retroactively. So too it is stated below in Shulchan Aruch siman 331 seif 48, that one may ask release regarding terumot and maasrot if he regrets them, etc. This is astonishing from the Mishnah in chapter 5 of Terumot: a se’ah of tamei terumah that fell into less than one hundred of chullin must rot, implying that it has no remedy. If so, the remedy should have been taught, as the Mishnah afterward taught many remedies where there is some remedy; since here it did not teach a remedy, this shows there is no remedy. So too it is explained in the Rambam regarding this, for he wrote that he leaves them until they rot. Likewise in Perek HaNoder Min HaYerek, which we will mention nearby, Rashi writes there: “They must rot, and they have no remedy.” Why did he not say that he should ask release for the terumah he made, so that afterward it would be permitted to a Jew and he would separate another terumah? One cannot say it speaks in a way where she’eilah is not relevant, for that is not so. In Perek HaNoder Min HaYerek, Nedarim 59, the Gemara brings this Mishnah and infers from it: if it fell into one hundred, it rises and is nullified; but it is a davar she-yesh lo matirin, since she’eilah is possible, as stated there just before regarding a vow. It answers: a vow is different, because it is a mitzvah to ask release from it, based on Rabbi Natan, who said that anyone who maintains it is as though he built a private altar; this is unlike terumah, where there is no mitzvah to ask release from it, and therefore it is not a davar she-yesh lo matirin. In any event, she’eilah is relevant to it, only it is not a mitzvah. If so, the question returns to its place: why should it be left to rot when it fell into less than one hundred? Why do we not say that he should go to a sage and have it permitted? Why should it not be like challah here? I am further astonished by the conclusion in Perek HaNoder that terumah is not a davar she-yesh lo matirin because there is no mitzvah to ask release from it. How is this different from every davar she-yesh lo matirin, where we rule in siman 102 that if the permissive act is in his power without loss, it is a davar she-yesh lo matirin? Here too with terumah, in any event it is in his power to go and ask release from a Torah scholar, even though it is not a mitzvah. A further great astonishment: if she’eilah is effective because of the loss of a mixture of challah or terumah, where do we ever find karet for one who slaughters kodashim outside, or death by Heaven for terumah? Certainly out of fear of punishment he would ask a sage. We will bring a question like this nearby in the name of the Raavad and in the name of Tosafot in Gittin. Were it not for the holy mouth of the Semag, I would say that she’eilah is effective for terumot and maasrot only where he regrets the giving itself, retracting from it because of some reason he has even without the mixture. But not because of the mixture, for every opening and regret must uproot the essence of the vow, such that if the vow remains in force, some harm will necessarily follow from it. This is unlike here, where certainly he could vow and make this piece terumah or challah and guard it from becoming mixed; it is not necessary that if this piece is challah it will become mixed. Therefore this is not an opening or regret because of the mixture, for the essential she’eilah must be on the body of the matter, which he regrets because of itself. So the Ran explicitly explained in Nedarim there, that she’eilah regarding terumot is by means of an opening and regret like other vows. So too the Rashbam explained in Perek Yesh Nochalin, Bava Batra 120b: “There is she’eilah for vows, and the same applies to all hekdeshot, so that if one slaughtered kodashim outside and became liable to karet, and afterward went to a sage to permit his consecration, and the sage found him an opening of regret and uprooted the consecration from its root, treating it as mistaken consecration, it is revealed retroactively that it was never consecrated, and he is exempt from karet. The same applies to anything made sacred by the utterance of his lips, such as terumot and maasrot, for if he wishes he can ask release regarding them and they return to being tevel, as stated in Perek HaNoder Min HaYerek”; end quote. Thus the master taught us that the permission here is not because the mixture causes him to ask about what he initially made terumah, but specifically through an opening of regret regarding the body of the matter, just as with one who slaughters kodashim outside, who cannot uproot the kodashim because he slaughtered them outside but only because of the body of the matter. Regarding this, the Sages indeed said that one may ask release from vows and hekdeshot. This is unlike where he has no regret about the body of the matter initially, but the mixture causes him to regret; that is not effective at all. Therefore in that Nedarim passage, when it says that regarding something forbidden by a vow of konam it is not nullified in a majority because it is a davar she-yesh lo matirin through she’eilah, this means if he finds an opening of regret regarding the essence of the matter. The Gemara asks on this: if so, with one hundred it should not rise and become nullified, since it is a davar she-yesh lo matirin. It does not ask: why should it rot with less than one hundred, rather than saying he should ask release? For how can we tell him to ask release when he has no opening of regret regarding the body of the matter? Therefore it must rot. Rather, the essential question is: just as you say regarding a vow, since there is a possibility if he has an opening of regret regarding the essence of the matter, it is called a davar she-yesh lo matirin, so too let us say regarding terumah that it should not be nullified in one hundred. It answers that regarding a vow, it is indeed a davar she-yesh lo matirin, since presumably he would not maintain the vow without the mixture, for it is a mitzvah to ask release from it; therefore it is certain, and although he has not asked, it is as though he asked, and we are not lenient with its mixture. This is unlike terumah: without the mixture it does not stand to be asked about, and therefore the mixture helps it be nullified, since it has no other permission and is not called a davar she-yesh lo matirin. But certainly, if he came to us with an opening of regret regarding the essence of the matter, it would help him in all she’eilot, even regarding terumah by itself; only it is not relevant to instruct him to ask about the terumah when the regret is only because of the mixture. Therefore the Mishnah said it must rot. This matter is exactly the same with challah here, for certainly it is not relevant to instruct him to ask because of the mixture. Another proof is from what Rabbeinu Tam wrote regarding Kol Nidrei, brought by the Beit Yosef in Orach Chaim siman 619: “Furthermore, the sage does not permit without regret”; end quote. This refers to those vows that he already transgressed, where Kol Nidrei does not help. Why do we not say that the fact he transgressed them is itself the release? Rather, regret is needed regarding the essence of the vow itself. Even according to the Rosh, who disagrees there with Rabbeinu Tam and wrote, “What he challenged, that we require regret: we are witnesses that anyone who violates his vow regrets it from the outset in order to escape punishment”; end quote, this is not comparable to here. There, when he sees that he is transgressing and will have punishment, he truly regrets having vowed at all, for the vow causes him punishment, and immediately from the time of the vow fear of punishment rests upon him, since he is of the sort who does not keep his vow. Therefore he regrets the essence of the vow. This is unlike separating terumah and challah, which does not stand to be mixed into chullin, only that it happened to him incidentally; thus there is no uprooting of the vow from its root. I have written this according to the view of the Rosh. But in truth, even the Rosh’s words are astonishing, for he dismissed the words of Rabbeinu Tam, pillar of the world, with a dismissal that appears obvious even to common people, but in truth is not. According to this, you would never find lashes for a nazir or death for one who eats terumah, for they would go to a sage to permit them out of fear of punishment. So too it is proven from the words of Tosafot, Perek HaSholeach 91, s.v. ve-afke’inhu, where they say that we establish it on its presumption, for otherwise, why is a nazir who drinks wine liable? Why does he receive lashes if they say to him, “Do not drink”? It is an uncertain warning, since perhaps he will ask release from his nezirut; end quote. If you would think that she’eilah helps because of fear of punishment, why call it uncertain warning? He will certainly ask release from his nezirut; where then do we find a nazir receiving lashes? In Chiddushei Halakhot, Maharsha asked this and answered that certainly if they know that he intentionally transgressed his nezirut, the sage will not entertain his request so that he not receive lashes; but an uncertain warning still exists, for perhaps this nazir will come to a sage who does not know about his nezirut and ask release; end quote. His words were not precise. If the law requires entertaining this nazir who transgressed, why indeed should the sage not permit him, since he has a permission? And if there is no permission for one who transgressed intentionally, then if he does not reveal to the sage that he transgressed intentionally and the sage permits him, the permission is no permission at all. How can you call this an uncertain warning? It is impossible for him to be exempted from liability even after she’eilah, since it was mistaken. This is similar to what they said in Perek HaSholeach, that one must specify the vow because of a matter of prohibition; whenever he does not specify the vow, the permission is no permission at all. So too exactly here. Indeed there is an a fortiori: the requirement to specify the vow is rabbinic, while here, regarding one who transgressed intentionally, there is no Torah-level permission. Rather, it is clear that because of fear of punishment alone he has no permission at all to ask about his nezirut, but only if he truly has an opening and regret from the essence of the vow itself. Regarding this Tosafot properly said that it is an uncertain warning, perhaps he will find an opening and regret. Whenever he says from the essence of the matter that he regrets the original vow even if he had not transgressed it, and gives a reason for his retraction, he certainly does not receive lashes, as we find regarding outside slaughter of kodashim that we mentioned. This is clear. The words of the Semag here require great study to resolve, and although I am unworthy to disagree with him, nevertheless my soul has stated its portion: one should not rely on this ruling and be lenient because of it. Perhaps the Semag himself intended only where he has regret regarding the essence of the challah, and it was copied in this way in his name.
My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that one must be careful when challah falls into a hot dish and the pot does not contain enough to nullify the challah in the dish: it should be sold or given to a kohen where there is a majority of chullin, and if the vessel is earthenware it requires breaking, for hagalah is impossible; end quote. It has already been explained that we do not give to a kohen nowadays. As for his stringency here, I do not know why we should not rely leniently in a rabbinic matter to nullify in the majority, as I mentioned above in the name of the Darkhei Moshe.
