Its mixture is prohibited until one hundred and one. At first glance this means when the challah itself remains in the mixture but cannot be identified. If the challah was removed from the cooked food, perhaps ordinary sixty would suffice even when the food is of the same type. Tosafot in Chullin 99, however, imply according to Rashi that even after the actual forbidden item has been removed, a same-type mixture involving terumah-like prohibitions still requires one hundred and one. Although Tosafot later offer another approach, Rashi's view remains substantial and R. Shimshon in Orlah also writes similarly. Darkei Moshe cites Rashba that after the fact it can be nullified in a majority.

Similarly, below in siman 331, seif 48, one may annul terumah and ma'aser where one regrets the designation. This is difficult from the Mishnah in Terumot: if one se'ah of impure terumah fell into fewer than one hundred se'ah of chullin, the mixture must rot. The Mishnah gives no remedy through annulment, even though it lists other possible remedies. Rambam likewise rules it must be left until it rots. Nedarim 59 explicitly cites that Mishnah and asks why terumah should be nullified in one hundred if it is a davar sheyesh lo matirin through annulment. The Gemara answers that vows are different because there is a mitzvah to seek annulment, following R. Natan, while with terumah there is no mitzvah to do so. This shows annulment exists for terumah, yet the Mishnah still says the mixture must rot rather than advising annulment. Furthermore, if annulment is always available without loss, why is terumah not a davar sheyesh lo matirin just like any item whose remedy lies in one's hands? Even more difficult, if the financial loss caused by a mixture itself creates sufficient grounds for annulment, then there should never be karet for slaughtering sacred offerings outside or death at the hands of Heaven for eating terumah, because the offender could simply seek annulment after the act to escape punishment. Were it not for the authority of Semag, I would say that annulment of terumah and challah works only where the person independently regrets the original designation itself, not merely because an accidental mixture later makes that designation inconvenient. A valid petach and regret must undermine the original act. Ran in Nedarim says expressly that annulment of terumah operates through ordinary petach and charatah. Rashbam in Bava Batra likewise explains that if one slaughtered consecrated property outside and later found a genuine basis to regret the consecration itself, the consecration is retroactively uprooted and he is exempt. The same applies to terumah and ma'aser. Thus the mixture itself cannot manufacture the regret. This explains the Nedarim passage. A konam is considered a davar sheyesh lo matirin because there is an affirmative reason to seek annulment of the vow whenever a genuine opening exists. Terumah, however, is not normally designated for annulment and there is no mitzvah to seek one. Therefore its mixture can be nullified. If the owner independently had a true opening to regret the original terumah, annulment would certainly work, but we do not tell him to create regret merely because it became mixed. Rabbenu Tam's comments concerning Kol Nidrei support this, for he says a sage does not annul without genuine regret. Even Rosh, who says one who violated his vow is presumed to regret it because of punishment, is not comparable. There the vow itself directly generated the danger of punishment from the moment it was made in a person likely to violate it, so fear of punishment can reveal regret of the vow itself. Terumah and challah are not designated with any expectation of later mixture; the accident does not uproot the original designation. Indeed, Rosh's formulation itself is difficult, because if fear of punishment automatically creates annulment, how could a nazir ever receive lashes or one who eats terumah incur heavenly death? Tosafot in Gittin raise exactly such a problem regarding a nazir, describing the warning as doubtful because perhaps he will later annul his nezirus. Maharsha answers that a sage would refuse to annul if he knew the nazir intentionally violated it. But if the law truly allowed annulment because of fear of punishment, why should the sage refuse? And if intentional violation invalidates the annulment, hiding that fact from the sage would make the annulment itself defective. The correct conclusion is that fear of punishment alone does not create a valid basis to annul. Only a genuine opening and regret concerning the original vow does. Therefore Semag's ruling here requires substantial study, and although I am unworthy to dispute him, one should not rely upon it leniently unless there is genuine regret concerning the original challah designation itself.
Bach wrote that if challah fell into a hot cooked dish and there is not enough to nullify it, one should sell or give it to a kohen where chullin is the majority, and an earthenware pot would require breaking because it cannot be kashered. As explained, nowadays we do not ordinarily give challah to a kohen. I also do not know why he was so stringent regarding the mixture. Since the prohibition is rabbinic, we should rely on majority nullification, as I cited above from Darkei Moshe.
