If one vowed by the Table in the Temple, etc. With due respect, the Rama did not correctly understand the Mordechai from which this gloss was taken. Maharam was asked about one who swore by the sacred table, by one of the sacred vessels, or by a table without qualification. He answered that if one says, 'This loaf is forbidden to me like the Table of the Temple or like one of the Temple vessels,' this is a complete vow because he associates the object with something itself consecrated. But one who says merely, 'I vow by the table,' has said nothing, just as one who vows 'by the Torah' while it lies on the ground has said nothing; only when he holds it and his intent is to the Divine Names written in it does the oath take effect. A table contains no Divine Names. The Rama understood the Mordechai's distinction as between the sacred Table and an ordinary table, but that is not correct. An unqualified 'table' is no weaker than an unqualified lamb or sheepfold in the Mishnah, because ordinary versions of those things are also common and nevertheless unspecified vows are interpreted stringently. The true distinction is between language of hatfasah, 'this loaf shall be like the sacred Table,' which is a vow whether he says sacred Table or an unqualified table understood in that context, and language of oath, 'I swear or vow by the table,' which is ineffective whether the table is sacred or ordinary. Even swearing by a Torah scroll lying on the ground is ineffective because the sanctified parchment is not itself a Divine Name; all the more so a table. Thus there is no substantive distinction between the sacred Table and an ordinary table: in both cases hatfasah can create a vow, while saying 'I swear by the table' does not create an oath.
