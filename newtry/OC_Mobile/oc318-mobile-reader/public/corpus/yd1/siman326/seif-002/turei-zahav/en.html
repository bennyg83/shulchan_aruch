He makes a dough to divide it as dough. Meaning, he intends to give each person less than the minimum measure for challah. But if he does not intend to divide it as dough — even though he intends to divide it after baking — it is obligated. So wrote the Beit Yosef, and this disagrees with the Semag.
And it is written in the responsum of R. Binyamin z"l, §1: the custom in some places is that an Israelite baker makes a large dough and women come and each takes from him a measure of challah, and each takes challah from the dough she took from the baker — and they act correctly in doing so. For the matter depends on the person's intent, just as we say here leniently — that if he intends to divide it as dough it is exempt — so too we follow his intent stringently, and it is as if each piece of dough was kneaded by itself, and each one is obligated to take challah from her own measure. The Bach disputed this, see there; he wrote that he received from his teachers that it is an outright prohibition. I, the younger one, say: if it is a received tradition, we accept it; but if it is a legal argument, there is a response — for I can justify the custom from a different angle. This is based on the Gemara in chapter Kol HaGet (Gittin 30a): one who lends money to a kohen, [intending] to separate [tithes] on his behalf from his portion — he separates on his behalf on the presumption that the kohen is still alive, and is not concerned that perhaps the kohen has died. The explanation: when he separates his tithes and sells the terumah and retains the money for himself as repayment of the debt — this is on behalf of the kohen. And the Gemara asks: but even though it does not come to his hand [the kohen does not physically receive the terumah], the Mishnah still says he separates on his behalf — and since it does not reach his hand, who acquires this terumah for the kohen that this person should receive it as his debt, and how has he discharged the obligation of giving? It answers: we are dealing with a kohen who is his regular recipient (mukarei kehunah) — he is not accustomed to giving terumah to any other kohen. Therefore, since it is obvious that he will give it to him, the other kohanim relinquished their claim to it and it is as if it had reached his hand — and this is effective. There are further resolutions in the Gemara that resolve it differently; in any case they do not dispute this resolution on legal grounds — they only find it difficult because the Mishnah did not state "mukarei kehunah," implying they agree legally that for those who are his regulars it is as if it came to their hand. Similarly with this baker who has regulars who regularly buy from him every Shabbat eve — they buy on the condition that they will separate challah, and it is as if they bought from him before he made the dough and it came to their hand at the time of obligation, which is the kneading. And it is obvious that two people who make a dough to divide it — each getting the challah measure — so that neither of them can separate challah on behalf of the other's portion if he is particular about this and will not act as his agent against his will — so too here: the baker's intent is certainly to act in an effective manner, and it is as if the baker granted each of them their portion at the outset before kneading the dough — since they are his regulars and will certainly take from him — he grants them their portion at the time of kneading, and he cannot take challah from the dough as it would be as if it is not his. At the time of division each takes her portion, separates, and recites a blessing. This is how it seems to me to justify the custom — if it is done in the manner I described, where women regularly take from this baker every Shabbat eve. But not in any other manner. This is my humble opinion. With a non-Jewish baker it is obviously not applicable at all, since he only grants [ownership] after the division. And at a wedding where women make one large bread and many women take from that dough a measure and each separately takes challah and then they reconstitute the dough and bake it as one loaf — this is obviously forbidden, and so too the rabbi wrote in that responsum.
