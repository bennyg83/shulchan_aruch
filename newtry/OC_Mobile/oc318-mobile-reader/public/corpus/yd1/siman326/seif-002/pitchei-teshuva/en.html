If one intends to divide the dough, it is exempt. Levush, Derishah, and Bach write that this applies only when one intends to distribute it among many people. Dividing it for oneself is not considered making it in order to divide. They write this to justify the custom of women on Erev Shabbos to knead only the amount requiring challah, separate challah with a blessing, and then divide the dough into small loaves called kitkes, each of which lacks the requisite measure, because it is not being divided among many people. Responsa Beis Ephraim, Yoreh Deah, siman 69, writes that Tosafos in Berachos 38 and other authorities clearly reject this distinction. Tiferes LeMoshe also notes this. Maadanei Melech, Laws of Challah, proposes another distinction: exemption applies only where one divides the dough so that it will not all be baked now, but rather for separate meals, such as morning and evening. If one divides a batch of dough in order to bake all of it immediately, it is not exempt. Tiferes LeMoshe likewise rejects this distinction, since it is not implied by the Tosafos cited above. Beis Ephraim justifies the custom differently: the dough is exempt only when one intends to divide it in a manner that will never later result in combination, so that the division is enduring. In this respect there is no difference between one person and many. But if one intends to combine the loaves during baking by removing them and placing them together in one basket, so that the division of the dough will not endure, it is not considered dough made in order to divide.
