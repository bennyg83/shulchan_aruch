To divide it. Meaning, to give each person less than the minimum measure for challah. A responsum of the Maharam [R. Meir of Rothenburg] writes that the custom in some places is that an Israelite baker makes a large dough and women come and each takes from him the measure of challah, and each takes challah from the dough she took from the baker — and they act correctly in doing so. The Bach disputes this and writes that he received from his teachers that it is an outright prohibition — since when the baker kneaded and separated challah, he already exempted the entire dough; how then can each one subsequently recite a blessing on her own? The Taz labored to justify the custom — but only if the women are accustomed to buy from this baker every Shabbat eve, and not in any other manner. (See there, where he wrote to justify it: this baker has regulars who are accustomed to buy on the condition that they will separate challah, and it is as if they bought from him before he made the dough and it came to their hand at the time of obligation — namely the kneading — and it is impossible for the baker to take challah since it is as if it is not his.) He further wrote in that responsum of the Maharam: however, the custom at weddings — where a large bread is made which is placed on the table at the time of the sermon, and from it many women each separately take challah — even though each woman takes a measure of dough, nonetheless since they all kneaded it together at once, only one challah should be separated; therefore one should protest against the women who take more than one challah from that bread. The Shakh wrote that these are obvious matters, and so too I observed from my eminent mother, the Rabbanit, and from other righteous and wise women who were scrupulous about this. End quote. With a non-Jewish baker it is obvious that these laws do not apply at all, since he only grants [ownership] after the division. Taz. See above, beginning of siman 330.
