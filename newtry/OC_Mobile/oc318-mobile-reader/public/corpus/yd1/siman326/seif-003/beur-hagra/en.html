And specifically when he mixed them, etc. This is a great error. The Semag did at first write this according to Reish Lakish, but afterward he wrote that according to Rabbi Yochanan there is no distinction whether it was done knowingly or unknowingly, so long as it is ultimately destined to be divided. These are the words of Sefer HaTerumah: women who gave flour to a baker, etc. But it is difficult from the case of one who makes dough together with a non-Jew, for there no individual share contains the required measure. The Ri answered that this applies where they mixed without their knowledge, or where they intend to divide it at the end. And so it is in the Yerushalmi: one who smooths his fellow's stack without his knowledge, Rabbi Yochanan obligates and Reish Lakish exempts. Reish Lakish challenges Rabbi Yochanan from women who gave flour to a baker, and Rabbi Yochanan answers that dough made in order to be divided is exempt. The case of a baker is different, for perhaps he will not find customers and he will make it into one dough. Thus if two people, or two students, mix their dough knowingly and intend to eat it together, it is obligated according to all; and even if it was not knowingly, according to Rabbi Yochanan it is obligated. The Rama's ruling is therefore difficult, for it rules like Reish Lakish. The Raavad also rules like Reish Lakish, but only where one person is involved; most of the early and later authorities disagree. And there is a question from seif 2: if one person makes dough intending to divide it into small doughs in his house, should it be exempt? That cannot be, for then the loaves of a thanksgiving offering, cooked wafers, and the like would be exempt, and the ancestral custom has always been to separate challah on Shabbat and Yom Tov. Rather, when the Yerushalmi says "to divide," it means to divide among many people. So the Shach wrote. We therefore distinguish between one woman and two women: one woman is obligated even if she intends to divide, but two women are obligated only if they do not intend to divide. And "intends to divide" means while it is still dough, not after baking. Although Sefer HaTerumah and the Semag phrase it as "they intend to eat it together," that wording is not precise; the Rashba, Beit Yosef, and Shulchan Aruch all write specifically "in dough."
