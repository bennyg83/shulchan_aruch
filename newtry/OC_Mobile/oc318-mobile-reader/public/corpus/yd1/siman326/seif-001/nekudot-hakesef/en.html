(Siman 326, Taz seif katan 1) He objected. Taz objects to Ateret Zahav. In my opinion, Ateret Zahav's words are correct, true, and clear, and Terumat HaDeshen also holds this way, as I will explain. Several significant laws emerge from the discussion, so I must elaborate somewhat. Taz wished to say that when a person is particular that two doughs not mix, such as where one contains saffron, actual adhesion is required for purposes of separating challah from that which is mukaf. This is imprecise. Even regarding a pure dough and an impure dough, the Mishnah at the end of the first chapter of Challah says that one places less than an egg's bulk between them. This implies that mere contact suffices and actual adhesion is unnecessary. Sotah 30 likewise explicitly treats that Mishnah as a case of contact, and Tosafot in Pesachim 46b, Sefer HaTerumah, the beginning of chapter 2 of Challah, Terumat HaDeshen, Rosh in Hilchot Challah, and other poskim all state explicitly that contact alone works for a pure and impure dough and that actual adhesion is not required. Perhaps Taz's requirement of adhesion refers to doughs not contained in one basket, while the Mishnah and poskim who permit contact alone discuss doughs in a basket. But the plain meaning of his words is that the Mishnah itself concerns adhesion and that even contact inside a basket would not help, which is incorrect. Furthermore, it appears that in the present case neither contact nor basket combination is required. If being particular about mixing prevented basket combination even merely for the requirement of mukaf when each dough independently contains the challah measure, there would be a difficulty with Tur and Shulchan Aruch at the end of siman 325. They rule without qualification that when each dough contains the required amount, one needs no basket combination at all, only that both be before him. They do not distinguish between a person who is particular about their mixing and one who is not, although immediately afterward in siman 326 they do make this distinction for purposes of combining smaller doughs into one obligation. They should have said that the earlier rule applies only when he is not particular, while where he is particular even basket combination is ineffective. The same difficulty applies even more strongly to Rama here in siman 326, who writes without qualification that when one dough contains the required amount one may separate from it for the other, without requiring contact or adhesion. All the later authorities quote him without adding such a condition, and no one understood him as requiring it. Rabbenu Yerucham likewise rules in the name of Ri bar Yehudah and Riva that for mukaf one does not even need a basket, and he does not distinguish whether the owner is particular. Later, when discussing actual combination, he explicitly makes that distinction. Rambam also distinguishes particularity only regarding combination. It is therefore clear that all these authorities hold that particularity makes no difference for mukaf. There is good reason for this. For purposes of combining two sub-minimum doughs, the basis of liability is that we legally view them as one combined dough. If the owner is particular that they remain separate, that conceptual combination fails. For mukaf, however, where each dough is already obligated and one merely separates from one for the other, they need not be combined into one dough. This is explicit in Sefer Yereim, cited by Mordechai in Perek Elu Overin, and is also the view of Tosafot in Niddah 7a, Sefer HaTerumah, R. Shimshon in his commentary to Challah according to his first answer, and Agudah in the name of some Geonim. Thus all these authorities distinguish between mukaf, where particularity does not matter, and combination, where everyone agrees that it does matter, as stated in the Mishnah at the beginning of chapter 4 of Challah, the Yerushalmi, Rash, and all the poskim. It further appears that even authorities who distinguish particularity for mukaf do so only in the exceptional case of purity and impurity, where the owner is extremely particular because even contact itself would cause the pure dough to become impure. They needed this special distinction only to resolve the Mishnah of the pure and impure doughs. It may therefore apply only there, not to ordinary forms of particularity. I believe Rosh also holds this. He writes that a pure and impure dough are different, citing the Yerushalmi that a vessel does not combine things whose mixture a person is particular to prevent, and that a pure and impure dough are treated as things one is particular not to mix. There the owner is especially particular because the instant they touch, even without actual intermingling, impurity transfers. Only for that reason might contact itself be required. Sefer HaTerumah's second answer likewise says that because he is particular about their touching due to impurity, basket combination does not suffice. The Yerushalmi's wording supports this. This is why Rosh says specifically, "A pure and impure dough are different," rather than merely saying, "There the owner is particular." According to this, Tur, Shulchan Aruch, Rabbenu Yerucham, and the later authorities can all follow Rosh while still omitting the distinction in our ordinary practice, because the rules of purity and impurity do not presently apply. R. Peretz in Hagahot Semak writes regarding mukaf that the owner must not be particular, without expressly mentioning impurity, but I believe he means a case comparable to pure and impure dough, where the owner objects even to contact. Authorities who prove their approach from the case of pure and impure dough hold that even there, despite particularity about contact, the ordinary rules of mukaf remain. It also appears to me clear that Terumat HaDeshen, siman 189, understands the matter this way. His question is whether a woman who kneaded two doughs, putting saffron into one but not the other, where neither separately contains the challah measure, may combine them through adhesion to create a challah obligation. He initially writes that from several Geonim it appears they can be combined through adhesion. The gloss to Tosafot in Niddah, Semag, Sefer HaTerumah according to its second answer, Rosh in Hilchot Challah, and Mordechai in Perek Elu Overin explain that basket combination does not help a pure and impure dough for mukaf, and actual contact is required because the owner is particular about their mixture. From this explanation, Terumat HaDeshen initially infers that if contact, and certainly adhesion, helps even in the worse case of purity and impurity where the owner objects even to contact, then it should certainly help in the saffron case. At that stage he assumed that the rules of mukaf and combination were equivalent. He then writes that Hagahot Ashri and Yoreh Deah expressly say that where one is particular about mixture, neither contact nor adhesion combines the doughs, and that Rosh and R. Shimshon likewise imply this. He therefore distinguishes: where each dough independently contains the challah measure, contact or adhesion can establish mukaf even though the owner is particular, and that is the case of the pure and impure doughs. But where neither dough contains the measure and one seeks to combine them to create the obligation itself, contact or adhesion does not help if the owner is particular. Thus Terumat HaDeshen's purpose is precisely to establish a distinction between mukaf and combination. For combination, even ordinary particularity such as saffron prevents contact and adhesion from creating one liability. Otherwise, Rosh and Tur's explicit ruling that even adhesion does not combine coarse and fine bread where one is particular would conflict by a kal vachomer with the rule that contact works for pure and impure doughs, where the objection extends even to contact. The answer must be that contact works for mukaf because mukaf is a more lenient concept than combining sub-minimum doughs. Therefore, for pure and impure doughs, contact works despite particularity. In ordinary cases such as coarse and fine bread or saffron, mukaf may not require even contact at all. Terumat HaDeshen then cites Sefer Yereim, R. Shimshon according to his first answer, and Tosafot in Niddah, who do not distinguish particularity even in the pure and impure case for mukaf, and ultimately rules that for his actual question, which concerned combining sub-minimum doughs, adhesion does not help. This is the proper sequence of Terumat HaDeshen's discussion. He never ruled that saffron-related particularity requires adhesion for mukaf. Still, one point in Terumat HaDeshen remains difficult. At the end he says that in his case the owner is certainly particular because the saffron is insufficient in appearance and flavor for both doughs, "and most opinions hold that one should distinguish," and so forth. Why say "most opinions"? In his actual case, which is combination of sub-minimum doughs, all authorities agree that particularity matters, as stated in the Mishnah and Yerushalmi at the beginning of chapter 4 of Challah. The dispute among the authorities concerns only mukaf, not combination. In any event, Ateret Zahav's ruling emerges correctly. This is also why Rama writes without qualification that if each dough contains the required amount, one may separate from one for the other. All this is clear. However, if there is a difficulty, it concerns the basic rule of mukaf even where the owner is not particular. Shulchan Aruch at the end of siman 325 follows Tur and Rosh that one needs neither contact nor basket combination, based on Beit Yosef's rejection of Sefer HaTerumah in favor of Rosh, Rashba in his laws of challah, and Rambam. In my opinion this requires study. Semag, in the name of Ri, holds that mukaf requires basket combination even without contact, and Tosafot in Perek Elu Overin write similarly in the name of Ri. Elsewhere Tosafot in his name require even contact, and Tosafot in Niddah and Rokeach likewise require it. Perhaps these are two different authorities named Ri, but in any case all these sources require at least basket combination. R. Peretz in Hagahot Semak and Responsa Maimoniyot on Hilchot Zeraim likewise follow Sefer HaTerumah. Thus, many great authorities actually disagree with Rosh. Rosh's proofs from wine jars and from five sacks on a threshing floor are not decisive because dough may have a different rule, as R. Shimshon writes in Ma'aser Sheni. Beit Yosef says Rambam also appears to agree with Rosh, but I do not see that implication. Rambam may instead agree with Ri in Tosafot that actual contact is required even for mukaf and that no distinction exists between pure and impure dough. The Mishnah's mention of an impure dough could be teaching that such an arrangement is permitted despite causing impurity to ordinary produce. Rambam writes at the end of Hilchot Bikkurim that one may cause impurity to ordinary produce in Eretz Yisrael and therefore, with two doughs, one pure and one impure, one takes the amount of challah for both from dough from which challah has not been removed, places it between them near the pure dough, and draws the impure dough toward the pure one in order to separate from that which is mukaf. Although one could say Rambam requires contact only in the pure and impure case, this is unproven. Even if ordinary cases do not require contact, perhaps they require a basket. In truth, Rambam's plain language suggests that for mukaf he does not distinguish pure from impure and generally requires contact in dough. Rambam's ruling in Hilchot Terumot that two storehouses in one house or two barrels can be tithed from one for another is no proof, because, as explained, dough may differ. Beit Yosef also cites Rashba's laws of challah, but I do not have that work before me and therefore cannot respond to that proof. Nevertheless, Rabbenu Yerucham in the name of Ri bar Yehudah and Riva does follow Rosh, Hagahot Responsa Maimoniyot cites Ritzba similarly, Ran at the end of Perek Elu Overin appears to agree, and Bartenura on Challah chapter 2, Mishnah 4, likewise does. Still, Tosafot in Perek Elu Overin in the name of Ri explicitly says that basket combination of two doughs each below the required amount works only when they touch within the basket. Semag says the same in Ri's name, as do R. Peretz, Sefer HaTerumah, R. Shimshon according to one answer, Bartenura, and Rosh in Hilchot Challah, siman 4. Accordingly, Beit Yosef's statement on Tur that Semag in the name of Ri appears to require contact for basket combination is imprecise if it implies Tur does not. Tur writes that if the doughs do not adhere, meaning actual adhesion, but are in one basket, the basket combines them. He does not state the greater novelty that even when they do not touch at all the basket combines them. This indicates that Tur too means they do not adhere but do touch. Perhaps Beit Yosef means only that Semag states this more explicitly than Tur, but that is strained. In any event, he should not have cited only Semag in Ri's name, since Rosh and the other authorities state the same. The practical conclusion is as follows. Two doughs, neither of which independently contains the challah measure but which together do, and which the owner does not mind mixing, can combine through a basket only if they touch one another inside the basket. If each dough independently contains the challah measure and he wishes to separate from one for the other, according to the primary view neither contact nor a basket is needed, provided both are before him; some disagree and require a basket, though not contact. For the requirement of mukaf, ordinary particularity about mixing the doughs does not change the rule. Only where he is so particular that he objects even to their touching, such as one pure and one impure dough or a comparable case, is there a special concern. This discussion concerns dough. For other kinds of produce, mukaf does not require even a basket.
