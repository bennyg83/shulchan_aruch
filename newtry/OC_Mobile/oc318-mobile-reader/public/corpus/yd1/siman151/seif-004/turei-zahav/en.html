A place where they were accustomed not to sell. Meaning: they were suspected of bestiality. And for large livestock the reason is lest he lend it to a non-Jew or rent it and work with it on Shabbat and transgress the negative commandment — for the animal belongs to a Jew; or the "trying out" concern: sometimes he sells it to him close to sunset on erev Shabbat and says to him "come, try it out," and it hears its voice and goes because of him and he is pleased — making it as though he drives his animal on Shabbat. The Tur wrote in the name of the Ramban: when he sells to a butcher — certainly for slaughter and it is permitted; and to a landowner — certainly for work and it is forbidden. Seemingly the two derivations contradict each other: from one who is neither butcher nor landowner we derive stringency from the first clause and leniency from the second clause. It appears his principal intent: we follow him whether for leniency or stringency — if he is a butcher and most of the world buys for work, we follow him for leniency; and so the reverse for stringency; but with one who is neither butcher nor landowner we follow the majority for what purpose they buy. This is proven from the Rosh's words that Beit Yosef brings; and the Perishah strained greatly in explaining the Ramban's words — see there if you wish.
The custom of leniency throughout. The reason given by Beit Yosef in the name of Semag: the "trying out" concern no longer applies in our times, as we are not so expert in [discerning] its voice; and also it is not our custom to lend or rent to a non-Jew. The Ran wrote: that is why they are lenient even to purchase in order to profit from its sale; and nonetheless, a conscientious person should be concerned and minimize it. But regarding lending and renting one should protest against those who are lenient.
