Designated. This implies that where it is uncertain whether the item is intended for idolatry or for another purpose, we rule leniently. Taz. Shach writes that an item specifically designated for idolatry may never be sold to them, even outside their festival and even nowadays. Bach adds that it may not be sold to any non-Jew, even one who is not a priest, because once it is designated for idolatry he certainly wants it for that use.
For commerce. We do not fear that he will sell it to others, because we are commanded concerning direct lifnei iver but not lifnei de-lifnei. Taz writes that where there is concern that a Jew will ultimately transgress, lifnei de-lifnei does apply, as with wheat that became wet and may not be sold to a non-Jew before Pesach lest he resell it to a Jew. See Even HaEzer, end of siman 5, in the gloss.
Pure. Bach writes that nowadays non-Jews are not particular about this, and therefore even non-pure frankincense may not be sold. If it is evident that he purchases for commerce, however, it is plainly permitted even with frankincense, as stated above.
To an ordinary person. We do not fear that he will give it to a priest, because lifnei de-lifnei is not prohibited.
Those who are stringent. Shach writes that the views do not truly disagree. All agree that it is permitted with a non-Jew or apostate. The stringent view concerns an observant Jew whom one is obligated to prevent from sinning. One is not obligated to separate a non-Jew or apostate Jew from prohibition. Shach further explains the contemporary lenient practice: the non-Jews among whom we live direct their worship to the Creator of heaven and earth, though they associate another entity with Him. We have not found lifnei iver in this, because descendants of Noach are not prohibited from such association.
