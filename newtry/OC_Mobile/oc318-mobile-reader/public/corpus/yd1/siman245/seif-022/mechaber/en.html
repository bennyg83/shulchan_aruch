If a resident of a courtyard or alley wishes to become a teacher of children, his neighbors may not prevent him. Likewise, if another teacher opens a school beside an existing teacher so that other children, or even the first teacher’s pupils, will attend him, the first may not prevent him, as it is written, “Hashem desired, for the sake of His righteousness, to magnify Torah and make it glorious.” Rama: When a rabbi resides in a city and teaches the public, another scholar may also come and teach there, even if this somewhat diminishes the first rabbi’s livelihood. Thus, even if the community accepted the first as its rabbi and pays him, the second may reside there and exercise rabbinic authority like the first, provided he is great and fit for the position. See Mahariv, siman 151, and Mahara’i, rulings siman 158. A visiting scholar, however, may not deprive the resident rabbi of fees for weddings and kiddushin, since those fees form part of the resident rabbi’s stipend. He may officiate if the fee is given to the established rabbi. He may also adjudicate between local litigants who approach him, since the city rabbi may not be acceptable to both parties. He may not, however, issue public rulings of prohibition and permission or deliver sermons in a manner that assumes authority in another rabbi’s jurisdiction. One established as rabbi of a city may not be removed from his position even if a greater scholar arrives; see Rivash, siman 271. His son and grandson retain precedence over others whenever they fill their ancestors’ place in fear of Heaven and possess some scholarship. Where the custom is to appoint a rabbi for a fixed term, or to select whomever the community chooses, the community has that authority. But once the congregation has accepted a rabbi, especially with the consent of the governing authority, no scholar may assert authority over him and remove him.
