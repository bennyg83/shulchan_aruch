(Likkut) And if the kohen seized it. This is Rambam's view. Most poskim disagree and rule, as Rama does, that it is taken back from him. This appears to be the straightforward conclusion of Bava Metzia 6b. Rabbah seems to hold so, Rav Chananya supports him, and Abaye concludes similarly. Tosafot there and Rosh rule this way, as do the other poskim. Rambam, however, follows Rashba in siman 311, who explains that the support cited there is not decisive. According to some, the earlier discussion concerns a doubtful firstborn donkey. In the conclusion of the Gemara, that case differs because the animal was already established in the Israelite's possession and the kohen never acquired the firstborn donkey itself, while the substitute lamb was likewise established in the owner's possession. A firstborn kosher animal and other monetary doubts are different. Abaye's statement merely rejects the attempted proof, and the earlier support from Rav Chananya consequently falls away. Rashba brings proof for Rambam from the general rule "the burden of proof rests on the claimant." If seizure always prevailed, that formulation would be too absolute. Rabbah's statement "actually" can be read merely as a rejection: even if one assumed seizure were effective, there would still be no proof. Rambam applies the principle consistently to all doubtful cases, whether the doubt arises from competing claims, from the facts themselves such as a doubtful firstborn, or from unresolved law. In each, the party holding the property has the stronger position, and unauthorized seizure does not reverse that. Several passages support this: Bava Kamma 35b and 46a state the burden-of-proof rule broadly; Ketubot 20a applies presumptive possession between competing witnesses; Bechorot 49a has R. Akiva ruling that if payment was made in a doubtful case it is not reclaimed; Bava Metzia 79a, 102b, and 103a likewise distinguish based on who possesses the money or property; Bava Batra 105b and 34b, Gittin 60b, Ketubot 64a and 84b, and other passages similarly show that possession has legal force in uncertainty. Tosafot in Ketubot 20a distinguish a doubtful firstborn because the one who seizes there is himself uncertain, whereas a person making a definite claim may benefit from possession. They also distinguish seizure before the doubt arose. Nimukei Yosef gives further qualifications, such as matters destined to be clarified and possession obtained with authorization. These distinctions underlie the dispute between Rambam and Shulchan Aruch on one side and Rosh and Baal HaMaor on the other regarding seizure in an unresolved legal doubt. In my view, Rambam's position is primary.
