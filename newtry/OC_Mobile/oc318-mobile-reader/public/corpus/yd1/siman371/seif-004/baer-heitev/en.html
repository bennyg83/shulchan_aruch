They are stringent. The Shach wrote: nevertheless, one should not be stringent in a house of Tzidduk HaDin; on the contrary, that doorway comes to convey tumah to the tent. Piskei Maharai. Maadanei Melekh wrote that this reason suffices only if it has one doorway through which they bring in and remove the dead. But if it has two doorways, one through which they bring him in and a second through which they remove him, as in the Tzidduk HaDin house of Prague, one should be stringent that kohanim not enter through the doorway from which the dead is removed. The Taz wrote that the essential matter depends on the doorway of the house where the dead lies, for the tumah is destined to leave through there, and therefore it conveys tumah even when locked. But regarding other houses, what advantage is there in their being locked? One must say that the Mechaber's phrase “if all the doorways of the houses are locked” is not precise. End quote. Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 103, disagrees; after bringing proofs he wrote that it is clear that in a Tzidduk HaDin house there is no concern at all if kohanim enter it, and even when there is a dead person in the city, kohanim may enter in advance whether it has one doorway or two. Opening. One that has four by four, as stated in Levush.
The projections. The Taz explained that this means the room in which the dead lies also opens toward the projections, so that the house is open on two sides.
To the house. Meaning to a house whose doorway opens toward the projections. Taz.
Open. The Shach wrote: if they are closed, they are tahor. According to this, even where tumah enters some house, if the kohanim intend to close the doors and windows of their homes and not leave until after the dead is removed, they need not leave their houses. Therefore, a kohen who is in a house or room touched by the tumah, and it is locked by door and windows, and he learns that tumah has reached him, may not leave the room until after the dead is removed; for as long as the room is not opened the tumah has not entered it, and when he opens it the tumah will enter. The Derishah wrote that he once did even more: when a dead person was with him on Shabbat, he ordered him taken out and placed in the cellar beneath the house, whose doorway opened outward and had no roof over it, while the kohanim remained in the house. However, one must ask about placing a dead body in a cellar with the cellar door closed, for the exit from the grave requires an opening of a tefach, as written in siman 372. So too here, even though the cellar has much empty space around the dead, if the doorway does not have an opening of a tefach, kohanim may not be above the cellar while it is closed. If, however, the cellar door is made like what is called a gitter in German, although no single hole has a tefach, since the holes combine to several tefachim of opening, there is some basis to be lenient that it counts as an opening of a tefach. See Shaar Efraim, question 93, and Shevut Yaakov, question 85. The Taz disagrees: it is clear from the Mishnah in Ohalot that holes of a lattice do not combine to a tefach. He also rejects the permissibility for kohanim to close their doors and windows, because a closed door is held by a key and iron hinges, and this does not interpose as proven above; we have never heard of kohanim relying on this leniency. The sages were so lenient for a kohen that they did not tell him until he dressed, yet they did not use this leniency, so it is certainly not correct. End quote. In Nekudot HaKesef he challenged the Taz and wrote that the Taz follows his own view at the beginning of the siman, which has already been rejected; on the contrary, the Rambam implies that it does interpose. One can also say the Derishah speaks where the door can stand without support, as in most houses. He further wonders at the Taz and Derishah for writing plainly that a hole in a door requires an opening of a tefach, since Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Tarfon dispute this in Ohalot chapter 14, and Rabbi Akiva holds its measure is a full fist; the halakhah follows Rabbi Akiva against his colleague, and the Rambam rules so explicitly. One must say according to them that this applies only to an ordinary hole made initially by a craftsman, but with a hole made for bringing things in and out, Rabbi Akiva concedes to Rabbi Tarfon. If so, however, they should have specified that case, and the Rambam did not distinguish, implying that in every case the measure is a fist; this requires study. Maharshal wrote that in a place with an eruv where beams with a circumference of a tefach run from alley to alley, and the beam ends enter beneath the roofs on either side, tumah enters beneath them; the beam conveys tumah from roof to roof and spreads through all the houses on both sides. Therefore when there is a dead person in one of the houses, a kohen may not be in any house of the alleyways connected by the eruv beam. The Bach wrote that the beam must be round so that it has the width of a tefach. I remember that when we studied in the house of my teacher, R. Moshe z"l, and a doorway opened from the rabbi's courtyard to the synagogue courtyard, and the lintel on one side reached beneath the roof of a house containing a dead person, my teacher forbade kohanim to exit and enter through that doorway as long as the dead was in the house. The Derishah wrote that he heard this is specifically with a partition that has a small roof over it, or a partition with a lintel a tefach wide over it. But a partition extending from house to house with no roof over it, and no doorway open in the partition, even if the beam on it is a tefach thick and the roofs of the houses on both sides overhang their edges, does not convey tumah; since the middle of the partition has no roof or covering, and there is no doorway, it is considered sealed in the middle and cannot carry tumah. The Taz infers from the Derishah that if there is a clear partition without a roof but with a doorway in its middle, it conveys tumah from house to house, since at the head of the partition there is a tent from the roof of the impure house, even though the doorway does not reach the part beneath the roof. The Taz strongly objects: since there is a break in the partition, what brings the tumah from beneath the roof to the doorway in the middle? Even with a break of less than three we do not apply lavud stringently. Maharshal forbade only where the covering lay without a partition beneath it, extending from under the roof of the impure house to under the roof of another house, so there was open space under the covering without an intervening sealed partition. But where there is a partition built on the ground and no tefach projection adjacent to the part under the second roof, there is no conveyance of tumah, like two adjacent houses with no roof projecting above them, where tumah does not enter from one to the other even though both are open, for projecting roofs are required. It appears that there is no concern for tumah when there is a built partition, even with a doorway in its middle, unless it too has a small roof or a tefach projection from the side entering beneath the impure house to the part reaching under the second roof. The Shach wrote that the roof must be a tent of a tefach from beginning to end, but wherever there is a break between them with a high wall, even if it is many tefachim thick, since there is no tent of a tefach the tumah has no place to travel. Many great authorities agreed, and this is the simple custom.

To compel. It appears that if it is a nefel, where there is no issue of honor, we compel him to remove it from its place so that the kohanim may enter their homes. So wrote the Bach. This is not the Taz's view; rather, if the kohen wants to send someone there to remove the nefel elsewhere, the father of the nefel cannot prevent him, since the kohen is harmed and the father loses nothing.
