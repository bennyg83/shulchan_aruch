If all the openings of the houses are locked, etc. So it is also in the Tur, but this is not precise. The main matter depends on the opening of the house in which the corpse is located, for the end of the tumah is to go out through there, and therefore it conveys tumah even though it is locked. But with other houses, what advantage is there in their being locked? Likewise what he wrote afterward, that those on the side of the courtyard are all locked: even if they are open, as long as that opening of the house in which the tumah is located is locked, the tumah does not go out beneath the projections, since the house containing the corpse has an opening on another side. Many say that there is a scribal error in the Tur’s words, and it should read, “if all the openings of the house, etc.” So too afterward it should read, “and that one on the side of the courtyard is locked.” This is also in the Rosh, who mentions only that opening of the house containing the corpse, which has two openings, one toward the projections and one toward the alley, and the matter depends on which of them is open, for we say that the tumah exits there.
And there are those who permit. The reason for this is that we do not say “the end of the tumah is to go out” renders tamei except through that opening by which they first remove it from the ohel, and not from the open air, since through that exit the ohel becomes tahor. Therefore that opening becomes tamei, but not from there onward.
And if one of them was also opened. Meaning that the house containing the corpse was also opened toward the projections, namely that the same house was opened on both sides. Therefore beneath the projections is also tamei, even though the other houses underneath them are locked, as we mentioned nearby.
It enters beneath them into the house. Meaning any house whose opening is open toward the projections.
Maharshal brought one gloss as follows: “I say that in a place where there is an eruv and they made beams with a tefach circumference from one alley to another, and the ends of the beams enter beneath the roofs on either side, and tumah enters beneath it, the beam brings the tumah from roof to roof and it spreads through all the houses on either side. If so, when there is a corpse, may the Merciful One protect us, in one of the houses, a kohen may not be in any house of the houses in the alleys connected by the eruv beam. I remember that when we were studying in the house of my teacher Rabbi Meir, of blessed memory, and there was an opening from the rabbi’s courtyard to the synagogue courtyard, and the lintel of the opening reached on one side beneath the ceiling of the house in which there was a corpse, my teacher forbade kohanim to go out and come in through that opening as long as the corpse was in the house.” Thus the language of Responsa Maharar Chaim. It is written in the Derishah: it appears to my humble opinion, and I also heard, that this is specifically with a partition that has a small roof over it, or a partition with a lintel above it a tefach wide, and an open doorway through which to go out and come in, so that a lintel is found over it because people enter and exit through it. But a partition extending from house to house with no roof over it, and with no open doorway in the partition, even if the beam placed upon it is a tefach thick, and the roofs of the houses on both sides of the partition at their edges overhang it, nevertheless it does not bring tumah, because the middle of the partition has no roof over it and no name of ceiling, since there is no doorway there; it is considered closed in the middle and has no power to conduct tumah; end quote. It is implied from his words that if there is an open partition with no roof over it and an opening in its middle, it brings the tumah from house to house, since from the top of the partition there is an ohel from the roof of the tamei house, even though the opening does not reach that section which is under the roof; this is because it has the name of a lintel over it, since people enter and exit there. I am very astonished by this. Since there is an interruption in the portion of the partition that is closed before the beginning of the opening, between the part that reaches under the roof and the opening, who brings the tumah from beneath the roof to the opening in the middle? Even when there is an interruption of less than three we do not say lavud to be stringent. They forbade in the responsum I brought in the name of Maharshal only because the ceiling lay without a partition beneath it, from under the roof of the tamei house to under the roof of the other house, so that there was open space under the ceiling from the tamei house to the other house without an interruption of a closed partition. This is unlike this matter, where there is an interruption because of the partition built on the ground and there is no tefach projection near the section beneath the second roof. Certainly there is no bringing of tumah here at all. It is like two adjacent houses with no roof protruding over them, where tumah does not enter from house to house even though both are open, for protruding roofs are required, as written here in the Shulchan Aruch. It appears that one should not be concerned for tumah where there is a built partition, even if there is an opening in its middle, not through the whole partition, unless there is also a small roof over it or a tefach projection from that side that enters beneath the tamei house to the section reaching beneath the second roof.
All the houses whose openings are open, etc. The reason is that since there are roofs over the entrances of all the houses, and in one of them there is a corpse, the tumah goes through the opening of its house beneath the roofs and enters other houses that are open beneath their roof. But if there is no roof over the entrances, and the walls of the houses next to the tamei house are closed up to their roof from the courtyard side, there is no tumah for those houses, even though they are open toward the street. Although there is one roof from the tamei house on the side toward the adjacent house, nevertheless there is no place for the tumah to enter the other house, since on the side where the opening is, there is no roof. It is written in the Derishah: even if the tumah comes from the opening of the tamei house to beneath its roof, and from there from roof to roof of another house, nevertheless if the kohanim intend to close the entrances and windows of their houses and not leave until after the corpse is removed, they need not leave their houses. Greater than this I did when a dead person was with me on Shabbat, and I commanded that he be taken out and placed in a cellar beneath the house, whose cellar opening faced outside and had no roof over the cellar opening, and the kohanim remained in the house. But there is a difficulty regarding placing the corpse in the cellar with the cellar door closed in the cellar opening, for we require the exit from the grave to have an opening of a tefach, as written in siman 372, that nowadays, since it is entirely closed, even if it has an airspace of a tefach, everything opposite it is tamei. So too here, although in the cellar there is a large space around the corpse, nevertheless there is no tefach opening at the exit of the cellar opening so long as it is closed, and the kohanim are in the house above the cellar. However, if holes are made in the cellar door, like what is called in the vernacular “gigator,” although no single hole has a tefach, nevertheless, since by combining the holes together there is an opening of several tefachim, there is a side to be lenient that it is included in “its exit has an opening of a tefach”; end quote. I am astonished that a holy mouth would say this against explicit mishnayot, which I brought at the beginning of this siman, that the holes of a gigator do not combine to a tefach. They said explicitly in Ohalot chapter 12 that tumah does not exit from the house when there is no opening of a tefach in one place. If so, the tumah remains in the house and it is like a sealed grave, conveying tumah up to the sky. How did he write leniently because of combining the holes to let the tumah out, since the cellar is closed on all sides? Although the door is not called an interposition, as I will write nearby, nevertheless the cellar is sealed. Therefore one should not rely at all on this permission of combining the holes. Also what he wrote permitting kohanim to close their entrances and windows is impossible, for closing the entrance is by means of a key, and it does not interpose, as stated above at the beginning of this siman, seif 1, which I brought in the name of the Rambam. Furthermore, the door is hung by iron hinges, and such a thing does not interpose, as I wrote above with clear proof. We have never heard that kohanim practice according to this permission. How much our teachers were lenient out of concern for the kohen, not to tell him until he had dressed himself, yet they did not practice this permission. Rather, certainly it is not so. This appears to me clear.
They cannot force, etc. The reason is in Responsa Maharil, as follows: “But if they are accustomed to delay them for their honor, so that many may hear about him and involve themselves with him, or to purify him in his house, if so how can we force them to degrade him against the custom? Here the rule that the damager must distance himself does not apply, since he is under compulsion, for since the custom is so, it is so”; end quote. My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that if it is a stillborn child, where there is no concern for honor, they force him to remove it from his house for the sake of the kohanim, for the damager must distance himself; end quote. I was astonished by the words of the above responsum, for the rule that one can force him to remove it because the damager must distance himself does not apply here at all, as stated in Choshen Mishpat siman 155, Shulchan Aruch seif 32: if one had a tree near his fellow’s pit, the owner of the pit cannot say, “The roots of the tree are damaging my pit,” because this is damage that comes by itself after time, and at the time he planted it, it did not damage him, etc. All the more so here, where he never performs any act to damage him, but it is from Heaven. How can he force him? It appears that this too was the intent of the responsum when it wrote that he is under compulsion, meaning that it happened by itself. What it concluded afterward, “since the custom is so, it is so,” in any case, it is proper to emend so, for how does compulsion relate to custom? According to this, even with a stillborn child one cannot force him to remove it, since he is not called a damager. However, if the kohen wants to send someone there to remove the stillborn child to another place, the father of the stillborn child cannot prevent him, since the kohen is being harmed and the father loses nothing. So it appears in my humble opinion.
