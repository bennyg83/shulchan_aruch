(Siman 371, Taz seif katan 3) He wishes to prove that a partition formed by a door or window does not help whenever it cannot stand without iron hinges or some other iron component, even though those components serve the structure. In my opinion this is incorrect. If so, whenever the Mishnah and poskim state without qualification that doors and windows interpose against tumah, they should have specified that this is only when they can stand without hinges and a fixed lock. Moreover, why does the Mishnah in Ohalot chapter 6 single out the case of those burying a corpse who supported the door with a key and say that if the door can stand independently it interposes? No such qualification is stated anywhere else when a door is said to interpose. Taz also misunderstands Rambam. Rambam's actual language is: "If not, the house is impure, because the person is found to be the one who interposed against the tumah, and a person and utensils convey tumah but do not interpose, as we explained." Rambam mentions only that the person created the partition, not that the key as a utensil failed to interpose. Rambam means that the person himself maintained the closure by holding the key. Had he not held it, the door could not stand shut; therefore the person is the effective interposition, and a person does not interpose. Rambam does not discuss the key as a utensil at all. Indeed, the key is considered attached to the door, as his commentary to the Mishnah states explicitly. His concluding words about people and utensils conveying but not interposing simply repeat a general formula used earlier in Hilchot Tumat Met and are not intended to classify this key. Rambam's commentary to the Mishnah proves this clearly. He says that if the doorway can remain closed even after the person removes his hand from the key, the house is pure; if removing his hand causes the door to open, the house is impure because the person carrying the corpse was the one interposing against tumah. Thus the issue is the person's support, not the key's material. Indeed, Rambam implies the opposite of Taz: even if the door can stand only because of the key, it interposes, provided it can remain closed without the person's hand. Tosafot and Mordechai may agree in practical law, though they explain the Mishnah differently. They understand "if the door can stand independently" as without the key, because otherwise the closing would be temporary. Their case, however, can be understood as a loose key temporarily wedged there rather than one permanently attached to the door. If the key is permanently attached and made for closing the door, it is not a temporary closure. A door and its attached lock are considered components made for the ground and are not susceptible to tumah, as stated in Kelim and Rambam. Hinges fixed to doors or windows similarly become part of the structure. R. Shimshon's explanation likewise need not mean a permanently attached key. Even if the key itself is not susceptible to tumah, according to one explanation any closure dependent upon a separate implement is not considered a valid interposition because it does not stand independently. Mordechai and Agudah follow that approach, as does Rama and Bach. All this is clear.

Derishah may also be speaking where the door can remain closed without a person's support, as is usual in most houses. Taz's additional argument from the hinges is not a separate argument at all, but the same point repeated. In truth all of it is incorrect, as explained above. His claim that such a practice was never heard of proves nothing. It is ordinarily impractical for a person to remain sealed in a room like a prisoner, unable to enter or leave and unable to allow others to enter. It is also possible that the earlier authorities addressed places where the openings could not practically be sealed.
