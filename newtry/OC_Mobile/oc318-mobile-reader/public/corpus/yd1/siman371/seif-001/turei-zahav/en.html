In an opening one handbreadth wide. This must be in one continuous place. If the total reaches a handbreadth only by combining many small openings, as in a lattice, it does not transmit tumah. This is taught in Ohalot chapter 13, where Beit Hillel require the full measure in one place, both to bring tumah in and to let it out. The same principle applies to the smaller bore-size measure of a window made for light and to the handbreadth measure of a window made for use. The Rambam explicitly rules in chapter 14 of Hilchot Tumat Met that the handbreadth must be in one place; otherwise the opening is considered sealed. He says the same in his commentary to Ohalot chapter 8 regarding a latticed window. See also what I write below in seif 4 concerning the Derishah.
And anything susceptible to tumah. This also refers to the beginning of the ruling. Even when the entire opening is sealed, the material used must not be susceptible to tumah. It should be sealed with wood or stone that is not a utensil, as the Beit Yosef cites from the Rif.
Unless it can stand without support. Tosafot in Bava Batra 19 explain that anything requiring support is considered only a temporary seal. They also offer another reason: the support itself must not be susceptible to tumah. Even if the sealing object is not susceptible, if it is held in place by something that is, it is treated as though it too were susceptible. The Rash and Rambam rule accordingly regarding the mishnah of the bearers of a corpse passing through a portico. Certainly, if one packs enough wood and stones into a window that the walls of the opening hold them in place, this is effective and interposes, for it is not considered externally supported.
An incident occurred in which tumah was present in a house adjoining a synagogue and entered through the women’s section window. A certain authority ordered the window sealed with the small curtain ordinarily hanging there from iron hooks for modesty. I said that he erred. Ohalot chapter 8 expressly teaches that a sheet or mat made as a tent interposes before tumah, but if not made as a tent it does not. The Rash explains that when fashioned as a tent it interposes even though susceptible to tumah, but otherwise it does not. The Rambam explains that merely stretched material lacking the slope and walls of a tent is not a tent. Here there was only a covering over a window, neither a wall nor a tent. Furthermore, the curtain was suspended from iron rings, so iron maintained it. The Rambam rules that if one closes a door and props it with a key, or a person supports it, the house is pure only if the door can stand by itself. Otherwise the person or utensil is what interposes, and persons and utensils transmit tumah but do not interpose. Tosafot likewise require that the barrier stand by means of something not susceptible to tumah. Although metal rings used alone may be pure, Rashi explains that when attached to a strap they are susceptible; here the rings together with the curtain form a utensil and resemble a key. Even according to an interpretation that the supporting key itself is not susceptible to tumah, it still does not interpose because a barrier maintained by a utensil is not deemed a valid tent. The apparent contradiction between Kelim chapter 11, which exempts a lock made for attachment to the ground, and Ohalot chapter 6, where a door supported by a key does not interpose, can be resolved in several ways. Either any externally supported barrier is temporary and invalid, regardless of whether the support is susceptible; or the key in Ohalot is an ordinary movable key and is susceptible; or, as Rabbeinu Yitzchak found in a commentary to the mishnah, even a key not susceptible to tumah invalidates the barrier because a tent maintained by a utensil is not a valid interposition. The Mordechai adopts the first explanation, requiring the sealing object to stand in the window without support. His citation of the key teaches that the matter does not depend solely upon susceptibility to tumah. It follows that a door or window does not interpose if it cannot stand without iron hinges or another iron support. Even if those items were made for attachment to the ground and therefore are not susceptible to tumah, they do not interpose. Accordingly, the locked doors mentioned in seif 4 are effective only when they can stand without such support. One should not rely upon the Derishah’s lenient view that merely locking the house interposes. For the same reason, an iron lattice placed in a window does not interpose, since it is susceptible to tumah and is not formed as a tent. Therefore a kohen may not remain in the adjoining house on the basis of sealing the doorway or window in this manner.
