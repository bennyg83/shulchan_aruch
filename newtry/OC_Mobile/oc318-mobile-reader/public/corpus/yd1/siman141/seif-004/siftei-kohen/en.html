Such as four faces together. Rashi explained: face of ox, human, lion, eagle — for one creature, like the sacred creatures, as written "do not make with Me" — those dwelling with Me — end. And it appears what he said "ox" is not specific, for we say in Chagigah (12b) Ezekiel came and changed it to cherub; rather he specified ox because at the time the verse "do not make with Me" was stated there was still an ox; and nevertheless now that there is a cherub, forbidden to make their four faces together, for in any case it is included in "do not make with Me" — those dwelling with Me; alternatively: to tell us even now when ox is not in the chariot it is nevertheless forbidden to make four faces together including ox, which was there when the verse was stated.
And likewise the form of a human being by itself. This is derived from 'You shall not make with Me,' which is understood as 'do not make an image of Me,' meaning the human form in which Hashem appears to prophets. One might ask: if the four faces together are forbidden, why is a separate prohibition for a human face necessary? Tosafos answer that it teaches liability even where one finds an existing human face and completes the other three faces. Hagahos Semak gives the same answer. Alternatively, one transgresses two prohibitions. Tosafos in Rosh Hashanah and the Ran add that one might otherwise have thought a composite of four faces is less like the divine image than a human face alone.
It is forbidden to make them even for decoration. Rambam writes this in chapter 3. In older copies the wording is 'even for a non-Jew,' and the Tur also has that reading. Both formulations express the same rule: since Scripture objects to the act of making, there is no distinction between making an image for decoration and making it for idolatry.
It is forbidden to retain them. This is because of suspicion. As explained above in seif katan 17, it is likewise forbidden to tell a non-Jew to make them for him, even if he does not intend to retain them, because instructing a non-Jew is rabbinically prohibited even outside the laws of Shabbos.
However if he finds them permitted. Meaning: for benefit permitted, provided he does not leave them — for obviously not better than made by a gentile which is forbidden to leave; and so proven in the Tur — although forbidden to leave, permitted for benefit to the finder — see there — investigate.
When does this apply? To a raised image, etc. Meaning that for these forms the prohibition of making and retaining applies specifically when they are raised; if engraved or sunken, one may make them and certainly retain them. This follows from seif 5 concerning a signet ring bearing a human image. The reason is that the prohibition applies to an image resembling the actual being, which appears raised. Accordingly, drawings on a wall or board, although flat, are treated as sunken because they are not raised like the actual figure. By contrast, forms of the sun, moon, and similar heavenly bodies, which appear recessed in the firmament, are forbidden even when sunken. The Levush's explanation linking the distinction to whether a form is ordinarily worshipped is mistaken. The rules of finding, making, and retaining arise from different reasons: finding depends on whether there is reason to suspect worship; making depends on resemblance to the heavenly or human form prohibited by Scripture; retaining depends upon suspicion. Thus a human or composite face shown in a form that clearly was worshipped could be forbidden when found, yet the prohibition of making still depends upon whether it is raised. Conversely, an ordinary sun or moon form may be permitted when found nowadays but remains forbidden to make even sunken. The Tur's statement that forms forbidden when found are forbidden even when sunken means only that when the circumstances show an image was made for idolatry, the prohibition on finding it does not depend upon whether it is raised or sunken. It does not define the independent prohibition of making or retaining. It further appears that all these prohibitions require a complete image, such as a human figure with two eyes, a complete nose, and the body. Half a figure, as some artists depict only one side of a form, is not prohibited.
All permitted. Both to make them and to leave them.
And some permit in the public domain. Meaning: to leave them, since there is no suspicion in the public; but to make them there is no distinction between individual and public. And R. Yerucham wrote: although in the public there is no suspicion, the matter is unseemly for them — Beit Yosef brings him in Beit HaBechira (and wrote that with two there is no suspicion — and it appears there is proof for his words from the beginning of Berakhot).
