Faces. Rashi explains the faces of an ox, human, lion, and eagle combined in one creature, like the holy Chayot. After Yechezkel replaced the ox with a cherub, it is forbidden to make the four current faces together because they are among the beings that minister before Hashem. Alternatively, even now it remains forbidden to make the original four faces including the ox, since that was their form when the verse was given. Shach.
For decoration. Some versions read 'for a non-Jew.' Both readings lead to the same law, because the verse forbids making without distinction. Taz asks why the four-faced form requires separate mention when a human form alone is already forbidden. Ran answers that one might think adding other faces to the human face permits it, so the law teaches otherwise. Taz is reluctant to infer that combining a human face with only one or two additional faces would be permitted, since such a novelty would have been stated explicitly. He instead suggests that when removing the prohibition, damaging only the human face may not suffice; all the faces must be impaired. Nekudot HaKesef notes that Tosafot and Ramach explicitly support Ran's explanation.
To retain them. Because of suspicion. It is likewise forbidden to instruct a non-Jew to make them even if one will not retain them, because instructing a non-Jew is rabbinically prohibited even outside Shabbat law. Shach.
Permitted. Meaning permitted for benefit, provided one does not retain them. Shach.
Recessed. Because the celestial bodies also appear recessed in the firmament. Shach writes that prohibited forms must be complete, such as a human with two eyes, a complete nose, and the whole body. Half a figure, such as a side profile, is permitted. Taz cites Ramban that everything biblically forbidden under 'You shall not make with Me,' including the four faces, angels, sun, and moon, is forbidden whether protruding or recessed. Although the Mechaber does not appear to rule this way, one should not be lenient against Ramban, especially since the Tur and Ran agree.
Permitted. Both to make and retain them. In a public setting, only retention is permitted because suspicion does not arise publicly; making remains forbidden equally to an individual and a community. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that although no suspicion exists publicly, it is still unbecoming. With two people, suspicion does not arise. Shach.
