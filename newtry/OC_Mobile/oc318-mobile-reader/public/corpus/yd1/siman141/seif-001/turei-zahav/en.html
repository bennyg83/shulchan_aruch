All images, etc. In the Mishnah, Rabbi Meir and the Sages disagree. Rabbi Meir holds that since that form was worshipped in one place, we issue a decree concerning all other places on its account. The Sages did not issue such a decree unless it has a sword in its hand, etc., as written here in the Shulchan Aruch. Nevertheless, it appears that even if it has a sword in its hand, this renders it forbidden only when it is a form that we know is worshipped in such a manner, similar to Rabbi Meir's prohibition, which is because of a decree. If so, there is no distinction whether that form is a human form or another form. The Tur writes below in this siman, “A standing human form, etc., with a staff in its hand, etc.” The Beit Yosef writes here that the Tur's words imply specifically a human form, but not other forms. It appears that his intent is that regarding a human form, the reason is that it is known that people worship it, unlike other forms. However, if it were established for us that they also worship other forms, the same law would apply to them. Therefore, the Rambam wrote, and the Shulchan Aruch copied here, “and the form had in its hand, etc.,” without mentioning a human form, for the reason we have stated. It appears that what the Gemara states, “Rav Yehudah said in the name of Shmuel: This concerns a royal statue,” likewise indicates that it is known to be worshipped, as Rashi wrote. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that what the Shulchan Aruch stated without qualification concerning a form is incorrect. This is difficult, for is this the Shulchan Aruch's own ruling? It is the ruling of the Rambam in chapter 7 of Hilchos Avodah Zarah, and its intent is as we have explained.
For they are ordinarily made for the sake of idol worship. The Tur writes concerning this, “Since a doubt concerning idol worship is forbidden, for ordinarily, etc.” This is surprising. Since it is ordinarily so, why does he need the reason that a doubt is ruled stringently? It is also difficult that he afterwards says that in cities they were certainly made for decoration. Why is that certain? It is also possible that they were made for idol worship. It appears that although a doubt concerning idol worship is ruled stringently, nevertheless, wherever there is a rationale for permitting and a rationale for forbidding, it is proper to be lenient. We rule everywhere that we do not establish a prohibition on the basis of a doubt, and we are stringent because of a doubt only where the prohibited status was already established. Therefore, since there is a rationale that it was made for decoration, we do not establish the rationale for prohibition as certain, because the permissive rationale is supported by the principle that we do not establish a prohibition. In villages, however, where it is not customary to make such a form for decoration, the rationale that it was made for idol worship is equal to the permissive possibility. Each side has a weakness. The prohibited possibility is weakened because we do not establish a prohibition, while the permissive possibility is weakened because it is not customary to make it for decoration. The two possibilities are therefore equal, and because of the doubt we rule stringently regarding a non-Jew. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that the Tur's intent is to prevent this from being a compound doubt, perhaps it was not made for idol worship, and even if it was made for idol worship, perhaps they nullified it. This is difficult, because there is no nullification here. Even if one dragged it, that would not constitute nullification so long as its form was not diminished. Here we are discussing whole images, for if they were broken, that would indeed constitute nullification, as stated afterwards.
