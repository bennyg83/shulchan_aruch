In cities. Shach notes an apparent contradiction. The first clause implies that doubtful images are permitted, while the latter implies that they are forbidden unless certainly ornamental. He explains that the first clause merely describes ordinary village practice: such images are presumed made for idolatry. In any doubt they are forbidden unless it is known with certainty that they were made as decoration. Perishah agrees, unlike Bach.
The city. Meaning the city itself, as this word often means in the Gemara and authorities. This wording implies that any image standing at the entrance to the city holding a staff or similar symbol is forbidden, not only a human form. Nowadays the circumstances govern: in cities and villages alike, one follows whether the evidence indicates idolatrous purpose or mere decoration. Shach.
An image. Meaning an image whose features demonstrate that it was made for worship. A pendant worn on the neck is permitted only when it is known that no one bowed to it. Otherwise it is no better than an honored vessel bearing an image commonly worshipped, where we suspect that the vessel itself was worshipped. With an actual idol there is no distinction between honored and degraded placement; that distinction applies only to vessels, where a degraded image is presumed decorative. Ran cites Rashba that vessels engraved with idolatrous images in our time are forbidden, though Ran questions whether today such images are made only as memorial symbols. Rabbeinu Yerucham similarly writes that human or other sculpted images may be forbidden, but perhaps contemporary non-Jews are not devoted to these images and make them only for decoration. They may then be permitted for benefit, though protruding images should not be retained because of suspicion. In a Torah doubt one should be stringent. An image ordinarily bowed to is forbidden according to all. Shach.
