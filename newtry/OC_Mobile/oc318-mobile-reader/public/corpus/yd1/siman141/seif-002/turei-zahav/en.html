Standing on its base. This means that it has a broad pedestal. In that case, the pieces do not appear to be fragments, but rather to have been fashioned this way originally, with only this limb present, and therefore there was no nullification. It is implied here that when it was worshipped while whole and a part broke off, this constitutes nullification. This is difficult, for at the end of siman 146 the Tur rules that an idol that broke by itself remains forbidden. If so, we should be concerned here that this piece may have broken off by itself. Why is it permitted when it is found not standing on its base? This is a doubt concerning a Torah prohibition. It appears that one can answer that there the Tur writes that it remains forbidden only where an ordinary craftsman could restore it. Here, however, where it broke and the piece was lost, an ordinary craftsman cannot restore it. I later saw that Rashi, at the end of the chapter Kol HaTzelamim, asks this question and answers that an idol breaking by itself is uncommon, and we follow the majority assumption that a non-Jew broke it. The Tur writes in the name of the Rambam that it is forbidden even when it is not standing on its base. The Tur challenges him, for the Gemara established Shmuel's ruling specifically where it stands on its base. The Beit Yosef answers that the Rambam holds that Shmuel also maintains that an idol that broke by itself is permitted, and therefore Shmuel required the condition that it stands on its base. According to us, however, who rule that an idol that broke by itself remains forbidden, fragments of an idol are forbidden, for it was certainly worshipped and the breaking does not effect nullification. He answered well, for Tosafos likewise write there that Shmuel follows Reish Lakish, who permits an idol that broke by itself. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, disagreed and answered that the Rambam distinguishes between images and an actual idol. Regarding images, it must stand on its base, but regarding an idol it is forbidden even without this. This is difficult, for Shmuel explicitly permits even fragments of an idol, whereas the Rambam forbids them, writing that since it is known that this form was worshipped, it remains forbidden until it is known that the non-Jew nullified it. The Beit Yosef cites this. Necessarily, the Rambam rules unlike Shmuel, as the Beit Yosef wrote.
