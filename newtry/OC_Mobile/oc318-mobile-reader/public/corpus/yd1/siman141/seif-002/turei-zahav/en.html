Standing on its base. Meaning that it has a broad base-seat, so that the fragments do not appear to be broken pieces but rather were made that way from the outset — and there was only that one member remaining — and there is no nullification here. This implies that when the idol was worshipped while it was whole and broke off from it afterward, that constitutes nullification. And this is difficult — for at the end of siman 146 the Tur ruled that an idol that broke by itself is forbidden; and if so, we should be concerned here that perhaps this broke off by itself — and why is the fragment permitted to be found when it is not standing on its base? This is a biblical doubt! And it seems one may say: since the Tur wrote there that the prohibition applies only when an ordinary person can restore it — and here it broke off and was lost, so an ordinary person cannot restore it. Afterward I saw in Rashi at the end of chapter Kol HaTzelamim who raised this difficulty and answered that breaking by itself is uncommon, and we follow the majority — that a gentile broke it. And the Tur wrote in the name of Rambam that it is forbidden even when not standing on its base; and the Tur challenged him, since the Talmud established this leniency specifically according to Shmuel — only when standing on its base; and Beit Yosef answered that Rambam holds Shmuel also maintained that an idol which broke by itself is permitted — and therefore needed this qualification; but according to us, for whom an idol that broke by itself is forbidden, the fragments of an idol that was certainly worshipped are forbidden and nullification does not help — and Beit Yosef answered well, as Tosafot there also wrote that Shmuel holds like R' Lakish that an idol that broke by itself is permitted. And my father-in-law of blessed memory disagreed and answered that Rambam distinguishes between tzalamim and alilim — that with statues it is specifically when standing on its base, but with an idol it is forbidden even without that; and this is difficult, for Shmuel explicitly permits even the fragments of an idol — and Rambam forbids it, writing that since it is known that this form was worshipped, it remains in its forbidden state until it is known that the gentile nullified it — and Beit Yosef cites him; therefore one must conclude that Rambam ruled not like Shmuel — and as Beit Yosef wrote.
