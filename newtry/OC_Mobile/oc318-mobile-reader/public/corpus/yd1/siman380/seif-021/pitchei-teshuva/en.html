They close their shop. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 324, concerning a custom whereby an onen formally sells his store and merchandise to an acquaintance, who conducts business throughout the mourning period and afterward sells it back to the original owner. Chatam Sofer reports that he heard from senior sages that where partners were in business and a close relative of one partner was dying, they instructed that partner to send a notice through a trusted communal agent dissolving the partnership and stating that he no longer wished to remain a partner. This allowed the remaining partner to conduct business during the mourning period, provided the mourner received no profit from the business done during those days. If afterward the other partner acted generously and absorbed some benefit into the account, that was not a problem. This ruling is sound because the sale or dissolution occurred while the relative was still alive. In the case before him, however, where the purchaser stands in the same shop, onlookers will assume he is merely the mourner's employee. Even if they know of the formal sale through communal trustees, it appears to them as a joke and mere legal fiction. The owner is an onen who cannot even answer Amen to a blessing, so who permitted him to sell his merchandise at that time? Nevertheless, Chatam Sofer finds some basis to defend those who permit the arrangement. In practice many merchants cannot cease business for all seven days and otherwise seek permissions after three days on grounds of substantial loss, traveling caravans, lack of livelihood, and the like. They therefore chose the lesser problem, relying upon lenient views during aninut to permit another person to stand in the shop through a sale arrangement, thereby freeing the mourner to observe the mourning period properly. Although Chatam Sofer does not approve of the leniency, he writes that one should not protest forcefully, and it is better that people act unknowingly than knowingly transgress; see there.
But he may work privately. See Responsa Mishkenot Yaakov, siman 68, who writes that the position of Rif and Rambam appears more correct: they do not distinguish among types of partnership and prohibit the partner's activity in all cases. This also appears from the Yerushalmi, which makes no distinction. The Rosh argued that the Bavli disagrees with the Yerushalmi, but Mishkenot Yaakov answers that the two cases are not comparable. In the case of Marion, each party owned one ox, neither sufficient to plow alone, and they partnered so that both oxen would plow each person's field. When one became a mourner, the arrangement was suspended, because his ox was effectively hired or obligated to the other. This is not comparable to partners in a store, where both are partners in the labor and the business belongs jointly to both; when one works, he is acting as the other's agent and this is prohibited. Accordingly, partnership in a store should be stringent even in private, whereas the case of paired oxen may be permitted even publicly; see there.
Inside his house. See Responsa Raanach II, siman 93, who writes that even if partners stipulated from the outset that if one became a mourner the other would conduct the partnership business during that week and keep all that week's profit, while the mourner would later receive another week's profit in exchange, this remains prohibited. Since the prohibition is based on appearance, the stipulation does not help, because the public does not know that such a condition was made. Although in the laws of Shabbat a Jew and non-Jew who are partners may rely upon such an advance stipulation, that case differs because Shabbat recurs constantly and observers assume that a condition was made. Mourning is an occasional event, so people will not make that assumption; see there.
