The second person is exempt from his oath. The Beit Yosef writes this in the name of Rabbeinu Yeruham and the Sar of Shantz. Rabbeinu Yeruham brought proof from the first chapter of Sotah. It appears that the proof is from the end of that chapter, where Moshe said to Yosef: The oath Hashem swore that He would redeem us has arrived, and the oath you imposed upon Israel has arrived. If you reveal yourself, well and good. If not, we are released from your oath. Why did Moshe first mention Hashem’s oath to redeem them? It would have sufficed to say that the oath Yosef imposed had arrived. The reason is that Moshe wished to conclude that if Yosef did not reveal himself, they would be permanently free of the oath. Without the introductory statement, they might still have been required to return on another occasion for his bones. Yosef had said, “You shall bring my bones up from here with you.” Perhaps “with you” meant only that if possible they should take them along, but otherwise they should return later. However, Yosef had also said, “Hashem will surely remember you, and you shall bring up,” which appears superfluous unless the oath was fundamentally dependent upon that redemption. Moshe therefore began by saying that the time of redemption had arrived. If Yosef did not reveal himself then, they were fully released. From this we learn a general principle. When an oath contains a condition upon which the essence of the matter depends, the condition must be fulfilled. If it fails, even through duress, the oath does not remain. For example, if one contracts with another to supply specified merchandise for a fair, when that is the time it can be sold and not afterward, and the supplier is prevented from delivering it at that time but can deliver later, the buyer is certainly not obligated to accept it later. Although duress exempts the supplier from a penalty he undertook if he failed to deliver on time, for he never obligated himself under such circumstances, the buyer likewise committed only if the goods arrived at the crucial time. If the condition is not essential and duress prevents timely fulfillment, the underlying agreement is not void. We find a similar distinction regarding a sale for a particular time where the seller presses for payment and it is not paid, in Choshen Mishpat, siman 190, seif 16. Seif 15 explains this applies when it is known that he sold only because he needed the money. If not, the sale is not void. So too in many cases, only a condition essential to the act causes the act to be void when unfulfilled. If the act does not fundamentally depend upon it, the act remains. Therefore, when a man and woman swear to marry and fix a date in the oath, and duress prevents one from marrying then, the principal oath to marry is not void, because the match does not fundamentally depend upon that date. Further proof is siman 228, seif 41, in the Rosh’s name, that even after the date passes the oath remains. From the Rashba and the responsum of the Ramban cited there, it is clear according to all opinions that the marriage oath is not void merely because the date passed. If one party intentionally delayed and violated the oath to marry by the date, the other is entirely released, even if the first later wishes to marry, because each party’s oath depended upon the other’s fulfillment. If one intentionally violates even a minor element, the corresponding oath is void. When both were prepared but duress prevented one, however, the oath remains and they must marry after the duress passes. If the duress is of a kind that clearly was not included in the oath, he should seek release from a sage. This is clear. Therefore Mahariv, siman 136, cited by the Rama nearby, writes that if the date passed because of duress, the oath certainly was not void. He adds that when one failed to appear by the appointed date and it is not established that he was prevented, it is as though he stood before us and said he did not wish to marry at that time. Nevertheless, the other party should seek release. Although Mahariv writes that this release is merely an added precaution, it appears to be strict law. At the beginning of Kesubos the Sages enacted rules because of modest and immodest women, lest the husband have been prevented from appearing while she assumes he was not. Here too, perhaps he was prevented, so release is required. I write this because my revered father-in-law challenged Mahariv: The person accepted the cherem or handshake oath only on condition that the other appear at the agreed place and time. Since he did not appear, even through duress, the other bears no penalty and may marry someone else. He therefore preferred the Sar of Shantz and said release is only an extra precaution. These words are astonishing, as explained. The Sar of Shantz likewise did not permit a case of duress. He wrote “the one who obstructed,” implying intentional obstruction. Regarding duress, all agree that the other party is not released. The Rama’s ruling is primary and is applied daily. Mahariv also writes that we often see parties withdraw from a match and pay the penalty without release, and no one objects that the ancient communal cherem remains. Nevertheless, the customary engagement document says that the penalty does not exempt from the cherem, implying that they presently accept a new cherem in addition to the ancient one. It therefore appears that this new cherem requires release by a sage. So it appears to me.
