Nevertheless, when she wishes, etc. If she does not wish to marry him, he is liable for a vain oath according to this opinion, because when he swore to marry her he swore to something not within his power, as he cannot compel her. It appears that this opinion agrees regarding one who says, “I will give one hundred zuz to so-and-so,” that it is not a vain oath. He can perform the act of giving. If the other person refuses to accept, he did not swear concerning acceptance. Marriage differs, because he cannot perform it at all while she refuses. The alternative opinion holds that even “I will marry her” is not vain, similar to “I will give.” He is obligated to ask her to marry him, and if she refuses his oath is not nullified and he bears no punishment. He obligated himself only for the benefit of the person urging him to give the money or marry the woman. If they refuse, he is free of blame. This is the substance of the Rivash, siman 387, who is the authority behind the alternative opinion. If one swore, “I will not marry any woman except so-and-so,” and she later refused to marry him, at first glance he may marry another without release, because there is a mitzvah to marry and an oath to nullify a mitzvah does not take effect. However, from the Ran cited in siman 237, seif 6, I learned that release is required. The oath takes effect through inclusion, because whenever she is willing to marry him, the prohibition against every other woman applies. Therefore, even now that she refuses, the oath remains in effect and requires release. So it appears to me. Although the oath already includes women through whom he cannot fulfill procreation, as the Rama writes in siman 239, seif 8, there is a practical difference if he explicitly swore concerning all women capable of bearing children.
