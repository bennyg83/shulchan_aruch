But if, etc. There.
Even though, etc. Since the prohibition is derived only through exposition, the oath nevertheless takes effect upon him. See Ran to Nedarim 5a, s.v. Ha Kamashma Lan, and Bava Kamma 91b. We rule according to the authority who says that a person is not permitted to do so, for the anonymous Mishnah there follows his view. Although in Taanit 11a we rule according to Rabbi Elazar, fasting is different, since Shmuel himself explains the passage there as referring to one who sits in fasting. Ran maintains that although Bava Kamma there says that the matter is a tannaitic dispute, this refers to the statement in Shevuot, “I will bring proof from one who swore,” etc. The phrase “he has permission to do so” implies that it is entirely permitted, but no one disputes that the oath takes effect. This requires study. See also Ran in his novellae and what I wrote in siman 239, seif 6.
And he swore, etc. See Rashi there, s.v. Leitei, etc. Rambam, however, maintains that the matter remains contingent: if his fellow fulfills the condition, his oath is fulfilled, and if not, the oath is violated. This is not properly termed a vain oath, because a vain oath concerns only the past, as stated there, 20b, but rather a false oath, as stated there, 25b, “it is not recognizable,” etc. Rivash, responsum 387.
But a transgression, etc. In the preceding cases he receives lashes for a vain oath, as stated in the Mishnah there. Only in the final case, where one swears concerning his fellow, Rambam writes that he does not receive lashes; see there. His words are very difficult, and Kesef Mishneh and Lechem Mishneh already struggled with them. The essential explanation is that he does not receive lashes because the oath remains contingent until his fellow acts, and it is therefore a prohibition that involves no action, like one who says, “I will eat this loaf today,” according to Rabbi Yochanan there, 3b. Rambam's wording should be corrected as follows: it is an uncertain warning. Meaning, had it been known that his fellow would not act, this would have been a false or vain oath and he would receive lashes. Afterward, however, it becomes a prohibition involving no action, for which lashes are not administered. This is also how the text should read in chapter 5, halachah 2. Thus, when he is warned at the time of the oath, the warning is uncertain, and afterward the violation is a prohibition involving no action, for which there are no lashes, as Rambam writes in the Laws of Sanhedrin. The wording there became corrupted. The abbreviation meaning “a prohibition involving no action” was likewise corrupted, and the text was changed to “that is written explicitly in the Torah,” which is an obvious error. Lechem Mishneh's explanation is exceedingly forced. This is also the meaning of “and there is also no,” etc.: although he does not receive lashes and it remains possible that the condition will be fulfilled, nevertheless it may not be fulfilled, and the Torah prohibition still exists.
Unless, etc. There, 29b.
Or, etc. There, 36a.
And even though, etc. This follows his approach, according to which he does not transgress at that point.
And all the more so, etc. This is in order that he not transgress, and he is therefore commanded by Torah law, as explained above.
And some say, etc. This follows from what is stated there, 25a, “that I will give,” etc., where his intent is conditional upon that person accepting it. A proof is brought from the end of chapter 8 of Nedarim: one who says to his fellow, etc., “come and take,” etc. The Gemara does not establish the case as one who said, “if I do not give,” etc., because in such a case he need not say anything at all. Rather, it says, etc., for he is permitted even without this, since the other person does not wish to accept. See Tosafot to Gittin 30a, s.v. Mi, and the words of Rabbeinu Yitzchak there. Although the Gemara there, 74b, states, “and Rabbi said concerning his remedy,” etc., that case is different, because even if he said, “it is forgiven to you,” it would not help. This is unlike vows, as explained there, because his intention was merely to cause the other person distress. See Ran and Rivash there.
