That it is somewhat exaggerated, etc. These words are incomprehensible. If the statement is not completely true, how may he swear in order to strengthen it? The Levush writes that since people ordinarily strengthen their statements with slight exaggeration, this too is allowed. That is also imprecise, for how may one swear to something untrue? The source is the Mordechai in the chapter “Two types of oaths.” The Yerushalmi states that one who swears that two are two receives lashes for a vain oath. The Rashbam asks that the Mishnah speaks specifically of an oath concerning something untrue, whereas an oath concerning truth is not vain. He answers that where a statement seems somewhat exaggerated and he swears to strengthen it, it is not a vain oath. But if he swears that two are two, affirming what requires no affirmation, it is vain. Thus, the Mordechai intended to permit only an oath concerning something actually true, but which appears exaggerated to people and is not believed. The oath is needed to establish the truth. All the more so, if the matter appears highly exaggerated but is true, he may swear, for the oath is not purposeless. Only when he swears to something plainly known by everyone does the oath become vain. If this was the Rama’s intent, there was no need to write it, for the Mechaber already ruled in seif 4 concerning something known to everyone, which includes the Mordechai’s rule. Nor should the Rama have written it in this seif, which concerns matters regarded by the world as false. Therefore, no leniency may be learned from this gloss to swear based upon a private terminology that differs from ordinary speech. The Rif explicitly concludes in the second chapter of Shevuos that we do not follow a person’s private understanding when he calls an object by an unusual name. Since everyone does not call it a camel, his view is nullified to the common usage, and it is a vain oath. His private intention helps only when everyone, or at least the majority, calls the object by that name. If only a minority does, his usage is likewise nullified. Only in monetary law, at the beginning of Bava Kamma, do we say that although the majority calls one vessel by one name, we do not follow the majority because monetary law does not follow a majority. A severe oath is different. This appears obvious. Anyone who derives leniency from this gloss has no foundation. I suspect the Rama saw only the Mordechai’s abbreviated index and not the Mordechai’s full words.

Specifically regarding a positive commandment. This means a case where there is a positive commandment to perform an act, and he swore inclusively not to perform that act together with other acts. If, however, he swore to transgress a negative commandment and included it with other acts he would perform, the inclusion is ineffective.
