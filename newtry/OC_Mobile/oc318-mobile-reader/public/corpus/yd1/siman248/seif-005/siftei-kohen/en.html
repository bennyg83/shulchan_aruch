He certainly approves, etc. At first glance this appears to follow the reasoning above, that a husband ordinarily does not object to his wife's conduct in this matter. Yet in Choshen Mishpat, siman 81, seif 7, the Rama writes that if someone contracted with another person to teach the latter's friend's son in the presence of the boy's father, and the father remained silent, his silence is deemed an admission, even though the father said nothing, since he should have considered the matter and protested. This is from the Mordechai, chapter Zeh Borer. If so, the present ruling should seemingly apply not only to his wife but to any other person. One cannot distinguish by saying that there the arrangement was made only after stating that the father would pay, for in that case the ruling would be obvious without the reasoning that he should have considered the matter, since it would plainly constitute an admission. It therefore appears that specifically there, where the teacher was hired in the father's presence, we say that the father should have realized that the teacher intended him to pay for his son. He is therefore obligated because silence is deemed an admission. But if the teacher was not hired in his presence, although he knew of the hiring, we do not assume that he approved and undertook to pay. Here the novelty is that regarding his wife, even though she did not hire him in her husband's presence, once he knew and remained silent, we assume that he approved her action. This is logical.
Immediately. It appears that this means immediately upon hearing of it, even if he heard only several days after she hired the teacher. If he remained silent when he heard, even after several days, he becomes retroactively obligated also for what the teacher had already taught, for it is revealed retroactively that he approved her action. Even if she conducts business, etc. Even if he allowed her to conduct business, she is no more than an ordinary guardian, and he may remove her from that authority whenever he wishes. Ibid.
