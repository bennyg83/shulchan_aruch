Without their knowledge and consent. Rivash writes that if one vowed with the consent of the community, even if the majority agree to release him, their view is disregarded until everyone agrees. He explicitly said 'the community,' including adults and minors, indicating that he did not rely merely upon the majority. If the petitioner now says that he intended only the majority, a Torah scholar is believed, as where one vowed 'by cherem' and later said he meant a fisherman's net. Although above in siman 208 we say that nowadays everyone is treated as an unlearned person, that applies to requiring formal release. Here we would be saying that even release is insufficient, and we do not impose so severe a stringency. If, however, he expressly named several individuals and vowed according to the wishes of each, he certainly meant that all must agree. His later claim that he intended only the majority is disregarded.
And some are stringent, etc. They reason that the various people's regrets are not identical, as one regrets for one reason and another for a different reason. For a mitzvah, however, all agree and share one regret. According to this, if they all express the same regret, release is possible. Likewise, one who accepted a ban or excommunication with public consent can be released with that public's consent according to all opinions, because a ban requires neither an opening nor regret, as above in seif 8 and below in seif 25. Ramban and Mechaber in seif 30 also write this. It further appears that all agree that when the public themselves come and express regret, even for different reasons, each person may be released according to his own regret. The stringent authorities mean only that one may not release everyone automatically through a single presumed regret. This differs from Beit Yosef's implication. Responsa Maimoniyot at the end of Sefer Hafla'ah, however, implies like Beit Yosef and doubts whether any vow made with public consent may be released for a discretionary matter. Responsa Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrachi, siman 50, is likewise stringent even when the public agrees.
And it is proper to be stringent, etc. Nevertheless, a beit din that released such a vow should not be disciplined, because most great authorities permit it and their reasoning appears primary. Bach.
Unless there is a mitzvah, etc. In that case they may release him even without the public's consent, because the public presumably desires the release.
So that his fellow will come to pray. This applies even if there is no minyan there. Ibid.
Likewise, to make peace, etc. The same applies to ending any other dispute, because peace is great. One may not derive from this a leniency for another matter that is not such a significant mitzvah. Beit Yosef in the name of Responsa Rashba.
It is considered a mitzvah matter. Beit Yosef cites Rivash that removing the danger of oaths and injury from a public thoroughfare is among the greatest mitzvah needs. Responsa Menahem Azariah, siman 48, writes that one who swore not to accept public office may be released for a mitzvah if no one in the country is more suitable. Beit Yosef also writes that avoiding separation from one's wife and remaining in the city where she lives is a mitzvah matter. This is obvious. See below, seifim katanim 95 and 104.
One who swore not to benefit from his father, etc. Darchei Moshe infers that even where one swore directly against performing a mitzvah, he may be released, unlike Responsa Ramban, siman 1030. This is also the implication of the authorities and of Gittin 36a concerning the schoolteacher whom Rav Acha caused to vow. This differs from a vow made for another's benefit, where some say that even for a mitzvah the vow cannot be released without the other's consent. A vow made with public consent is different, because the public is presumed to favor release for any mitzvah.
But if he said merely, etc. Ran, Rashba, and Rivash hold that even an unspecified statement, 'with the consent of the public,' cannot be released. It appears proper to be stringent initially.
And some say that if he vowed, etc. Maharik, shoresh 52, writes that if the three named people have a direct connection to the vow, all agree it is considered a vow made with public consent. Presumably he referred to those present who were involved in the matter. Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe cite this. If he said to them, 'I vow with your consent,' it is certainly considered public consent according to all opinions, and is stronger than merely saying, 'with the consent of so-and-so, so-and-so, and so-and-so.'
Four people who swore together, etc. Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 69, writes that if they swore with the consent of each individual among them, this is not called public consent. Had each sworn with the consent of all the others, it would appear to be public consent, but they said only that all swore with the consent of each one. This distinction is not compelling.
Except in pressing circumstances, etc. Rama based this on Beit Yosef's citation of Maharik, shoresh 52, who found in Rabbi Avigdor's name that a vow made with public consent cannot be released initially, but if three already released it, the release is valid. Although this differs from most authorities, it may serve as support for the lenient opinions. Beit Yosef also cites Rashba that if such a vow was released, the release is valid after the fact, and although some great decisors disagree, Rabbeinu Tam may be relied upon in pressing circumstances. It appears, however, that Rashba cannot refer to an explicitly identified public. Rabbeinu Tam nowhere says that release works after the fact in that case. On the contrary, all authorities, and Rashba himself in HaSholeach, explain that the Gemara's concern that a sage might release a vow applies after the fact, while initially release without the beneficiary is forbidden. The Gemara then answers that the vow is imposed with public consent, proving that even after the fact release is ineffective according to Rabbeinu Tam. All authorities rule this way. Maharam Padua, responsum 68, also disputes Rabbi Avigdor and correctly notes that 'it has no release' implies even after the fact. The Gemara in HaSholeach and at the end of Mumim likewise proves this, because otherwise the original concern that he may go to a sage or three laymen would remain. Therefore, no leniency should be granted even in pressing circumstances. Rashba's responsum instead concerns one who said only, 'with public consent,' without identifying the people. In that case, Rabbeinu Tam holds that release is effective after the fact. Beit Yosef earlier cites Rashba that although we do not follow Rabbeinu Tam initially, after the fact his view may be relied upon in pressing circumstances. This is clear.
It is not considered public consent. Only when one says, 'with your consent,' or names specific people and thereby incorporates their decision into the essence of the vow, is it called public consent. The present case is different. Beit Yosef cites Rivash that a document stating that an obligation was accepted with public consent has the same law as public consent in the Gemara, but this is not compelling. Darchei Moshe cites him. Initially, however, one should be stringent and not release it, because some authorities hold that even an unspecified statement of public consent has no release, as stated above.
