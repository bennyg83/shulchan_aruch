But one does not open with the honor of Heaven, etc. The Tur writes that one should not say, “Had you known that you were committing a transgression through your vows, would you have vowed?” The Beit Yosef asked why this differs from the opening clause concerning violation of “You shall not take revenge,” and therefore altered the wording here to, “that you were treating the honor of the King lightly.” This correction is unnecessary. The Tur’s words are correct. There is a distinction between common transgressions, such as refusing to lend one’s utensils, in which most people stumble. A person is therefore not ashamed to say that he would still have vowed if he did not truly regret it. Regarding a less common transgression, however, he may be ashamed to admit that he would nevertheless have vowed. See what I write in the following seif.
For a person is not so brazen, etc. It appears to me that since the concern is that this is not truly in his heart, this opening is ineffective when others propose it to him, for he is ashamed to answer truthfully. If, however, a person comes on his own and says, “Please release my vow, because I have now learned that making a vow, or failing to release it, involves a transgression and is like building a private altar,” this is a valid opening. There is no concern that he is too embarrassed to say that he would not have refrained, since he could have remained silent altogether. The wording of the Gemara and poskim is precise: “We do not open with the honor of Heaven,” meaning that we do not propose it, but he may raise it himself. The same applies to the ruling in seif 12 that we open only with a foreseeable occurrence. The Rambam writes explicitly in chapter 6 of Hilchos Shevuos: “We do not open with an unforeseen development,” but concludes, “If he himself regretted because of the new development and changed his mind, they may release him.” The Rama likewise writes in seif 13, “But if he regrets from the outset,” etc. This is the same principle.
