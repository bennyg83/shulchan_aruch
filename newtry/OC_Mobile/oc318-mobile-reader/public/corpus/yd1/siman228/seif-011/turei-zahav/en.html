For example, if he forbade them from his property, etc. The Gemara explains that since he was brazen once, he will be brazen again. Since he already had the audacity to forbid his father from his property, if he does not truly regret it he will also have the audacity at the time of release to say that he would not have refrained because of his father’s honor. Therefore, if he says that he regrets, we may assume the regret is genuine. The reason resembles what I wrote in seif 10. Concerning a common transgression, we do not say that he is ashamed to admit that he would have vowed despite knowing the prohibition. Here, although casting off the obligation to support one’s parents is uncommon, even without an explicit Torah command reason itself dictates honoring one’s father, as the nations do, and everyone regards failure to do so as sinful. Since this person already broke through the universal standard of conduct, he will not now be ashamed to say that even had he known he was obligated to support them he would have vowed. If he says otherwise, he truly regrets it. My revered father-in-law distinguished that “You shall not take revenge” and honoring parents are explicit in the Torah, and since he had the audacity to transgress an explicit Torah command he will also now speak without shame. His words are difficult, for if he already knew the prohibitions, what opening exists in saying, “Had you known,” etc.?
