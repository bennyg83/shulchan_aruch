He must specify to them the vow and its cause. Rashi explains that a widow may have vowed to the orphans, “All fruits in the world shall be forbidden to me if I derived any benefit from my kesubah.” If she later comes merely to release her prohibition of fruits and omits the condition, a stumbling block may result. If she states the entire vow, the sage will not release it. From this we learn that one must state before the sage every element of the vow, for perhaps if he states it fully the sage will refuse to release it. The Gemara also gives the reason of a matter involving prohibition, meaning that he may have forbidden all fruits upon himself if he transgresses a habitual sin. If he omits that condition when seeking release and mentions only the fruit prohibition, a stumbling block may result. The Yerushalmi relates that a man came before R’ Yudan and said, “Release my vow.” He asked, “What did you vow?” The man said, “I vowed that I would not profit.” R’ Yudan asked, “Do people act that way?” He answered, “I meant that I would not gamble.” R’ Yudan said, “Blessed is He Who chose the words of the Sages, who said that one must specify the vow, for had you not specified it I would have released it.” The Beit Yosef inferred that it is insufficient to state the words of the vow; one must also explain why he made it. The Rashba likewise wrote that he must specify the cause. It is clear that two matters require caution. First, he may not omit a condition from the vow. Second, even where there was no condition, he may not substitute different wording, as did the gambler who used a cleaner expression so the sage would not understand the matter. This is the Shulchan Aruch’s intent. “Specify the vow” means not to change its language. “And the cause” means not to omit what he stated when vowing, such as, “if I commit such-and-such a transgression.” If he states the vow exactly as made, without alteration or omission, neither the Gemara nor the poskim indicate that the sage must investigate what private reason motivated him. On the contrary, the Yerushalmi proves otherwise. Had R’ Yudan not found the expression strange, he would not have asked further. Tosafos in Arachin 23b states explicitly that one need specify only the language of the vow, but not why he vowed. The Perishah was astonished that even careful rabbis do not investigate the cause and suggested distinctions. According to what I have written, they were never required to ask about an unexpressed reason. They need only ensure that the condition was not shortened and that the wording was not altered. If he states the vow exactly, there is no need to ask what was in his heart.
