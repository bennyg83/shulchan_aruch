With the permission of one of them. This is from a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef. The Rashba derives it from the chapter “One who borrows,” 94b, according to R’ Yonasan, whose opinion we follow. The verse, “He cursed his father and his mother,” implies both together and also each one separately, unless the Torah explicitly states “together,” as it did concerning an ox and donkey. So too, when he was placed under oath, the intent was that he not lend except with the permission of both or of either one. This is difficult. Since the wording bears two meanings, how do we know that the person administering the oath intended the lenient meaning, that permission of one suffices? We rule in siman 208 that unspecified vows are interpreted stringently. All the more so should we follow the simpler meaning, both together. Rashi likewise explains on the verse, “And Hashem struck every firstborn,” that the conjunction is additive, as when one says, “so-and-so and so-and-so.” The simple implication is conjunction. We also find that the Tur and other poskim write regarding a vow made according to many, “according to so-and-so and so-and-so.” Why do we not say that it means either one and is not according to many? Mahari’s writings, siman 73, imply that where one says “or so-and-so” and then uses collective language, both are required. Where no collective language is used, the obligor might still claim that he bound himself only to what both would say, but not that “and” automatically means “or.” Kiddushin likewise teaches, “Be betrothed to me with this and this and this,” and we do not say that it means either item. It therefore appears that the Rashba did not permit lending with the consent of one merely because the wording can mean “or.” Rather, he teaches that this is at least a possible meaning, with a practical consequence if one of them dies. Then permission of the survivor suffices, because the father never intended the prohibition to last forever if one died. A similar rationale appears in a responsum of the Ramban, siman 249, cited by the Rama in seif 20, that when the person according to whose understanding the vow was made dies, his successors stand in his place if the matter was for their benefit, because the one who vowed did not intend eternal prohibition. Here, if there is no benefit to them, the deceased’s successors are irrelevant. The Rama correctly placed this ruling in siman 216, seif 7, where he wrote that one of them died. Although he explained that whenever “so-and-so and so-and-so” is stated it can mean either one, he means only that such an interpretation is possible. Therefore, after one dies, we may interpret it as “or.” This is the intent of the Rama’s cross-reference here.
