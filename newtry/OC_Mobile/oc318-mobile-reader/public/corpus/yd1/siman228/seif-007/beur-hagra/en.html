Nevertheless, etc. The vow must be uprooted retroactively, as stated in Ketubot 74b: “What is the difference between a sage,” etc. This is also why Rabban Gamliel himself labored to find an opening for Rav Sechora, and follows from the expression, “Is your heart still upon you as it was then?” See the Rosh there.
Some say that they return, etc.; and some say, etc. See Mordechai at the end of chapter 3 of Shevuot, who discusses this at length. In brief, Maharam writes that Nedarim chapter 3 concludes that one may open with regret, and the entire discussion there implies that simple regret suffices, as in “Do you still have the same resolve?” This is unlike Rashi in chapter HaDar, who requires an opening based on a circumstance. Likewise, the passage says that one who presents an opening says, “Had you known,” etc. Tosafot, Eruvin 64b, s.v. “they open,” cite three forms of regret: a wholly new circumstance, with which one may not open; a circumstance already existing but unknown, with which all agree one may open; and a circumstance not yet present but regularly anticipated, concerning which there is a dispute. The question, “Is your heart still upon you?” after an opening was found, teaches the court to caution the petitioner not to lie. The language implies that the circumstance arose only after the vow.
