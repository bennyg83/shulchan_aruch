And there are those who say that they return and seek release, etc. This is the opinion of Rabbeinu Yosef Bechor Shor cited by the Mordechai. I do not know why this was brought as a separate opinion, for the Shulchan Aruch itself already cited it in the words, “And there is one who requires,” etc.
An opening and also regret. The Beit Yosef cites from the Mordechai in the name of Rabbeinu Yosef Bechor Shor regarding the incident in the fourth chapter of Nedarim where a person came before R’ Assi, who asked him, “Do you now regret it?” He answered, “No,” according to one explanation as a question, and R’ Assi released him. Some great authorities understood that R’ Assi asked only this and therefore practiced that when releasing a vow one need ask only, “Do you regret it?” and when he answers yes they release him. The Mordechai objects that R’ Assi holds that one may not open with regret. It therefore explains that the person had already stated the opening and reason for his regret, and only afterward R’ Assi asked, “Do you truly regret it?” meaning, be careful not to lie. The Mordechai therefore writes that regret alone is insufficient and one must investigate the reason for the regret. It cites Maharam that although his teachers practiced release through regret alone, and he initially followed them, he later retracted and practiced like Rabbeinu Yosef, never releasing a vow without both an opening and regret. Since Maharam was stringent, who may be lenient? The Rama’s statement that the custom is to be stringent by turning the regret into an opening is correct, because otherwise perhaps he regrets only now but did not regret from the outset. An opening is effective when he says that had he known this matter he would never have vowed. Nevertheless, this does not fully satisfy Rabbeinu Yosef’s stringency, which requires knowing the reason for his change of heart. Whoever seeks to act in the best manner should do so.
