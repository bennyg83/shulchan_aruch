But excommunication, etc. Responsa Maharam Alashkar, siman 49, writes that if they decreed under ban that it should apply even to one who changes residence and lives in another city, the ban also binds later generations. It may further be that even without explicit wording it binds them, because this differs from the rule that one who does not intend to return need not observe the stringencies of the place he left. Here the ban rests personally upon those subject to it. See above, siman 214, seif katan 6, and below, seif 29.
For the ban does not, etc. Responsa Rosh, klal 108, rules that if a community enacted an ordinance or ban requiring a prohibited act, such as lending to them with interest, the ordinance and ban are void. Beit Yosef cites this below in siman 239.
And some are stringent, etc. Rama writes this because Darchei Moshe cites another Responsa Rosh stating that a community had declared every document not written by the city scribe invalid, and later enacted that such documents should be valid. The second enactment stands, although they acted improperly by enacting it before releasing the first ban. According to Rama, Rosh's responsa would contradict one another. It appears, however, that the second responsum concerns documents written by others after the second enactment but before formal release of the first ban. It teaches that the documents were not invalid, because by law any handwriting is valid, and only the first enactment sought to disqualify them. Once the first ban is later released, the second enactment takes effect retroactively. Rosh writes that a sage uproots a vow from its inception, so when the ban is released it is as though it never existed and the second enactment stands. This requires reconciliation with the rule above in seif 26 that communal bans are released only prospectively. Perhaps the case concerned release by a sage. If the first ban was never released, the second enactment certainly has no force, as Rosh writes elsewhere. He also writes that if they agreed to release the first ban, the ban is nullified and the sale is valid, but if they did not agree, the sale is not valid. This proves that absent release the second act has no force. Responsa Rabbi Aharon Sasson, siman 210, similarly suggests that the second enactment may indeed be void, yet the document need not be invalid because it is intrinsically valid. He leaves the matter requiring further consideration, and may have intended this same explanation.
