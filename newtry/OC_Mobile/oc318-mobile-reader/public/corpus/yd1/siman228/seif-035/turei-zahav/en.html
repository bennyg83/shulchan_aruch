But excommunication and cherem, etc. The reason appears to be that an oath binds the person himself regarding objects, and he cannot bind another person regarding any object. Excommunication and cherem, however, are a curse upon anyone who performs a specified act, and no person may place himself within that curse.
And there are those who are stringent. The Rama wrote this based on a responsum of the Rosh, klal 9, siman 4, concerning a community that enacted under cherem that every document must be written only by the official scribe. They later enacted under cherem that two appointed men should write each document and the scribe should write only the final line. The Rosh wrote that a community that enacted a cherem and later wishes to enact a different regulation may do so, provided it first releases the earlier cherem. If it did not mention release, it acted improperly and placed a stumbling block before the community. Nevertheless, documents written under the second enactment are not invalid, and the second enactment stands. This does not resemble Rava’s rule that whenever the Torah says not to do something, if one did it the act is ineffective. That rule concerns mitzvos that cannot be abolished. Here, where the enactment can be abolished, Rava agrees that the act is effective. The Rama understood that the Rosh was stringent and upheld the second cherem, and therefore wrote here, “There are those who are stringent.” In truth, this is very difficult. The second cherem did not take effect at all, as the Rosh himself wrote in that klal, ruling 6. The Rosh referred only to documents made under the second enactment and said that the enactment itself stands, but not its cherem. They had authority to alter the rule, and although they acted in violation of the first cherem, their act remains effective. It resembles one who swore not to sell and then sold, where the sale is valid, as written in Choshen Mishpat, siman 208, unlike the Rama’s ruling later in siman 230. However, a responsum of the Rosh, klal 5, ruling 4, concerns a community that enacted under cherem that no synagogue seat could be sold except with the owner’s consent, not by beit din for a debt. Later they enacted under cherem that beit din could sell seats of anyone who failed to pay an assessment. Many seats were sold under the second enactment, and objections were later raised based on the first. The Rosh wrote that if the second enactment released the first cherem, the sales stand, but if it did not, the sales are void. We must say that when the Rosh wrote the responsum in klal 5 he held that the sale was void because of the rule in Temurah, as the Hagahos Mordechai cited by the Rama in siman 230 likewise holds. In klal 9, however, he retracted and held that this case differs from Rava’s rule, as he wrote explicitly. In Choshen Mishpat, siman 208, I wrote what appears primary. See there. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 163, for many laws of communal enactments.
