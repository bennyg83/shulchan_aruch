In an uncommon matter. This is what our Sages call an uncommon unforeseen development. An occurrence that is common may be used as an opening and uproots the vow as one made in error. Nevertheless, it is not literally a mistaken vow like those in siman 232, which require no release at all. There, at the time of the act itself he was mistaken about a particular fact. Here, the later event merely reveals what he would have done. So wrote the Beit Yosef in the name of a responsum of the Ramban.
And that person became a Torah scholar. Tosafos in the chapter “Even though” asked how R’ Akiva released Ben Kalba Savua’s vow by asking, “Had you known he would become a great man, would you have vowed?” We do not open with an unforeseen development. Tosafos answered that since R’ Akiva had gone to the study hall, and it is common for one who goes to study to become great, it was foreseeable. This answer is strained, for the sugya appears to imply that Ben Kalba Savua did not know when he vowed that R’ Akiva had gone to study. The Semag and the responsum of the Ramban cited by the Beit Yosef offer another answer. “We do not open with an unforeseen development” means only that others may not propose it as an opening. If he himself regrets from the outset because of the later development, the vow may be released. The Rama cites this in seif 13. There was therefore no difficulty from R’ Akiva, for Ben Kalba Savua completely regretted the original vow once R’ Akiva became a Torah scholar and explicitly said that he regretted it from the outset. The prohibition applies only when others tell him of the new circumstance and he merely says, “I did not vow with that in mind,” without explicitly saying that he now regrets the original vow. In such a case we say that he regrets only from the time of the new event onward. If, however, after hearing it from others he explicitly says that he now regrets having made the vow initially, it is effective. This is also clear from the Rambam cited by the Beit Yosef. Although the Beit Yosef thought Rabbeinu Yosef Bechor Shor disagreed, the Rama correctly ruled like the many great authorities. In my view, Bechor Shor also agrees when he explicitly uproots the vow from its origin and regrets it on his own.
Death is uncommon. This is so even for a sick person, since most sick people recover. Beit Yosef in the name of a responsum of the Ramban.
