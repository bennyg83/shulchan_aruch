There is one who, etc. See below, siman 334, seif 32.
Who vowed during distress, etc. If one vowed to fast and the fast causes neglect of Torah study, the vow may be released, because Torah study is equal to all the mitzvot and one may not fast in a manner that causes its neglect. Likewise, a vow preventing him from slaughtering, inspecting, or issuing halachic rulings may be released when no other qualified person is available, because each of these is a mitzvah. Bach cites this from Responsa Rosh. It appears that even a vow made with public consent may be released for these purposes, because they are mitzvah matters. If fasting does not cause him to neglect study, however, he obviously cannot be released. Bedek HaBayit cites Rashba that this is a mitzvah upon which all agree, because one who afflicts himself may become unable to maintain Torah study and sound reasoning. Everything depends upon the person's nature. Some eat bread with salt and are content, while others suffer unless they sit by a pot of meat. Each should act according to his own constitution. See above, seif katan 95.
Or for a great need, etc. This means where there is coercion or the town's sages determine that the vow cannot be maintained. Responsa Binyamin Ze'ev states this. Rama, at the end of responsum 103, likewise writes that a vow made during distress ordinarily has no release because it is like a vow made with Hashem's consent, but he agrees to release it for a mitzvah or to prevent a stumbling block. Otherwise it may not be released, as explained above in seif 21 regarding public consent.
Nevertheless, if they released it, etc. Rama follows his position above in seif 21. It was already explained there that a vow made with public consent has no release even after the fact, and the same should apply here. Rama states here without qualification that a vow made during distress is permitted after the fact, while above he allowed reliance on such release only in pressing circumstances and great need. His basis in Darchei Moshe was Responsa Ramban, siman 253, concerning a sick person who vowed not to eat cheese because it harmed his illness and later regretted the vow. Ramban writes that ordinary regret cannot release him, because at the time of the vow the prohibition was genuinely necessary. He may nevertheless be released through another opening. Darchei Moshe inferred that a vow made during distress can be released. This comparison is surprising. A true vow made during distress resembles Yaakov's vow, 'If God will be with me and guard me on this road,' or one who vowed while imprisoned that if freed he would travel to Eretz Yisrael, or one in danger at sea who vowed to ascend to Jerusalem. A vow not to eat harmful cheese during illness is different. Responsa Binyamin Ze'ev explains that a vow made during distress cannot be released because one who vows for the benefit of another who helped him cannot be released without that person's consent. All the more so where Hashem answered him in distress, for no greater benefit exists, and who can say that Hashem consents to the release. This reasoning does not apply to avoiding cheese during illness. Therefore, the cheese vow and similar vows may be released initially, while a true vow made during distress remains binding even after an attempted release. Responsa Maharam Mintz, however, holds that a vow made during distress may be released and supports this from Agudah and other authorities.
