His fellow. Maharik wrote that the same applies if he swore before his agent: they do not release him except with the sender's knowledge or with the agent's own knowledge, for a person's agent is like himself. From the fact that one must notify him, it implies that even where there is no suspicion of shame, it is forbidden. So too it appears from the Rosh, who wrote: according to the Yerushalmi's reasoning, even at the outset they release the vow outside his presence, provided that they notify him of the release; but the reasoning of our Talmud is primary, and one should release only in his presence, meaning with his knowledge; end quote, Shach. The Taz explained "upon his fellow's mind" as meaning that his fellow upheld the vow and he wants to release it; the Shach explained it as meaning for the sake of his fellow's desire.
And his will. The Shach wrote: it appears that the Mechaber's view is also so, that we require that he inform him and that he agree willingly; and so wrote the Taz.
A benefit. The view of most poskim is that we specifically require a benefit. But if he forced him and he vowed according to his mind, whether because of love or fear, they may release it without his knowledge. The Rashbatz wrote that a worker may retract within his term even though he accepted upon himself with cherem and niddui to do the work for that whole time; he may seek release from his acceptance without the employer's consent. The Beit Yosef wrote about him that his words are not correct, for the employer does him a benefit by giving him work from which to earn his livelihood. The Shach agrees with the Rashbatz and rejected the Beit Yosef's words, because the benefit the employer gives him is that he will do the work for him, whereas we require specifically a benefit that comes because of the oath, as the Rivash wrote. However, Responsa R. Moshe Alashkar implies that in such a case it is called a benefit, and this requires study; end quote.
For the benefit. The Shach wrote: I do not know what he is saying. If it is to exclude where the oath is not for his fellow's benefit, such that they may release it, this is not so, for all the poskim hold that with a benefit, even though the oath is not for his fellow's benefit, one may not release it. Perhaps he wrote it because of the "some say" in the latter clause, for only where there is benefit to his fellow does suspicion apply; otherwise suspicion does not apply, for he will say, "because I have no benefit in it, he did not inform me." The Rivash wrote that whenever his fellow did not impose the vow upon him, even though he swore for his fellow's benefit, according to everyone his fellow's knowledge is not required. In Darkei Moshe he wrote about this that this is not the view of the poskim. But it appears to me that everyone agrees in this: the poskim speak only where he received a benefit, and the Rivash explicitly speaks where he did not receive a benefit; end quote.
His fellow. The Levush wrote: meaning that he made his vow dependent upon his fellow's mind, but his fellow did not impose it upon him; although he did him a benefit, they may release it without his knowledge. But it does not appear so to me. "Of his own accord" means without benefit, even though his fellow imposed it upon him. As a matter of law, it appears from the Darkei Moshe above that in such a case they may release it without his knowledge; end quote, Shach.
Who released. It implies that with a vow upon his fellow's mind, even when his fellow agrees to its release, release by a sage is required. Shach. It is explained in the Beit Yosef in the name of the Rivash that if one swore to many people and it is now revealed that they have no benefit from it, but only some individuals do, and he did not intend them, it is not called receiving a benefit.
That he specified. Meaning that if he vowed for his fellow's benefit, he must specify it at the time of release, as written in seif 14, that he must specify the cause for which he vowed.
To discipline him. However, if he did him no benefit, although one should not release it at the outset, nevertheless if they transgressed and released it, one should not discipline them. So wrote the Bach. The Beit Yosef wrote: nevertheless, all this is regarding the law, but where it is possible to come to desecration of the Name, one must forbid it, for there is nothing more severe than that, as we find regarding Tzidkiyahu, who was punished along with the Sanhedrin who released him, even though there was a mitzvah in that release.
A matter. For example, he vowed or swore to give him a gift, or to pay by a certain time. Maharik.
At the outset. The Shach wrote (the reason is that since his fellow benefits from his vow, agreed to it, and administered the oath); but where his fellow has no benefit at all from this person's vow and has no involvement in it, in such a case even at the outset he may seek release from it according to everyone, provided that he informs him that he sought release from his oath because of suspicion.
Forever. Whenever the vow is for his fellow's benefit, they do not release it at the outset without his knowledge and consent; and if it is not for his benefit, but he vowed before him, he must inform him because of suspicion. So it appears from the Rivash; end quote, Shach. (If one swore to many people and it is now revealed that they have no benefit from it, but only some individuals do, and he did not intend them, it is not called receiving a benefit. Turei Zahav. It is written in Sefer Mekor Baruch siman 20 that the rule that one who vows according to his fellow's mind may be released only with his fellow's knowledge and consent applies specifically where at the time of release it is still for his fellow's benefit. But if at the time of release there is no benefit at all to his fellow, even though when he swore it was for his fellow's benefit, they release him without his knowledge; see there.)
To release. The Shach wrote: it implies that even if he did him a benefit, they may release it this way, and this requires study. From where does he know this? In Responsa Ramban it says only that there is no suspicion because he died, and that is where he did him no benefit; but if he did him a benefit, logic says that if he died they cannot release it for him. So too is proven, etc., and this requires study; see there.
A mitzvah. The Taz disagrees with this and wrote that one should not rely on this leniency. Perhaps Rama also intended only that this be made a supporting factor where there is otherwise a basis for release, even though there is some hesitation in it; end quote. (See there in Shach, Kenesset HaGedolah, and Mahari ben Lev I, klal 5.)
And they swore. In Responsa Maharam Padua he wrote that this is specifically where both swore; but if one did not swear, it is not called a benefit merely by appeasement. He further wrote that it appears one should distinguish: specifically where the man agrees to take a woman, it is called a benefit for the woman; but where the woman agreed, it is not called a benefit, for a woman is content with anything, for "better to sit," etc. Maharik's view does not appear so, and the primary view is that one should not distinguish in this; so too is the master's view; end quote. See Shach.
To divorce. As written in Even HaEzer siman 77. Even according to the one who disagrees there, that is regarding a married woman, unlike here. Mahari Weil.
