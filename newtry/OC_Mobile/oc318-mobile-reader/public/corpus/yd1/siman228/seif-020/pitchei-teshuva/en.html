His intention was in exchange for some favor. Chatam Sofer explains that even Maharam Alashkar permits annulment without the benefactor's consent where the person retracts before beginning the employment or tenancy and has received no benefit. He applies this to a rabbi pressured into accepting a post, combining the absence of benefit, mitzvah considerations, and a credible claim of aversion, though he advises seeking the community's willing consent to avoid public scandal.
The oath was for Jethro's benefit. Shach holds that receiving a favor prevents annulment without the benefactor's consent even if the oath itself did not benefit him. Zera Avraham, Maharit, Rashba, Ran, and Shaar HaMelech support the opposite: where maintaining the oath provides the other party no benefit, prior favor alone does not bar annulment.

For a mitzvah need. Chatam Sofer suggests that a formal handclasp oath may be too severe for annulment even for a mitzvah. Noda BiYehudah limits the stringent view to where annulment harms the other party, as Moses' departure harmed Jethro; without such harm, all agree that a mitzvah need permits annulment.
To marry one another. Chatam Sofer discusses a young man and woman who swore to marry each other. The father cannot be compelled to provide a dowry, especially where the relationship arose improperly, but the pair must ordinarily keep their oath even without financial support. If one refuses to marry in the other's city or delays beyond the normal preparation period, the other may be released. Mutual waiver followed by annulment before three is the clearest solution.
