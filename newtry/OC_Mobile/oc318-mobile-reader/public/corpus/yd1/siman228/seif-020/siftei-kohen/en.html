He vowed with his fellow's consent, etc. Meaning, because of his fellow's wishes, as explained. Maharik, shoresh 52, writes that the same applies when one swore before the other person's agent. He may be released only with the principal's consent or the agent's own consent, because a person's agent is like himself. Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe cite this.
They release him only if they informed him, etc. This implies that even where there is no concern of suspicion or embarrassment, informing him is required. Rosh in HaSholeach likewise writes that according to the Yerushalmi's reasons one may initially release the vow outside the other's presence, provided he informs him of the release. The Bavli's reason is primary, and 'only in his presence' means with his knowledge. The other authorities also imply this.
Only if they informed him, etc. It appears that the Mechaber also requires that the other person be informed and willingly agree.
Because of some benefit, etc. Mordechai writes in HaSholeach that this applies when the other person induced him to vow in accordance with his wishes, whether through affection or fear. Most authorities, however, require an actual benefit, and this appears to be Tur and Shulchan Aruch's view. Beit Yosef cites Tashbetz that a worker may withdraw during the term of employment even if he accepted a ban or excommunication to work for the entire period, and he may seek release without the employer's consent. Rabbeinu Tam's rule requiring release in the beneficiary's presence applies only where the oath followed a benefit received. Beit Yosef objects that an employer does benefit the worker by providing employment and livelihood. This objection is not decisive. An employer ordinarily provides that benefit in exchange for work, not as consideration for the oath, and the relevant benefit must be given because of the oath itself, as Rivash, siman 461, and Rama write. Responsa Maharam Alashkar, siman 57, however, implies that such a case is considered a benefit. He rules that where one rented a house to another for a term and swore not to evict him during that term, the rent paid is a benefit. The matter requires study.
Because of the oath. A benefit he received for some unrelated reason does not make him a beneficiary for this law. Otherwise, how could Rabbeinu Tam prove the rule from Yosef's oath to Yaakov, which Pharaoh told him to release, as stated in Sotah 36b. No one receives benefit as naturally as a son from his father. Rivash.
Like Moshe. Scripture says, 'Moshe consented to dwell with the man,' meaning that he vowed, in accordance with Yitro's wishes, not to return to Egypt.
And the oath was for the benefit, etc. The wording is difficult. If it means to exclude a case where the oath does not benefit his fellow, and to say that even after receiving a benefit he may then be released, this is incorrect. Rosh, Tur, and the other authorities in Rabbeinu Tam's name imply that whenever he received a benefit from the other person he may not be released without him. Rivash likewise implies this, though he cites an opinion that even without a benefit, if the oath was for the other's benefit, it may not be released. But where a benefit was received, no authority permits release merely because the oath itself was not for the other's benefit. Perhaps this phrase was written because of the following dissenting opinion. Suspicion applies specifically where the oath benefits the other person; otherwise the other can say, 'Since the matter did not concern me, he had no reason to inform me.' This is also indicated by the Gemara in HaSholeach and at the end of Mumim. Beit Yosef cites Rivash that when the other person did not impose the vow, even if the vow benefited him, all agree that his consent is unnecessary. Darchei Moshe says this follows Mordechai's view that the other must have compelled him. It appears, however, that all authorities agree with Rivash here. They discuss one who received a benefit, whereas Rivash explicitly discusses one who did not.
But if he independently vowed in accordance with his fellow's wishes. Ateret Zekenim explains that he made the vow dependent on his fellow's consent, though the fellow did not impose it, and therefore even if the fellow had previously benefited him, he may be released without his consent. This appears incorrect. 'Independently' means without having received a benefit, even if his fellow induced him to vow. This is evident from Tur and the authorities' discussion of the Gemara's question in Gittin 35 and Yosef's oath to Yaakov in Sotah 36b. As a practical matter, Darchei Moshe's words cited in the preceding seif katan imply that in such a case he may be released without the other's consent.
He independently vowed in accordance with his fellow's wishes. Meaning, he vowed because of his fellow's desire, and the vow is fully binding immediately. If he made the vow conditional upon his fellow's decision, it does not take effect until the fellow confirms it, as below in siman 231. See also seifim 37 and 38.
And some say, etc. Although the other's consent and agreement are unnecessary, he must nevertheless be informed because of suspicion, as stated in the Yerushalmi. The Yerushalmi gives another reason, embarrassment, meaning that the petitioner will be embarrassed before the person for whose benefit he vowed. Rivash writes that according to this reason, mere notice is insufficient; where the vow benefited the other, it must be released specifically in his presence. Beit Yosef cites him, and Ran in Nedarim 65 writes likewise. One might ask why Rama did not cite this stringent view after already citing stringency regarding initial practice. In truth, Rosh in HaSholeach, cited above in seif katan 36, holds that even according to the embarrassment rationale it is enough to inform the other. Responsa Ramban, siman 248, Responsa Rashba cited by Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe, another Responsa Rashba beginning with the case of Reuven who made his son Chanoch swear not to lend his money, and Semag, negative commandment 241, all imply the same. This appears to be Rama's view. Some authorities, however, hold that suspicion applies only where he vowed in the other's actual presence. Ran, responsum 69, agrees and discusses it at length. Beit Yosef cites him below. It also appears that where one vowed in accordance with another's wishes, even if the other agrees to the release, a formal release is still required. See below, seif 38.
And even if he vowed in accordance with another because the other had benefited him, etc., the release is valid. This is the strict legal rule. Where release may cause desecration of Hashem's Name, however, it must be prohibited, because nothing is more severe. Tzidkiyahu and the Sanhedrin who released him were punished, even though there was a mitzvah involved in that release. Beit Yosef.
And the sage knew that it was for a benefit, etc. The wording is imprecise. Rivash discusses a vow made for the benefit of another, whereas the Mechaber did not yet specify that circumstance. It appears that the meaning is that if he vowed for his fellow's benefit, he must disclose everything, as above in seif 14, where the cause of the vow must be stated. This is evident from Gittin 35b, where the opinion requiring specification requires the widow to explain that she vowed for the orphans' benefit, namely that she had not benefited from her ketubah.
Nevertheless, the beit din, etc. Most authorities hold that such a release is ineffective even after the fact. If the other person did not provide him a benefit, however, then although one should not initially release without him, as Rama writes, judges who did so should not be disciplined, because many authorities allow it even initially. Bach writes likewise.
But if he swore to his fellow to do a particular act for him. For example, he vowed or swore to give him a gift or to pay him by a fixed time. Maharik.
Although they do not initially release him. This is because his fellow benefits from the vow, agreed to it, and caused him to swear. If the fellow receives no benefit from the vow, such as where one prohibited the other's benefit or the reverse, so that the other neither agreed to nor has any interest in the vow, all agree that he may initially seek release. He must merely inform the other that he sought release, because of suspicion, according to Rashba there.
And some say that one does not, etc. Meaning, whenever the vow benefits his fellow, it may not initially be released without the fellow's knowledge and consent. If it does not benefit him but was made in his presence, the petitioner must inform him because of suspicion. This is implied by Rivash.
And if the person for whose benefit he vowed died, etc. This implies that release is possible even if that person had provided a benefit, and Ateret Zekenim states this explicitly. The source is difficult. Responsa Ramban says only that he may be released and there is no suspicion once the person has died. This implies a case without a prior benefit, where the sole concern was suspicion. If the person had provided a benefit, release required his knowledge and consent, and it is reasonable that after his death release is impossible. Rashba's novellae to HaSholeach, Rosh there and in Nedarim, Semag, and Rivash, siman 370, also prove this from Pharaoh's statement to Yosef, 'Release your oath that you swore to your father.' They infer that one who received no benefit may be released, implying that one who did receive a benefit may not, even after the benefactor's death. Although distinctions can be suggested to permit it, the matter requires study.
Some say that for a mitzvah need, etc. This appears to mean even when the one who swore received a benefit. Ateret Zekenim asks why Hashem then told Moshe to release his vow before Yitro, since Moshe needed to go to Egypt to redeem Israel and no mitzvah is greater. He overlooked Rivash, siman 187, the source of this law, who raises the same contradiction to Tosafot. Nevertheless, Tosafot can be defended. Maharik, shoresh 52, and Rama below regarding a vow made with public consent write that a matter counts as a mitzvah only when the mitzvah cannot be fulfilled without releasing the oath. If it can be fulfilled without canceling the mitzvah, release is not justified. In Moshe's case, Hashem had many means of relief and salvation, and Israel could have been redeemed without Moshe's return. Alternatively, Hashem's will was that Moshe not go to Egypt until he released the vow, so before release his going was not yet a mitzvah.
A man and woman who accepted a ban, etc. Maharam Padua, responsum 70, writes that this applies only if both swore. If only one swore, the other's mere agreement is not considered a benefit. He says this is implied by Rashba's responsum concerning Reuven, who arranged a match between his son Chanoch and Shimon's daughter, after which Chanoch swore. Maharik cites it in shoresh 52. Maharam Padua further suggests distinguishing between a man's agreement to marry, which benefits the woman, and a woman's agreement to marry, which may not be considered a benefit because a woman generally prefers marriage. Maharik's position does not support this distinction. The primary ruling is that no such distinction should be made, and this is also Rama's view.
Even if he already married her, etc., he must divorce her. As explained in Even HaEzer, siman 77. Even the authority who disagrees there does so only regarding an already married woman, unlike the present case. Mahari Weil, siman 137.
See below, siman 239, regarding one who swore, etc. This is the correct reading, because the law appears there in seif 8. Ateret Zekenim also has an erroneous reading.
