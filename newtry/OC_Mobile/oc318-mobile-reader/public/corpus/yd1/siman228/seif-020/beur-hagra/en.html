He vowed on the understanding of, etc., through “provided that he specified.” This entire passage is the Tur’s language, except that Shulchan Aruch writes, “unless he informed the person upon whose understanding he vowed,” whereas the Tur writes, “except with the consent of the person upon whose understanding he vowed.” Rama’s gloss follows the explanation of Rabbi Eliezer of Metz cited by the Rosh. According to this view, consent is needed only where the other person conferred a benefit upon him. Otherwise, notification suffices, because the concern is suspicion. The differing formulations of the Rosh in Nedarim and Gittin reflect these two rationales.
Some say, etc. This is the Tur’s view, as above, and also that of Rabbi Eliezer of Metz and the Rosh in Gittin. See Ran, Nedarim, s.v. “it was taught,” who records both rationales and inclines toward requiring consent where benefit was received. Rivash, siman 370, rules similarly. According to the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, where no benefit was received, mere notification is sufficient.
Specifically where he vowed, etc. Tosafot, the Rosh, and the other authorities in Nedarim, as well as the Rosh and Rashba in Gittin, distinguish this case to answer Tosafot’s question there. Ran and the other authorities agree. Further proof comes from Sotah 36b, where Yosef was told to seek release from his oath to Yaakov, yet Pharaoh’s involvement was treated differently.
Because of the oath. Otherwise, Yaakov had bestowed many benefits upon Yosef, and Pharaoh likewise benefited Yosef.
As, etc. So too Nebuchadnezzar with Tzidkiyahu.
And the oath was, etc. In such a case shame is relevant, as Ran explains: the vow was made in the presence of the beneficiary, and release without his knowledge would cause embarrassment. This also answers Tosafot’s question in Gittin, since the orphans gained nothing from her refraining from the produce. Ran in Nedarim writes similarly.
Some say that he must, etc. This view combines both concerns, suspicion and shame, and is therefore stringent under both. Ran records this, and Rivash likewise concludes that one should be stringent for both rationales.
If they already, etc. This resolves the Sotah passage concerning Yosef’s oath to Yaakov according to the suspicion rationale. Regarding Yosef’s oath to Pharaoh, consent was unnecessary; notification alone sufficed, as explained below in seif katan 48. According to the shame rationale, however, actual consent would be required.
Even if he vowed, etc. Otherwise, what benefit would Nebuchadnezzar have gained from Tzidkiyahu’s oath, how could the Sanhedrin have released it, and how could the wholly righteous Tzidkiyahu have acted so? See Sanhedrin 103a. This answers Tosafot’s question in Gittin.
Provided that he specified, etc. As above from the Gittin passage, where she had to specify that she had vowed against the produce for the orphans’ benefit.
Some say, etc. Tzidkiyahu’s case appears to prove the opposite, for Scripture condemns both him and the Sanhedrin, and says of him, “He rebelled against King Nebuchadnezzar, who had adjured him by God.” Nevertheless, that proof is not conclusive. The punishment may have resulted from public desecration and the appearance of oath-breaking, even if the release was technically effective. The authorities therefore dispute whether the beneficiary’s consent is indispensable or whether lack of consent is only improper conduct.
If he swore, etc. See Ran there, who explains that the Gittin case too is not one in which the other party’s legal consent was indispensable.
Some say that he need not, etc. This follows the rationale of suspicion, which appears primary in the Gemara: one forbidden by a vow from benefiting from another, etc. Tosafot in Nedarim write that the Babylonian Gemara follows this rationale, and Rambam and most authorities rule accordingly. On this view, notification is generally sufficient unless the vow was made in return for a benefit, when consent is required.
If he died, etc. As above, there is no longer any suspicion. Shach’s comments in seif katan 53 are mistaken: according to this view, whether or not the deceased had benefited him makes no difference once he has died; consent is never required. Shach’s proof comes from authorities following the first view and was imported here incorrectly.
Nevertheless, if, etc. In such a case suspicion exists even with respect to the deceased’s heirs and legal representatives.
Some say that for the purpose of, etc. As above from Tzidkiyahu, whom the Sanhedrin released initially for a perceived need.
A man and a woman, etc. As above from Moshe and Yitro, even though the oath was made before Yitro had yet fulfilled his promise to give him his daughter.
He must divorce her. This is Rambam’s view. Although the other authorities disagree based on Gittin 49b, the woman is in any event not compelled to remain with him; all the more so where he has not yet married her, release may be granted.
