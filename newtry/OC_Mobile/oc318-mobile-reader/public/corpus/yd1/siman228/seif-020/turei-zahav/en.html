He vowed according to his fellow’s understanding. This means that his fellow maintained the vow and he now wishes to release it. The Perishah raised a difficulty from siman 231. In my opinion, those words never came from his mouth, but were written by a mistaken student.
Unless he informs him. The Yerushalmi gives the reason of suspicion, meaning that the other person should not suspect him of treating his vow lightly. Another opinion gives the reason of shame, meaning that he should be ashamed before the person for whose benefit he vowed and whom he now asks to release him. The Rivash explains that the practical difference is as follows. According to shame, when the vow was not for the other person’s benefit but was merely made in his presence, it may be released outside his presence. According to suspicion, even then there is concern unless he is informed. According to shame, if the vow was for his benefit it may not be released outside his presence even if he is informed. According to suspicion, informing him suffices. All agree that if the vow was not for his benefit but only made in his presence, it may be released outside his presence provided he is informed, and in his presence even against his will. Thus, when the person who administered the oath benefits from it, his consent is required, which follows the reason of shame. When he derives no benefit, consent is unnecessary and mere knowledge suffices, following the reason of suspicion. Therefore, the phrase here, “unless he informs him,” means that he must consent. The later phrase, “they may release it without his knowledge,” means without his consent, but he must still be informed because of suspicion. Although the Rama presents this later as an alternative opinion, it appears that the Shulchan Aruch itself intended this, for its language follows the Tur, and the Beit Yosef explains that in the first case the Tur requires actual consent.
But if he did so on his own. The usual case is mentioned, because people are not ordinarily placed under oath by others when those others derive no benefit. In truth, even if others administered the oath, when they derive no benefit only their knowledge is required, not their consent.
Because of a benefit. The Beit Yosef explains that an employee who accepted work from a homeowner is considered to have received a benefit. The Beit Yosef also cites the Rivash that if one swore to a group and it is later discovered that only certain individuals benefit, while he did not have them specifically in mind, this is not considered acceptance of a benefit.
That he specified the vow and the sage knew, etc. As explained above in seif 14.
And there are those who say that there is no distinction. This is the Rambam’s opinion. The Tur cites him: If Reuven administered an oath to Shimon and Shimon answered amen, Shimon may be released only in Reuven’s presence. Likewise, if Reuven swore that Shimon should not benefit from him and then regretted it, he may be released only in Shimon’s presence, even if Shimon is a non-Jew or a minor, so that the person affected by the vow should know that it was released and may therefore receive benefit. The Rambam does not depend upon whether a benefit was received or whether the oath was made according to the other person’s understanding. In every case, whether he was placed under oath or swore on his own, suspicion requires informing the person affected. The Rambam’s conclusion, “so that the person affected should know,” refers to the latter case, where Shimon must know, and from this we learn the first case as well. The Beit Yosef labored to explain the Rambam otherwise, unnecessarily. The wording, “Shimon should not benefit from him,” is difficult, for what suspicion exists? So long as Shimon does not know that the vow was released, he will not benefit. One must explain that Reuven himself may not confer benefit upon Shimon, and if Shimon sees him doing so he will suspect him. Therefore, Reuven must inform him. This is clear from the accurate Rambam text cited by the Beit Yosef, which reads that Reuven swore not to confer benefit upon Shimon.
That for the purpose of a mitzvah, etc. I am astonished that the Rama cited this as practical halacha. This reasoning appears in Tosafos and the Ran concerning why the Sanhedrin released Tzidkiyahu’s oath, namely, that he was greatly distressed by keeping the matter secret and was thereby prevented from serving Heaven. Nevertheless, we see that they were punished, as it states, “They sit upon the ground in silence.” The Beit Yosef likewise wrote that both he and they were punished because the matter involved a chillul Hashem. The Rivash, siman 94, used Tosafos only as an additional consideration alongside other grounds for leniency, and explicitly concluded there that strictly speaking no release was needed. In simanim 370 and 186, the Rivash states explicitly that Tosafos merely found a pretext because of the king’s fear and that they acted improperly. He concludes that whenever one swore for another person’s benefit, it may not be released in any manner without that person’s consent. One should therefore not rely on this leniency. Perhaps the Rama intended it only as an additional factor when sufficient grounds for release exist independently.
