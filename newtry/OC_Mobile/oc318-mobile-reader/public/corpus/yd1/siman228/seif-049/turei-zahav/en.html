Half for this and half for that. The Beit Yosef cites the Rashba and derives this from, “Half for Aharon and half for his sons.” Were it not for the authority of his holy mouth, I would say the cases are not comparable. There, the rule resembles Choshen Mishpat, siman 253, seif 24, where a dying person says, “My property is for so-and-so and so-and-so,” and we assume that from the outset he intended half for each. Here, however, they said that the money should pay their debts, which implies all their debts, and that they should build a synagogue, meaning the entire synagogue. One who swears to pay his debts does not fulfill the oath by paying only part. The Shulchan Aruch, siman 229, seif 1, states that if one vowed to repay his fellow and the fellow pardoned part, the remainder remains owed, showing that “to repay him” means the entire debt. The same is obvious from Choshen Mishpat, siman 73. Had they said that the money should be “for payment of debts and for construction of a synagogue,” it would imply that this money should assist both purposes. But when they said, “they shall pay their debts,” it implies that this sum itself will discharge the debts. We must therefore say that they were mistaken, thinking the money sufficient both to pay all debts and build the synagogue. Since it is insufficient, the vow was mistaken and void from the outset, or else they should first pay the debts and use whatever remains for the synagogue. Even according to the Rashba, if the community states that this was its intention, we should accept its explanation. The Beit Yosef cites the Rivash that where wording can be interpreted either stringently or leniently, we accept the person’s explanation. All the more so here may the community interpret its own words in the manner explained. It appears that the Rashba would agree.
