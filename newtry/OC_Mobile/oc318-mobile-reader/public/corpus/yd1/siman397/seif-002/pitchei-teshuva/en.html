Two say. See Baer Heitev and Responsa Yeriot HaOhel, siman 38, which concludes in accordance with the authorities who require mourning where one does not know whether the report was received within thirty days or after thirty days. The new Responsa Radbaz, siman 62, rules likewise, as do Responsa Yad Eliyahu, siman 44, and Bechor Shor in his novellae to Moed Katan 20b. See also Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 33; Teshuat Chen, siman 17; Atzei Arazim to Even HaEzer 17, seif katan 114; Noda BiYehudah, first edition, Even HaEzer, siman 31; Noda BiYehudah, second edition, Yoreh Deah, siman 208; Mutzal MeEsh, siman 25; and Shaar HaMelech, Hilchot Ishut 4:17. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Yoreh Deah, siman 77, discusses a letter stating that the recipient's father had died "eight days ago," where it was uncertain whether eight days had already elapsed before the letter was written, in which case more than thirty days had passed by the time it arrived and the report was distant, or whether the father had been dead eight days on the date of writing, in which case the report was still recent. Although Bach and other authorities require mourning where there is doubt whether a report is distant, Beit Ephraim rules that here mourning is not required because the natural meaning of the phrase indicates that eight days had already elapsed, as in the verses "these twenty years I have been in your house" and "these two years the famine has been in the land." Since the writer was learned, he presumably chose the scriptural expression precisely. One may also combine the minority view that hearing on the thirtieth day is already a distant report. Responsa Yad Eliyahu, siman 44, discusses a letter from afar whose wording could mean either that one's rebbe died or that one's father died, and the report came after thirty days. He rules that in either case the recipient must rend, and the keriah will suffice even if it is later learned that the deceased was his father, analogous to siman 340:24. Regarding laundering and haircutting, however, he need not observe the restrictions because the doubt concerns rabbinic law. He further writes that if the doubt is between two deceased persons, one being the father of one recipient and the other being the father of the second, and it is within thirty days, both recipients must mourn. Although doubtful mourning is generally treated leniently, here one of the two certainly has an obligation, so both must observe mourning out of doubt. He compares this to siman 374:8 in the Rama regarding a child of doubtful paternity. The responsum also indicates that if there were an additional doubt whether the report was itself within thirty days, creating a double doubt, both would be exempt.
