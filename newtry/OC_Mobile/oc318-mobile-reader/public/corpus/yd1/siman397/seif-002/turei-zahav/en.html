He does not mourn. The reason is that since it is a doubt we rule leniently, as written by Maharam Mintz siman 95: one who hears that a relative died but does not know whether within thirty days or after thirty days — whether he is obligated to mourn; and he elaborated on this at length. This is the sum of his words: there is reasoning to say that since they ruled leniently in matters of mourning because it is a rabbinic doubt — and he cited in the name of Laws of Mourning of Maharam that one should not be stringent in doubtful mourning, because that stringency leads to leniency: he would observe mourning on Shabbat through private acts and would cancel Torah study for all seven days. But based on the principle of presumption [chazakah] it seems appropriate to be stringent — for we learned in chapter Kol HaGet [Gittin 28a]: one who brings a get and left it with an elderly or ill person gives it to her on the presumption that he is still alive, etc.; and he cited many laws that follow presumption; therefore one may say: establish the deceased upon his presumption and it is only now that he has died. But he did not reach a definitive ruling, and he concluded that he has no clear proof either for obligating or for exempting. And in the responsum of Maharai ibn Lev he ruled obligating based on that [Mishnah] in chapter Kol HaGet that I mentioned; and similarly my father-in-law of blessed memory ruled here. And in my humble opinion this does not appear correct, based on clear proofs as I will explain with God's help.
First: from what we say in the Mishnah in Pesachim chapter Ha-Ishah [Pesachim 91a]: an onen and one who clears stones from a pile [over a person without knowing whether he will find him alive or dead, etc.] may slaughter the Pesach sacrifice on his behalf [because he is still in the presumption of being tahor, etc.; therefore if disqualification subsequently occurs to them, they are exempt from performing the second Pesach, since at the time of sprinkling they were fit]; except for the stone-clearer who finds the dead person beneath him — he is obligated to perform the second Pesach since he was tamei from the beginning [meaning before the shechitah, since he had spread an ohel over the impurity from the moment he began to clear]. And Tosafot wrote: it is puzzling to R' Yitzchak — why not say regarding the stone-clearer too that he was alive when the clearing began? And the answer: since he was found dead, we must say he was dead from the beginning, for all [instances of] tumah are [dated] as of the moment of their discovery — end of their words. Behold before you that we do not say "establish him on his presumption of being alive, and only now did he die" — and do not say we cannot learn from the laws of tumah about prohibitions [i.e., mourning], and likewise one cannot derive from one area of law to another; for in that Mishnah we derive from it that he is obligated to perform the second Pesach, and we do not consider this a doubt lest he might be bringing non-sacred animals into the Temple courtyard — rather we treat it as a certainty that he was dead from the start, and we learn from tumah similarly; here too we learn that he is exempt from mourning since we say: once it is established that he is now dead, we consider him to have been dead from the start; and do not say the stone-clearer case is different because once the rocks fell on him there was a presumption he was certainly dead — that is not so, for Tosafot wrote that all instances of tumah are dated as of the moment of discovery, which implies this applies in every case; and further, there is certainly no logical necessity that he died immediately, for we find that the tanna stated unspecified "one who clears stones from a pile" — which includes even the immediate moment that the rocks fell on him.
Second: from chapter HaMadir [Ketubot 77a]: Rav Yehudah said in the name of Shemuel — one who exchanges a cow for a donkey and the owner of the donkey pulled the cow [effecting his acquisition] but the owner of the cow did not manage to pull the donkey before the donkey died — the burden of proof falls on the owner of the donkey to bring evidence that his donkey was alive at the time the cow was pulled [for otherwise the sale is void and he must return the cow]. The difficulty is: why must he bring proof for this — why do we not establish the donkey on its presumption [chazakah] that it was alive beforehand, and only now did it die? And similarly in the conclusion there [the Talmud states] that Rami bar Yehezkiel ruled: the burden of proof falls on the owner of the cow to prove it died first — since the doubt arose in his domain [that is, in the domain of the donkey owner who had already performed the act of pulling on the cow]. Why did [Rami bar Yehezkiel] need to say this is because the doubt arose in his domain? Let the presumption of being alive as in chapter Kol HaGet mentioned above [protect the donkey owner] — what is the difference between the presumption that a person is alive or that an animal is alive?
Third: from what Tur and Shulchan Arukh wrote in Even HaEzer siman 17: if a slain [man] was found and they recognize him by his physical appearance but do not know when he was killed — some say we presume he was killed within three days [of the last time he was known to be alive] and [a witness] may testify about him [permitting his wife to remarry]; and there are those who forbid; and it is explained in Beit Yosef in the name of Rashba that we rule stringently. The difficulty: why not say he was killed only now [i.e., recently] and establish him on his presumption of being alive as previously, just as in chapter Kol HaGet? Therefore it is necessary to answer all three of these powerful difficulties: we only say in chapter Kol HaGet that we establish him on his presumption of being alive — specifically when we do not yet know with certainty that he has died; but not in these three cases where we now know certainly that he has died — there we indeed say he was already dead [earlier], as the plain implication of Tosafot's words in Pesachim that I cited, who wrote: since he was found dead, we must say he was dead from the beginning. And one should not raise a difficulty from other presumptions where we say the presumption holds even in such a case — for example regarding a mikveh [Niddah 2a] where we challenge: let us say it became lacking only now; and likewise in siman 81 above regarding cheese made from an animal's milk when the animal was later found to be a tereifah — we say it became a tereifah only now; and similarly in many places. It seems to me correct to say that death is different — for every living being is destined to die and is inevitably directed toward it without any doubt; therefore we rightly say that as long as it is not made clear that he died, we say he is still alive; but once it is made clear that he did die, the presumption of life departs for the purpose of saying he died only now — rather we say he was already dead from before, since he was already destined for this outcome. And this reasoning — that it is "destined for this" — we find in Tur, Choshen Mishpat siman 65: "and you cannot say 'establish the document on its presumption that it has not been paid,' because the document is destined to be paid" — end of his words. This teaches that this reasoning diminishes the force of the presumption; similarly we should say so regarding this presumption of being alive — that you cannot say "he died only now," because he was destined for this; and since we have attained this conclusion, let us also say here that he is not obligated to mourn based on the doubt arising from the presumption — for you cannot say "he died only now" specifically, and therefore on these proofs I relied and ruled as a practical decision that there is no obligation of mourning for this doubt — especially after the great foundational principle that in matters of mourning we rule leniently even more than in other rabbinic matters, and it is not appropriate to be stringent [in a way] that results in leniency [such as violating Shabbat with mourning practices, as I mentioned in the earlier responsum]. Maharam Mintz further wrote in the aforementioned responsum: since I have no clear proof in either direction — even if there is an authority who ruled there is no mourning in such a case of doubt [whether it falls within thirty days] — let me clarify what his law is if he does not know for certain until after thirty days, and it becomes known retroactively that when the tidings reached him it was within thirty days: whether we treat it as a distant tidings [shemu'ah rechokah] or we say that since he had knowledge within thirty days, even though thirty days have passed he must mourn; and he attempted to learn this law from what is written in Tractate Semachot of Maharam regarding twins where one died within thirty days and the other survived past thirty days: Maharam was uncertain there whether [the surviving twin] mourns for the deceased — if we say the deceased had also established viability [bar kayyama, i.e., would have survived more than thirty days], since a woman cannot conceive twice consecutively, then by R' Chananel's explanation the death is a nefal [stillborn] — or perhaps it is treated as distant tidings since thirty days have now passed; or perhaps it is not comparable to distant tidings, since there he had no knowledge at all that death occurred, whereas here [in the twin case] the death was known but hung in suspense until the uncertainty was resolved. Maharam Mintz concluded it is exempt [since thirty days have passed], as if it were distant tidings; and he was still uncertain whether in this case it is not treated as distant tidings — for at any moment the obligation of mourning could take effect if one hears that it was within thirty days, unlike the twin case mentioned, where it is impossible to know within thirty days whether the first one was viable or a stillbirth; and he concludes it requires further study whether he is obligated to mourn — end of his words. And in my humble opinion there is no mourning here, since in any event thirty days have now passed from the day of death, and he is no worse than one who inadvertently or deliberately did not observe mourning at all until after thirty days — in that case certainly he has no mourning thereafter, as written here in seif 1: "one who inadvertently or deliberately fulfills [the mourning] until the end of all thirty days" — which implies that after thirty days there is no make-up obligation at all, as it seems to me. And I subsequently saw in the responsum of Maharam of Lublin who cited these words of Shulchan Arukh as they are and ruled accordingly — though he, in his sharpness, did not examine the matter as thoroughly as needed.
