Provided that she sensed its discharge. Although it is explained below, in siman 190, that even if she sensed nothing and merely found a stain on her body or garment, she is impure when the stain is of the larger measure, namely more than the size of a gris, as explained there, the Mechaber here intends to teach that she is impure even from a drop of blood as small as a mustard seed. He therefore states without qualification, provided that she sensed it. This is likewise the intent of the Tur, whose wording parallels that of the Mechaber.
As small as a mustard seed. The same applies even to less than this. Shach.
Seven clean days. Nowadays, because we are not expert in distinguishing the relevant categories, the Sages treated every woman as a major zavah. The Mechaber therefore did not record the distinctions defining which case is niddah and which is major zavah. The Levush, however, copied them and wrote in the language of the Tur as follows: If she saw blood during the eleven days for three consecutive days and thereby became a major zavah, she does not return to the days of niddah until she has observed seven clean days. The days of niddah then begin, followed by the days of zivah, and so on continually. This accords with the view of Ramban and Rashi, and not with the view of Rambam, as explained at length in Beit Yosef. Shach, in seif katan 4, copied only the beginning of the Levush's words. He therefore understood that the Levush followed Rambam and challenged him. In truth, however, the Levush's words precisely follow the language of the Tur, which accords with Rashi and Ramban. This is clear to anyone who examines the matter.
There is no distinction between an unmarried woman and a married woman regarding the prohibition of niddah. The Gemara states at the end of HaMapelet that Rabbi Meir taught: Why did the Torah ordain seven days for a niddah? Because her husband is accustomed to her and may become weary of her. The Torah therefore said that she should be impure for seven days, so that she will be as beloved to her husband as at the time she entered the chuppah. According to this reasoning, an unmarried woman, to whom this rationale does not apply, should not be impure for seven days. Yet the implication of the Rama here is that there is no distinction in any respect. This is also evident from what the poskim wrote concerning a bride, as explained below in siman 192. One must therefore say that this teaching is only an exposition offering a reason, while the verses themselves indicate that the law applies both to a married and an unmarried woman. Rivash wrote similarly in siman 425, and the verse states that she shall be impure for seven days, implying that this applies in every case. Alternatively, Rabbi Meir asked why the Torah prescribed seven days for a niddah, but did not ask the same regarding a zavah. This presents no difficulty, because many forms of impurity last seven days, such as that of a zav or one impure through contact with a corpse, while others last only until evening. The central question regarding niddah is a dilemma. If her impurity is severe, why do we say that even if she continuously discharges blood throughout all seven days, she may immerse that evening? This is explicit in the Gemara and is stated by the Tur in this siman; see Beit Yosef. If her impurity is not so severe, why should even the smallest sighting render her impure for seven days rather than only until evening? We therefore need Rabbi Meir's rationale, that she should remain beloved to her husband. A zavah, however, unquestionably requires seven clean days even though that rationale does not apply. Since the Sages now treated all women as major zavot, there is necessarily no distinction between married and unmarried women, and each requires seven clean days. Alternatively, Rabbi Meir follows the approach of Rabbi Shimon, who interprets the reason underlying a verse, as stated in Bava Metzia 115 concerning taking security from a widow. The halachah does not follow Rabbi Shimon, as explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 97. Accordingly, the halachah likewise does not follow Rabbi Meir in this rationale, and no distinction should be made at all.
