(Siman 100 in the Shulchan Aruch, seif 1.) A beriah — that is, for example, an ant, etc. Regarding whether a flea constitutes a beriah or not — there is a dispute among the later authorities. The fundamental issue is that this law depends on the dispute between Ri of Orliens and Rabbenu Tam brought by Tosafot in the first chapter of Shabbat: according to Rabbenu Tam, who holds one is liable for [killing it on] Shabbat, therefore it has life and properly constitutes a beriah. But according to Ri, who holds one is exempt for [killing it on] Shabbat, this implies it has no life, and the status of beriah does not apply to it. See Responsa Tzemach Tzedek.
[Marginal note:] And see Kreiti u'Pleiti, which brought the dispute among the poskim whether [for something to constitute a beriah] it must have been forbidden from the beginning of its creation. The view of the Rosh is that we do not require it to have been forbidden from the beginning of its creation — only that if it is divided and its name is no longer upon it, it is considered its beriah. He wrote that from this emerges a great rule: the tiny worms that form in the rottenness of fruits — as long as they have not separated [from the fruit] they are permitted; therefore there is no prohibition from the beginning of their creation, etc. Accordingly, specifically according to the Rosh's view mentioned above, they constitute a beriah. But we who follow the Rashba's ruling — that we require specifically that it was forbidden from the beginning of its creation — it is not called a beriah, etc. Nevertheless, Heaven forbid to be lenient in this, etc. Regarding this, the renowned late Gaon, our teacher R. Betzalel Hakohen z"l, Moreh Tzedek of Vilna, wrote in the margin of his copy of the Kreiti u'Pleiti, and this is his language: See Responsa Tur HaEven siman 26, which disagrees with this and brought the language of the Rashba z"l in Torat HaBayit HaAroch, Beit 4, Sha'ar 1, where he wrote as follows: "And what beriah is not nullified? Specifically [something] that is forbidden due to itself — like an ant and the gid hanasheh, whose prohibition is due to themselves from the beginning of their creation. But something that is permitted as a creature yet received its prohibition due to something else — like a gored ox, the birds of the metzora, and a treifah hen — this is not a beriah that is not nullified." End quote of the Rashba z"l. It is implied that "forbidden due to itself" and "from the beginning of its creation" are one and the same thing — meaning, it is forbidden due to itself on account of its creation; its creation and its form caused the prohibition, in that the Torah forbade that creature because it takes such-and-such a form. For example, an ant: its creation caused its prohibition because it is in the form of an ant (i.e., in the form of a sheretz or creeping thing of the earth) — it certainly constitutes a beriah and is not nullified, regardless of whether it was actually forbidden at the moment of its formation or in the middle of its days, since its creation caused the prohibition, it is as though it was forbidden from the beginning of its creation — for from the beginning of its creation it was [destined to be] forbidden in this manner, and that prohibition took effect upon it from the beginning of its creation, to be forbidden subsequently from the moment it stirs and moves from place to place. But if it is not forbidden due to itself from the beginning of its creation, but rather received the prohibition circumstantially — like the birds of the metzora, the gored ox, neveilah, and treifah — even if it became neveilah or treifah from the very beginning of its creation, its form of creation did not cause the prohibition; rather, something else caused it. Therefore it is nullified, because what turned the ox into a gored ox caused the prohibition — not the form of its creation. End of the words of the aforementioned Gaon, with some paraphrase for clarity (for the author's language there is unclear and imprecise; therefore I have arranged his words here in language that seems clear to me, in my humble opinion). However, in my humble opinion, all of this is insufficient to refute the words of the Gaon, author of the Pleti z"l, and there is room for the opposing side to dispute and say: regarding the prohibition of neveilah as well — is this prohibition not applicable to all living creatures? For fish and locusts are not subject to the laws of neveilah and treifah. If so, animals, wild animals, and birds — to which the law of neveilah and treifah does apply — their form causes their prohibition. What then is the difference to me between the prohibition of treifah and the prohibition of shekatzim and remashim? Deliberate carefully. Thus far the words of the late Gaon, our teacher R. Betzalel Hakohen z"l, Moreh Tzedek of the community of Vilna, in the margin of [his copy of] the Pleti.
(siman 100 seif 2) Thing that is beriah cooked with heter — if not recognized all forbidden, gravy nosen taam; if recognized discard and others, gravy sixty except gid hanasheh no taste forbids — no nosen taam in gidim; fat forbids, sixty for gravy; thigh with gid — recognize and discard, rest permitted if sixty against fat; not recognized all pieces forbidden; sixty in pot for fat permits gravy; melted gid needs sixty — Shach issur davuk — investigate; d'rabbanan doubt lenient especially gid fat.
