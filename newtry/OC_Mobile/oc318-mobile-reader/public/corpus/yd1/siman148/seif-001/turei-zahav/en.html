To lend. "Lending" applies to something that returns in its specific form, such as an object; and "borrowing money" applies to coins, which are given for spending. And everything mentioned here is that the gentile goes and gives thanks to his idol because the Jew needed him — and in the Talmud (7a) they asked: does the prohibition apply because of "benefit" — meaning the gentile prospers and goes and gives thanks to idolatry, and the Jew thereby violates "you shall not bring it to your lips" — or perhaps because of "before the blind"? Rashi explains "before the blind": because the Jew sells him a beast and he sacrifices it to idolatry, and the gentile was also commanded concerning idolatry, so do not cause him to be punished through you. What is the practical difference? That the gentile has his own beast — if the prohibition is because of "benefit," he prospers regardless; but if because of "before the blind," there is no prohibition since he has the means without the Jew. Now according to Rashi and Rambam, whose ruling the Shulchan Arukh follows here — that there is a prohibition in selling to and buying from a gentile — this is very perplexing, since there is another practical difference: if due to "benefit" it is forbidden even with something not suitable for sacrifice, and if due to "before the blind" there is no prohibition except with something suitable for sacrifice. And Tosafot already raised this at the beginning of the chapter in the name of R' Tam — and therefore R' Tam maintains that giving thanks to the idol is not applicable to buying and selling, as the Tur writes in his name. One must say for Rashi and Rambam that certainly one can make this practical distinction — but the Talmud teaches a novelty: that even with a beast suitable for sacrifice there is also a practical difference — that the gentile has it without the Jew — and when the Talmud says "what is the practical difference" it means: that the gentile has his own beast — if due to "benefit" the prohibition applies even there, and if due to "before the blind" — not; for in that case he had the means independently. And it emerges from here that in buying and selling, giving thanks to the idol is applicable, and the Jew violates "you shall not bring it to your lips." And my father-in-law of blessed memory raised the difficulty: why did Rashi say that in giving thanks to the idol one violates "you shall not bring it to your lips" — why is it not also forbidden because of "before the blind"? And he answered: because with giving thanks there is no "before the blind," since it is like embracing and kissing the idol, about which the gentile was not commanded, since the Jew is also not liable to death for it; and I am puzzled here — for certainly a Jew is liable to death for giving thanks to idolatry, as stated in chapter Arba Mitot (Sanhedrin 60a) in the mishnah: "one who says 'it is my god,' etc." — and it is explicitly stated there: "all who give thanks to idolatry are as one who denies the entire Torah." And there is no original difficulty in Rashi's position — since it is clarified in the Talmud in chapter 1 of Avodah Zarah (6a) that "before the blind" only applies where the gentile cannot perform the act without the Jew — like handing a cup of wine to a Nazirite on the other side of a river, since the Nazirite could not get the wine himself; but if the gentile could have done it himself, and the Jew only assists and facilitates — there is no "before the blind"; and as the Mordechai wrote at the beginning of Avodah Zarah, cited by Rema in siman 151, regarding selling to them items related to their worship — if they could buy elsewhere; and even according to the stricter view he cites there, that is only rabbinically, not biblically. But "you shall not bring it to your lips" applies here — for in any event it was heard through him, even if it would have been heard anyway — for now it was heard "on your lips."
