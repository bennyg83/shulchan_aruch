To lend. 'She'eilah' applies to an object that is returned intact, while 'halva'ah' applies to money, which is given to be spent. All the transactions mentioned here are prohibited because the non-Jew may go and give thanks to his idol, saying that the Jew needed him. The Gemara asks whether the reason is this resulting benefit and thanksgiving, or the prohibition of placing a stumbling block before the blind, such as selling him an animal fit for an idolatrous offering. The practical difference is where the non-Jew already owns such an animal: if the concern is benefit and thanksgiving, the transaction remains forbidden; if it is lifnei iver, he already has the means independently. According to Rashi and Rambam, whose view the Shulchan Aruch follows, buying and selling themselves may be prohibited. Tosafos question this, because if the issue were lifnei iver there should also be a difference between an item fit for sacrifice and one that is not. Rabbeinu Tam therefore explains differently. According to Rashi and Rambam, however, one can answer that the Gemara chose a more novel practical distinction even with an animal fit for sacrifice: whether the prohibition remains when the non-Jew already possesses another. The central question remains whether ordinary commercial benefit can itself prompt idolatrous thanksgiving.
