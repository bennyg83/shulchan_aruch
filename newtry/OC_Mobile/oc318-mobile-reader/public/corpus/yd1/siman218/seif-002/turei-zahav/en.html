We follow his intention. These are the words of the Rashbatz, cited by the Beit Yosef. Although this ruling began with great authorities and ended with great authorities, it is nevertheless Torah and I must study it, for it is difficult not only from the words of the Gemara but also from his own words, as we will explain. His statement that we follow the person’s intention is astonishing. We rule like Shmuel that one’s mouth and heart must be in agreement, as written above in siman 210. What difference does his intention make when he explicitly said that he would not make wine, which implies that wine already made is not included? It is stated explicitly regarding, “You shall not make for yourself a carved image”: I know only that one may not make it; from where do we know that one may not retain one already made? The Torah therefore states, “You shall not have.” Thus, something already made is not included in the words, “you shall not make.” The Terumas HaDeshen, siman 277, wrote that we judge according to his intention even where it conflicts with the words uttered by his lips, etc. It is obvious that he meant only that we should not interpret the wording according to its simplest implication, as written above regarding the prohibition against wearing wool according to the Rosh. Nevertheless, the language can still be interpreted consistently with his intention. This is not so where the intention completely contradicts the words, as here. The Rashbatz’s proof from the case in seif 1, where one vows, “Wool shall not come upon me,” and we follow his intention, is not comparable. There the intention does not contradict the wording at all, whereas here it does. Moreover, he contradicts his own words. At the end of siman 208, the Beit Yosef cites the Rashbatz concerning one who said that he would not marry a woman within three years. If he used wording that means he would not perform the marriage ceremony with her, and then transgressed and married her, he is like one who swore regarding a loaf and then ate it, and he may seek release from the oath. If, however, he used wording meaning that she should not be his wife within three years, release is unavailable, etc. The Shulchan Aruch ruled accordingly. Thus, we follow the meaning of the words and do not say that when he stated that he would not perform the marriage ceremony, he meant that she should not be his wife. The same applies here. When he says that he will not make wine, he does not mean that he should have no wine. These are precisely the same matter. It is even a kal vachomer. There, it is likely that he was not particular about the marriage ceremony itself, for people do not ordinarily care about the exact time of the ceremony. Rather, he did not want the burden of marriage hanging around his neck and did not wish to be occupied with his wife’s needs. He included this within the act of marriage because marriage is the beginning, and he referred to the beginning. Nevertheless, we say that we follow the plain meaning of his wording. All the more so here, where he did not intend to cause himself a loss because he feared danger by forbidding wine that he already possessed. Rather, he intended not to involve himself in producing new wine, which means foregoing future profit. He therefore said specifically that he would not make wine. Certainly we should follow the wording and forbid only what actually emerged from his lips. Furthermore, if two types of merchandise were dangerous to sell to a non-Jew and he swore concerning only one of them, would we say that we follow his intention because he feared the danger and therefore forbid the second type as well? Certainly not. The same applies here, for avoiding future profit and saving oneself from an existing loss are two different categories, just as we find this distinction regarding a financial loss on Chol HaMoed and in the laws of mourning, siman 380, seif 6. One is not comparable to the other, and the same applies here.
And all the more so a community, etc. The Rama derived this from the ruling of the Rashbatz cited by the Shulchan Aruch. I say that even if that ruling has no basis, as we argued, this law is nevertheless correct. It is explicit in the words of the Rosh in the first chapter of Chullin regarding the Gemara’s unresolved question whether the limbs of animals slaughtered by stabbing, which the Jewish people brought with them into Eretz Yisrael, were permitted. The Rosh explains that the practical difference concerns one who forbids upon himself a certain species from a specified time onward, and when that time arrives he still possesses some of that species that he had been eating until then. The question is whether the remainder is permitted. Alternatively, it concerns a beit din that wished to forbid a certain item, whether what Jews already possessed from that species is included. Since the matter remained unresolved, we rule stringently in a Torah matter. Nevertheless, one might say that on the contrary, this proves leniency here. Since the question remained unresolved, every unresolved rabbinic matter is treated leniently, and here the prohibition is rabbinic, for it arises only from a communal enactment. I likewise saw in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 281, that he cited one of the great authorities who ruled concerning a communal enactment not to sell meat above the fixed price. One person possessed meat that he had slaughtered before the enactment in order to sell it at a higher price. That authority permitted it because the meat was already in his possession before the enactment, and he brought proof from this unresolved question. The author of Terumas HaDeshen wrote that this applied because it was merely an enactment and was not made under a formal curse. If, however, they imposed a cherem, it becomes a Torah matter and we rule stringently. He then wrote: “Although I have some hesitation concerning that ruling, for the Semag writes that one who swears not to enter into the communal enactment has sworn falsely, etc., which indicates that a communal enactment itself has Torah force,” and he concludes that where vows are only rabbinic, such as a vow concerning something intangible and similar cases, the unresolved question is rabbinic and we rule leniently. According to this, there is no basis for the Rama’s phrase, “all the more so,” regarding a communal enactment. The Shulchan Aruch first discussed an oath, which is a Torah matter, and how can the Rama derive from this a communal enactment that is not from the Torah? Although the Terumas HaDeshen wrote that he had some hesitation regarding an enactment, it is clear there that he did not intend to disagree and rule stringently so as to overturn the practical ruling of that great authority. In truth, I already wrote in siman 228, seif 33, a sound resolution to the Terumas HaDeshen’s hesitation. Only an enactment intended to safeguard against sin has Torah force. An enactment intended to preserve money or for other reasons unrelated to sin does not. I later saw that the Maharshal, in the first chapter of Chullin, siman 36, cited the Rosh’s explanation of the unresolved question. He wrote that the Rosh’s first answer, concerning one who forbids something upon himself, would mean that it is a rabbinic doubt, for whenever one forbids an item upon himself in the language of a vow by saying, “I will not eat such-and-such,” without associating it with an already prohibited matter, the prohibition is only rabbinic. The Maharshal disagrees with the Rosh on this point, for with vows we follow ordinary speech, and according to ordinary speech it is forbidden to eat that species once the specified time arrives. He therefore accepts the Rosh’s second answer, concerning a beit din that wished to forbid something, where the doubt is rabbinic and we rule leniently. This applies specifically where the beit din merely wished to prohibit it. If, however, they decreed under cherem and curse not to eat that item, it is a Torah matter, for a beit din has the authority to impose cherem and excommunication even beyond the strict law when their intention is to safeguard against sin. The doubt is then a Torah doubt and we rule stringently. Accordingly, when the sages of Russia once decreed at a large public gathering that no one should drink ordinary Jewish wine unless reliable witnesses testified to its kashrus, and they set a time for the decree, even wine already remaining in their possession became forbidden, for it was a Torah doubt, end quote. Thus, regarding an enactment made without cherem, his words clearly require leniency in a case of doubt. Since the case cited by the Rama is a rabbinic doubt, we rule leniently, and there is no prohibition unless the communal enactment was made under cherem. Even then there is a practical difference if the remaining item became mixed into another mixture in a manner involving doubt, for it would constitute a double doubt. The underlying prohibition itself is uncertain, since the Gemara left it unresolved. The law concerning when a doubtful prohibition that becomes mixed creates a double doubt was explained above in siman 110, at the end of seif 9, where we required two mixtures. If, however, the source were the Rashbatz’s ruling and the item were certainly forbidden, it would not be considered a double doubt until there were three mixtures, as written there in siman 110. Truth directs its own path, and this is only a doubtful prohibition, as the Rosh stated explicitly. This appears entirely clear to me.
