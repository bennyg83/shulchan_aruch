All the more so, a community, etc. This is the Rama's view, derived in Darkei Moshe from the Rashbatz, unlike Terumas HaDeshen, siman 281. In my humble opinion, the Rashbatz does not contradict Terumas HaDeshen. Terumas HaDeshen concludes that if the community enacted the matter under cherem, it is certainly a Torah prohibition like an oath and is forbidden. His uncertainty concerns only an ordinary communal enactment without cherem, whether it is treated as Torah law or rabbinic law. See there, where this is explicit, although Beit Yosef cites it briefly. The Rashbatz explicitly discusses an oath, which is a Torah prohibition. Maharshal likewise distinguishes this explicitly in the first chapter of Chullin, siman 36. One may further distinguish that the Rashbatz speaks specifically of an individual who swore, where vows follow ordinary human usage, and according to ordinary usage the item is forbidden. A communal enactment is different. Maharshal makes this distinction explicitly. He cites the unresolved question of Rabbi Yirmiyah concerning meat from animals slaughtered by stabbing that the Jewish people brought with them into Eretz Yisrael. The Rosh writes that this affects one who forbade a type of food upon himself beginning at a certain time, but possessed some of that food from before the time arrived, and also a case where beit din sought to prohibit a food, such as gentile cheese or cooked foods, and a Jew already possessed some of it. Maharshal explains that a full Torah vow or oath requires stringency because the question remains unresolved, whereas an ordinary rabbinic form of vow permits what was already in his possession because a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently. He nevertheless rejects applying ordinary human usage in vows to other prohibitions, and concludes that the latter application, to an enactment of beit din, is primary. If beit din merely sought to prohibit the item, the unresolved rabbinic doubt is treated leniently. If beit din imposed the prohibition under cherem and curse, it is treated as Torah law, for beit din has authority, when empowered by the government, to impose penalties beyond the strict law in order to strengthen observance. I have cited his words at length because they contain several legal distinctions. His objection to the Rosh is not valid, because the Rosh addresses a vow limited by time, not the general rule that vows follow ordinary human usage. That rule is obvious. The Rosh addresses a place or time where ordinary usage is not otherwise clear, since usage varies by place and time, as explained in the previous siman. In any event, Maharshal's words establish distinctions between an enactment under cherem and an ordinary enactment, and between an individual and a community, unlike the Rama.
