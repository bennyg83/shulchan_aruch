By wearing it. If he did not suffer specifically through wearing, there is no proof that his vow was limited to wearing, and even loading or covering may be forbidden. Mabit writes that a man who vowed after a dangerous pleasure voyage never again to enter a ship with his wife was understood to forbid only such leisure travel, not necessary relocation. He similarly interprets a vow not to sit, eat, or drink together according to the dispute that prompted it, permitting later social eating once the parties had separated their households and expenses.
By clear presumption. Taz explains that where a gift is made to another person, the recipient acquires according to the giver's spoken language unless a compelling presumption proves another intent. In a personal vow, however, the language may be interpreted in light of the circumstances that generated it. If a gift expression itself has two possible meanings, the giver may choose the interpretation favorable to him, and the burden of proof rests upon the claimant.
