And he suffered while wearing it, etc. But if he did not suffer while wearing it, there is no proof of his intent, and it is forbidden even for loading and covering. The responsa of Mabit, part 1, siman 117, page 23, column 4, discusses one who entered a ship with his wife for a sea outing. A storm arose, and while calming her so that she would not fear, he swore that he would never again enter a ship at sea with his wife. Later he needed to travel by ship in order to settle in another city. It appears that his intent concerned only an outing. See there, where he discusses this at length. Mabit further writes in part 2, siman 61, page 32, column 2, regarding two people who lived in one house, ate at one table, conducted business in partnership, and worked in one shop. A quarrel arose concerning payment of the rent for the house and shop, and one of them suddenly swore that they would not sit together in one house and would not eat or drink together. His oath is interpreted according to reason. He is forbidden only to live together in one house as before and to eat and drink in the house from one common fund. Once they have separated from one another and from the house, and each maintains his own household expenses, he may eat in the other person's home. Certainly he may eat with him elsewhere, including at a wedding celebration or a bris. The proof is from the cases of one who was carrying wool and one who was wearing clothing, for we follow the person's intent and the circumstances in which the vow was made.
All this applies specifically, etc., unless there is a clearly demonstrated assessment, etc. Mabit writes in part 4, siman 57, page 46, column 4, that the distinction applies in a matter involving a monetary claim. Where no monetary claim is involved, however, even if he was adjured without qualification, we follow the evident assessment of intent. This conclusion is not compelling. The unqualified wording of the Rama also implies that there is no distinction.
