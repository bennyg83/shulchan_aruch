Based on an evident presumptive inference. The words of this gloss are those of Rivash siman 195; and you should know that there the matter concerned a case where a person was requesting from the community that they accept an elderly scribe together with a young man; and the community accepted both of them by vow in general terms. Afterward they dismissed the elder because he did not act properly, and some individuals wished to say that the young man was also dismissed — since both were accepted through the requester's petition, and his petition was that the young man be together with the elder; so since the elder was dismissed, the young man was automatically dismissed too. To this Rivash answered: since the language of the community's acceptance implies they did not make one dependent on the other, we do not heed the requester's petition — and we do not say we follow the evident presumptive inference — meaning that certainly they intended as the requester requested — just as here we follow the evident presumptive inference regarding "konam wool," for that applies specifically when one vows between himself and himself; there we follow his intent, since we require that his mouth and heart be aligned. But when one gives a gift to another, all that the language of the gift encompasses we follow, and we do not follow an evident presumptive inference — unless the inference is like the case of "one whose son went abroad" in Choshen Mishpat siman 246 seif 1. To this point his intent is clarified: that if the language points in a different direction — even though based on the heart's intent there is reason to understand that some matter was in his mind by virtue of which the meaning of the language should be annulled — we do not say so when it is for the benefit of another, since the other has acquired through the giver's language; and we do not require the mouth and heart to be aligned here — unless there is an evident presumptive inference. Whereas in one who vows between himself and himself, we void the language according to its apparent meaning and interpret it differently — like the first case in this seif, where according to the apparent meaning it implies a prohibition of wearing [according to Rosh], while according to Rambam both meanings are implied as written in seif 1; nevertheless, from his intention we interpret his words as referring to slinging backward, where the prohibition applies — and only in this do they differ. But actually: if one gave a gift to another and used language with two meanings, the choice certainly lies with the giver to interpret it favorably, and the one who would extract money from another must bring proof. In this way you will understand this gloss, which is somewhat obscure.
