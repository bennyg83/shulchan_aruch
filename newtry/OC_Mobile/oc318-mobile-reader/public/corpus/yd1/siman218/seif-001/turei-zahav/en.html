And he was distressed by wearing it. This is the Rambam’s opinion, that even regarding a prohibition against wearing, proof is required that he intended wearing. He derived this from the Baraisa that teaches that he was wearing it and it caused him distress. The Beit Yosef wrote that the Rosh disagrees and holds that no such proof is required, for the same law applies when one vows without qualification, since the statement implies only wearing. The Baraisa’s statement that he was wearing wool and it caused him distress is not precise. This is also the Tur’s opinion, for he wrote that he was wearing them and made a vow, but did not write that they caused him distress, as he wrote in the first clause. One must still explain why the Tur wrote that he was wearing them, for according to the Rosh he should not have needed to mention wearing either, since the same law applies even when one speaks without qualification. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, discussed this question at length and answered that wearing is at least required. In my humble opinion, nothing is required. Rather, the Tur wrote this as a greater novelty. Although he was wearing them and they caused him no distress, reason would suggest that he did not intend to forbid wearing them, since we see that he was wearing them at the time of the vow and suffered no distress from them. Nevertheless, we say that he intended to forbid wearing them. All the more so where he was not wearing them at all. This appears straightforward.
Through a clearly proven assessment. The words of this gloss are from the Rivash, siman 195. You should know that there the case concerned a person who asked the community to appoint an elderly scribe together with a young man. The community accepted both of them through an unspecified vow. They later removed the elderly man because he acted improperly, and certain individuals wished to argue that the young man was also removed. Since they had accepted both through the requester’s proposal, and his proposal was that the young man serve with the elder, the removal of the elder should automatically remove the young man as well. The Rivash answered that since the wording of the community’s acceptance indicates that they did not make one dependent upon the other, we disregard the requester’s proposal. We do not say that we should follow an assessment that they certainly intended to accept them according to his proposal, as we do here regarding one who says, “Wool is a konam.” Only one who vows privately is judged according to his intention, because his mouth and heart must be in agreement. When one gives a gift to another, however, whenever the wording of the gift includes the recipient, we follow that wording and do not follow even a compelling assessment, unless it is an assessment like the case of one whose son traveled overseas, discussed in Choshen Mishpat, siman 246, seif 1. His intention is therefore clear. If the wording indicates one matter, even though one may infer from his inner intention that he had something else in mind that would negate the plain meaning of his words, we do not say so. Since it is for the benefit of the other person, the recipient acquires through the giver’s wording, and in such a case the giver’s mouth and heart need not be in agreement unless there is a clearly proven assessment. This differs from one who vows privately, where we set aside the plain implication of his wording and interpret it in accordance with another meaning. In the first case, according to the Rosh the words imply a prohibition against wearing, while according to the Rambam both meanings are included, as written in seif 1. Nevertheless, from his intention we interpret his words to mean that he forbade placing it over his shoulder, and not wearing it. This is the particular point upon which they disagree. If, however, one gave a gift to another and used wording with two possible meanings, the giver certainly has the right to interpret it in the manner more favorable to himself, and the burden of proof rests upon the one seeking to extract property from another. In this manner you can understand this otherwise obscure gloss.
