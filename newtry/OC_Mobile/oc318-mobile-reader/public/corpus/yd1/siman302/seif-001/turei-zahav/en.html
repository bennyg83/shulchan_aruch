Since he examined it and did not find it. The Tur writes, “or he should sever the thread,” and so forth, as I quoted nearby. This is from the Gemara in Niddah 61a: one who placed a linen thread in a woolen garment and severed it, but does not know whether he severed it or did not sever it, may regard the garment as permitted. What is the reason? By Torah law the verse states “shaatnez,” which requires that the materials be combed, spun, and woven together; the prohibition in this case is therefore a rabbinic decree. Since it is not known whether he severed the thread, it is permitted. The Beit Yosef asks that this wording implies that one may not deliberately sever it initially, whereas the Tur writes “or he should sever it,” implying that even initially one may deliberately sever it. I would add a further difficulty. How is this different from the law in siman 110 concerning a mixture containing a prohibited item that is not nullified because of its importance? If one item fell from the mixture into the sea, we say that the prohibited item was the one that fell, but only if it fell on its own. If a person caused it to fall, even inadvertently, the mixture remains prohibited. Here too the threads of kilayim are considered important, as stated in siman 299 by the Beit Yosef in the name of the Semag. Moreover, I have demonstrated there that all authorities hold that such a thread is not nullified. Yet here they permitted the garment even when a person deliberately removed one thread. The case there as well concerns a prohibition that is only rabbinic in this respect, since by Torah law one item is nullified in two even when it is an important item. From where, then, did the Tur derive a distinction between the cases? Because of this difficulty, it appears that the Tur’s position may be explained as follows. He inferred that even initially it is permitted. For if there were a prohibition against doing this deliberately, why did the Sages permit the garment when a person severed one of the threads? They should have decreed against this case as well because of the possibility that one might act deliberately. There is also an apparent redundancy in the Gemara, which says, “and he severed it, but does not know whether he severed it.” It could simply have said, “he does not know whether he severed it or not.” Why state first, “and he severed it”? One cannot explain that it is known that he severed the prohibited thread but it is uncertain whether he severed all of it. That is not a meaningful distinction, for whether there is doubt that he did not sever it at all or doubt that he did not sever all of it, a remaining portion of the prohibited thread has the same status as the whole. Rather, the meaning is certainly that after the mixture became known, he intentionally went and removed one of the threads in order to rely upon the possibility that it was the prohibited thread. He does not know whether he in fact removed the prohibited thread or not, and nevertheless the garment is permitted. The case here concerns rabbinic kilayim, as the Tur writes regarding a garment into which one thread was woven, and the Gemara likewise speaks of that case. Since there is a substantial loss here, for one may not even sell such a garment to a non-Jew, the Sages were lenient and allowed reliance upon the assumption that the prohibited thread was the one removed. This differs from the case in siman 110, where one may sell the mixture to a non-Jew and the loss is therefore minor; there the Sages decreed that one may not rely upon this assumption when he deliberately causes an item to be removed. We similarly find in many places that the Sages were specifically concerned to be lenient in a case of substantial loss. This, in my view, is the Tur’s position. For this reason the Rambam did not cite the ruling concerning severing the thread, for he holds that the case involves a Torah prohibition, as stated in siman 299, seif 1.
When does this apply? To rabbinic kilayim, etc. The Rambam writes: “We have already explained that every prohibition arising from uncertainty is of rabbinic origin; therefore they were lenient in a case of uncertainty.” The Raavad comments that it is true that the law governing uncertainties is not itself from the Torah, but nevertheless we hold that an uncertainty involving a Torah prohibition is ruled stringently, while an uncertainty involving a rabbinic prohibition is ruled leniently. The Kesef Mishneh writes in response to the Raavad’s objection that the Sages who established the rule that an uncertainty involving a Torah prohibition is treated stringently also ruled that this particular uncertainty should be treated leniently because it is an uncommon occurrence. His words are difficult. Why should a mixture of linen among wool be considered uncommon? Furthermore, according to his understanding of the Raavad, why did the Raavad direct his objection to the Rambam rather than to the Gemara itself, since this very law appears in a baraita in the Gemara? It appears instead that the Rambam was troubled by the Gemara’s statement, “since it is not known, say that perhaps it slipped out,” which indicates that it is not certain that the linen slipped out but is merely a doubt. The decisors likewise use the expression “perhaps it slipped out.” Why, then, are we lenient in an uncertainty involving a Torah prohibition? The Rambam therefore answered that all prohibitions arising from uncertainty are rabbinic. There is no Torah-level uncertainty here requiring stringency, because the garment was subsequently examined, and examination is effective to remove the Torah prohibition. The only remaining concern is that perhaps the examination was not performed properly, and that concern is a rabbinic uncertainty. The Raavad was not satisfied with this explanation and therefore objected that, nevertheless, we hold that an uncertainty concerning a Torah prohibition is treated stringently. The Raavad’s view is that the permissive consideration here is itself treated as a certainty, just as we find in the chapter Elu Tereifot regarding an uncertainty whether an animal was clawed, where the Gemara says, “perhaps it merely greeted it.” The Ran writes that all such formulations of “perhaps” used there are treated by us as certainties, and so forth. In any event, all of this proves that the discussion here concerns Torah-level kilayim that became lost within a garment. This is also evident from the Gemara in Niddah 61a, which compares this examination to the Mishnah’s rule that one examines until reaching bedrock. There, as Rashi explains from the Mishnah, the case concerns an olive-volume of a corpse contained within a mound. Nevertheless, the examination is effective. So too, dyeing the garment as an examination for kilayim is effective even with respect to a Torah prohibition. The Tur, however, is difficult, for after stating this law he writes: “When does this apply? To rabbinic kilayim. But with Torah-level kilayim we do not rely upon an uncertainty to be lenient.” This appears to contradict the Gemara cited above. It therefore seems clear that the Tur’s statement, “When does this apply? To rabbinic kilayim,” refers only to the second alternative that he stated, namely, “or he should sever one thread from it, and it is permitted, for we assume that the thread of kilayim was the one he removed.” It does not refer to the first alternative, which is the dyeing test. This is also clear from the Tur’s wording: “but with Torah-level kilayim we do not rely upon an uncertainty to be lenient.” The expression “we do not rely” belongs to the second alternative, where he explicitly wrote that “we rely” upon the assumption, whereas with respect to the first alternative the terminology of relying upon an assumption is inapplicable. Such terminology applies to an act already performed in a case of uncertainty, where we attribute the act to the permitted possibility, which is precisely the latter case. This also explains why the Tur had to elaborate and say, “we do not rely upon an uncertainty to be lenient”; he could otherwise simply have written, “but not with Torah-level kilayim.” It therefore appears clear as I have written. There is no dispute on this point between the Tur and the Rambam and Raavad; all agree that dyeing is an effective examination even for Torah-level kilayim. The Beit Yosef writes that the phrase “When does this apply?” refers to the dyeing test as well, and according to him the Tur disagrees with the Rambam and Raavad. The Rama here followed the Beit Yosef and wrote, “When does this apply,” and so forth. It appears to me, with due respect for their honor, that they did not examine the matter as fully as required, for the Gemara explicitly proves that the dyeing test is effective even for a Torah prohibition, just as examination is effective for Torah-level corpse impurity, as I have explained.
