Liability. If the lender does not trust the borrower concerning a loss of principal or profit, he should write in the iska document that the borrower is not believed concerning a loss of principal except on the testimony of reputable valid witnesses, and concerning profit he is believed only upon taking a stringent oath acceptable to the holder of the document. This excludes those who customarily write in the document that he is not believed concerning profit either except through valid witnesses, for this constitutes outright interest. Thus wrote Ateret Zekenim and Bach. It appears that this applies specifically here, where there is an iska document. In an actual loan, however, where the interest is forbidden by the Torah, it is prohibited to stipulate even concerning the principal that the borrower will not be believed except on the testimony of two valid witnesses, as stated below in siman 177. These are the words of Siftei Kohen.
He stipulated. The Perishah wrote that even if he did not stipulate this at the outset, any amount of compensation is sufficient. This differs from an iska, discussed below at the beginning of siman 177, where he pays him compensation as an idle worker. There, he works simultaneously with his own half and the lender's half. Here, by contrast, when he conducts the business there is no loan at all; it is only comparable to interest paid in advance or afterward. Therefore, even without a prior stipulation, any amount is sufficient. In the Derishah he wrote that it appears that if he stipulated with him at the outset and gives him some compensation so that the one conducting the business accepts liability, this is permissible. It resembles stipulating that he receive a small amount for his labor, and it resembles stipulating with an unpaid custodian that he have the liability of a borrower, as stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 291. Turei Zahav disputes this. He writes that the essential basis for permission in this case is that, when the money earns a maneh, it is still a deposit in his possession and not a loan. If he accepts liability even then, the money is in his possession as a loan, and this constitutes outright interest. What benefit is there in the lender paying the borrower compensation for accepting liability? This resembles one who gives a borrower a gift so that the borrower will borrow money from him at interest; the present case is precisely the same. Therefore, this proposed permission should be disregarded. See below, siman 177, se'if 6, and siman 172, se'if 3. He further wrote concerning the permitted stipulation that the borrower will not be believed about loss of principal except on the testimony of two valid witnesses: this applies only if those witnesses have some knowledge of the borrower's business. It does not apply where people select witnesses who know nothing at all about the commercial dealings, doing so specifically so that they will be unable to testify to a loss. This is certainly completely prohibited, for the arrangement makes gain likely and removes all possibility of loss. Levush wrote that a remedy can be found for those who lend by means of documents: the borrower should write that he owes the lender merchandise currently in his home, which he sells to the lender cheaply and undertakes to provide at a specified time. If he fails to do so, he will owe a large sum, even twice the amount the lender gave him. This is entirely permissible, provided that it is true and known that the borrower possesses that merchandise in his home at that time, and that he immediately transfers the merchandise to the lender through a valid act of acquisition performed in connection with the debt. These are his words, and they are based on Shulchan Aruch, siman 177, se'if 18. It appears clear to me that this permission applies only if he did not first say, "Lend me money," but instead immediately said, "I have merchandise, and I will sell it to you cheaply," etc. If he first said, "Lend me money," and only afterward arranged this permitted form with him, it is prohibited. We likewise find in siman 163, se'if 3: if one said, "Lend me a maneh," and the other answered, "I do not have a maneh, but I have wheat worth a maneh," etc., it is prohibited because he first said, "Lend me." One should therefore not rely on this arrangement if he first requested a monetary loan. Nekudot HaKesef wrote that this is not comparable to siman 163. There, for what purpose does he sell him the wheat, since he then buys it back from him cheaply? This is an evasion of the prohibition against interest. Moreover, there he sells goods worth ninety for one hundred, charging more in exchange for waiting for the money, and this is outright interest. Here, however, even though he said, "Lend me," it may be that the lender needs the money to purchase merchandise and therefore does not wish to lend it. When he sees that this person is selling cheaply, he buys from him. There is no prohibition in doing so when the seller possesses the merchandise, for every person may sell his goods for less than their value. There is no evasion of the prohibition against interest because the merchandise initially belonged to the seller.
