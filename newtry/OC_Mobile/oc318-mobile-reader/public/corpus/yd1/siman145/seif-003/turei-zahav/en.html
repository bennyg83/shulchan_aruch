The house itself is worshipped. For otherwise, if it merely serves as a place in which to set up idolatry, we rule that appurtenances of idolatry do not become forbidden until they are actually used. But if the house itself is worshipped, it becomes forbidden immediately, because we rule at the beginning of siman 139 that idolatry belonging to a non-Jew becomes forbidden immediately. What the Tur writes here, 'if it belongs to a Jew,' etc., means that in any event a non-Jew made it and later sold it to a Jew. But if a Jew made it, it does not become forbidden until it is worshipped. So wrote the Beit Yosef. I saw someone explain the case here as involving an apostate Jew, whose status in this matter would be like that of a non-Jew. His statement is utterly mistaken, for in this respect and for all punishments of the Torah his status remains that of a Jew, as written at the beginning of siman 139. The Ran writes that although regarding a tree it does not become forbidden, as above at the beginning of this siman, merely because one bowed to it, and we do not say it was originally detached and later attached, a tree is different because its roots are strongly attached to the ground and it is considered attached from the outset, as stated at the beginning of Chullin. Rashi explains that the case of a tree concerns one planted from a seed, but if one planted a cutting, it is in fact detached and later attached and is treated as detached property.
