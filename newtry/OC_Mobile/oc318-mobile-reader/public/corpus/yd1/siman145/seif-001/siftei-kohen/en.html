And even stones, etc. This is Rambam's and the Tur's view. The Ran questions it: since the Gemara leaves the matter as a dispute, it should be treated as a Torah-level doubt stringently; he writes that Ramah indeed rules stringently, and the Beit Yosef cites him. The Bach and Kesef Mishneh attempt to defend Rambam, but their explanations are difficult, and Rabbeinu Yerucham and Raavad likewise rule stringently. Nevertheless, Rambam's view appears clear. The Bavli is uncertain whether Chizkiyah permits and R' Yochanan forbids or the reverse, while the Jerusalem Talmud explicitly records that Chizkiyah permits and R' Yochanan forbids. We do not abandon what is explicit in the Jerusalem Talmud because the Bavli is uncertain about the attribution. Moreover, R' Yochanan himself compiled the Jerusalem Talmud, so it is clear that he is the one who forbids; consequently the halacha follows Chizkiyah, who permits. I later found the same explanation in Toras Chaim.
And public springs. The Levush writes that 'public' is not precise and merely follows the Gemara's wording; the same law applies to a privately owned river or spring, because the reason is that something attached to the ground cannot become forbidden through worship. The distinction between public and private applies only with respect to use for libations. His words are correct according to the Gemara. What the Prisha writes, that with a private spring perhaps it was dug for idolatry and becomes forbidden through the act of digging, requires study. Detached water, even if public, can become forbidden if worshipped; attached water cannot become forbidden even if private. This is why Rambam and the Tur may specifically mention public springs: even for public water, the permission for ordinary benefit applies only where the water remains attached to its source.
And trees etc. It implies even a shoot is considered attached and is not forbidden when one bows to them; and this is per the Ran's view; and he wrote it is not comparable to a house (seif 3) which is forbidden — because what was detached and finally attached is like detached — for here since the tree has roots in the ground it is considered attached; and so the later authorities ruled.
That was not planted etc. But one planted for idolatry is forbidden — and this is the asherah stated in the Torah, Gemara, and poskim; and planting alone for an idol makes it forbidden even if he did not bow to it afterwards; and specifically planted by a gentile — but planted by Israel it is not forbidden until it is worshipped, as below.
The body of the tree. Meaning: all that is there at the time of worship — fruits, branches, and leaves — all are permitted; and only what grows out after it was worshipped is forbidden. So wrote Beit Yosef and the later authorities; and so explained in the Rambam's words.
