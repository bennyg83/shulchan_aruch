In their place. Taz writes that even if they were completely uprooted, provided this was not done by human action, they do not become forbidden.
Public. The same applies to a privately owned river or spring, because the underlying reason is that attached property cannot become forbidden. The distinction between private and public applies only to libations. Shach and Taz. Nekudot HaKesef clarifies that public ownership alone does not permit detached property; even public property is permitted only because it remains attached.
Become forbidden. Shach infers that even a planted shoot is considered attached and is not forbidden merely by bowing to it. This differs from a house, which is detached property later attached and retains the status of detached property. A tree has roots in the ground and is truly attached. If planted for idolatry, however, it is forbidden; this is the asherah of the Torah, even if no one later bowed to it. This applies when a non-Jew planted it. A Jew's planting does not prohibit until he worships it.
That it produces. All fruit, branches, and leaves present when it was worshipped remain permitted. Only later growth is forbidden. Beit Yosef and the later authorities.
