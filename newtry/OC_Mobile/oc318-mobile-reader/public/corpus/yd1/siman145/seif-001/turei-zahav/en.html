Anything that has no human handiwork, etc. For it is written "their gods upon the mountains" — and not "the mountains their gods."
And even mountain stones that became dislodged. Meaning: they were not completely uprooted, for no person lifted them — so wrote the Kesef Mishneh; and in my humble opinion it appears they were completely uprooted, only not done by a person — for in chapter R. Yishmael at 59a he compares to this: water detached from its source by a wave.
And likewise public rivers and springs. Rambam and the Tur also write this. The difficulty is that in Avodah Zarah 59 the Gemara challenges R' Yochanan's statement that public water cannot become forbidden: if private water can become forbidden, why should that be so when water attached to the ground is not prohibited through worship? The Gemara answers that the non-Jew scooped the water up with his hand, making it detached. Thus when the water remains attached, even private water should be permitted. The Levush independently raised this point. The Prisha suggests that public water is permitted because we do not suspect it was dug for idolatry, whereas with private water perhaps it was dug for that purpose and thereby became prohibited through an act. This is difficult. The Gemara explicitly indicates that private attached water is also permitted. Moreover, digging the spring does not constitute an act upon the water itself; the worship is directed toward water that emerges on its own. The Gemara therefore finds a prohibition only where the non-Jew physically detached the water. Tosafos likewise indicate that naturally flowing public water is permitted. It appears that the distinction between public and private water applies not to ordinary benefit but to suitability for libations. This resolves Rambam and the Tur: attached water is permitted for ordinary benefit even when privately owned, while the special reference to public springs addresses the law of libations.
