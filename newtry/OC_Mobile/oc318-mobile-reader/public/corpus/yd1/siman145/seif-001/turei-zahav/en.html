Anything that has no human handiwork, etc. For it is written "their gods upon the mountains" — and not "the mountains their gods."
And even mountain stones that became dislodged. Meaning: they were not completely uprooted, for no person lifted them — so wrote the Kesef Mishneh; and in my humble opinion it appears they were completely uprooted, only not done by a person — for in chapter R. Yishmael at 59a he compares to this: water detached from its source by a wave.
And likewise rivers and springs belonging to the public. So too wrote Rambam and the Tur — and this is difficult: for in chapter R' Yishmael (Avodah Zarah 59), the Talmud challenges R' Yochanan's statement in the name of R' Shimon b. Yehozadak — "water belonging to the public is not forbidden" — [implying] that water of an individual is forbidden; but we can derive that even that is not forbidden since it is attached to the ground! The Talmud answers: it is not necessary — the case is where the gentile struck it with his hand — meaning he detached it. Hence if it belonged to an individual it would be forbidden; and if so, here where we deal with attached water, the same challenge should apply to the decisors: water of an individual too is not forbidden, since it is attached! And so the Levush raised this from his own reasoning — that attached water is not forbidden even for an individual. And in Perisha it is written: the reason water of the public is permitted is that we do not say they dug it for idolatry; but water of an individual — we say he dug it for idolatry and it is forbidden even when attached, since he performed an act upon it. And he was not precise regarding the Talmud I cited, which explicitly challenges: even water of an individual is permitted since it is still attached — the only case of forbidden individual water the Talmud can find is where he struck it with his hand and made it detached. And furthermore, what he wrote — that it is forbidden through an act — does not seem correct to me: even though they said in seif 7 that one who digs cisterns, etc., is forbidden because of an act — that is different, since there he prostrates himself before the cistern which is his own handiwork; but here where he prostrates himself before water that arrives of its own accord — even though it comes because of the digging — in what he prostrated himself before, he did not himself perform an act; and this is proven from the fact that the Talmud I cited could not find a case where an individual's water is forbidden unless when he struck it with his hand and made it detached. And furthermore, it is clarified in Tosafot (47a) who gave the reason that water flowing of itself and belonging to the public is permitted, and that of an individual is forbidden — and the Talmud challenges: but it is attached — and answers: he detached it with his hand. In order to resolve the above difficulty it seems to me: since in chapter Kol HaTzelamim (Avodah Zarah 47) Rabba asked: one who prostrates before a spring — what is the status of its water for libations? — and the conclusion there seems to be that even for the altar, water of the public is not forbidden; but water of an individual is forbidden even when attached — since with respect to the altar there is a case of "worshipped item in attachment" near the altar, as we say above that Rava resolved for Rami bar Chama that there is a worshipped item in something attached near the altar. Therefore the decisors wrote that springs of the public are not forbidden at all, even for the altar; and one should not say that even when not attached it is permitted for the altar since they belong to the public — for this only applies to springs of fresh running water specifically brought for libations; and furthermore, the principal ruling comes to permit attached water, and incidentally teaches us that even for the altar there is a leniency — namely when they belong to the public. So it seems to me to strain this interpretation and settle their words — and I am puzzled that Beit Yosef did not notice this.
