And even receiving permission, etc. The Raavad, the Rashba in responsum 111, and the Rivash in siman 271 hold that receiving permission is effective even within three parsangs.
All this applies to one's primary teacher. All this follows the Rambam's view. Others maintain that even a colleague-student is forbidden within three parsangs, as stated below.
Others say, literally, etc. The word 'literally' does not refer to 'his teacher,' but to 'in his presence.' Meaning, directly in his presence even a colleague-student is forbidden.
Others say that a full student, etc. These authorities do not distinguish between an occasional ruling and a fixed practice, as is evident from their words; see there. This also appears from the Tur.
Others say this applies specifically where he regularly comes. Meaning, at set intervals, such as market day or Mondays and Thursdays. It appears that market day, like Mondays and Thursdays, means a weekly market day. An annual market, such as a fair, is not considered regular attendance.
But if he does not regularly come, etc., it is permitted whenever he is beyond three parsangs. This applies even if he is his primary teacher, as stated in the Mordechai there.
Beyond twelve mil it is permitted, etc. This implies even without receiving permission. Tosafot at the beginning of Sanhedrin 5b likewise proves this. They write that when Rav Hamnuna did not rule in Charta of Argiz during the lifetime of Rav Huna his teacher, he had not received permission, although he was beyond three parsangs. In Eruvin 63a the Gemara says that only during Rav Huna's lifetime did he refrain from ruling, whereas during Rav Chisda's lifetime he ruled, because he was a colleague-student. The Hagahot Maimoniyot, however, chapter 5 of Hilchot Talmud Torah, writes that permission is nevertheless required. See Maharik, siman 170, who discusses at length how to reconcile their words.
Although he received permission, etc. This refers to a full student, as the ruling concludes that he must receive permission from all his primary teachers. Regarding a colleague-student, Maharik also writes that permission from his teacher is sufficient. Maharik further implies that if one teacher is primary and the others are not, he must receive permission from the primary teacher.
And the term 'primary teachers,' etc., means, etc. The Rama's words appear to mean that according to this view, even if one did not learn most of his wisdom from a teacher, so long as he has not grown to be near his teacher's level he is called his student. Yet in my humble opinion this is incorrect. The Rambam and Maharik do not disagree. Maharik himself writes at the beginning of the responsum that only a primary student, meaning one who learned most of his wisdom from his teacher, is bound by all these laws. One who did not learn most of his wisdom from him owes only the limited honor of standing before him and rending garments for him. Maharik proves from the Rambam that the prohibitions against establishing a study hall, ruling in one's teacher's presence, disputing him, and similar matters apply only to a primary teacher. Even according to those who rule like Rabbi Yosi, that illuminating even one mishnah creates a teacher-student bond, this applies only to standing and rending garments, not to all the other laws. Maharik's later ruling creates a separate principle: even where one learned most of his wisdom from his teacher, if he later became equally learned he is a colleague-student. He proves this from Reish Lakish, who was a full student of Rabbi Yochanan and was ordained by him, yet later disputed and ruled before him. The Darkei Moshe and the Rivash likewise support this. But where one did not learn most of his wisdom from a teacher, he is not called his full student even if he never reached the teacher's level. The Rivash explicitly defines a colleague-student as one who did not learn most of his wisdom from him. The objection that one cannot have several primary teachers is unfounded, for Rashi explains that one may have learned most of his Scripture from one teacher, most of his Mishnah from another, most of his Gemara from another, and similarly Midrash, Aggadah, Kabbalah, and other areas of Torah. Thus one may indeed have several primary teachers. This also accords with the Mechaber below, seif 30, who defines a non-primary teacher as one from whom he did not learn most of his wisdom, while here requiring permission from all his primary teachers.
It is effective to permit him to rule. Meaning beyond three parsangs. Within three parsangs, permission from one teacher is not effective. This implies that permission from all his teachers is effective, or from his sole teacher if he has only one. This follows the Rashba and those who agree with him. Others disagree and hold that within three parsangs permission is ineffective, as above in seif katan 4.
