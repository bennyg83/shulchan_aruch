Not to rule until age forty. Shevut Yaakov limits this to rulings in ritual prohibition and permission. One may adjudicate monetary law before forty, though sitting alone should be avoided unless no greater authority is present; joining a panel of three is permitted.
In an explicit Mishnah. Levushei Serad distinguishes a ruling supported by two reasons, one contradicted by an explicit source and one by accepted analytical consensus. The legal status depends on which reason the decisor actually relied upon. Shevut Yaakov also rules that where an agent discarded food after an erroneous ruling in an explicit matter, both decisor and agent are exempt from payment.
He may permit. Mishkenot Yaakov defends Rama against Shach: a second sage may permit based on a true received tradition only where the first sage would acknowledge that tradition. If the first sage would still dispute it, the second may not override him.
Even if he erred. See Shach and Panim Meirot, who challenges his entire analysis.
The second may not forbid. Radbaz disagrees and rules that if the first decisor permitted through an error of judgment, the ruling may be reversed and the item forbidden even against his will. The first decisor himself may not continue acting leniently when the majority concludes he erred.
In another case. Shivat Tzion limits the second sage's freedom to rule differently to a case actually brought before him. He should not volunteer to overturn another sage's ruling without being asked.
