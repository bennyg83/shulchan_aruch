Until forty years. This implies forty years from birth, as Rashi and the Ran explain. Tosafot in Sotah 22b explains instead forty years from when he began learning.
If there is someone greater than he. This implies that if they are equal he may rule even before forty, following Rashi and the Ran. Tosafot explains that the restriction applies where he equals the city's senior scholar in wisdom, but the senior has reached the age of ruling while he has not. If he is the greater scholar of the city, he may rule even before that age. This explains why the Rif and Rosh omit the Gemara's qualification regarding equals: they hold like Tosafot, and it is obvious that the city's greatest scholar must rule, for otherwise who will? Their view should therefore be treated stringently.
Greater than he. Or older than he in the city, as Rashi explains in Sotah. The elder must nevertheless equal him in wisdom. The Agudah likewise writes that 'many victims she has felled' refers to a Torah scholar who rules before forty while there is an elder in the city who knows as much as he does.
A sage who prohibited, etc., his colleague may not, etc. This applies once the ruling has taken effect. But if both sages are together in the study hall, the second may permit, as the poskim cited below explain.
His colleague may not, etc. According to the Raavad, Ran, and Rivash, this applies even if the second sage is greater in wisdom and number. Maharik, however, appears to hold that the rule applies only to an equal colleague; one greater than the first may permit what the first forbade. The Ran and Rabbeinu Yerucham also state that the restriction applies where they are equal, but a greater sage may permit even a matter dependent on reasoning.
To permit, etc. Even if the second sage did permit it, it remains forbidden. So write the Ran in the Raavad's name and the Rivash, siman 379.
But if he has a received tradition, etc. The Rama follows the Darkei Moshe citing Rabbeinu Yerucham and Rashi. Yet Tosafot, Hagahot Ashri, Maharshal, Piskei Tosafot, Piskei Maharam, the Ran, and the Rashba hold that even one who has a received tradition may not overturn the first sage's prohibition. The supposed proof from Rashi at the end of Kol HaYad appears opposite: the later sage relied on his tradition only because he was certain of purity and because the first sage himself ordinarily ruled it pure, having initially ruled stringently merely out of honor. Had he remained uncertain, he could not have relied on his tradition against the first sage's ruling. The texts cited by those who permit may have had another version of Rashi. Our version, together with Tosafot and the Semag, supports the stringent ruling. They explain the cases where one sage forbade and another permitted as cases in which both were together in the study hall, disagreed before either ruling spread, and neither ruling had yet taken effect. The Rosh and Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise appear to accept this as the primary halachah.
A received tradition that he erred, etc. Meaning, he has a received tradition that the law is otherwise, so the first sage's ruling was mistaken.
In a matter of Mishnah. See Choshen Mishpat, beginning of siman 25, for the definition of an error in a matter of Mishnah.
Even if he erred in discretionary judgment. This applies specifically where the error can be established, such as when two Tannaim, Amoraim, or poskim disagree, the first authority ruled like one, and accepted practice follows the other. Tosafot, the Rosh, and Rabbeinu Yerucham explain that the second sage must first demonstrate the error and cause the first to retract. If the dispute is merely one of reasoning and the second cannot prove the error from a Mishnah or an Amora, he may state his own opinion but may not permit what the first forbade, because the item has acquired the status of a prohibited object through the first ruling. This remains so even if the first sage is persuaded by the second's reasoning, unless the error can be established from an authoritative source or from accepted practice. Where the original ruling was demonstrably mistaken, the status of a prohibited object never validly took effect. The Raavad and Rashba can likewise be understood this way: an unresolved matter of judgment cannot be reversed, whereas a demonstrable error in judgment is treated like an error in a Mishnah. This interpretation harmonizes their view with Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Gemara in Sanhedrin, and is contrary to the implication of the Levush that any retraction based on reasoning permits reversal.
Even if the first permitted and his ruling already took effect, etc. The Darkei Moshe cites the Rosh in the Yerushalmi's name, but Tosafot, the Semag, Hagahot Maimoniyot, Maharshal in Maharam's name, and other authorities disagree. They hold that one may not permit what another sage already forbade, but one may forbid what another permitted even after the permissive ruling took effect. They explain the Yerushalmi accordingly. This also follows the Raavad, Rashba, Ran, and Rivash, who ground the restriction in the item's having become a prohibited object, a rationale that applies only where the first sage forbade it.
But in another case, etc. If the first sage prohibited as a stringency, protective measure, or fence, the second may not permit even in another case. Maharshal writes this according to the Mordechai.
