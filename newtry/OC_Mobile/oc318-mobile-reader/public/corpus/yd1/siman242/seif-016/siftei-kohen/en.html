He says to him with reverence and honor. He says, 'Shalom alecha, Rabbi.' If his teacher greeted him first, he responds, 'Shalom alecha, Rabbi and my master.' This is the correct text, as in the Tur, Rambam, and old editions of the Mechaber.
He should not remove his tefillin, etc. See Be'er Sheva, page 72, column 4, and the Beit Yosef and Bach to Orach Chaim, siman 38, who discuss this at length. See also the end of Orach Chaim, siman 27.
He receives permission from his teacher, etc. It appears that if his father is seated at a meal with others, he first asks permission from his father and then from the others. This is the wording in the versions of Birkat HaMazon and is the practice. If both his father and his primary teacher, who taught him without payment, are reclining, he first asks permission from his teacher and then from his father, as below in seif 34.
He should not pray, etc. The reason for all these restrictions is that he should not place himself on an equal footing with his teacher. Tosafot explains that praying directly behind him appears as though he bows to his teacher. Rabbeinu Yonah explains it as creating an interruption. Rav Hai Gaon writes that the prohibition against praying alongside one's teacher applies only in private. In a congregation one does not display special honor in this manner, and it is permitted to pray alongside him. The Beit Yosef and other authorities rule likewise.
Nor behind him, etc. The Gemara in Berachot states that a colleague-student is permitted. The Mechaber rules likewise in Orach Chaim, siman 90, seif 25. Yet the Beit Yosef there writes that to pray with one's back toward his teacher is forbidden even for a colleague-student, because it is a great disrespect.
Beyond four cubits everything is permitted. The Beit Yosef writes that four cubits of distance helps only when praying behind one's teacher while facing him. To pray with one's back toward his teacher remains forbidden even at a greater distance. The Tur, however, appears to permit even this beyond four cubits, and the Bach agrees, because it is considered a separate domain.
If he was already there, etc. Mahari Weil derives this from the Mordechai's ruling that if a Jew was already in a bathhouse and non-Jews later entered, he need not leave. This requires study, for there the reason is that they entered his boundary, which does not necessarily apply to the honor of one's teacher.
This is the widespread practice, etc. The Rama writes likewise in Even HaEzer, end of siman 23.
Specifically where he did not say, etc. The Ran derives this from the account that the people took leave of Shlomo and then, after remaining overnight in the city, took leave again. The Ran explains that they initially intended to depart the next day, so they did not need a second farewell until they delayed an additional day. Yet the text he cites appears mistaken, for Scripture and the Gemara state that they were sent on the twenty-third, not the twenty-fourth. According to the correct text and the plain meaning of the verse, they had intended to return home on the twenty-third, and nevertheless needed to take leave again. This implies that a second farewell is required in every case, and the distinction requires study.
He should not decide between his teacher's statements, etc. See above, siman 240, seif 2.
Until he is hidden from his sight, etc. Or until the teacher sits, as below in siman 244, seif 9.
Some say a person is not obligated, etc. Although the Rambam, Tur, and poskim say that one is 'not permitted,' the Rama explains that this means he is not obligated. The Bach and Semag write likewise.
But in the presence of others, etc. Others say that since the Torah equated fear of one's teacher with fear of Heaven, one must stand even one hundred times a day. If one received the Divine Presence many times in a day, would he not be obligated to stand each time? This is from the Rosh and Tur.
