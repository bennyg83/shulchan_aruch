Because they are suspected of bestiality. A gentile is prohibited in this, as Scripture says, "and they shall become one flesh," excluding animals and beasts whose flesh does not become one with his. There is therefore a prohibition of lifnei iver, placing a stumbling block before the blind. This is stated at the beginning of Perek Ein Ma'amidin. It implies that the only concern is lifnei iver. If the animal was already left with the gentile and the prohibition was violated, there is no resulting prohibition upon eating the animal, even if it is a kosher species. Otherwise, the Gemara should have taught that a kosher animal becomes forbidden after the fact. Rather, the meat remains permitted even if a gentile certainly committed bestiality with it, and the same applies if a Jew did so. The reason is that Bava Kamma 96 teaches regarding an animal with which a transgression was committed that not all oxen are destined for the altar, as Rashi explains there. Thus the animal is disqualified from the altar but remains permitted to ordinary use. At first glance one might bring proof to prohibit from Tosafot, Zevachim 71a, s.v. berove'a, which says that according to Rashi an animal known to have committed or undergone such an act by one witness or by admission of the owners is prohibited to the altar but not to ordinary consumption, whereas with two witnesses it would be prohibited even to ordinary use, and this also appears in Perek HaIsh Mekadesh. Temurah 28 likewise says that testimony of two witnesses disqualifies it from eating. In truth, those sources prove the opposite. The reason it becomes prohibited for eating when there are two witnesses is, as Rashi explains in Temurah, because the animal is then liable to stoning. That applies only after its judgment has been concluded. Rashi in Kiddushin 56 likewise says an animal that committed or underwent such an act is prohibited to ordinary use only after the judgment is concluded. The Gemara in Bava Kamma 41 states explicitly: from "the ox shall surely be stoned" do we not already know it is a carcass and a carcass is prohibited to eat? Why then does Scripture add, "its flesh shall not be eaten"? To teach that if it was slaughtered after its judgment was concluded, its meat is prohibited. It follows expressly that if slaughtered before conclusion of judgment, it is permitted. Accordingly, the Temurah passage and Tosafot in Zevachim refer to after the animal was sentenced to stoning. Nowadays, therefore, there is no prohibition upon eating it even if a Jew committed bestiality with it in the presence of two witnesses, since there is no judicial conclusion sentencing the animal. I saw someone rule the meat prohibited in such a case, and that ruling is mistaken.
