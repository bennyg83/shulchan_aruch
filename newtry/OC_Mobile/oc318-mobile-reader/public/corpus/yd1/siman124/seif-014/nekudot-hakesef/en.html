(Ibid.) There is room for great astonishment, etc.; if so, why did the Tur write, etc. This is no difficulty. The Tur stated the matter according to the approach of his father and teacher, the Rosh. At the end of chapter Rabbi Yishmael, the Rosh cites Rabbeinu Tam, and Beit Yosef cites his words at the beginning of siman 125. Rabbeinu Tam explicitly maintains that a non-Jew's unintentional contact through another object is permitted even for drinking, as stated in the Rosh and throughout the poskim. Yet the Rosh repeatedly states there that intentional contact through another object is forbidden even for benefit. In the incident of the non-Jew who climbed a palm tree and, upon descending, touched the wine with the end of a palm branch, the Rosh discusses Rashi's view that unintentional contact through another object forbids the wine for drinking. He writes that Rabbeinu Tam challenges this from the later passage concerning one who took a barrel and threw it into a pit in anger. The Gemara permits it specifically when done in anger, but not otherwise, and answers that the barrel continued moving through the non-Jew's force until it fell into the pit. When it was not done in anger, we are concerned that he touched and poured a libation; when done in anger, we are not concerned. Although he touches the wine through the barrel, this is unintentional contact through another object and is permitted for drinking. Alternatively, when not done in anger it is intentional contact through another object and is forbidden for benefit. The Sages permitted measuring with a rod only because he intended to measure, but if he inserted the rod without that purpose the wine is forbidden. Rabbeinu Tam therefore adopts the version that does not mention the end of the palm branch, but states that the non-Jew touched the wine itself, making it unintentional bodily contact. Contact through another object without intent, however, is permitted even for drinking. Thus, although Rabbeinu Tam permits unintentional contact through another object even for drinking, he forbids intentional contact through another object even for benefit. This demonstrates that intentional contact is more severe than force exerted by the non-Jew. The same implication arises from the other poskim, and the Ran himself also indicates that some maintain that intentional contact through another object is more severe than the non-Jew's force; see there.
