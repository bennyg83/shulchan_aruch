Know that his statement, 'If the spigot was inserted through the entire bottom and the non-Jew removed it, the wine is forbidden even for us,' is his own formulation and is not found in Mordechai. In truth, the ruling is incorrect. One who examines Mordechai will see that the passage is a responsum of Rabbi Shmuel bar Baruch, who ruled that even if the thief touched the barrel with a knife, since he intended only to pierce it so that the wine would flow out, this is force exerted unintentionally and is permitted according to Rabbeinu Tam's reading. The ruling that, for us, contact through another object is permitted even when intentional appears much later in Mordechai in the name of Rabbi Chizkiyah. Mordechai contains many similar responsa permitting wine because the non-Jew's contact through another object was unintentional. All of them state the law as it applies under the Gemara's rules, with a practical difference in a place where actual idolaters are present. For us, however, it is clear that even if the non-Jew intentionally removed the spigot, the wine is permitted, since intentional contact through another object is permitted for us. Rama's statement that the non-Jew intended only to pierce the barrel is nevertheless accurate, as stated in Mordechai. Its practical significance is that the wine is permitted even where there is no financial loss, whereas we rely on the principle that contemporary non-Jews are not idolaters only in a case of loss. His further challenge from Rama's ruling at the end of siman 129 concerning a maidservant who is not believed is likewise no difficulty. Rama concludes there that in the present era, when non-Jews do not pour libations, the wine is permitted. This conclusion refers to everything mentioned previously, as I wrote there in Shach, seif katan 42 and 43, unlike what he wrote there in paragraph 32. His citation of siman 129, seif 1, is certainly a scribal error and should read siman 130, seif 1, namely, as he himself wrote there in paragraph 1, although his conclusion there is incorrect. His proposed reason for forbidding the wine even for us when the non-Jew removes the spigot, namely, that he likely touched the flowing stream with his hand, is astonishing. Even if he touched it, he did not intend to touch the wine, and unintentional contact by a non-Jew is permitted for us even when made with his hand. His citation from Mordechai, chapter Rabbi Yishmael, actually refutes him. Mordechai states that when the non-Jew removed and replaced the spigot, it was impossible that his hand did not touch the flowing stream, making this unintentional bodily contact and therefore forbidden. Mordechai thus forbids it only because unintentional bodily contact was forbidden under the original law. For us, where unintentional contact is permitted, the wine is plainly permitted. Mordechai's ruling in chapter HaSocher concerning a wide opening refers to intentional contact. See further what I wrote at the end of siman 129 and the beginning of siman 130.
