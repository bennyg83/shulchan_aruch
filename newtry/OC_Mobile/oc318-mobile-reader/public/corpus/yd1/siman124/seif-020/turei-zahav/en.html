And they brought him up from there dead. The wording implies that he died before being brought up. However, if he was alive when brought up and then died immediately afterward, we are concerned that at that moment he offered praise to his deity, as stated in the Gemara, that it is regarded by him like their festival day. Rashi explains: “He goes and gives thanks for having been saved, and presumably he poured it as a libation while being brought up.” End quote. It appears that the Gemara needed this reasoning and did not simply say that he presumably poured it as a libation like other non-Jews, since at that point he was no longer under duress. Rather, it is discussing even a case in which at that moment he was dying and in mortal danger; nevertheless, he gives thanks and praise that he was saved from immediate death. According to this, what the Tur wrote is clear: “Therefore, a minor who does not understand the nature of idolatry and its attendants, or an adult who fell into a pit and came up dead, the wine is permitted for benefit.” The Beit Yosef explained that the Tur repeated the matters, first stating the rule generally and then giving a detail for each case. Regarding the minor, he gave the detail that he does not understand the nature of idolatry; regarding one who fell into a pit, the Beit Yosef did not know what new detail was given and said that it was mentioned incidentally together with the others. According to what I wrote, however, it is clear. The Tur teaches that it is specifically when he came up dead, meaning that he was dead at the time he was brought up, and not otherwise. For had he died afterward, the Tur should have said, “he came up and died.” A parallel appears in the variant readings in the chapter Eilu Tereifos regarding worms, whether the text reads “it separated dead” or “it separated and died,” as the Beit Yosef cites above in siman 84. This is what the Tur teaches, that only when he was dead at the time he was brought up is the wine permitted for benefit. This appears to me correct. So too it appears from the words of the lengthy Terumas HaDeshen cited by the Beit Yosef, who wrote that the same applies to any other compulsion that does not involve imminent death, which is considered compulsion. The Gemara teaches that one should not say that since he fell into a pit and was close to death, he is panicked, and even if he came up alive he would not turn his attention to pour the wine as a libation. It therefore teaches that once he was saved, he turns his attention and gives thanks, etc.
And it also rolled outside, so that he remained under duress even at the end. It is permitted for benefit. This is the correct text.
