(Ibid., seif 27, gloss) A non-Jew who touched a Jew's wine in order to provoke him, etc. An incident occurred while I was in Moravia. A non-Jew touched my wine, and I refused to drink it. He said to me: 'Drink, for the wine is permitted. Ask your father and he will tell you that it is permitted, because I did this only to provoke you so that you would not drink, and wine touched merely to provoke is permitted.' I asked my father and teacher, the gaon of blessed memory, what the ruling was. He answered that the wine was forbidden. The reason wine touched merely to provoke is permitted is that the non-Jew's purpose is only to provoke. Since this non-Jew knew the law, however, his act was not truly one of provocation, and the original prohibition returns as though he had not acted merely to provoke. One might object that wine touched to provoke is permitted even when the non-Jew is no longer present, because he did not intend to pour a libation but only to provoke. Here too, he did not intend to pour a libation, but only to demonstrate that he knew the law. Nevertheless, the matter may be explained as follows. Since he knew that wine touched merely to provoke is permitted, he never acted merely to provoke; rather, he touched it with intent to render it forbidden. His instruction to ask about the law was designed to cause the Jew to stumble and drink forbidden wine. This is unlike a non-Jew who does not know the law and touches the wine merely to cause the Jew a loss by preventing him from drinking it; in that case the wine is permitted. Here, because he knew that an act done merely to provoke does not forbid the wine, his act must be viewed as intended to render it forbidden. The matter requires study.
