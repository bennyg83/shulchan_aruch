In the Shach it is further explained that specifically if he does not want it at all, we do not compel him; but if he wants to receive it later, once it develops a blemish, they would compel him. See Chatam Sofer responsa siman 302, who gives a sound reason for this distinction. See Chavot Yair responsa simanim 38, 39, and 40, regarding an incident where Reuven sold Shimon a pregnant cow and Shimon had not yet paid. On the first night after the sale it gave birth to a male. Neither the seller nor the buyer had sold the fetus before birth, since they did not remember and did not imagine that its time to give birth had arrived. The buyer claimed it was a mistaken sale and did not want to pay. They compromised: the buyer would deduct two and a half gold coins from the sale price and take the cow and the calf, and so it was done. The buyer kept the cow with the calf for about seven weeks, and afterward sent it to one kohen, who did not want to accept it. With whom is the law? The judges of the holy community of Frankfurt answered that it is obvious that we do not compel the kohen to accept it, as Mahari wrote. In addition, one can argue that there is not such great denigration of the priestly gifts here, since in the kohen's place there is no place to keep the bekhor in its sanctity, avoiding shearing and work; there is also concern that a blemish will be made intentionally, especially if the kohen's claim is correct that the buyer was negligent in not selling it to a non-Jew. Certainly he need not accept it. The same question was asked before the Gaon Rabbi K. of Metz z"l with a slight variation, and he concluded that the buyer was not negligent at all for several reasons, and therefore the kohanim must tend to the bekhor. However, since from the question it appears to be a doubtful bekhor, like the law of one who buys an animal from a non-Jew, we do not compel them by force; rather, we inform them that if they refuse, they are denigrating the King's gift, and any kohen who does not acknowledge these gifts has no share in kehunah. It is proper that the buyer and the kohanim split between them the two and a half gold coins deducted from the purchase. The author z"l also concluded that the buyer was not negligent at all, and he discusses further at length in a similar matter involving claims between buyer and seller, but this is not the place. He further wrote that he was asked about one who sold in an ineffective manner, as many ignorant people commonly do and stumble in this. The decisor ruled that it had not left the sanctity of a bekhor, and afterward the kohanim refused to accept it, saying that the seller was negligent by not selling it in an effective manner. He ruled that although this is close to intentional, for he should have asked a sage, nevertheless he is not called negligent but inadvertent, since he acted according to what he had heard and what he had seen from the corrupted practice.
