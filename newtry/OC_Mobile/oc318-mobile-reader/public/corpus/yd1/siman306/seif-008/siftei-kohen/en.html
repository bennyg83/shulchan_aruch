He may keep it for all twelve months. From its birth if its months were completed, or until a year is completed from the eighth day if its months were not completed. So wrote the Atteret Zahav, based on the Beit Yosef, and so wrote the Perishah. But in truth, this is not implied by the Rambam and Semag, positive commandment 211, whose language is like the Mechaber's. They wrote in the opening clause that if it was born blemished, he counts for it from the day it was born, etc., for it was fit for eating on the day of its birth, etc. This implies specifically where it was born blemished. Likewise, from the fact that they write here anonymously and do not distinguish, it implies that since it was not born blemished, we always count from the eighth day from birth. Although it is known that its months were completed, nevertheless it was not fit for eating on its day of birth, for it was unblemished. So wrote Rashi and Tosafot on the Talmud in the first chapter of Rosh Hashanah at the beginning of 7a: "this is with an unblemished animal," where he counts from the eighth day; "this is with a blemished animal," where he counts from the day it was born. The Gemara asks: can one eat a blemished animal? It answers: where he knows its months were completed. Rashi explains: a blemished animal that was born with its blemish and stands to be slaughtered outside, from the day it is born it is fit for this, and we count from when it is born. Tosafot wrote likewise: this is with an animal blemished from the outset; but an unblemished animal that later became blemished, since it was not fit for eating, we count from the time it became acceptable, meaning from the eighth day when it became acceptable as a sacrifice. So it appears; otherwise the Talmud's question would return to its place, "can one eat a blemished animal?" In truth, the Tur's phrase here, "he may keep it until a year from its birth is completed," pressed the Beit Yosef and those with him, and therefore they wrote this. They apparently understood that the Talmud's question, "can one eat a blemished animal?" was only because perhaps it was a nefel, but not because it was unblemished, since afterward it became blemished, for in any case we count from day eight when the blemish fell within its year even though after day eight it was unblemished. But in truth it appears that there is a scribal error in the Tur, and it should read: he may keep it until a year from the eighth day from its birth is completed. The Tur himself wrote in the opening clause that if it was born with its blemish and we know its months were completed, etc.; thus specifically born with its blemish. Moreover, otherwise he should have combined them all in one clause. Also, why would he need to write here "from its birth"? He should have written plainly. Moreover, according to that reading one must say that here it speaks where its months were completed, and this is not implied. If he relied on what he wrote in the opening clause, he should not have written "from its birth" at all. Rather, it is plainly a scribal error, and it should read "from the eighth day from its birth." On the contrary, it comes to teach that here there is no distinction, and we always count a year from the eighth day from its birth, since it was born unblemished and afterward a blemish fell in it. Nevertheless, we count from the eighth day even though then it was not fit for eating, for it is no worse than an unblemished animal, for which we count from day eight because it is fit for acceptance as a sacrifice. The same applies here. But when it is fit neither for acceptance nor for eating, certainly even if its months were completed we count from day eight, as we said.
