To accept it. The kohen commits a prohibition if he refuses to accept it. Nevertheless, beit din does not compel him, for he may tell the giver: Bring proof that it is a firstborn and I will care for it. Shach.
To provoke him. If it is evident that the owner acts merely to harass him, the kohen need not accept it. Mahari Weil. The Shach writes that the Rama's words can be reconciled only by emending them to read: This applies specifically to a doubt that arose by itself, but not to a doubt resulting from a dispute among the authorities, such as where one purchased a cow that was already giving milk from a non-Jew, or where the Jew was negligent although he could have sold the firstborn. Even so, the distinction between an inherent doubt and a dispute among the authorities requires study. Mahari Weil's responsum concerning a milk-producing cow purchased from a non-Jew states that the kohen must accept it, despite the dispute whether prior milk exempts from the law of the firstborn. It seems that leniency there rested on several doubts, including that the animal had been sold to a non-Jew for money, which some authorities regard as a valid sale, as stated in siman 320, seif 6, and that some authorities exempt a cow that had already produced milk. A single doubt arising from a dispute among the authorities is not sufficient for leniency unless the law fundamentally follows the lenient view and the contrary practice is merely a stringency. In such a case, perhaps the kohen need not accept it. Mahari Weil also ruled that when a poor kohen owns a firstborn and gives it to a wealthy kohen, the wealthy kohen need not accept it.
