To accept it. See Baer Heitev. Shach further explains that if the kohen does not want the firstborn at all, he is not compelled to accept it. But if he wishes to accept it later, after a blemish develops, he can be compelled to accept it now. Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 302, gives a sound reason for this distinction. See Responsa Chavot Yair, simanim 38 to 40, regarding Reuven who sold Shimon a pregnant cow before payment had been made. On the first night after the sale it gave birth to a male. Neither seller nor purchaser had sold the fetus to a gentile before birth because they had forgotten the matter and did not realize birth was imminent. The purchaser claimed it was a mistaken transaction and refused to pay. They compromised that he would deduct two and a half gold coins from the purchase price and take both cow and calf. He kept them about seven weeks and then sent the firstborn to a kohen, who refused to accept it. The judges of Frankfurt ruled that the kohen could not simply be compelled, following Terumat HaDeshen. They added that perhaps there was not even such serious disrespect to priestly gifts because where the kohen lived there was no suitable place to keep a firstborn while preserving the prohibitions against shearing and work, and there was concern it might be intentionally blemished. Certainly, if the kohen's claim was correct that the purchaser had negligently failed to sell to a gentile, he need not accept it. The same question, with some changes, was asked of the gaon Maharik of Metz. He concluded for several reasons that the purchaser had not been negligent, so the kohanim were responsible to deal with the firstborn. Since it appeared to be only a doubtful firstborn, like one purchasing an animal from a gentile, they were not subjected to physical coercion. Rather, they were informed that refusal would amount to despising the King's gift, and a kohen who does not acknowledge these gifts has no share in kehunah. He considered it proper for the purchaser and kohanim to divide among themselves the two and a half gold coins deducted from the sale. Chavot Yair himself also concluded that the purchaser was not negligent and discusses at length the related monetary claims between buyer and seller. He further reports a case where a person sold in a legally ineffective manner, following an erroneous common practice, and the rav ruled that the animal retained firstborn sanctity. The kohanim then refused to accept it, arguing that the seller had been negligent by failing to sell properly. He ruled that although the seller was close to intentional wrongdoing because he should have asked a competent rav, he was not legally a willful wrongdoer but an inadvertent one, since he relied on what he had heard and on an established but defective custom.
Where he was negligent. See Responsa Chut HaShani, siman 26. After an extensive halachic discussion, he concludes that according to Mahari Weil, with a definite firstborn the kohen must accept it even if the owner negligently failed to sell it. With a doubtful firstborn, where negligence is clearly proven, such as where he was warned and instructed to sell to a gentile but did not, or where he intentionally sought to provoke the kohen, the kohen need not accept it. If negligence is not clearly proven, he must accept it. Chut HaShani himself limits reliance on Mahari Weil to a calf born from a milking cow, which by strict law is exempt from firstborn status but regarding which communities adopted a stringency. In such a case, negligence can cancel the customary stringency and allow the kohen to refuse. With other doubtful firstborn animals, however, the kohen must accept even where negligence is evident. He adds that one who is lenient and refuses even a milking-cow case where negligence was not proven has not lost. He corrects Rama's text accordingly, reading that the cow was purchased from a gentile and could have been resold, meaning an obvious case of negligence. See also the preceding seif katan from Chavot Yair. Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 302, also discusses this at length. He concludes that when owners negligently failed to use available means to exempt the animal from firstborn status, Chazal can penalize them for creating a stumbling block with sacred property. Nevertheless, the kohen is not automatically exempt from accepting it. If the owner cannot realistically be compelled because he is violent or powerful, the kohen is at least compelled to perform his mitzvah. If the owner can be compelled, it is preferable to impose responsibility upon him rather than the kohen, in order to create a protective fence against negligent conduct. The kohen is then exempt, although there remains some wrongdoing in his refusal. If the owner deliberately acted to provoke the kohen, however, refusal is not treated as despising priestly gifts; on the contrary, the kohen should refuse so people do not make sport of kohanim. In such a case the Israelite can be compelled to care for even a doubtful firstborn because of the possible disgrace of sacred property. Such an owner may also be considered suspected of intentionally blemishing the firstborn because care has been forced upon him. See siman 314, seif 10.
