To accept it. See Baer Heitev. The Shach further explains that only if the kohen does not want the firstborn at all do we not compel him. If he wishes to accept it later, after it develops a blemish, he may be compelled to accept it now. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 302, who gives a sound reason for this distinction. See Responsa Chavat Yair, simanim 38, 39, and 40, regarding a case where Reuven sold Shimon a pregnant cow that had not yet been paid for. On the first night after the sale it gave birth to a male. Neither seller nor buyer had sold the fetus to a non-Jew before birth because neither remembered or anticipated that the time of birth had arrived. The buyer claimed the sale was a mistaken transaction and refused to pay. They compromised that the buyer would deduct two and a half gold coins from the purchase price and take both cow and calf. He kept them for about seven weeks, then sent the firstborn to a kohen who refused to accept it. The judges of Frankfurt ruled that the kohen cannot be compelled to accept it, as Mahari writes. They further reasoned that there was not necessarily severe degradation of the priestly gifts, because at the kohen's location there was no suitable place to preserve the firstborn in its sanctity, guarding against shearing and work, and there was also concern that it might be intentionally blemished. In particular, if the kohen's claim was correct that the buyer had been negligent by not selling to a non-Jew, he certainly need not accept it. The same case was brought, with some differences, before the gaon Maharak of Metz. He concluded for several reasons that the buyer had not been negligent and that the kohanim are therefore required to care for the firstborn. Yet since the facts indicated it was only a doubtful firstborn, like an animal purchased from a non-Jew, they are not forcibly compelled. Rather they are informed that if they refuse, they are degrading the King's gift, and any kohen who does not acknowledge these gifts has no share in the priesthood. He recommended that the buyer and the kohanim divide between them the two and a half gold coins deducted from the purchase price. The author of Chavat Yair likewise concluded that the buyer had not been negligent and discusses at length the claims between buyer and seller. He further records a case where someone used an invalid form of sale, as many unlearned people unfortunately do, and the halachic authority ruled that the animal remained subject to firstborn sanctity. The kohanim refused to accept it, claiming that the seller had been negligent in not making a valid sale. He ruled that although this is close to intentional wrongdoing because the seller should have asked a competent authority, it is not legally classified as negligence but as inadvertence, because the man acted according to what he had heard and the defective practice he had observed.
Where he was negligent. See Responsa Chut HaShani, siman 26, who discusses this extensively. He concludes that according to Mahari Weil, with a definite firstborn a kohen must accept it even if the owner was negligent in failing to sell it. With a doubtful firstborn, where negligence is demonstrable, such as when he was warned and instructed to sell it to a non-Jew but failed to do so, or where it is clear that he intentionally acted to provoke the kohen, the kohen is exempt from accepting it. If negligence is not demonstrated, he must accept it. The author himself argues that Mahari Weil should be relied upon only in the case of a calf born to a milk-producing cow, which by strict law would be exempt from firstborn status but regarding which the custom is to be stringent. In such a case, negligence cancels the customary stringency and the kohen may refuse it. In other doubtful cases, however, the kohen must accept it even where negligence is clear. Nevertheless, one who is lenient in the case of a milk cow and refuses even absent demonstrable negligence has not lost. He also corrects a textual error in the Rama. See there. Chatam Sofer, siman 302, likewise discusses this at length. He concludes that when owners negligently failed to take measures to exempt the animal from firstborn sanctity, the Sages penalized them because they caused a potential mishap with consecrated property. Even so, that alone does not exempt the kohen from his mitzvah to accept it. If the owner is too powerful to compel, the kohen must still be required to fulfill his mitzvah. Where the owner can be compelled, however, it is preferable to penalize the owner, to erect a fence against negligence, rather than force the kohen, although the kohen's refusal still involves wrongdoing. If the owner's purpose is specifically to provoke the kohen, then refusal is not degradation of priestly gifts, and it is a mitzvah for the kohen not to accept it so that people not make sport of kohanim. In such a case the Israelite is required to care even for a doubtful firstborn because of possible degradation of consecrated property, and the person is apparently suspected with respect to that animal of intentionally blemishing it, since he is forced to care for it.
