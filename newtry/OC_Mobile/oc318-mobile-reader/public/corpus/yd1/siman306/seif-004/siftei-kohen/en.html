The kohen must accept it, etc. This means the kohen commits a prohibition if he does not accept it; nevertheless, we do not compel him, as the Or Zarua wrote. What the Or Zarua wrote in tractate Yom Tov, that Rashi's explanation implies we compel him, speaks where he wants to accept it later. So it is in the rulings of Terumat HaDeshen siman 167 from Teshuvat HaLevi, which is Mahari Weil responsa siman 204, and so it is in Hagahot Mordechai, chapter HaIshah Rabbah. Regarding a milking cow, the kohen need not accept it, for although we rule stringently, the kohen can say to the giver: go bring proof that it is a bekhor and I will tend to it, etc.; see there. One must say according to Mahari Weil that there he means they do not compel him.
But in a place, etc., such as where he bought a cow, etc. This is not precise. The Darkhei Moshe's language is: Mahari Weil ruled in siman 127 that even a doubtful bekhor must be accepted, specifically from a Jew; but a poor kohen who has a bekhor and gives it to a wealthy kohen need not have it accepted. But if the Jew was negligent by not selling it to a non-Jew before it was born, it appears that he need not accept it, etc. Terumat HaDeshen wrote in his rulings, siman 168, that what Maharam wrote, that one must accept a doubtful bekhor, applies specifically where the doubt arose on its own; but one who bought a milking cow from a non-Jew need not have it accepted. These are the words of the Darkhei Moshe. The Rav therefore mixed the matters here. In truth, I do not know what negligence applies in buying a milking cow, unless he could not sell the bekhor. And if he was negligent, why mention a doubtful bekhor? Even a definite bekhor need not be accepted, as implied by Mahari Weil responsa siman 127. What Terumat HaDeshen wrote in his rulings, siman 168, that one who buys a milking cow need not have it accepted, is because of the reason he wrote: Maharam did not speak of a doubt dependent on a dispute of the great authorities, but of a doubt that arose on its own, as we find many doubts in tractate Bekhorot. But in such a case, where there are several doubts in disputes of the great authorities, meaning whether milk exempts from bekhorah as below siman 316 seif 3, if we go to be stringent because of a karet prohibition in order to satisfy everyone, we do not go so far as to be stringent regarding priestly gifts. These are his words. I do not know how to reconcile the Rav's words unless we emend them: specifically with a doubt that comes on its own; but with a doubt that comes from a dispute of the great authorities, such as where he bought a milking cow from a non-Jew, or where the Jew was negligent because he could have sold the bekhor to a non-Jew before it was born, etc. The Atteret Zahav copied the Rav's words and did not notice. Nevertheless, the law still requires study regarding Terumat HaDeshen's distinction between a doubt that arises on its own and a doubt involving a dispute of the great authorities, for from Teshuvat HaLevi brought there, which is Mahari Weil responsa siman 204, it does not seem so. In the case of one who bought a milking cow from a non-Jew, he wrote that the kohen must accept it, and learned this from Maharam's doubtful bekhor, even though he brought there the views of the poskim whether milk exempts from bekhorah or not. Perhaps since Terumat HaDeshen saw Mahari Weil's responsum and nevertheless did not rule like him, we follow Terumat HaDeshen, who is later. It also appears that Terumat HaDeshen there did not rely only on this reason, for it is forced to distinguish between a doubt arising on its own and a doubt depending on a dispute of authorities. Unless we say that Terumat HaDeshen holds that in a doubtful bekhor we compel the kohen, and teaches that in a doubt dependent on a dispute of the great authorities we do not compel him, for he can say kim li like the permissive authorities. But this is forced, for then Terumat HaDeshen would disagree with Mahari Weil's responsum above, which wrote that we do not compel even in a doubtful bekhor. Furthermore, it implies that regarding a dispute of the great authorities he says the kohen need not accept it at all, even if we do not compel him, and there is no prohibition in not accepting it. Rather, it appears that in Terumat HaDeshen's case there were several doubts in disputes of the authorities: he had sold to the non-Jew with money, which according to some poskim is a valid sale, as below siman 320 seif 6; and it was also milking, which according to some poskim exempts from bekhorah. Therefore he was lenient. But with only one doubt in a dispute of the great authorities, one should not be lenient unless the primary law appears to follow the lenient view and we are only being stringent; in such a case perhaps the kohen need not accept it. So it appears to me.
