And to receive its rental payment, and if it broke, they appraise it for him, etc. The Tur writes that it is permitted to fix its value and stipulate, etc.; yet at the end he concludes that when it breaks, he is liable only for the value it had at the time it broke. These statements appear contradictory. The Beit Yosef explained them as he did, but it appears to me that at first the Tur is not discussing a case in which, even if the item later declines in value, he must nevertheless pay the previously fixed amount. Rather, he comes only to teach us that it is permitted to set a valuation according to the market price at that time, and we do not say that fixing the value renders it like a loan. In the latter clause, however, he teaches that if it later declines in value, he pays according to the later market price, as the Rama wrote at the end of seif 4 in the name of some authorities.
Likewise, it is permitted to rent out a pot, etc. The Tur explains that this contains a greater novelty than the first clause. In the first clause, the arrangement does not acquire the status of a loan until the end, when the utensil breaks. Until that time it remains a rental and stays within the owner's domain with respect to a decline in value, and with respect to depreciation if the renter returns the utensil itself. The payment is therefore for that period of rental. However, even regarding a copper pot, whose deterioration begins immediately when one starts using it, because the copper continually wears away, so that it immediately becomes a loan, nevertheless it is permitted to receive payment. This is because the copper that remains has deteriorated from its original condition, and he is paying for that deterioration.
