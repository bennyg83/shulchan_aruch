And meat, etc.—it implies that requiring seal within seal for meat is even where slaughterers are Israelite; and it is difficult regarding what the Taz wrote in siman 1, seif 4, that one who finds his animals and chickens properly slaughtered in the market is permitted because of majority of Israelite slaughterers—for there we do not know who slaughtered, therefore we follow the majority; but here, meat in a non-Jew's hand, we say he switched it with nevelah he had; and so the Mechaber in siman 63, seif 1—and the Sages already forbade all meat found in a non-Jew's hand even if all sellers and slaughterers are Israelite, end of his words. However, there is difficulty: the Rav wrote there at the end of the siman that if meat was in a non-Jew's hand where most sellers are Israelites it is kosher—perhaps here it deals where most sellers are not Israelites; and it seems to distinguish: there it deals only with meat found in a non-Jew's hand that they bought from the market, therefore where sellers are Israelites we permit, for no concern he bought nevelah, and no concern he switched with nevelah in his house—for what purpose would he switch, it is all his; but here, sending or depositing with a non-Jew, there is concern he switched with nevelah he still has; and one can further distinguish between sender or depositor where meat stays long in his hand and he has opportunity for nevelah and to switch, versus buying from the market; and as the Taz wrote in siman 128 regarding a ger toshav: forbidden to leave wine with him a long time, permitted a short time—for the same reason, no concern of switching in short time—see there; and see seif 6 and what is written there.
And a piece of fish, etc.—specifically a piece of fish; for a whole fish or half would be recognizable by fin and scale, as above siman 83.
Deposited or sent, etc.—it seems likewise l'chatchila permitted to send or deposit Torah-forbidden things with seal within seal and rabbinically forbidden things with one seal; and so the Mechaber wrote explicitly below, siman 130—it is simple.
In the hand of a non-Jew—not specifically; the same applies to a suspect Israelite who eats things the public are not accustomed to be lenient about, as the Mechaber writes in this siman, seif 8, and siman 119 (and see Rav HaMaggid, chapter 8 of Mishneh Torah, law 10, and understand). Rather, he mentioned non-Jew here because wine applies only with a non-Jew—for the reason for wine is affection of libation, he troubles and forges, as in s.k. 5—and this does not apply to a suspect Israelite unless he is suspect to libate wine for idolatry, then he is like a full non-Jew, as siman 119, s.k. 15; or he did not mention suspect Israelite because of cheese and the like, a matter the public are lenient about, as will be explained in s.k. 15. That he did not mention suspect to switch is obvious—understand.
But cooked wine, etc., and everything whose prohibition is from the scribes, etc.—the Mechaber's view: everything depends on whether forbidden from the Torah or rabbinically—for meat, concern he switched with nevelah, and fish with impure fish, which are Torah prohibitions; and wine, even though stam yeinam is only d'rabbanan, as below siman 123, nevertheless the Rashba wrote that because of affection of libation they trouble and forge even with one seal, therefore they were stringent like Torah; but cooked wine, beer, etc.—all those, even if switched, involve only rabbinic prohibition, as will be explained; since there is one seal we are not concerned for forgery—this is the Rambam's and Rashba's view (and R' Yerucham, end of 55). But the Ran wrote in the name of the Yerushalmi: the reason for wine, meat, and fish needing seal within seal is because the prohibition is intrinsic; but chamfig, where the prohibition is only through mixture, as explained in siman 114 and 115—and bread, though bread itself is forbidden, nevertheless because the prohibition is rabbinic, one seal suffices, end of his words; and so the Ra'ah in Bedek HaBayit, folio 118. He also holds that vinegar, since unfit for libation, we are not concerned he troubles and switches, since stam yeinam is d'rabbanan; wine is different because of affection of libation—he troubles and forges, as I will write in s.k. 6—for the Mechaber and his supporters; and so explicit from the Ran. It must be that the Ran and supporters hold milk and cheese are not called rabbinic prohibition here since there is concern for Torah-prohibited mixture—and understand. Rashi explained the reason: for chavivta their value is dear, he troubles and forges with one seal, unlike other things. I elaborated because the Rav in Torat Chatas, klal 32, law 14, wrote it is good to be stringent like all explanations; also in Simanei Torat Chacham he wrote many reasons for chavivta why seal within seal is needed, and everything similar that is Torah prohibition or rabbinic with intrinsic prohibition like chavivta, or rabbinic without intrinsic prohibition but considered like chavivta in every respect—needs seal within seal, end of his words; and so in Sefer Api Ravrevi. It is difficult why he stated here like the Mechaber, and why wonder greatly at Simanei Torat Chacham and Api Ravrevi: 'or rabbinic and its prohibition intrinsic'—for even for the Ran, in such a case one seal suffices like bread, as explicit in his words and the Ra'ah; perhaps one can say: specifically for bread, whose prohibition is rabbinic with no Torah root, as siman 112—we say so; but not for all other rabbinic prohibitions. And when he cited the Ran in Torat Chatas he added 'and bread even though its prohibition is intrinsic, nevertheless since its root is rabbinic, one seal suffices'—but the Ran is not in these words, and the Ran seems to hold even rabbinic prohibitions with a Torah root and intrinsic prohibition suffice with one seal, for he compared vinegar to bread, etc. See s.k. 7 and understand. Also, if so, he need not state the reason of wine because of affection of libation—for since it has a Torah root, it properly needs seal within seal—and understand.
And what the Mechaber wrote, 'such as honey'—he cited the Rambam's language; nevertheless in the Mechaber's words it must deal with mixing so much honey that the wine's taste changed, for then there is no stam yeinam, as below siman 123; but if only a little honey was mixed, the Mechaber holds there is stam yeinam concern, unlike the Rambam, as will be explained there; therefore in such a case one needs seal within seal here too—see below siman 130, seif 3 (and see responsum Mabit, part 2, siman 208).
And what he wrote 'and likewise vinegar'—meaning vinegar of wine; and so explicit below siman 130, seif 3—it is when it bubbles as thrown on the ground, then it is full vinegar unfit for libation, as below siman 123, seif 6—see there. The reason: why concern—for affection of libation he troubles and forges? It is unfit for libation. If lest he switch with vinegar of libated wine—since stam yeinam is only d'rabbanan we are not concerned. The same for morayes, cooked wine, and wine mixed with other things—we are not concerned the non-Jew switches his morayes, cooked wine, and mixed wine, since all are forbidden only because of stam yeinam which is d'rabbanan—understand.
And milk, etc.—even though concern he switches with impure milk, a Torah prohibition, as explained in siman 1—it may be said impure milk is recognizable as turbid, as Avodah Zarah 35b: pure white, impure green; therefore no concern he switches all or most, only perhaps a little forbidden only d'rabbanan—for min b'mino is nullified d'oraisa, as siman 98; and so answered in Perishah, seif 4; and similarly Or Zarua, klal 48, end of law 5; or as Or Zarua, klal 45, law 7: since most milk among us is pure, stam milk is d'rabbanan. Bach on the Perishah wrote: they permitted him against his master who wrote against the Gemara and against Terumat HaDeshen the Arukh what did not occur to him—he thought milk has Torah prohibition; rather, one seal suffices because he has no profit—and so the Tur. It is reversed: he wrote against the Gemara and Terumat HaDeshen, and in the Tur it is explicit per the correct text in old books, which he agreed to: for milk one seal suffices since the prohibition is rabbinic, as the Rambam, Mechaber, and R' Yerucham, and Or Zarua in many places. But certainly, as with fish and other things he has profit switching pure for impure, so with milk—unless we come to 'he has no profit,' as the Taz wrote in this siman, seif 7 (from the Gemara and all poskim): we are not concerned he switched unless he benefits from switching—for whoever does not benefit is permitted even without any seal. The main point is as I wrote.
And morayes—also concern he switches with their morayes, forbidden where they put wine in it, as the Taz in siman 114, seif 1; but where they do not put wine, or wine is dearer than morayes, clearly no seal needed; and so the Mechaber there.
And bread—only concern he switches with their bread, rabbinically forbidden, as siman 112; therefore where they practice leniency with non-Jewish bread, clearly no seal needed.
And cheese—even though concern he switched with their cheese forbidden because they curdle with nevelah stomach lining, as the Taz in siman 115, seif 2—nevertheless d'oraisa the nevelah lining is nullified in minority; what is never nullified is d'rabbanan since it is a curdling agent, as above siman 87; or even if known they curdled with herbs or other things it is forbidden, as the Mechaber in siman 115—a rabbinic decree; or mainly: since we can say perhaps he did not curdle with nevelah lining, and even so forbidden—for we say 'perhaps he curdled'—it is properly d'rabbanan—understand.
Deposited in a non-Jew's hand—and the same for sent—understand well.
And some say, etc.—and below siman 130 the Mechaber states the first view without a disputant—and understand.
And even the sender, if he informed, etc.—this follows the second view, unlike the first—understand well.
If he informed his fellow the form of the seal and also told the non-Jew, etc.—it seems what the Taz wrote: if he informed his fellow, etc., is not precise—for the same if he does not inform his fellow but tells the non-Jew he informed, he still fears, thinking it true; for the Taz wrote in seif 2: once sealed properly, etc., where one suffices one need not return to see—and it implies even per the 'some say' view that a depositor needs only one seal, they wrote so; and so the Rav in hagahah below siman 130, seif 2—the same for a sender who told the non-Jew he informed his fellow the seal's form: no need to return and see. Also, if with a sender one seal requires the recipient to return and see—what purpose informing the non-Jew? Since the fellow knows the seal's form and must return and see, he will see visually if forged or not—why is this worse than a depositor? Rather, the Taz teaches: when he informed his fellow the seal's form, it does not suffice until he also tells the non-Jew he informed him; and customarily when one tells the non-Jew so, he presumably informs. He wrote 'and also told the non-Jew' last, not the reverse. Nevertheless, even when sending with two seals it is proper to inform the fellow the seal's form so he will inspect, as the Taz in siman 130, end of seif 8—see there; perhaps therefore they also mentioned this when he informed the form—to say that in such a case one may do so l'chatchila.
Only for a suspect Israelite—for he is worse than a non-Jew in this matter, who is trusted they will believe him and does not fear much. It implies for meat, wine, and fish a suspect Israelite needs two seals, and for chamfig one seal. It implies per 'some say' there is no distinction between depositor and sender. 'Suspect Israelite' means suspect to switch—not suspect to eat what he sends, for we hold one suspect to eat forbidden things is not suspect to switch; perhaps those things the public are not lenient about—he is also suspect to switch if suspect to eat them, as the Mechaber in seif 8 and siman 119. But the Rashba wrote eating non-Jewish cheese is lenient in the public's eyes; and so Torat HaBayit; and Beit Yosef brings it in siman 119; and the Tur in siman 119: one suspect regarding cheese is not suspect to switch—and so from the poskim; see siman 119, s.k. 52.
