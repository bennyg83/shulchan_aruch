(Siman 372, seif 1, gloss) A kohen who is lying naked in a tent with a deceased person. Shach, seif katan 2, implies that the rule of eventual tumah exiting is only rabbinic. Tiferet LeMoshe challenges him: although Rashi at the beginning of Beitzah says so, the end of Beitzah appears to treat it as Torah law, as Beit Yosef cites from Piskei Mahari, and Rashi in Perek Hadar calls it halachah leMoshe miSinai. Magen Avraham, Orach Chayyim siman 311, likewise says Shach was imprecise. According to Sha'ar HaMelech, Hilchot Eruvin, however, there is no difficulty. He explains that according to Rif and Rambam everyone agrees the rule itself is rabbinic. The Gemara in Beitzah says R. Oshaya is stringent regarding bereirah there as he is in Torah law only because the rabbinic law has a Torah root. Other authorities, including R. Oshaya and Rava in Perek Hadar, permit bereirah regarding eventual tumah exit precisely because the rule itself is rabbinic. Rif and Rambam rule that way. Pnei Yehoshua in Beitzah likewise explains it so. In my work on Rambam, Hilchot Issurei Mizbeach, I reconciled Rambam on this basis and proved from other places that this is his position. I later found that Pnei Yehoshua at the beginning of the third chapter of Berachot wrote a separate kuntres defending Shach and brought evidence for him. His chiddushim to Sukkah likewise indicate that Rashi and Rambam hold transmission of tumah from house to house through windows and a tefach opening is only rabbinic. Mishkenot Yaakov later raised the same objection to Shach from Rashi at the end of Beitzah and suggested the matter depends on whether we apply bereirah in Torah law, but he did not note that the issue had already been discussed and resolved through Sha'ar HaMelech and Pnei Yehoshua.
(There) It is forbidden to wait until he clothes himself. This appears to apply specifically where the kohen entered the prohibited situation through an initial act, such as entering a house where the deceased was already present or entering a cemetery. In such a case continued presence is treated as an extension of the original act, analogous to kilayim where Kesef Mishneh says remaining in the garment can be considered an act because the person initially dressed in violation. But if the kohen entered the house while no deceased was present and lay there naked permissibly, and only afterward someone died in the house, continued presence is not itself an act. Tosafot Yevamot 90b and several Rambam rulings imply this. In that case the kohen may remain until he dresses, because human dignity can override a Torah prohibition where the person violates only through passive inaction, as Berachot 20 teaches.
I saw in glosses to Shulchan Aruch by R. Ozer that he challenged Rama from Tosafot and Rashba Berachot 20, who derive from met mitzvah that the prohibition of kohen tumah is overridden for human dignity. Pnei Yehoshua answers that met mitzvah is different because, in addition to human dignity, there is a positive mitzvah to bury the deceased. His explanation of the Gemara's question "let us derive from there" through the Pesach offering is somewhat forced. It can, however, be clarified based on Lechem Mishneh, Hilchot Avel chapter 12, and on that basis Rambam in Hilchot Kilayim can be reconciled, as I explain in my chiddushim.
