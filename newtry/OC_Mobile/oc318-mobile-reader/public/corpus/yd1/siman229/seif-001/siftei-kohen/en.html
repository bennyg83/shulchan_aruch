They are all released, etc. This implies that here he need not alter his wording, unlike below, siman 232, seif 8. Here, since the error was not in the essential basis of the vow, but it was released through annulment, they are all released in every case. This is also written in Darkei Moshe, siman 232: “Nevertheless, it appears from the Tur that one should distinguish between a vow that became permitted by itself and a vow that a sage annulled. Therefore, above in siman 229, he did not write that he must alter his wording.” The Bach likewise rules this way, and this is the primary view.
According to Rambam, etc., the first is released, etc. For it is as though he said: this second person shall be like the first, and this third person shall be like the second.
To this one, to this one, to this one, etc. This is without the conjunction “and,” so it is as though he said, “Konam to this one, konam to this one, konam to this one,” since he need not say “konam” explicitly for each individual. Beit Yosef writes in the name of Rivash, in responsum 291, that one who vowed or swore not to eat grapes and not to wear silk garments, and then sought annulment for one of them, the second prohibition is not released according to Rambam. It appears from his words that the same applies to one who says, “I swear that I will not eat and that I will not drink”: if he seeks annulment for one, the second is not released. However, if he swore not to eat figs and grapes, or not to drink wine, milk, and honey, and he sought annulment for one of them, the others are released, unless his fellow was pressing him when he swore. Beit Yosef concludes that it has already been explained that some disagree with Rambam, and Darkei Moshe cites this. It appears that if he said, “Konam grapes upon me; konam figs upon me,” even those who disagree with Rambam would regard these as two separate vows, since he said “konam” twice, as below, siman 238, seif 36, in the gloss. The Gemara likewise establishes the Mishnah in the final chapter of Nedarim, 87b, which states, “Konam, if I taste a fig, and grapes, if I taste them, these are two vows,” in accordance with Rabbi Shimon. See there. Ran writes there that according to Rabbi Shimon, who requires an oath for each individual, here too they are not considered two vows unless she says “if I taste” regarding each one. But if she said, “Konam if I taste, neither fig nor grape,” Rabbi Shimon regards it as one vow.
