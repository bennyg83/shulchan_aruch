Those preceding him remain forbidden. There is a scribal error in the Tur. It should read: Those below him remain forbidden, and those above him are permitted.
And a third person heard and said, “And I.” The same applies even up to one hundred people, as in the opening clause, for each person associates his vow with the one before him. This is the Gemara’s conclusion.
And he associated honey with bread, etc. Meaning, if the final vow was released, that final item is permitted while all the others remain forbidden. If the middle vow was released, etc.
Until he seeks release from it, etc. The Tur writes at the end of siman 238: “An oath that I will not eat this loaf, an oath that I will not eat this loaf. The second oath does not take effect. If he seeks release from the first, the second takes effect,” end quote. Since it states that the second does not take effect, it implies that he cannot release the second before releasing the first, for the second has not yet taken effect. This resembles what is written in siman 228, seif 17, that a vow may not be released until it takes effect. So too it is implied by the Rosh in the second chapter of Nedarim, 18a: “Thus, so long as he has not sought release from the first, the second does not exist,” end quote. Since it does not exist, how can it be released? The Rambam’s words at the end of chapter 6 of Hilchos Shevuos, however, do not imply this. He writes: “An oath that I will not eat today, an oath that I will not eat today, an oath that I will not eat today. If he seeks release from the first, he is liable because of the second. If he then seeks release from the second, he is liable because of the third. If he seeks release only from the third, he remains liable because of the first and second. If he seeks release from the second, he remains liable because of the first. Why, then, did they say that an oath does not take effect upon an oath? It means that if he did not seek release and ate, he is liable only once,” end quote. This implies that he can release the second or third even before releasing the first. We must therefore say that this differs from siman 228, seif 17. There, the oath does not yet exist at all. Here, however, the second and third oaths exist, but have not yet found a place to take effect. Mahari L. Chaviv proposed this explanation of the Rambam in responsum 139. Based on it, he proposed an additional leniency. Just as siman 228, seif 46, states that several vows of one person may be released through a single release, so too one who swore regarding the same matter many times should require only one release for all of them. This is unlike the Shulchan Aruch there, which requires a separate release for each oath because the second does not take effect before release of the first. In my humble opinion, the Shulchan Aruch ruled correctly there. First, according to the Rosh and Tur cited above, the second oath does not take effect at all. They therefore certainly disagree with the Rambam and hold that it cannot be released before the first is released. This is also evident from the fact that they did not distinguish between an oath that has not taken effect in this case and the case in siman 228. They should have taught such a distinction, for it would be a major novelty. Evidently, they do not accept the Rambam’s view. Furthermore, even according to the Rambam, who permits release of the second before the first, it does not appear that he accepts Mahari L. Chaviv’s further leniency of releasing them all at one time. There is a sound reason for this distinction. Releasing the second before the first contains nothing that contradicts the release, for only one oath is being released. When, however, he releases the first together with the second, the moment the sage says, “It is permitted,” regarding the first, he thereby gives force to the second so that it now takes effect. How can the sage’s statement continue and release the second as well, when the two effects contradict one another? When this one falls, the other rises. One release cannot therefore apply to both. Reason itself requires this conclusion. Anyone who wishes to dispute it must bring clear proof, and the Rambam’s words provide no proof. Even the first leniency, to release the second before the first, should not be relied upon in a Torah matter, since the Rosh and Tur imply otherwise, as the Shulchan Aruch ruled in siman 228. There is further proof from the Rosh’s responsum at the end of klal 12. One who stated at the time of his vow that no sage would be able to release that vow has thereby made another vow. It is like a vow within a vow. They must first release the vow that no one may release it, etc. Thus, the Rosh compares one who vowed that he would not seek release, the case mentioned here in seif 9, to the law in siman 228, seif 46, of a vow within a vow, meaning that he vowed twice regarding one matter, as discussed in the second chapter of Nedarim, 17a. The Rosh writes that they must first release the vow presently in force. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote on the basis of Mahari L. Chaviv’s words that the Beit Yosef had erred. The matter is clear as I have written. Mahari L. Chaviv himself wrote in that responsum that he had seen another authority rule against him, though he disagreed.
