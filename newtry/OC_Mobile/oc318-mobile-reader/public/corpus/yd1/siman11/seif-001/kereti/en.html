In what case are these words said—when there is a torch, etc.; these matters are in the Gemara. Rashi gave a reason in darkness: perhaps he will not slaughter the majority; the Rashba gave a reason: perhaps he will delay or press or make chaladah—see Peleti what he wrote on this. Why did the Rashba explain a different reason from Rashi's words?
In a dark place—this can be even according to Rashi's explanation; and we do not say "but he can go to a place of light and inspect the simanim"—for if so, at night too he could go to a place where candles burn and inspect; rather we are concerned lest he forget.
He should not slaughter; and if he began to slaughter and the candles went out, he finishes—for it is b'dieved.
He should slaughter; and by moonlight too he should not slaughter, for its light is not like the light of a torch—and the same law for checking chametz. And the Gemara that answers what is taught in chapter 1: at night one should not slaughter, and in chapter 1 one may slaughter—this where there is a torch, and this where there is no torch; and it did not answer this where there is moonlight and this where there is no moonlight—from which we learn that even with moonlight it is nevertheless forbidden.
And if he slaughtered—the later authorities wrote: by saying "it is clear to me I did not press and did not delay and did not make chaladah." And it appears it is not precise that he must say—only a presumption that he acted thus; and if he were doubtful he would say so; therefore in Tosafot HaRosh this is not mentioned that he must say.
His slaughter is valid; and in any case he must inspect whether the majority was slaughtered, and we are not concerned lest meanwhile it convulsed and the majority was formed.
And two candles—in Maharil it implies they help even when not braided; and it must be said he holds the reason one needs a torch is so it will not be easy to extinguish—and if so, when there are two separate candles it is obvious there is no concern that if one goes out the second burns.
Torch—and they wrote: a lamp that has two wicks is a torch. And behold our plain candles: they twist together several very thin threads of flax or cotton and dip them in tallow until thick fat adheres—and those are the candles; if so, it can be like a lamp that has many wicks and has the law of a torch. From this it appears permitted to slaughter at night with a single candle, and one does not object—for our plain candles are a torch. And one should be stringent initially that many candles should burn in the slaughterer's room.
