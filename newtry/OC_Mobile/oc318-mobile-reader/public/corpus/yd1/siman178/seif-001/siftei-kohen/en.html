And he shall not grow his forelock. That is, he shall not shave on the sides etc. — so wrote Eitan HaEzrachi, following what Beit Yosef wrote that "he shall not shave" etc., meaning he shall not shave his forelock. But Bach disagreed and wrote it is a matter unto itself: "he shall not grow his forelock" means those who grow their hair for adornment and beauty like maidens do — as a manner of haughtiness and arrogance; and likewise Perishah.
Of idol worship etc. But those built for their council to confer about their matters and for law — there is no prohibition to build in the style of those halls — Bach; and see there.
Red garments etc. For it is not the way of modest people to dress in red, and the color black is the way of modesty and humility — as we say: one whose evil inclination overcomes him should dress in black and wrap himself in black, etc.; and furthermore there is a tradition in our hands to be particular about red garments for our people — Maharik there.
But a matter, etc. See Maharik, who discusses this at length and brings several proofs. One proof he cites is from Rashi in the chapter Kol HaBasar, Chullin, daf 107, concerning the statement that failure to wash before eating caused one to be fed pork. Rashi explains that a Jewish shopkeeper sold kosher meat to Jews and neveilah to non-Jews. A Jew came to eat and did not wash his hands, so the shopkeeper fed him pork. This apparently proves that a Jew need not distinguish himself from non-Jews by his clothing. End of Maharik's argument. My father, the Gaon, of blessed memory, said that this is astonishing. The incident cited by Rashi appears in Midrash Rabbah, Parashas Balak, and it explicitly states that it occurred during a time of persecution, etc., until the customer said, 'Privately, I am a Jew.' See there. It is clear that they served Hashem secretly, and handwashing was a sign known to the shopkeeper rather than any other identifying sign. During a time of persecution one is certainly permitted to change his clothing so that people will not recognize him as a Jew, as Maharik himself wrote there and as explained above in siman 157, seif 2. This requires further study.
