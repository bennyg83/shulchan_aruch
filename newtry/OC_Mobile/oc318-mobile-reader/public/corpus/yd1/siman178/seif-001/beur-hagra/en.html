One may not, etc. As it is written, “and in their statutes you shall not walk.” In Torat Kohanim, Parashat Acharei Mot: “and in their statutes you shall not walk” means you shall not walk in their customs, in the things fixed for them, such as theaters and circuses. Rabbi Meir says: these are the ways of the Amorites that the sages listed. Rabbi Yehudah says: that you should not shave the back, not grow a forelock, and not cut a komi haircut. The Rambam in Sefer HaMitzvot, negative commandment 30, explained that “such as theaters,” etc., are kinds of their assemblies where they would gather for idolatry; this is why he writes “and one should not build,” etc. In Sifrei, Parashat Re'eh: “Take care,” a negative commandment, “lest you be ensnared after them,” lest you be drawn after them or resemble them, etc.; do not say, since they go out in abgita, I too will go out in abgita; since they go out in argaman, I too will go out in argaman; since they go out in kilusin, I too will go out in kilusin. All these are kinds of garments. This is why he writes, “one should not resemble them,” and, etc. The Rambam wrote: you already know the verse, “and against all who wear foreign clothing.” At the end of Sanhedrin chapter 8 it says even changing, etc. The Rif there explained that the non-Jews of that time made the straps of their shoes red, while Jews made theirs black so that they would not wear non-Jewish clothing. So too the Arukh explained. Likewise regarding the woman wearing a karbalta at the beginning of Berachot chapter 3, 20a, Rav Adda tore it because it was non-Jewish clothing.
And one should not grow, etc. In Torat Kohanim as above, unlike the Beit Yosef; see Shach. And one should not shave, etc. Avodah Zarah chapter 2, 29a; Kiddushin last chapter, 76b; Sanhedrin chapters 2 and 6, regarding four hundred children, etc.; and Tosefta Shabbat chapter 7: these are things of the ways of the Amorites: one who cuts a komi haircut and one who makes a forelock, etc. And one should not shave, etc. This is the komi haircut of the Tosefta and Torat Kohanim, as above, and at the end of Bava Kamma chapter 7 and the end of Sotah.
And not, etc.; but, etc. As above.
And all this, etc. Like the shoe strap case above; see Shach s.k. 3 and Rashi in Sanhedrin there s.v. “shoe strap,” etc., such as where there is, etc. So too one can explain the Rif's words above, and likewise the karbalta case, as the Arukh wrote: a red garment like a rooster's crest, which Jewish women do not normally cover themselves with because it is immodest, etc. A proof is that in Meilah 17a it says he went and cut his hair in komi style, but does not say that he wore their clothing. Or in a matter, etc. As stated in Torat Kohanim above: in their fixed customs, etc. Meaning, a chok is something without reason, as stated in Yoma chapter 6, 67b, “and My statutes,” etc., and as Rashi wrote in Parashat Kedoshim: chukkim are royal decrees with no reason for the matter. The Ramban wrote that certainly they have reasons, but their reasons are not revealed, like a royal decree which the king fixes in his kingdom without revealing its benefit. All those listed at the end of Shabbat chapter 6 and in the Tosefta chapter 7 are of this kind, as the Gemara says there: granted, loading him with stones, etc.; and there, anything with healing purpose is not, etc. This is what he writes, “but,” etc., and similarly what they do, etc., as stated in the Tosefta there: one who says, “Do not interrupt between us, lest our love be interrupted,” has the prohibition of ways of the Amorites; but if it is for honor, it is permitted. Although the Amorites habituated themselves to these practices, it is nevertheless permitted. Maharik. So too the Ran wrote in the first chapter of Avodah Zarah regarding what is stated there, that they burn for kings, etc.: the Torah forbade only idolatrous statutes and empty practices, all of which have some idolatrous purpose; but things with a reason are permitted. Burning for kings has a reason: to burn for their honor so that no one else use them, etc. This is what he writes, “therefore they said,” etc.; so too in the Gemara there 11a: if it is a statute, etc.; rather, according to all, etc.; rather it is an honor, etc. But in Sanhedrin 52b it implies the opposite, for there it says: it was taught, Rabbi Yehudah said to them, etc., and the rabbis, etc. Otherwise, although there is a great reason, namely avoiding a degrading death, it would still be forbidden. There it says, if you do not say so, etc.; rather, etc., implying that otherwise it is forbidden. See Tosafot there s.v. “rather,” etc., and in Avodah Zarah there s.v. “and if,” etc. The core of their words is that there are two kinds of statutes: one for idolatry, which is forbidden even if written in the Torah; and one of empty foolishness, which is permitted specifically if written in the Torah. According to this, what it says there “rather, it is importance” does not mean it has a reason, as the Ran says, and therefore is permitted; rather, it means their statute is to burn for an important person, not for idolatry. Therefore Sanhedrin properly says, “if you do not say so,” etc. According to Maharik, one must say the opposite, that it has no reason. Still, this requires study, for sword has a reason. One can say that had sword not been written, we would not judge with sword at all; this is what it says, “since sword is written,” etc. At first glance it is difficult because decapitation is not written, but the Ran's words are very astonishing, as are the Rav's words.
(Likkut) And all this, etc.; therefore they said, etc. I have already written that the Ran's words are astonishing, and Maharik's words also do not appear correct from Sanhedrin 52: it was taught, Rabbi Yehudah said to them, etc. After all, sword has a reason. Although I pressed to reconcile them, in law it does not appear to me. So too burning for kings certainly has a reason, as the Ran wrote, and nevertheless the Gemara asks “if you do not say so,” etc. Rather, certainly everything is forbidden; only learning from them is forbidden, as stated there: since it is written, we do not learn from them. It is not specifically because it is written in the Torah; rather, anything we would do without them is permitted, and so too with clothing. But anything they wear as clothing unique to them is forbidden, as the plain words of the Rif above imply, even a shoe strap, etc.; certainly one strap is not because of immodesty. This is what it says: even a shoe strap, etc., and as stated in the Sifrei above, “since they go out,” etc. But a garment that we would wear anyway is permitted. Therefore all of Maharik's proofs are not difficult, because it was not unique to them specifically. So wrote the Rambam: “unique to them,” and similarly in every matter. Maharik's words do not appear correct at all.
