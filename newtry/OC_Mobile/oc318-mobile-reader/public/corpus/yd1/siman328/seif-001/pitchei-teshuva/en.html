He writes that by strict law it is exempt from challah because it is unfit for Jewish consumption, but rabbinically it should be obligated because of appearance, like dog food dough at the end of siman 330, which is liable where it has the appearance of bread even when shepherds do not eat it. Nevertheless, no blessing should be recited. One who wishes to be stringent and separate with a form of blessing may follow a practice he received from his teachers in such doubtful cases: recite as prayer the verses beginning "David blessed," including "Blessed are You, Hashem, God of Israel" and continuing through "Yours, Hashem, is the kingdom," which contain the Divine Name and kingship, and then conclude, "Blessed is He Who commanded us to separate challah." In this form there is no concern of a blessing in vain. He further notes that Pnei Yehoshua on Berachot 12 appears to allow saying in Aramaic, "Blessed is the Merciful One, Master, Who commanded us to separate challah," because no formal Divine Name and kingship are uttered in Hebrew, while it would still fulfill the requirement of a blessing. But this is not decisive, since even in another language mention of God's Name in vain may be prohibited. See there.

Magen Avraham, siman 8, seif katan 2, writes that separating challah is not as direct a mitzvah as some others, since one does it primarily to permit one's food, and therefore the blessing may be recited seated. According to that reasoning, even a fully clothed woman could bless seated. Yeshuot Yaakov, written by my relative the Gaon, Av Beit Din of Lemberg, rejects this. He distinguishes that where it is possible to perform the matter standing, one may not choose to sit, and therefore tzitzit is blessed standing. Here, where a woman in the case under discussion cannot stand while uncovered, she may sit. The underlying concern with sitting is that it may appear disrespectful, as if the fear of Heaven were not before him. Where sitting results from necessity, there is no disrespect.
