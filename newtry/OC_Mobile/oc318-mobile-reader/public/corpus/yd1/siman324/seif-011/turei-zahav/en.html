He placed leaven from a dough. Specifically leaven is not nullified in the dough; but a dough, as long as it does not have the minimum measure for challah, does not become obligated. So wrote the Beit Yosef in the name of the decisors.
If he has flour, etc. The Tur wrote: or if he wishes, he may bring a quarter-kav from his [other] flour and mix it with this dough and separate from it. End quote. This is from the interpretation of the Rashbam, and it is explained there that even in this case one separates only proportionally. It seems to me the reason is: since there is here a minimum measure for challah — namely from the leaven that came from an obligated source — and what he now adds in flour need not be calculated against the rest of the dough, but only against the obligated measure [of the leaven]. The Maharshal wrote that it is not necessary to mix it into the dough — he may even place it beside the dough if he wishes. End quote.
One forty-eighth. Of the entire dough when the leaven [renders the whole], since the leaven is not nullified and renders everything [as] tevel. Therefore if one separates from another place, it suffices to separate only according to the measure of the leaven alone — since the leaven is what causes the obligation and he is separating for it from a fully obligated source, namely from elsewhere. But this is not so if he has no flour from elsewhere and must separate from the dough itself — since he is not separating from a place that is obligated in challah in its own right, but only because the dough absorbed the flavor from the leaven and the whole became obligated — he must therefore separate the full measure from the entire dough, for we follow the act of separation. So it seems to me.
And the same applies if the leaven is exempt, etc. For example, after Pesach when one takes the leaven of a non-Jew and places it in the dough — one may separate from within the dough itself, and we are not concerned that the exempt leaven happened to come into his hand; rather, we say the entire [dough] became obligated through the blending. I find this puzzling, for Terumat HaDeshen, siman 190, writes that blending applies only to wine and oil alone, and requires that one separate a larger challah than the measure of the non-Jew's dough that became mixed in — for in either case that challah will contain some of the Israelite's dough. He writes that one should be stringent in this way; but then he writes that one can also question this approach, since women are accustomed to take a small piece of dough from the large dough and keep it as leaven to leaven another dough, and she takes this piece from the dough before separating challah so that it should be from the obligated [portion] — for if they took it after the separation the entire dough would be exempt and this leaven would also be exempt, like the non-Jew's leaven, requiring a remedy. Now in this case, if they take it before separation, one must be concerned that perhaps the non-Jew's leaven that had mixed in — being exempt — will come into her hand, and when she leavens [another dough] with this leaven derived from that piece, it too will become mixed in the second dough, and when she separates from the second dough, one must also be concerned that perhaps the piece taken from the first dough — being exempt — will come into her hand. However, one may say we are not concerned that the exact same exempt [portion] would come into his hand twice. End quote. This is what the Rema wrote: "But if he already proceeded, etc." And therefore one must be careful after Pesach to take challah in a larger amount than the measure of the non-Jew's leaven, as is concluded there in Terumat HaDeshen.
