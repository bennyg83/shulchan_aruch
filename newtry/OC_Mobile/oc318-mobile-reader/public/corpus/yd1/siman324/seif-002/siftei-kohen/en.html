The five species of grain. Since the obligation of challah applies equally to all five, they combine.
When he mixed them as flour. And kneaded them together. Since they were kneaded together, the entire dough appears uniform, as though it were of one species.
Spelt and rye, etc. Barley may be tithed for rye and conversely, since they are one species. Although where neither dough has the requisite measure, many more combinations are effective, such as wheat with spelt, spelt with all the others, and similarly regarding the remaining grains, here wheat may be separated only for spelt, but not spelt for oats or barley; likewise barley and oats may not be separated for rye. It appears that the reason is as follows. For combining doughs to create an obligation of challah, the Torah makes the law depend upon dough, as it says, 'your doughs.' Therefore, when neither has the requisite amount, all sufficiently similar doughs combine and become liable even if they are not one species. But to exempt one liable dough by separating challah from another species, since the Torah calls challah terumah, its law parallels terumah, which may not be separated from one species for another even where the doughs are similar.
It is liable in challah. Since they did not combine while mixed, no obligation arose until after division, when each received an additional quarter-kav of its own species. Each then contained five quarter-kav measures of one species and independently became liable.
It is exempt. Since they once became liable and were then exempted upon division, adding a quarter-kav to each does not create a new obligation, as explained below in siman 326. Reference 80.
