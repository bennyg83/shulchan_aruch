One should not eulogize him. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Yoreh Deah siman 78, discusses this extensively and argues that according to major Rishonim, as long as the deceased died within the preceding thirty days, a eulogy is permitted even during the thirty days before a festival, because such a person is still considered a recent death. Only where more than thirty days have already passed since death, while one is within thirty days of the festival, is the death considered old and the eulogy forbidden. Radbaz, new responsa siman 54, holds similarly. Nevertheless, Beit Ephraim concludes that one should not depart from Shulchan Aruch, which all Israel accepted. If, however, a great leader of the generation dies and the needs of the time require that all talmidei chachamim eulogize him, the honor of Torah justifies a eulogy even within thirty days of the festival, and he himself ruled this way in practice. Yaavetz likewise saw grounds for leniency, although his statement that the prohibition applies only to one's own relative requires study. Agudat Ezov, in Alon Bachut derush 6, cites Shevut Yaakov, part 2, as permitting a eulogy for a talmid chacham within thirty days before a festival because of Torah honor. He rejects that proof, but writes that the prohibition certainly does not apply where no relatives of the deceased are present. On reviewing Shevut Yaakov, part 2, end of siman 25, I found that he himself makes essentially this distinction: for one's own deceased, grief is intense and there is concern, according to Rav, that one will spend festival funds on the eulogy, or according to Shmuel, that the grief will be revived during the festival. But when one eulogizes solely for Torah honor and not as a personal mourner, the heart is not stirred to the same degree. Torah honor is therefore only an additional supporting factor. Agudat Ezov further writes that Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur are not treated like pilgrimage festivals for this rule.
