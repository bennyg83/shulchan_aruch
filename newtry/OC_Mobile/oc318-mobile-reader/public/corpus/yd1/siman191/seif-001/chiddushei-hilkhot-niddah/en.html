The woman, etc. Regarding the difficulty from an examination cloth that was not checked, see above, siman 190; see there.
"And some say that one may permit only when she is sitting," etc. This is the view of the Tur and the Rosh: even R' Yose disagrees with R' Meir only where the urine spurts, for then there is one favorable factor; but where it flows while she is standing, there are two adverse factors and R' Yose agrees that she is impure. Bach asks: if so, why does the Gemara ask, "If they purify with one doubt, all the more so with two doubts"? With two doubts she is pure even when standing and flowing. This is no difficulty. The Gemara asks this only after concluding that even R' Meir purifies in a case of two doubts. Necessarily, two doubts here are equivalent to more than one favorable factor, because R' Meir is stringent where there is only one favorable factor yet is lenient with two doubts. This shows that because there is also a doubt whether the blood came from the woman at all, the case is treated like two favorable factors, and even R' Meir is lenient. The Gemara can then properly ask that if R' Yose permits with only one doubt, where there is only one favorable factor, then all the more so he certainly permits with two doubts, which necessarily count as two favorable factors because even R' Meir permits there. The matter is straightforward.
"But if it was found in a basin used by a man and a woman," etc., she is pure in every circumstance. Shach in seif katan 6 writes that this means where the blood was found inside the basin. If he means specifically where she was sitting, his words are unfounded, because when she stands there is one adverse factor and yet she is permitted through a double doubt; the same applies where she sits and the urine flows, which likewise has only one adverse factor and is permitted through a double doubt. If he means where she stood, then at least she must have urinated in a stream, which is one favorable factor. In that formulation his words are not refuted, but he still has no proof. His wording indicates that he inferred it from the Mishnah's phrase "upon the water," but that inference is incorrect, because the Mishnah also deals with a sitting woman. His position therefore has no basis.
"Only in a woman who has a veset." See Shach, who discusses at length in seif katan 7 why a fixed veset should be necessary. The argument "otherwise she would never become impure" does not apply, since she can become impure when she sees blood not in connection with urination. Shach therefore explains that Maharil too deals with a woman whose veset blood appears only when she urinates, and for that reason he required some form of veset. In my view this cannot be correct. If this woman never sees her veset blood except together with urine, then it is established before us that she does pass uterine blood together with urine. How then could Maharil permit even where she lacks a fixed veset and merely has an ordinary pattern not to see earlier than, for example, 20 days, although at times she sees on day 21 or 23, as is explicit in the responsum? Since she has no fixed veset regarding which we can invoke the presumption that her period comes at its time, if after only 10 days she sees blood with urine, on what basis can we declare it pure? We already know that her uterine blood can emerge with urine. Why should blood found with urine after 10 days be pure while blood found with urine after 20 days is impure? This is very difficult, particularly when one seeks to purify a woman concerning whom it is clear that blood from the source emerges with her urine, since according to this theory she sees only in that manner. She has no fixed veset, merely a habit not to see earlier than a certain time; if she once or twice sees earlier, shall she therefore be pure? Moreover, why did Maharil labor so greatly to permit this woman merely because she usually does not see with her urine except at a defined time, when at that defined time even an internal cloth examination would render her impure? If the concern is only that on one occasion she changed her pattern, why such extensive discussion when ordinarily she sees only at that defined time? If "she sees from 20 days to 20 days" meant an ordinary sighting unrelated to urination, then the Shach's original question has no force at all, because once she also sees at other times without urinating, she clearly can become impure. Therefore the plain truth is that Maharil means that she sees every 20 days in the ordinary sense, not only when urinating, and the responsum itself indicates this, unlike the Shach. The answer to the Shach's question is that Maharil's statement "otherwise she would never be impure" does not mean that she would remain pure throughout her entire life, for obviously she could become impure if she saw blood not while urinating. Rather, his meaning is that whenever blood appears with urination, that type of sighting would always be declared pure. That cannot be correct for a woman who has no fixed or even bounded time of seeing, who at times sees now and at times later. Over many days and years it is inevitable that on some occasion her real uterine flow will happen to coincide with urination. Indeed, the warmth of urination can itself precipitate blood, as the authorities write. If we always purify her in such a circumstance, we will sometimes purify a woman who actually saw ordinary uterine blood. This is Maharil's concern. If, however, she has at least a bounded pattern, such as not seeing before 20 days, then within those earlier days we have partially escaped this concern: we may say that it is unlikely that uterine blood happened to emerge with the urine when she is not prone to see during that period. When there is no such boundary, who can determine whether the sighting adjacent to urination was her actual veset? This is the correct explanation, and one who studies Maharil will see that these words are clear and correct. See Birkei Yosef.
"And if she found blood within the urine and also upon the examination cloth," etc., some say that she is impure. Shach in seif katan 8 writes that this is unnecessary, because above we already ruled stringently except where she urinates in a stream. Besides the fact that one can answer that above we say: if the blood came from the urine, why was it found on the rim and not inside the basin, whereas with an examination it is quite possible that remnants of a drop of blood from the urine remained attached to the sides of the vaginal passage and were then found on the cloth, so there is no adverse factor at all, it also appears that according to this opinion, when blood is found on the cloth we do not attribute it to urine at all. Attribution to urine applies only to blood seen while urinating; blood found on an examination cloth has no inherent connection to urination. Therefore, if she examined with a cloth that had not been checked, this is governed by the law explained above in siman 190. If instead we said that this is simply a case where she saw blood with urine and also on the cloth, and she is forbidden only as in the case where blood is found on the rim of the basin, as Shach explains, then her impurity would be only because of the doubt that the urine may have returned to the source. With an unchecked cloth there would then be a double doubt: perhaps the blood came from elsewhere, and even if it came from the examination, perhaps it came from the urine. The ruling therefore teaches that in the context of an examination we do not attribute the blood to urine at all, and the case reverts to the law above in siman 190, where even an unchecked cloth can render her impure. This is proper.
"Only small granules like sand," etc. See Taz, seif katan 4, who discusses this at length and concludes that even if earth-like particles and granules are found not at the time of urination, the Rama's ruling applies. Chacham Tzvi objected in his responsa that Ran and Rama deal only with particles found after urination, not otherwise. Shevut Yaakov defended Taz, reasoning that Ran's point is that women do not ordinarily discharge blood in the form of granules, so this remains uncommon even when not associated with urination. These words are incorrect. The case necessarily concerns granules that dissolve, as Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe write, and as Shach states in seif katan 11. If so, the Gemara's discussion of a woman who miscarries things resembling gnats or barley grains is difficult: the Sages say that she should place them in water, and if they dissolve she is impure. Does a woman ordinarily see blood in the form of gnats and the like? What difference is there between gnats and granules? Ran deals even where she feels no pain, as Taz himself writes. Rather, heaven forbid that we permit when the particles dissolve, for then they are actual blood. The point is only that where they appear with urine there is an independent ground for leniency: R' Yose purifies entirely even with all the adverse factors, while R' Meir is stringent because he is concerned for a minority case that perhaps the urine returned to the source and brought blood with it. Ran holds that one may say this regarding blood, but not regarding granules, because it is more plausible to attribute granules to the urinary tract, which derives from the kidneys and through which urine is filtered and passes, than to the uterus, where formation of granules is uncommon. But where the particles are seen not with urine and there is no reason at all to attribute them to the urinary tract, and ordinary internal examinations are presumed to reach the uterine source, why should one connect them to urine? We attribute them to the source; if they dissolve, they are dissolved blood and she is impure. This is especially true in the Taz's case, where she felt pain above the uterine area. What connection do the kidneys have there, when they lie toward the back near the end of the spine? It is much clearer that the blood came from the source. Shevut Yaakov sought to distinguish between granules and the case where she sees matter like sand, saying that the latter results from an injury in the kidneys. What then will he answer concerning a woman who discharged something like peels, where physicians testified that she had an intestinal wound, yet if the matter dissolved she was impure? See what I wrote above in siman 188. I am astonished that this eminent authority relied upon fine distinctions without roots in the Talmud or earlier poskim to permit such a severe prohibition, especially in the case of an internal examination, which can involve a Torah prohibition. Had he used such subtle reasoning to be stringent we would not necessarily accept it, all the more so to be lenient. With due respect, his criticism of Chacham Tzvi as mere excess language was inappropriate, for Chacham Tzvi's words here are clear. Heaven forbid permitting granules when there was no urination. Reliance upon physicians is a different issue, as explained above in siman 188; absent that, granules must not be permitted, as explained, for there is no relevant difference between granules, peels, and similar matter.
