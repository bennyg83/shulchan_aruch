The Rama appears to equate Responsa Rashba with the glosses of the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Batra in the name of the abridged Or Zarua, distinguishing between the donor himself and a deceased donor's heirs. The heirs are considered to be in possession of the property, and therefore the rule that a doubt concerning prohibition is resolved stringently does not apply against them. Responsa Rashba, at the end of siman 656, however, does not appear to make such a distinction. The Rashba writes that if it is uncertain whether the donor intended to retract, logic dictates leniency for the heir, since the property is presumed to belong to its owner, and heirs stand in the owner's place automatically. The donor consecrated it only conditionally, and the poor, who claim that the condition was fulfilled, must prove it. This resembles the baraita in the chapter Mi SheMet: the owner may recover the property from them without proof, whereas they may recover it from him only with proof. Although this is a doubt concerning consecrated property, we do not apply the rule that a doubt concerning prohibition is resolved stringently. First, property consecrated for the poor is ordinary monetary property, and we tell them to bring proof and collect, as in the discussion in Chullin 79a concerning priestly gifts from an animal of mixed species. Accordingly, whether the consecration is for the poor or for a synagogue, whenever there is an initial doubt whether the consecration ever took effect and the opposing party has a presumption of ownership, we leave the money with its presumptive owner. Thus, in the chapter Mi SheMet, concerning a dangerously ill person who recovered and ordinarily retracts his gift, the Gemara asks what the law is if he consecrated all his property, declared all his property ownerless, or distributed all his property to the poor. The questions remain unresolved, and the unresolved questions there are treated leniently. The Rambam likewise ruled so. Even according to those who rule stringently there, this case should be treated leniently. There, the consecration certainly took effect, and the doubt is only whether recovery allows him to retract. Here, the doubt is whether the consecration took effect at all, and doubtful consecration cannot remove property from its owner. If the Rama's distinction were correct, what proof could the Rashba bring from priestly gifts and from a recovered donor, where no heirs are involved? Rather, there is no distinction. It is always treated as a monetary doubt and resolved leniently for the party in possession. Mahari likewise implies this explicitly in his rulings, at the end of siman 73, regarding consecration made through an asmachta. He writes that one cannot impose liability merely by applying stringency to a dispute among authorities concerning charity. In Bava Batra 148b the Gemara asks what the law is if a dangerously ill person consecrated, declared ownerless, or distributed all his property to the poor. The Or Zarua ruled that the property remains with its presumptive owner and the poor do not acquire it, since the questions concerning consecration remain unresolved. The Rosh, who ruled that the poor acquire it, relied on another reason, as is evident there. Responsa Rivash, siman 170, likewise makes clear that no distinction of this kind exists. The glosses of the Mordechai also require study. They cite the Sifra and Yerushalmi concerning leket, shikchah, and peah to prove that a doubt concerning charity is resolved stringently. Yet this baraita is cited in our Gemara in Chullin 134a, and Rava explains that the stringency applies because the standing crop already had a presumptive obligation of leket. This implies that without such a presumption, the doubt would be monetary and resolved leniently. Abaye likewise challenges from dough prepared before conversion, which is exempt from challah, whereas dough prepared after conversion is obligated. In a doubtful case it is obligated, and the Gemara answers that a doubt concerning prohibition is resolved stringently, whereas a monetary doubt is resolved leniently. Thus, a doubt involving gifts to the poor is a monetary doubt, not a doubt concerning prohibition. Otherwise, Rava would not have needed the reason that the standing crop had a presumptive obligation. I later found that the Ran, at the beginning of Nedarim 7a, discusses the Gemara's question whether a partial declaration is effective for charity, as where one says, ‘This coin is for charity, and this one also.’ The Ran cites the Rashba, who ruled stringently concerning peah and charity because the question remained unresolved, and the Ramban likewise ruled that every unresolved question concerning prohibition is resolved stringently. The Ran objects that the sugya at the end of the chapter HaZeroa VeHaLechayayim explicitly treats a doubt concerning money of the poor as a monetary doubt, resolved leniently for the defendant. He further cites Yoma 9a and concludes that a doubt concerning money of the poor is not considered a doubt concerning prohibition at all, but a monetary doubt resolved leniently. Their words therefore do not appear correct. This itself requires study, since in the responsum cited above the Rashba also treats the matter as a monetary doubt and resolves it leniently. Mahari's statement that the Rosh ruled the poor acquired for another reason also implies that he agrees that an ordinary doubt concerning the poor is monetary and resolved leniently. Yet at the beginning of Nedarim the Rosh wrote concerning the unresolved question of saying, ‘This coin is for charity, and this one also,’ that an unresolved Torah question is treated stringently. It appears that the Rosh needed another reason in Mi SheMet because the Gemara's question there also involved declaring property ownerless, and all agree that a doubt concerning ownerless property is monetary and resolved leniently, as the Ran wrote there. I later found that Responsa R. Betzalel, siman 15, cites Responsa Ran, siman 1, and Responsa Rivash, siman 160, who ruled that the matter is a monetary doubt and is resolved leniently. It appears there that this is the accepted law. The Mechaber and Rama likewise ruled plainly in Choshen Mishpat, siman 250, seif 3, regarding the question in Mi SheMet concerning one who consecrated all his property, that if he recovered he may retract. Even if the poor seized the property, it is removed from them, following the Rivash's rule that seizure does not help in an unresolved question of the Gemara. See Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 1, siman 5. I later found that Responsa Mahari Weil, at the end of siman 173, writes: ‘Should you argue that because the woman instructed that the money be given to charity, the matter is one of prohibition and a doubtful prohibition is resolved stringently, as in Mi SheMet where the Gemara asks about a dangerously ill person who consecrated all his property and the question remains unresolved, and the Rosh wrote that doubtful consecration is not annulled, there the person consecrated all his property without qualification, so it is possible that he intended the consecration to remain whether he lived or died, and the consecration began during his lifetime. In our case, however, the woman expressly stated that the gift should take effect after her death. It may therefore be said that the charity never began at all, and the assets remain in the heirs' possession. This resembles Rav's rule that money which one cannot recover through the courts does not become consecrated when he attempts to consecrate it. Here too, upon her death, the assets are in the heirs' possession.’ These matters require study.
