The community may, etc. Specifically the community may do so, but not the treasurer acting alone. This was already explained in siman 256, seif katan 8.
They may not redirect it, etc. The Mordechai, in the first chapter of Bava Batra, wrote in the name of Maharam that this must refer to a case where the items were already used and lit in the synagogue. If they were not yet lit there, then according to our ruling that designation alone has no legal effect, they may be redirected even to a non-mitzvah purpose. Although the donor may not retract and retain them for himself once he transferred them for lighting in the synagogue, because his pledge has become binding, the community may nevertheless redirect them even to a non-mitzvah purpose. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite this. The matter requires study, because the other authorities make no such distinction. On the contrary, from their distinction based on whether the donor's name has been forgotten, it appears that so long as his name has not been forgotten, redirecting the item is prohibited in every circumstance. Perhaps this is why the Mechaber and Rama omitted this ruling. It appears that the Mordechai discusses a case in which the owners consent. His novelty is that the teaching's phrase ‘it is forbidden to redirect’ indicates an actual prohibition even when the owners agree, but only after the items have already been used. Before use, designation alone has no legal effect. Consider this carefully.
But they may redirect it for a mitzvah purpose. The Mordechai wrote in the name of Maharam that it may even be redirected to a lesser mitzvah. The principle that we ascend in sanctity and do not descend applies specifically to sacred articles, not to objects used for a mitzvah. Darkei Moshe commented: ‘It appears to me that the Rosh, and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in seif 2, disagree, for they wrote that money designated for Torah study may not be redirected for the needs of a synagogue. Maharik likewise wrote in shoresh 128 in the name of the Rashba.’ This is also cited in the glosses to the Derishah, seif katan 26. In truth, Maharik wrote there that no authority could disagree with the Mordechai. Otherwise, the teaching should have distinguished within mitzvah purposes themselves, stating that one may redirect only to a mitzvah of equal importance, but not to a lesser mitzvah. Although the Rashba was asked whether charity funds may be redirected to a synagogue and replied that they may be redirected to a higher use, such as building a synagogue or purchasing a Sefer Torah, and although the Tur in Yoreh Deah wrote in the name of the Rosh that funds for Torah study may not be used for a synagogue, those cases concern a community unable to support both needs, so that redirecting the funds would cause Torah study to cease. Where the other mitzvah will not thereby be canceled, all agree that the redirection is permitted. The Derishah also cites the Mordechai in explanation of the Tur, indicating that he saw no disagreement. The Bach appears to distinguish the Rosh's case as involving sacred articles. He wrote that a lamp or menorah donated to a synagogue may, even after use, be redirected to another mitzvah, even a lesser one, because the rule of ascending in sanctity applies to sacred articles but not to mitzvah objects. A lamp and menorah are called mitzvah objects because they were given only to provide light for those praying from siddurim and machzorim, which serves the worshippers rather than the sacred object itself. Mahari likewise wrote that metal plates attached to a Sefer Torah are not sacred articles when they serve merely as identifying markers so that people do not mistakenly remove a Sefer Torah that was not designated for that day's reading. The same applies to synagogue lighting, which prevents mistakes in prayer. Maharik, however, does not appear to agree with this distinction. It seems that he held that money donated for synagogue needs has a stronger status than an ordinary mitzvah object, even though the money itself bears no sanctity. The Beit Yosef cites Maharik, shoresh 128, who wrote in the name of a responsum of the Rashba that the ruling concerning a Jew who donated a lamp or menorah applies only where the synagogue already has sufficient lamps and lighting. If its needs are not adequately supplied, the community may not redirect the donation. See Maharik at the end of that shoresh, where he cites another responsum attributed to the Rashba that contradicts this one, and concludes that the first is not actually a responsum of the Rashba. He further concludes that even if the donor's gift increases the lighting and permits the synagogue to be lit more generously, the community may redirect it so long as the ordinary requirement would still be adequately met without his donation. But if the regular lighting depends upon this donor, and at times the community would otherwise lack sufficient oil for proper illumination, they have no authority to redirect it.
If the donor was a non-Jew, etc. A non-Jew is likely to protest loudly and say, ‘I consecrated an item to the Jews' synagogue, and they sold it.’ This is stated in Responsa Rosh. A Jew, however, even if he refuses to heed the words of the sages and protests against their enactment, is not heeded, since no desecration of Hashem's Name results. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite this. See Responsa R. Eliyahu Mizrachi, siman 53, concerning this and other laws of consecrated property.
So long as the owner's name has not been forgotten. This implies that once the owner's name has been forgotten, the item may be redirected even to a non-mitzvah purpose. Tosafot wrote in Arachin 6a: ‘If the reference is to a non-mitzvah purpose, why mention a non-Jew? The same should apply to a Jew. The Gemara could likewise have asked: Why mention that the owner's name was forgotten? Even if it was not forgotten, the same should apply.’ The printed wording is necessarily erroneous, as is evident from several proofs to one who examines the passage. It should read: ‘Why mention that the owner's name was not forgotten? Even if it was forgotten, the same should apply.’ Tosafot inferred from the baraita's unqualified statement that it is forbidden to redirect the item even after the owner's name was forgotten, and the Gemara there establishes the case as redirecting it to a non-mitzvah purpose. Nevertheless, Tosafot provides no proof against the present ruling. Tosafot holds that even when a Jew donated the item, redirecting it to a non-mitzvah purpose is forbidden after his name has been forgotten, since they understood the baraita as applying in all circumstances, even to a Jewish donor. The authorities, the Tur, and the Shulchan Aruch do not rule this way. They hold that once the donor's name has been forgotten, the item may be redirected even to a non-mitzvah purpose. The same therefore applies to a non-Jewish donor, for once his name has been forgotten there is no basis to distinguish between a non-Jew and a Jew on the ground that the non-Jew will protest. This also resolves Tosafot's question there, that the Gemara could have asked why the baraita mentioned that the owner's name had been forgotten. It could not have asked that, because once the name has been forgotten, redirecting the item to a non-mitzvah purpose is permitted for both a non-Jewish and a Jewish donor. This is the primary ruling.
