For the needs of a synagogue, etc. This is a change to a higher level, since the sanctity of a beit midrash is greater than that of a synagogue, and one may convert a synagogue into a beit midrash, as explained in Orach Chaim, siman 153. See Responsa Maharam Galanti, siman 76.
Or Torah study. In Responsa Rosh, kelal 13, siman 14, the Rosh himself concludes: ‘There is no prohibition here on account of designation. Even according to the opinion that designation has legal effect, that applies only where one weaves a garment for a corpse. This case resembles yarn dyed for weaving, which all agree has no legal effect.’ This requires study. Even if this were a full designation, it is nevertheless permitted to transfer something from a lesser sanctity to a greater sanctity, for one may convert an actual synagogue into a beit midrash, as stated in the Gemara and explained in Orach Chaim, siman 153. Furthermore, when money has been prepared in this manner, it certainly constitutes a full consecration, for otherwise it could be redirected even to a lesser sanctity, as implied in Orach Chaim, siman 153. The Rosh likewise wrote in his responsum, siman 53, page 77, side 3: ‘Should you ask: When money was collected to build a synagogue or beit midrash, or to purchase a Sefer Torah, why may it be redirected only from a lesser sanctity to a greater one? It has not yet been used for a sacred purpose. Likewise, when we say that once the money reaches the treasurer it may no longer be redirected, there has still been only designation. The answer is that collecting money for a synagogue or beit midrash is an actual deed, not mere designation.’ See there. The Bach likewise wrote this in Orach Chaim, siman 153, seif 3. In truth, however, the Tur and Beit Yosef did not cite this concluding passage from the Rosh's responsum. This requires study.
But not from Torah study for the needs of a synagogue. Although above, siman 256, seif katan 7, the Rosh and Tur wrote that the townspeople may redirect charity funds even to a non-mitzvah purpose, that applies where they will later collect the money again. Here, however, they will not repay it. Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 15, page 108, side 1, likewise states that if they wish to redirect the funds temporarily to another mitzvah until money is collected for that other mitzvah, and they will then repay the original charity fund, all agree that this is permitted. See there.
All this applies where there is no established custom, etc. In such a place, even if the donor claims with certainty that he did not consecrate the item for communal ownership, he cannot remove it from its presumptive consecrated status unless, at the time of the donation, he expressly stipulated this publicly or before witnesses. Maharshal wrote this in Responsa, siman 15, and the Bach cites him. They wrote the same regarding a Sefer Torah, deriving it from Maharik, shoresh 161, concerning consecrated silver vessels. See there. Responsa Mahari Kohen of Krakow, siman 21, however, distinguishes between the cases. A Sefer Torah is ordinarily placed in a synagogue, and the donor's father's name is known in connection with it. Who, then, can remove it from the father's presumptive ownership? The fact that he placed the Sefer Torah there does not prove that it belongs to the congregation. In our times, the usual purpose of writing a Sefer Torah is to leave it where it can be read publicly. Its sanctity is also great, and it is not fitting to keep a Sefer Torah in every location. Thus, placing it in a synagogue does not prove that he consecrated it to the congregation. Silver vessels are different, since the custom is not to leave them permanently in a synagogue. Had they not been consecrated, they would have been brought to the synagogue only when needed and afterward returned to the owner's home. Maharik therefore wrote in shoresh 161 that silver vessels left there are presumed to belong to the congregation. Regarding a Sefer Torah, he wrote in shoresh 70 that it remains in the presumptive ownership of the person whose name is associated with it, meaning the person of whom people say, ‘This is so-and-so's Sefer Torah,’ for the reason explained above. He also discusses at length who may testify in such a case, since the townspeople have a personal interest in the testimony unless they formally remove that interest, and provided that another Sefer Torah is also available there. The Tur likewise wrote in siman 36 that all of them are interested parties with respect to the testimony. His reasoning is sound and persuasive. His distinction between silver vessels and a Sefer Torah is also correct, since an item should not be removed from its owner's presumptive possession without clear proof. I have discussed the law of a Sefer Torah at length because I heard that one rabbi ruled that even a Sefer Torah belongs to the consecrated property. I therefore found it necessary to bring proofs and reasons against his ruling.
But in a place where the custom is, etc. The Bach wrote that according to the custom of such places, a creditor may also collect his debt from a Sefer Torah kept in the aron kodesh. Since its owner may sell it in a time of financial distress, a creditor may likewise collect from it. His words require study, for below, siman 274, at the end of seif 2, he ruled that a creditor may not collect from a Sefer Torah, since the debtor may support himself from charity and need not sell it. Accordingly, the creditor should likewise be unable to collect it, for the creditor has no greater right than the debtor himself. Thus, the Bach's two rulings contradict one another. As a matter of practical law, his ruling here appears primary. The Tur, Choshen Mishpat, siman 97, likewise wrote this in the name of R. Yehudah of Barcelona, and the Shulchan Aruch ruled accordingly at the end of seif 23, that a creditor may collect his debt from a Sefer Torah. Responsa Mabit, part 2, siman 138, page 66, side 4, similarly discusses one who owed money by a written instrument and had a Sefer Torah kept in the synagogue in accordance with the custom. If the creditor finds no other property from which to collect, he may take the Sefer Torah. See there.
We follow the custom, for anyone who consecrates, etc. This requires study. The ruling is taken from Maharik, yet Maharik there appears hesitant and inclined to prohibit it. He wrote: ‘Regarding what you wrote, that such acts occur daily in those regions, since it is clear that sanctity takes effect upon the vessels by force of the laws of the Gemara, and you seek to remove that sanctity by force of custom, surely you know that custom does not override halachah unless it is an established custom instituted by the local sages, not merely by the multitude of householders, as the Mordechai wrote in the name of the Or Zarua at the beginning of the chapter HaPo'alim. He also wrote there that the custom must have been established before it can override the law of the Gemara. Furthermore, at the beginning of HaPo'alim he wrote that it must be a custom of the pious elders. A custom that has no Torah basis is merely an error in judgment, like the many defective customs that we do not follow. In my opinion, this is not an established custom at all.’ It must be that the Rama inferred from Maharik's concluding words, ‘In my opinion, this is not an established custom at all,’ that the decisive factor is whether the custom is established. The Bach likewise followed the Rama's formulation. This requires study.
