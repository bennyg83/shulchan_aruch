The soles are covered with processed goatskin, while opposite the heel some are made of wool. There is no kilayim prohibition because the heel is hard and does not become warmed. Derishah asks from seif 3, where a large garment with kilayim on one side is prohibited even on the other side. Why then may the rest of the foot benefit from footwear containing kilayim at the heel? He gives lengthy answers. In my opinion there is no difficulty. The rule in seif 3 applies specifically to something that has the status of clothing. As explained above in seif 10, any soft item worn as clothing is prohibited even without actual warmth. Something that is not considered clothing is prohibited only if it actually warms the body. Here the item does not have the status of ordinary clothing because leather lies beneath it and only the area opposite the heel contains wool. If that wool produced warmth it would be prohibited on account of the warmth, but since there is no warmth and this is not a garment, it is permitted. This also resolves Rambam's language cited by Tur and Rama here: a shoe made with kilayim and lacking a heel may be worn because the skin of the foot is hard and one does not benefit from it like from the rest of the body's skin. Many have asked why Rambam specifies that the shoe lacks a heel. According to what I have written, it is clear. It appears to refer precisely to our slippers, commonly called pantofel, where there is no shoe leather surrounding the heel at all. Therefore, one may place kilayim beneath the upper leather over the foot or beneath the sole where the foot rests. Since there is nothing surrounding the heel, the kilayim material within the slipper is not treated as a garment in its own right. The only possible prohibition would therefore be actual warming, and the foot does not receive such warming. This explains the common practice of wearing slippers lined with cloth on all sides even when a gentile sewed the lining with linen thread. If, however, the same lining is placed within a full shoe that also has leather surrounding the heel, it is prohibited as a kilayim garment. We regard the cloth sewn inside as though it were an independent garment, despite the leather exterior. My father-in-law's words here contain an obvious slip of the pen. He says the novelty is that even though it has no heel it is prohibited, whereas the law is precisely that it is permitted. Beit Yosef similarly says that where it has a heel and therefore appears like a garment, and so forth. He says "appears like a garment," not that it is literally a garment, because it is covered by leather, as I have explained.
