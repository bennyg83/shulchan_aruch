They sew in their normal manner. Meaning that although while sewing the garment is on their knees in the normal manner of tailors, it is permitted because a davar she'eino mitkaven is permitted. This contradicts the nearby ruling that one may not evade the tax with kilayim, which the beginning of HaGozel Batra explicitly calls a davar she'eino mitkaven according to R. Akiva in the name of R. Shimon. These laws are the Rambam's words, and the Beit Yosef explains that the tax case is forbidden because there he wears it in the manner of wearing, unlike here and with clothing sellers, who do not actually wear it but carry it on the shoulder. I do not understand this distinction, for by Torah law even partial wearing, such as wrapping on the hand, is forbidden. Why should he receive Torah lashes when wearing it to evade the tax? It appears that the Rambam inferred from the Mishnah's sequence that these two laws do not dispute. Rather, with the tax he benefits from the wearing, because without it he would have to pay the tax; that benefit is like the benefit of wearing kilayim. This resembles seif 12 regarding a hot egg. But clothing sellers do not depend on the wearing for the sale; they use it only to ease the load or show its measure, and could do so another way. Since he does not intend benefit, it is permitted. This seems the Rambam's view.
And in rain, because of the rain, etc. In the Rambam this language appears first regarding tailors, while regarding clothing sellers he writes: provided they do not intend in the sun that the kilayim on their shoulders protect them from the sun, and in rain that they warm themselves with it. One may ask why he did not write the same wording in the first clause, and why in the second clause he changed between sun and rain. It appears he used the normal case: by law there is no difference, and one who intends protection from rain is also forbidden. But with a clothing seller carrying on his shoulder, how can one say he protects himself from rain by placing a garment on his shoulder while the rest of his body becomes wet? Protection from sun, however, is understandable, as he lifts the garment against the sun to shade his whole body. In truth, any intended benefit from sun or rain is forbidden. Therefore the Tur and Shulchan Aruch omitted this distinction, as it has no practical difference. I wrote this because the Perishah cited the Rambam's wording about warming and wondered whether, if he does it not for warmth but to keep rain from ruining his clothing, it is permitted. He compared it to barber-cloths, which protect clothes from hair. This is astonishing: from barber-cloths the opposite is proven, for the Rosh writes that if they have a head-opening they are forbidden though made only to protect from dirt. Here too, where it is a real garment and he covers with it against rain-dirt, it is certainly forbidden.
