And this contradicts what he ruled nearby, that to steal the tax is forbidden with kilayim, and this is a davar she'eino mitkaven, as we say explicitly in this at the beginning of Perek HaGozel Batra, that Rabbi Akiva in the name of Rabbi Shimon permits to evade the tax with it for the reason of davar she'eino mitkaven. And these laws are the words of the Rambam, and the Beit Yosef explained according to his view that specifically there, where he wears in a manner of wearing, it is forbidden even when not intending, which is not so here, and similarly with sellers of clothing, he does not actually wear it upon him but carries it on his shoulder. And I did not understand this distinction, for from the Torah it is forbidden even with partial wearing, namely wrapping on his hand — so why should this be more severe that he is lashed d'oraisa when he wears to steal the tax, as is explicit in the Rambam, from this that there is wearing on part of the body? And further it is difficult, for the Tur wrote to permit regarding sellers of clothing even if they wear it in order to show its measure, and the Beit Yosef wrote that the Rambam disagrees with this, and it is astonishing in what they dispute in this. And my father-in-law of blessed memory wrote that according to the Rambam it is forbidden regarding the tax because it is a psik reisha, regarding which Rabbi Shimon admits that it is forbidden — and the sugya of HaGozel Batra that we mentioned escaped him, where we say explicitly that according to Rabbi Shimon it is permitted regarding the tax, for thus we heard from him in the beraita. And it appears to my humble opinion that the Rambam proved from the fact that the Tanna taught in chapter 1 these laws, namely the issur of kilayim to evade the tax, and afterwards a heter regarding sellers of clothing — one cannot say that they dispute one another as the Rash wrote, for this is not agreeable to the Rambam; rather, perforce the Tanna holds that there is a distinction in the matter: regarding the tax he has benefit from the wearing, for without the wearing he would be obligated to give the tax, so it turns out that the wearing is a necessity for him, and this benefit is like the benefit of any other wearer of a kilayim garment that the Torah forbade, and it is similar to what is written in seif 2 regarding a hot egg, that it is considered benefit that he is saved from the heat of the egg. Which is not so regarding sellers of clothing, where the sale does not depend on this, but he does so to lighten his load when it is upon him, or to show its measure, and it would be possible to do it another way; therefore the wearing is not a necessity for him, and since he does not intend for his benefit, it is permitted. So it appears to me according to the view of the Rambam.
And in rain, because of the rain, and so forth. Rambam writes first, regarding tailors carrying garments, essentially the wording cited here. Regarding clothing merchants, however, he says: provided that in the sun they do not intend that the kilayim garments on their shoulders protect them from the sun, and in the rain they do not intend to warm themselves with them. It is surprising why Rambam does not use the same wording in both places. It is also surprising why in the latter case he changes from "protection from the sun" to "warming in the rain" rather than saying "protection from the rain." It appears he speaks in the ordinary manner. By strict law there is no distinction; intentionally protecting oneself from rain is also prohibited. But when a clothing merchant carries a garment on his shoulder, it is not natural to say he protects himself from rain merely by putting cloth over his shoulder while the rest of his body becomes wet. Protection from sun is different: he can raise the garment above himself and thereby cast shade over his whole body. In truth, therefore, any intended benefit from sun or rain is forbidden. This is why Tur and Shulchan Aruch omit the distinction, even though Shulchan Aruch otherwise copies Rambam's language here. The detail concerning merchants has no practical legal difference. I write this because Derishah cites Rambam's phrase "to warm himself with it" and questions whether one who does not seek warmth but only wishes to prevent rain from damaging his clothing may do so. He then compares it to garments used during haircutting, which are permitted when they lack a head opening even though they protect one's clothing from hair, and suggests the same may apply to protection from rain. I am astonished if these words truly came from him. The haircutting garments prove the opposite. Rosh, cited by Beit Yosef, says that when people are being shorn they spread a sheet over themselves so their clothing will not become dirty; the Yerushalmi says prohibition applies when it is intended as clothing, meaning when it has a head opening. Thus, when there is a head opening it is prohibited even though its sole purpose is to protect the garments from dirt. The same applies here: this is a genuine garment, and if one covers himself with it to protect his clothes from rain, the prohibition certainly applies, as Tur and Shulchan Aruch imply. This appears entirely clear to me. See seif 10.
