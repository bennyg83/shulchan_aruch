See Taz, seif katan 7, and Shach, seif katan 8, who explain why this does not contradict the preceding seif, where even wearing kilayim to evade the customs duty is forbidden. See Responsa Chavat Yair, siman 188, regarding this. See also Merkavat HaMishneh, chapter 1 of Hilchot Shabbat and chapter 10 of Hilchot Kilayim, halachah 18, who challenges the Taz and Chavat Yair and writes that the Shach's explanation is also forced. He explains that an unintended act is permitted even where the prohibited physical act itself certainly occurs. Thus, if one's coins scattered before an idol, bowing to gather them is forbidden only because of appearance, even though he physically bows and this is certainly a case of an inevitable result. The principle is that something is not considered an inevitable prohibited result when one's intent is directed to a permitted end and the prohibited act is merely incidental. In our case, however, one intends to wear the garment in order to evade the tax, and it is impossible to wear it without becoming warmed by it. Therefore the intent to wear is treated as intent to warm oneself, and it is an inevitable result. Similarly, one who places a saddlecloth on his shoulders in order to carry a load has an inevitable result. Clothing merchants and tailors, however, do not intend at all to wear the clothing, but only to carry or work with it. Their case is not deemed an inevitable result even though it rests on their shoulders, because their purpose is only transport, which could be done in the hand or on a staff without warming themselves. The same applies to bearers of a corpse or animal, whose intent is merely to carry, and to tailors, whose intent is merely to sew. Even though one cannot conveniently sew without placing the material on his knees, this is still an unintended act, comparable to gathering coins before an idol, where the bowing is treated as unintended because the coins could be gathered without bowing. There it is nevertheless forbidden because of appearance, whereas clothing merchants and tailors present no appearance concern because everyone knows they are occupied with their work. He discusses this at great length.
