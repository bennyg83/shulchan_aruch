Permitted to put, etc. Because they nullify the taste of wine — so wrote the poskim.
Whether other beverages. And we do not decree beverages because of wine since it is merely incidental — Gemara; and accordingly it is forbidden to put beverages in them on a permanent basis; and so we hold above siman 122 seif 4 in Hagah regarding other forbidden vessels — see there.
And likewise it is permitted, etc. Rashba wrote several reasons, which Ateret Zekenim and Bach bring. Bach rejected his words and wrote that we do not hold like all these reasons; therefore this law written by Rashba and the Mechaber is not so, but ab initio it is forbidden to salt in a prohibited utensil, as above in siman 105 seif 12, and after the fact too it is forbidden if it was soaked in it for a full day, end of his words. His words are not compelling. Moreover, since we hold that one may put beer and other beverages into them, whatever the reason may be, all the more it is permitted to salt in them. Even if we do not hold like Rashba's reasons, nevertheless the law is certainly true. Rashba wrote all those reasons because he holds that beer is also permitted for those reasons, and he compares salting to liquids. If we say the reason is as the Tur wrote, because the wine impairs them, and so Rosh wrote that the taste of wine emitted into them is impaired from the outset, and perhaps Rashi too, who wrote that beer nullifies the taste of wine, intended this, unlike Bach in the latter kuntres, and as implied from Bach's words that this reason is primary, if so all the more salt nullifies the taste of wine. On the contrary, beer and liquids do not help to kasher the utensil, while strong salt water does help, as above in siman 135 end of seif 12. From Rashba it also implies that even if it remained a long time, it is permitted, for he wrote the reason that since they absorbed only enough for a kelipah, the small prohibition in them is nullified in the liquids and salting; see there. Although we do not hold like Rashba that a utensil that absorbed a small amount of prohibition may be used ab initio for permitted food, as above in siman 122 s.k. 2, nevertheless regarding after the fact we certainly hold like Rashba, that presumably there is sixty against the emission of the utensil, which absorbed only enough for a kelipah, as is implied by the words of all the poskim; this is clear. Although Rosh wrote that even though we estimate against the whole utensil and the water and beer do not have sixty to nullify the wine, nevertheless it is permitted because the taste of wine emitted into them is impaired from the outset, end quote, one must say he does not disagree with the poskim who hold that presumably there is sixty, for the Tur also holds like them. Rather, he means to say that even if there is not sixty, such as when one put only a small amount of liquid in it, it is permitted. Know this, for Rosh wrote in responsum, and Beit Yosef brings it at the beginning of this siman, that if one placed wine in a non-Jew's utensil, it is permitted because there is sixty against the kelipah; see there. Furthermore, from the words of Tur and Shulchan Aruch it implies that even if one puts beer and liquids in them for a long time, even though after twenty-four hours it is like soaking, and with soaking there is no distinction between salty and other liquids, nevertheless all liquids nullify the taste of wine absorbed in the walls of the utensil. This is not comparable to above in siman 105, for there the utensils contain other prohibitions that give a good taste into all liquids and into salty foods, unlike wine whose taste is weakened in other liquids, all the more in salty food. So too appears to be the Rav's view in Torat Chattat kelal 17, see there, and in the simanim: in din 1 he wrote that cheese molds of non-Jews ab initio require hagalah before salting cheeses in them, and in din 3 he wrote that one may salt meat in utensils of non-Jews in which they place wine for keeping. So too his view appears from the fact that he wrote here simply like the Mechaber, and so is the view of Ateret Zekenim.
