Since he nullified one limb, etc. The Beit Yosef explained the Rambam's intent, as the author of this ruling was troubled by what we rule in the dispute between Rav and Shmuel at the end of the chapter Kol HaTzelamim, cited by the Beit Yosef, concerning an idol that broke by itself. There, when an ordinary craftsman cannot restore it, it is nullified according to all opinions. Yet earlier, at the beginning of chapter 25, Rav and Rabbi Yochanan disagree concerning this, and we rule stringently like Rabbi Yochanan. The Rambam therefore explained that when Rav and Shmuel discuss a case in which an ordinary craftsman cannot restore it and it is permitted, they do not mean that it is permitted without any nullification. Rather, one of the pieces must in any event be nullified. Since it broke by itself, we regard all the pieces as though the idol were still whole. Therefore, just as a non-Jew's nullification of even one limb would have been effective while it was whole, so too it is effective now. But where an ordinary craftsman can restore it, each piece is itself regarded as an idol, and every piece must therefore be nullified separately. The Tur, however, wrote here that if an ordinary craftsman cannot restore it, it is nullified. This is certainly difficult, for we rule like Rabbi Yochanan that an idol which broke by itself is not nullified. It appears that the Tur also accepts the resolution cited by the Beit Yosef in the name of the Ramban and the Ran, that there is a distinction between a case in which the main body of the idol remains and one in which it does not. Accordingly, the earlier dispute between Rav and Rabbi Yochanan concerns a case where the main body remains and only a small portion broke off, of sufficient significance that the idol would have been nullified had the non-Jew broken it intentionally. In that case we rule that it is not nullified when it broke by itself. There it is not relevant to ask whether an ordinary craftsman can restore it, since the non-Jew has no desire to restore it. Its main body remains, and he continues to worship it as before. By contrast, the dispute between Rav and Shmuel concerns a case where the entire idol was broken. This is indicated by their phrase, "an ordinary craftsman can restore it," which shows that its worship has ceased so long as it is not restored. In such a case, the Tur correctly ruled that if it cannot be restored, it is nullified even though it broke by itself, for the non-Jew certainly nullified it after seeing that it was completely broken. One might ask why the Tur did not state the law where the main body remains. The answer is that it is learned from what the Tur wrote. If an idol that broke by itself is nullified only when an ordinary craftsman cannot restore it, it follows that where restoration is unnecessary because the main body remains, it is forbidden when it broke by itself. This appears to be the correct understanding of the Tur's view.
