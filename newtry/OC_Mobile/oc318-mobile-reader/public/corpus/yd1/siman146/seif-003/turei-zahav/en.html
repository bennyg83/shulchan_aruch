One who purchases scrap metal, etc. There is a passage in the Tur that requires explanation, and we will explain it with the help of Heaven. A Baraisa states: If he performed meshichah before paying the money, he should return it; if he performed meshichah after paying the money, he must cast it into the Dead Sea. The questioner in the Gemara sought to prove from this that meshichah does not acquire from a non-Jew. Rava rejected this proof, for in truth it is a mistaken transaction. Rather, in the first clause, where he paid its value, it appears to be an idol belonging to a Jew; in the latter clause, where he did not pay its value, it does not so appear. The Tur wrote: If he performed meshichah before paying the money, he should return it to the non-Jew, for it had not yet been acquired. If he performed meshichah after paying the money, it had already been acquired by him, it is an idol belonging to a Jew, it cannot be nullified, and he must cast it into the Dead Sea. The Rambam likewise ruled, etc. This is difficult, for the Gemara concludes that in every case there is no valid acquisition, since it is a mistaken transaction. The distinction is only whether it appears to have been acquired. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that when the Tur says, "it had already been acquired by him," this means according to the perception of observers, who will think that he acquired it, since everyone will assume that it was acquired by him. This is entirely unsatisfactory. In truth, acquisition from a non-Jew is effected either through meshichah according to Rabbeinu Tam, or through money according to Rashi, as the Tur wrote below in siman 320. Only in order to satisfy both opinions did he write there, regarding the sanctity of a firstborn animal, that both meshichah and payment should be performed. It is therefore impossible to explain the Tur here as following the Tanna of the Baraisa, who required both. If so, the Baraisa would follow neither Rashi nor Rabbeinu Tam. Since the law is that either one alone suffices, why should we be concerned for observers specifically when both were performed, but not when one alone was performed, for observers could likewise say that it had already been acquired by the Jew? This is especially difficult since the Tur made the prohibition dependent upon there having been an acquisition. Moreover, the language of the Tur does not imply this. The Perishah wrote that the Tur truly intended what he wrote in siman 320, and therefore required both payment and meshichah. This has already been rejected by what we wrote, for the words of the Tur are the words of the Baraisa. Furthermore, he was not precise in this matter. The Tur required both only as a stringency, such as to remove the animal from the possibility of firstborn sanctity, but not as a leniency. Here, however, this would produce a leniency, permitting him to return the idol whenever both forms of acquisition were not performed. This is plainly untenable. On the contrary, the Tur is stringent when either one was performed, as in siman 132, see there, because meshichah acquires with respect to one who measures wine into the vessel of a non-Jew, while money acquires where one advanced a dinar to a shopkeeper. How, then, could the Tur rule leniently here until both were performed? Clearly, this cannot be the explanation. It appears to me that the words of the Tur may be explained after noting another difficulty. Why did he need to write here that it is an idol belonging to a Jew, that it cannot be nullified, and that it must be cast into the Dead Sea? Did we not already know the law governing an idol belonging to a Jew? It therefore appears to me that since, according to the Gemara's conclusion, the matter depends upon payment, one may ask why the Baraisa requires meshichah at all. Meshichah accomplishes nothing here, because the transaction was mistaken, and the only concern is appearances. If so, we should likewise be concerned where there was payment alone, for according to Rashi money ordinarily effects acquisition, and observers may not know that the transaction was mistaken. One might therefore have said that the essential factor is payment, and that when the Tanna said, "after he paid the money he performed meshichah," meshichah was not meant precisely, but merely indicated that he drew the scrap metal to himself and then discovered the idol. The Tur therefore teaches that this is not so. The true principle is that whenever, had he known that the idol was among the scrap metal, he would legally have acquired it, then when the transaction was mistaken we decree because observers may think that he knew of it. But where, even had he known, the law would not have effected acquisition, we do not decree merely because observers might misunderstand his lack of knowledge. Thus, where he performed meshichah alone, even had he known of the idol he would not have acquired it. When he drew all the scrap metal to himself, he certainly intended to sort it afterward, retaining what was permitted and returning what was forbidden. He desires to acquire the permitted material, but does not desire to acquire the forbidden material, as we say in the chapter HaShoel. If, however, he already paid for the entire weight of the scrap metal, this cannot be said. Had he known that an idol was included, he would certainly have acquired it. Therefore, when he was unaware of it at the time of the purchase, the Sages were concerned for appearances, but specifically when both payment and meshichah occurred. One might ask why meshichah is needed, since payment alone would acquire it had he known, and we should therefore be concerned for observers even where he was unaware. The answer is that even if he knew and acquired the idol through payment, as long as it had not come into his possession he would not need to cast it into the Dead Sea. It would suffice for the non-Jew to nullify it, as we proved above, for the requirement applies specifically when it came into the Jew's possession. The Tur therefore had to write here that he must cast it into the Dead Sea, teaching the point explained above. The meaning of the Tur is thus as follows: One who purchased scrap metal and unknowingly found an idol among it, if he performed meshichah before paying, he should return it. There is no concern for observers, since even had he known of the idol the transaction would not yet have acquired it. But if he performed both acts, he may not return it, since had he known, it would already have been acquired. Should you ask why meshichah is necessary, the Tur answers that it is then an idol belonging to a Jew and must be cast into the Dead Sea, since it came into his possession; therefore the Sages were concerned even though he acted unknowingly. One may further ask why Rava established the Baraisa as involving a mistaken transaction and a concern for appearances, rather than as involving one who knew of the idol and requiring both acts of acquisition. The answer is that the Baraisa says that he "found" an idol, implying that he did not know of it at the time of the transaction. It should therefore have been permitted in every case. Why, then, is it forbidden when both acts occurred? Necessarily, it is because of observers, in accordance with the distinction explained above. According to our explanation, the Tur's words, "and so too wrote the Rambam," are also resolved. The Beit Yosef asked that this is an explicit Baraisa. According to what I have written, it is well understood, for the Tur teaches that both acts are required, and payment alone is insufficient, as explained above. Everything is thus properly resolved, with the help of Heaven.
