He sold it or pledged it, etc. The Mishnah states: If he sold it or pledged it, Rebbi says it is nullified, while the Sages say it is not nullified. The halacha is known to follow the Sages. The Gemara records a dispute: One opinion says that they disagree where it was given to a non-Jewish smith, but where it was given to a Jewish smith all agree that it is nullified. The other opinion says that they disagree even where it was given to a Jewish smith. The Tur ruled stringently, that even when it was given to a Jewish smith, the halacha follows the Sages, who say that it is not nullified. The Rambam ruled according to the first version, leniently when it was given to a Jewish smith. The Ran explained his reasoning from the Baraisa taught afterward: If he borrowed money upon it, or a collapse fell upon it, etc., it is not nullified. The Baraisa needed to teach this, for had it taught only that he borrowed upon it, one might have said that since he did not sell it, it is not nullified. The implication is that selling does effect nullification. This must refer to a Jewish smith and follow the Sages. Thus, selling is effective, but merely borrowing upon it is not. The Ran further wrote that according to Rebbi, pledging it nullifies it specifically when the non-Jew made it an absolute security, saying, "If I do not redeem it by such-and-such time, it shall belong to you," and the time arrived without his redeeming it. Only then is it nullified according to Rebbi, since this is comparable to a sale. Consequently, according to the Rambam, the halacha for us is likewise that such a pledge to a Jewish smith effects nullification. The Beit Yosef disagreed with the Ran and wrote that regarding a pledge there is no distinction whether it was made an absolute security or not. Even if it was made an absolute security to a Jewish smith, it is not nullified. He proved this because the Gemara, concerning one who borrowed upon it, did not say, "because he did not make it an absolute security." Why did it instead say, "because he did not sell it"? Evidently, with respect to a pledge, we do not distinguish whether it was made an absolute security or not. He also wrote that this follows from several passages in the Rambam. It appears that the Beit Yosef understands the Rambam as follows. The Rambam wrote: "If he sold it to a Jewish smith, it is nullified. If he pledged it, or sold it to a non-Jew or to a Jew who is not a smith, it is not nullified," end quote. Since the Rambam mentioned only a sale in the first clause, this indicates that even an absolute pledge does not help. Accordingly, the Rambam's latter words refer to two cases: If he pledged it, even to a Jewish smith, or if he sold it to one who is not a smith, it is not nullified. According to this explanation, however, it is difficult why the Shulchan Aruch wrote here, "and some permit it when given to a Jewish smith," which implies that this leniency applies even to a pledge. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, likewise wrote that the Shulchan Aruch's ruling, "some permit," has no application to a pledge. I am astonished by the Beit Yosef. The Gemara says, "But with a Jewish smith all agree that it is nullified," meaning that the Sages agree with Rebbi. Rebbi explicitly spoke in the Mishnah of both pledging and selling. How can we say that the Sages agreed with him only regarding a sale, while inventing a new dispute regarding a pledge, under which even a pledge to a Jewish smith would not be nullified according to the Sages? Rather, the words of the Ran are certainly primary. The pledge mentioned in the Mishnah means that it was made an absolute security. In that case, the Sages agree with Rebbi that it is nullified when given to a Jewish smith. The Baraisa's statement that one who borrowed upon it has not nullified it refers to an ordinary pledge, not made absolute, and it deals with a Jewish smith according to the Sages. Specifically an ordinary loan upon it does not nullify it. But if he made it an absolute security until a fixed time and failed to redeem it, it is like a complete sale, as written in Orach Chaim siman 441 regarding chametz belonging to a Jew that is in the possession of a non-Jew. The Gemara did not need to say, "because he did not make it an absolute security," since it said that he did not sell it, and an actual sale is certainly stronger. The Mishnah, which mentions a pledge, deals with an absolute pledge; therefore, according to Rebbi, it is nullified even when given to a non-Jewish smith. One might ask why the Mishnah needed to teach a pledge at all, since an absolute pledge is comparable to a sale. This is not difficult. Since the case of a non-Jewish smith is under discussion, one might have thought that even an absolute pledge is not nullified, because the non-Jew will reason that the lender values the idol and will not break it. The Mishnah therefore teaches that according to Rebbi it is nevertheless nullified. But in the Baraisa, which concerns a Jewish smith, this reasoning does not apply, and it is obvious that an absolute pledge is like an actual sale. The Rambam therefore properly mentioned only a sale in the first clause, and did not mention a pledge, lest one mistakenly think that even an ordinary pledge, which was not made absolute, is effective. In truth, however, an absolute pledge is a complete sale and effects nullification when given to a Jewish smith. In the latter clause, which deals with the possession of a non-Jew, the Rambam also mentioned a pledge as a progression from the lesser case to the greater. No form of pledge is effective there, and even if it were equivalent to a sale, a sale itself is ineffective. Indeed, in the Kesef Mishnah the Rav explicitly wrote in agreement with the Ran. This is also the meaning of the Shulchan Aruch here when it states, "and some permit it," namely, even in the case of a pledge, when it was made an absolute security, the time arrived, and he did not redeem it. The Rav should have explained here in the Shulchan Aruch which type of pledge is permitted, namely one made an absolute security that was not redeemed at its appointed time. This appears clear and primary to me.
And regarding accessories of idol worship, if he pledged them, etc. It appears obvious to me that here too, "pledged" means that he made them an absolute security for a fixed time and did not redeem them, as above. An ordinary pledge does not effect nullification.
