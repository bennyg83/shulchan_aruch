He sold or pledged it, etc. In the mishnah it is stated: he sold or pledged it — Rabbi says nullified and the Sages say not nullified; and it is known the halachah is like the Sages; and there is a dispute in the Gemara: one says with a gentile smith there is a dispute, but with an Israelite smith all agree it is nullified; and one says with an Israelite smith there is a dispute; and the Tur ruled stringently — even with an Israelite smith, like the Sages who say it is not nullified; and the Rambam ruled like the first version — leniently with an Israelite smith; and the Ran wrote his reason: since afterwards in a baraita it is taught: if he borrowed on it or a collapse fell on it, etc. — it is not nullified; and it is necessary: for if it taught "he borrowed on it" I would say since he did not sell it, it is not nullified — learn from this baraita that selling helps; and this is necessarily with an Israelite smith, and it is like the Rabbis; if so selling helps but borrowing on it — no; and the Ran wrote: that which it is nullified when he pledged it — for Rabbi specifically when he pledged it as collateral with him and said "if I do not redeem by such-and-such time it shall be yours" and the time arrived and he did not redeem — then specifically it is nullified for Rabbi, considered like a sale; and automatically for us the halachah is likewise with an Israelite smith for the Rambam; and Beit Yosef disagreed with the Ran and wrote that in a pledge there is no distinction whether pledged or not — even pledged to an Israelite smith it is not nullified; and he proved thus because the Gemara regarding "he borrowed on it" did not take "he did not pledge it," and why did it take "he did not sell it" — rather in a pledge we do not care whether pledged or not; and he wrote that so several places from the Rambam's words; and it appears he understands thus in the Rambam, for he wrote: "he sold it to an Israelite smith — nullified; he pledged it or sold it to a gentile or to an Israelite who is not a smith — not nullified" — until here his language; from that he did not take at the beginning only selling alone — learn that pledge through collateral does not help; and accordingly the Rambam's latter words are on two sides: and meaning: but if he pledged it, even to an Israelite smith, or sold to one who is not a smith — not nullified; and accordingly it is difficult why he wrote here "and some permit with an Israelite smith" — implying also in pledging. And Mahari Chalav also wrote that this ruling of Shulchan Aruch "some permit" does not exist in pledging; but I wonder at Beit Yosef — for we say in the Gemara "but with an Israelite smith all agree it is nullified" — meaning the Sages agree with Rabbi; and Rabbi explicitly took pledging or selling in the mishnah — and how can we say they did not agree with him except in selling, and make a new dispute in pledging that even with an Israelite smith there is no nullification for the Sages? Rather certainly the Ran's words are primary: in the mishnah's pledge, namely when he pledged it as collateral — the Sages agree with Rabbi that it is nullified; and the baraita "he borrowed on it" is not nullified deals without collateral and deals with an Israelite smith and is like the Rabbis; and specifically "he borrowed on it" — ordinary borrowing; but if he pledged it with him for a time and did not redeem it — it is like a complete sale, as written in Orach Chaim siman 441 regarding chametz of an Israelite in a gentile's hand; and the Talmud did not need to mention here "he did not sell it" — and automatically this is worse, namely an actual sale; and the mishnah that took pledging deals when pledged with him — therefore it is nullified for Rabbi even with a gentile smith; and one can say: why did the mishnah need to teach pledging in general, since we say it is like selling — there is no difficulty, for since the gentile smith in the mishnah comes to your mind, I might think the one who pledged is not nullified — for that gentile will say "the one who lent to me had regard for it and will not break it" — it teaches us that nevertheless it is nullified for Rabbi; but in the baraita with an Israelite smith it is not relevant to say so — obviously pledging is like an actual sale; and therefore the Rambam properly wrote at the beginning it is nullified, namely in selling, and did not mention pledging there so one not err to say even an ordinary pledge without collateral; but in truth pledging was a complete sale and helps with an Israelite smith; and in the latter part dealing with gentile property he also wrote pledging — a way of "not only this but also that," for any pledge does not help, and even if it were like selling — selling itself also does not help. And behold in Kesef Mishneh the Rav wrote plainly to agree with the Ran; and such are his words here in Shulchan Aruch "and some permit" — meaning even in pledging, namely when he pledged it with him and the time arrived and he did not redeem; and the Rav should have written this here in Shulchan Aruch and explained in what pledge they permit, namely with collateral and he did not redeem on time — so it appears clear to me, and the Ran's words are primary:
