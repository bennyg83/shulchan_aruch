She changed her sightings. This also refers to a woman who had a 20-day veses, mentioned in the preceding seif. There he discussed a change to equal intervals, in which the 20th becomes permitted and the 30th forbidden. Here he discusses a change to unequal intervals, in which she becomes entirely permitted so long as she has not later returned and seen.
On the day of the original veses. Since the case concerns an interval veses, not calendar dates, as explained above, “on the day of the original veses” must mean that she saw after the original interval, counting from the sighting on the 34th day, which was her last sighting. In that manner it is recognizable that she still remains within the framework of the original veses.
The same applies if she ceased, etc. In the Tur the wording is not “the same applies,” but “and if she ceased,” etc. The Beit Yosef explained that this too refers to the preceding case, meaning that three onos passed, each consisting of the 20 days on which she was accustomed to see, and she did not see. Thus, when the Tur says “after 20 days had passed,” it means after three periods of 20 days. She is then considered one whose blood has ceased and need not thereafter be concerned. This implies that if three onos of 20 days have not yet passed, the 20th day within them remains forbidden. Although she did not see on the second 20th day, she is forbidden on the third 20th day. The Beit Yosef was therefore compelled to explain that “if she ceased” applies specifically where three onos passed without a sighting. The Tur's later statement that she returned to see means that she saw on the day of the veses, as written here in the Shulchan Aruch. The Beit Yosef similarly cited above in this siman, s.v. “the Raavad wrote,” that if she returned and saw on that very day, even after many interruptions, etc. This is difficult for me. Since the case necessarily concerns intervals of days, as explained above, how can she be described as returning to see “on the day of the veses”? Her veses depends on a fixed number of days between one sighting and the next, yet here she did not see for a long time, so there is no previous sighting from which to calculate an interval. See the end of this comment for my resolution. The Maharshal, however, explained the Tur as follows: the reason is not that she is considered one whose blood has ceased, and the case need not involve three onos. Even if she failed to see only once and did not change to another sighting at all, she is no longer concerned for that day. The reason is that there is no 20-day interval, since she did not see. Were we to require concern for the next 20th day, it would actually be a 40-day interval from the last sighting. We cannot say that the day on which she was expected to see but did not see was merely an accident and is treated as though she did see, for ultimately she did not see. This differs from a case in which she changed to another day, such as the 30th. There she must be concerned for the 20th day following the 30th-day sighting, because that is the interval of her established veses. But where she did not see at all, no interval can be calculated. This is why above, when she changed from 20 to 30, the Tur states that when the 20th day after the 30th-day sighting arrives she is forbidden, rather than being concerned for the 10th day after the 30th, which would be the calendar date corresponding to the original 20th day. We thus see that it is more logical to attach the concern to an actual sighting than to a date on which she had merely been expected to see, in accordance with Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua at the end of Benos Kusim. All the more so where there was no sighting at all, we do not base a calculation on something that never occurred. Therefore, she need not even be concerned for the ordinary onah of 30 days from the last sighting, for that concern applies specifically to a woman who has no fixed veses. This woman has a fixed veses, but failed to see at its time, and no present indication of impurity exists. The Tur's statement “if she returned to see, she must be concerned,” applies even if she ceased for three onos. Whenever she stops and later sees, she returns to her original veses and must be concerned for it counting from this new sighting. If she had ceased for three onos, she was considered one whose blood had ceased and is concerned only for the 20th day from this new sighting, for we assume that she has returned to her original veses. But if she had not ceased for three onos and then saw, she must also be concerned for the interval between her previous sighting and the present sighting, as the Tur wrote above that both dates are forbidden. She must be concerned for the 20th and 30th, and likewise for 40 and 50. If three onos passed, however, she need not be concerned for this new long interval, because she was considered one whose blood had ceased. This is unlike a monthly veses. If Rosh Chodesh passed and she did not see, she must still be concerned for the next Rosh Chodesh until three Roshei Chodashim pass without a sighting, for a monthly veses must be uprooted three times. If not, we attribute the missed Rosh Chodesh to an incidental occurrence and she remains concerned for the next one. An interval veses is different. As soon as the 20th day arrives and she does not see, there is no veses for that day, since the interval depends on one sighting following another, and she does not know after how many days the next sighting will occur. End quote. Although one does not respond to a lion, and my father-in-law of blessed memory also cited and praised the Maharshal's view, it nevertheless appears astonishing to me. First, the Tur wrote that she returned to see, whether she had ceased to see or had changed to unequal sightings. This necessarily refers to ceasing for three onos, as the Maharshal himself also understood. Accordingly, the Tur's earlier words “if she ceased” must likewise concern the same circumstance, for the later statement refers back to it. Second, why did the Tur say “she ceased and did not see after the 20th day passed”? There are two unnecessary expressions. Why say “she ceased,” which repeats the same idea? And why say “after the 20th day passed” rather than simply “if she did not see on the 20th day, she need not be concerned”? It must mean three onos. She failed to see on the 20th day and then ceased for another three onos after that 20th day passed, seeing nothing at all. Third, according to the Maharshal there would be a distinction between interval vestos and monthly vestos in this matter. Yet the Tur later writes, “the uprooting of an interval veses is like the uprooting of a monthly veses,” implying that they are entirely equal, except that one is tied to its interval and one to its month. My father-in-law wished to insert the Maharshal's distinction into the phrase “one is concerned for its day,” interpreting it to mean only if she sees on that day, while for a monthly veses she is concerned for the month even if she does not see. This interpretation requires no refutation, for it is not implied by the wording at all. Furthermore, at the beginning of the siman the Tur explicitly equates interval vestos and date vestos regarding the rule that they are not uprooted in fewer than three occurrences. Just as a date veses is not uprooted by one failure to see, the same applies to an interval veses, for the Tur grouped them together. A further proof appears from Toras HaBayis HaAruch, folio 174: “How so? She saw, and after 20 days she saw again. When 20 days arrive she must be concerned for that day. If she saw again, she must also be concerned for the next 20 days. If she saw then, it is an established veses. If she did not see, it is uprooted and she need no longer be concerned for it.” End quote. This shows that only where the veses was not yet established, because she failed to see on the third occurrence, is she no longer concerned for the next 20th day. If it had already become established and she later failed to see on a 20th day, she certainly remains concerned when the next 20th day arrives. A further clear proof can be drawn from the very passage that the Maharshal cited in support of his position. At the end of Benos Kusim we rule like Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua that the 20-day interval is counted from the day she changed, meaning from the 30th-day sighting, and not from the 20th day on which she had been expected to see. Toras HaBayis HaAruch, folio 174, explains: “If she were to count 20 days from the 20th day on which she had been expected to see, she would be concerned lest the flow arrive early, after a shorter interval than her present sighting. This is unreasonable: she changed by delaying, and we would be concerned that she might now advance.” End quote. This shows that there was indeed a basis to count the interval from the day she had been expected to see, and we reject it only because she actually changed by delaying to a 30-day sighting. It follows that had she not seen at all, we would count the 20-day interval from the day on which she was accustomed to see, contrary to the Maharshal, who holds that it is impossible to calculate an interval from a time at which she was merely expected to see. The distinction that he made between interval vestos and monthly vestos when one sighting is missed is entirely unclear to me. An actual sighting does not cause the following interval sighting in such a manner that if this one is absent the next is also absent. What source is there for that? Since this woman has not become one whose blood has ceased, we may say that her failure to see once at the interval was incidental, that she still has excess blood, and that her flow will return at its normal interval, just as with a monthly veses. One cannot bring proof from the Beit Yosef at the end of the siman regarding a woman whose pregnancy and nursing period passed. There he wrote that otherwise it would be impossible to say she is concerned for the interval she had been accustomed to, since there is now no sighting from which to calculate an interval. That case is different, for during pregnancy and nursing she was entirely removed from all her vestos and is now like a new woman. She must therefore establish the interval anew. Here, however, she remains within her ordinary cycle, and it has not been uprooted three times. An interval may therefore be calculated from the day on which she was expected to see, as though she had seen. Thus, it appears to me that the Beit Yosef's explanation is primary, and that the Tur's case specifically concerns cessation for three onos. As for the Tur's later words “she returned to see,” it appears that each case follows its own law. Where she changed her sighting, it means that she returned to see after the original interval, 20 days after the sighting on the 34th day, and thus it is recognizable as the original veses. Where she ceased entirely, whenever she sees again she need only be concerned for the 20th day thereafter. This accords with the Ramban at the end of this siman, who wrote that once the days of pregnancy and nursing have passed and she sees once, she returns to her veses even if she did not see at the time of her veses. End quote. All the more so here should we say so, for both rulings are the Ramban's. This is also how the Shulchan Aruch rules at the end of this siman. It is therefore astonishing that here the Shulchan Aruch wrote that even after cessation she must return and see on the day of the veses, for this is unnecessary according to his own ruling at the end of the siman. Moreover, since this case concerns intervals of days, an interval cannot exist until there have been two sightings. It therefore appears that the Shulchan Aruch's phrase “on the day of the veses” at the end of this seif is imprecise. What I have written appears correct from every side, with God's help.
