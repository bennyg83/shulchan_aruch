(Taz s.k. 31) He elaborated in refuting Maharshal, but none of his arguments appear correct. The plain language of the Tur is understood by every careful reader in accordance with Maharshal. The Taz writes that just as the phrase “she changed” refers to all three onos, so too the Tur's preceding phrase, “If she interrupted, etc.,” must refer to the same manner because it follows upon that discussion. This is not correct. The phrase “if she interrupted” denotes an interruption, meaning that there was a break in her pattern of sightings, namely, she did not see when twenty days had passed. By contrast, the phrase “if she ceased seeing” denotes a complete cessation. The first clause teaches one novelty, and the latter clause teaches another. The first clause teaches that although she interrupted the pattern, meaning that she did not see even once on the twentieth day, she need no longer be concerned for the fortieth day. The latter clause teaches that even if she ceased seeing entirely for three onos, the original veset is not uprooted if she afterward returned and saw in accordance with the original veset. His further statement that, according to Maharshal, there would be a distinction in this respect between intervals of days and a veset of the month, although the Tur later writes explicitly that the uprooting of a veset of the month follows the same law as the uprooting of a veset of intervals, presents no difficulty. They are certainly equal regarding uprooting. Whenever she uproots the pattern by interrupting for three onos and does not see on the twentieth day even once, or when she changes to three equal sightings, then just as a veset of intervals is uprooted, so too a veset of the month is uprooted. Nevertheless, when she merely interrupted and did not see on the twentieth day, although she need not be concerned for a twenty-day interval, namely, for a sighting on the fortieth day, the veset of the twentieth day itself has not been uprooted. Therefore, when she sees again, she must immediately be concerned for the twentieth day. By contrast, when she interrupted for three onos, the veset is uprooted. Consequently, even if she sees again, she need not be concerned for the twentieth day unless she once again sees on the twentieth day, at which point the original veset returns. This answers everything the Taz wrote afterward, as I will explain. His further statement that the Tur writes at the beginning of the siman that a veset of intervals and a veset of days are equal in that they are not uprooted in fewer than three occurrences also presents no difficulty. Certainly, the veset is not uprooted in fewer than three occurrences. Nevertheless, she need not be concerned for a sighting on the fortieth day, because the intervals are not equal. The statement that it is not uprooted in fewer than three occurrences means that she need not be concerned for a veset of the month, as Maharshal wrote. The same applies in that, if she sees again afterward, she must immediately be concerned for the twentieth day, unlike a case in which she ceased seeing three times. His additional proof from Toras HaBayis HaAroch is no proof. Its language there is: “From what point does she count the twenty days? From the sighting on the thirtieth day, and not from the sighting on the twentieth day, on which she was accustomed to see, because the flow had not been accustomed to arrive in fewer than twenty days. Were she to count twenty days from the twentieth day, it would emerge, etc.” Thus, the essential reason is that the flow was not accustomed to arrive in fewer than twenty days. The same applies in the converse situation, where the flow was not accustomed to arrive after more than twenty days. Therefore, when she did not see on the twentieth day, she need not be concerned for the fortieth day. The Taz writes that he did not understand the distinction made by Maharshal. In my view, however, it is readily understood. With a veset of the month, although she did not see on this Rosh Chodesh, one may say that it was merely incidental that she did not see on this Rosh Chodesh, but that she will see on another Rosh Chodesh, since she is accustomed to see on Rosh Chodesh and may again see in accordance with her practice. With a veset of intervals, however, one cannot say this. If she sees only after forty days, she has not seen at an interval of twenty days. Thus, all of Maharshal's words are correct. The Taz then writes that the Tur's language is difficult and that the Shulchan Aruch is therefore astonishing. This is imprecise. Here, because she interrupted and did not see for three onos, the twenty-day veset was automatically uprooted entirely. Even if she later sees, she need not be concerned for the twentieth day, since she interrupted for three onos. This is unlike the case at the end of this siman. There, her veset was not uprooted. During pregnancy and nursing she merely did not have to be concerned for it because she was presumed not to see blood during those periods. Therefore, when the periods of pregnancy and nursing ended, as soon as she saw again she had to be concerned for the intervals to which she had previously been accustomed. That is not so here. Accordingly, the Taz's statement that the Shulchan Aruch's phrase “on the day of the veset” at the end of the se'if is imprecise is itself incorrect. The phrase is exact and forceful. Since she ceased seeing for three onos, the original veset does not return until she again sees on the day of the original veset. This means that she first sees once, and then sees again twenty days later. At that point the original veset returns in every respect, and she need not be concerned for a veset of the month, for it once again has all the laws of a fixed veset. All this is clear.
