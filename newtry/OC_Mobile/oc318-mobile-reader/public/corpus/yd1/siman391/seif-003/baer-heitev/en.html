Here too the Bach explained that one should permit a mourner for other relatives within thirty days, as in the case of escorting an orphan boy or girl to the chuppah; but the Siftei Kohen does not agree, as written in subsection 2, see there. The Taz wrote: and it seems to me that they permitted only escorting the groom under the chuppah, but not to enter to eat at the wedding feast — for even when marrying off an orphan boy or girl they did not permit eating at the feast unless it were in a situation where if he were not there the matter would be cancelled; and certainly one who escorts the groom under the chuppah is [less compelling] — it seems that those who are lenient in this are not following the correct practice, end quote. (And some have the custom to serve at the feast and then eat afterward with the servers after the feast has concluded — this one need not protest. But to recline at the table like the other guests is something that should be protested):
In his [the groom's] home. The Bach wrote: however, on the second night, even though the custom in the community of Krakow is not to recite "she-hasimcha bim'ono," a mourner may not eat at that feast — for it is not comparable to the second day where the groom has already consummated the commandment-union on the second night according to their custom, and therefore they make a fish meal on the second day on account of the blessing of fish as stated in the first chapter of Ketuvot, hence it is not called a feast of joy and they do not recite "asher bara" or "she-hasimcha bim'ono"; but on the second night before consummation when they do recite "asher bara," the marriage has not yet concluded. And what they do not recite — "she-hasimcha bim'ono" — is because men and women gather there, etc., see there. And the Taz wrote: according to the custom that the Rema wrote — not to eat at any feast — that fish meal too is forbidden, end quote:
