Even in a valley, etc. Although people are not particular about such passage, we hold that even a minor concession is forbidden to one who is prohibited from deriving benefit. The Bach writes that if he does not shorten his route, there is no benefit and it is permitted, no worse than paying his debt as discussed below. This does not appear correct to me. When another pays his debt, he derives no direct benefit and it is only an indirect cause, whereas here he himself benefits from walking through the property, so it is forbidden. This is also implied by Rashi, the Rosh, and the Ran below regarding one who says, “Benefit that leads to food is forbidden to me.” They write that he may not pass through the other's field to shorten his route to a feast, but they do not say this in the opening clause of the Mishnah. Evidently, only there is it forbidden specifically when he shortens his route, because otherwise there is no benefit leading to food. By shortening the route he reaches the food sooner. Here, however, all benefit is forbidden, so walking through the property is forbidden in every manner. Although Rashi also explains “walking through the property” in the Mishnah as passing through his courtyard to shorten his route, this is not precise, for the end of seif 4 below proves from the Gemara that walking through the property is forbidden in every manner.
And he may not chew wheat, etc. Although he does not eat it, he nevertheless derives benefit from the food itself.
With any article that is commonly rented, etc. Since articles of this kind are ordinarily rented, and the owner waives the rental fee, the recipient can use that money to buy food. The owner had prohibited him from benefit that leads to food. Rosh.
Likewise, he may not warm his hands with the other's loaf. I am surprised by the source for this ruling. The Yerushalmi asks only regarding one who vowed against benefit from a loaf, whether he may warm his hands with it. The question was unresolved and is ruled stringently, because perhaps a vow against benefit from the loaf includes warming oneself with it. But when he says, “Benefit that leads to food is forbidden to me,” warming oneself appears permitted, since it is not benefit that leads to food. I later saw that the Ateret Zekenim omitted this ruling, perhaps for the reason I have stated.
An axe, etc. The Yerushalmi at the beginning of chapter Ein Bein HaMudar states that one may lend him cups, bowls, and serving dishes, because they do not improve the food, but vessels into which the food is placed for cooking, namely pans and pots, are forbidden. Whether grinding, pressing, harvesting, and gathering grapes are forbidden remains a question. The Beit Yosef cites this. It must be explained that an axe used to split wood is worse, because it improves and prepares the food, since cooking is impossible without wood. Thus, an axe used to split wood assists in preparing the food. Cups, bowls, and serving dishes do not improve the food, since the food has already been properly prepared and is merely placed in them. Pans and pots are therefore forbidden because the food is cooked in them and they prepare it. The Yerushalmi further states: Rabbi Bun son of Rabbi Chiya said, according to this reasoning it should be forbidden to lend him money, for that is like giving him money with which to buy a loaf.
