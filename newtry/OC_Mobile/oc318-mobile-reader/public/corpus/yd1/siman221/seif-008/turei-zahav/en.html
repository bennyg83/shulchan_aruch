Even if we say that an artisan acquires through the improvement he creates in a utensil, that applies where there is tangible substance, such as wood that he fashions into a utensil. This artisan, however, merely painted it with pigments, which have no independent substance. So wrote the Rosh in a responsum cited by the Beit Yosef.
One who is prohibited from benefiting from another, the one who imposed the prohibition may, etc. The Gemara states that the prohibited person has nothing to eat. The Rosh and Tur wrote that this is not precise. The same law applies even if he has an abundance of food.
If the one who imposed the prohibition wishes to repay him. So wrote the Rosh and Ran. Their reasoning is that if he were legally obligated to pay, the other person would be his agent and would thereby provide him benefit, which is forbidden. It is clear that the same applies to the case where he says, “Anyone who provides food will not lose.” There too, he is not legally obligated to pay. This itself proves that payment can be connected to agency only if the provider is considered his agent. Otherwise, the one who made the vow could say, “You are not my legal adversary.” The Shulchan Aruch did not need to state this there, for it relied upon what it wrote in the first two clauses. One should not ask that it should have written it only in the first clause, following the Tur’s actual wording. One may answer that the Shulchan Aruch wished to prevent the error of understanding the Tur’s phrase, “They return and collect their fee,” as a legal obligation. It therefore teaches that payment is merely permitted if he wishes. In every case, the permission exists specifically because he is not legally obligated to pay. The Levush likewise wrote in siman 235, concerning one who says, “Anyone who provides food will not lose,” that he need not pay unless he wishes. The Prishah disagreed here and in siman 235. He argued that agency applies only when speaking to an individual, but when one addresses the public and says, “Anyone who provides food,” etc., there is no agency, yet he is nevertheless legally obligated to pay. It appears that he was entirely imprecise. In the chapter “One who supports,” Kesubos 70a, the Gemara says: It is unnecessary to teach that when one says, “Anyone who provides food will not lose,” it is obviously permitted. Rather, even if he says to a shopkeeper, “I do not know what to do,” since he speaks directly to him one might have thought that he is his agent; therefore the teaching informs us that it is nevertheless permitted. If it were true that in the case of, “Anyone who provides food will not lose,” he is legally obligated to pay and it is nevertheless permitted, whereas with the shopkeeper the permission depends upon there being no obligation to pay, then the former would contain a far greater novelty. Why would the Gemara present it as obvious? Apart from all this, there is no basis to say that the provider is not his agent yet can extract money from him. This is like one who pays another person’s debt, who need not be reimbursed, as stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 128. The entire question of prohibition or permission here depends upon whether he is legally obligated or exempt from payment. The Prishah’s words are therefore not halacha.
Anyone who hears my voice shall feed so-and-so. Here he must pay, even if he did not conclude, “will not lose,” because the provider is certainly his agent and will not suffer a loss through him. The Prishah wrote that even here he must say, “will not lose,” but this does not appear correct.
