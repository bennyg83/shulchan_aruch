He may, etc. This applies only when done without the debtor's knowledge, as the Ran writes and the Beit Yosef cites in siman 217, and as is clear from the authorities in this siman. The Bach rules in accordance with Rabbenu Chananel, Rabbenu Tam, the Rosh, and the Tur that one may not pay his debt when it is evidenced by a document or secured by collateral, since it is a definite debt. Chanan's ruling was stated only regarding support of one's wife and children, and similar oral debts. This also appears to be the view of the Sema in Choshen Mishpat, beginning of siman 128, seif katan 3. The Bach further writes that if the creditor agreed not to compel payment, but to allow the debtor to pay whenever he wished, another may pay the debt for one prohibited from benefit, even where there is a promissory note or collateral. It appears that collateral is comparable to a document only when the payer may satisfy the debt and retain the collateral for himself, rather than return it to the debtor. Although the creditor agreed not to compel payment, he is not obligated to return the collateral without payment, unless he expressly agreed to do so. If the payer is the one who prohibited the debtor from benefit, he must return the collateral to the debtor without charge. See below, siman 223, seif katan 5.
He must return it, etc. Even if the creditor was pressing him to pay, we say that the debtor might have appeased the creditor and persuaded him to waive the debt. The Mechaber writes this in Choshen Mishpat, beginning of siman 128.
And he may support, etc. Although the one prohibited from benefit gains because he need not provide their support, that benefit arises indirectly. Furthermore, they could have economized and supported themselves from their earnings, or gone from door to door seeking assistance.
But he may not feed his animal. Even if he gives it extra food in order to fatten it, this is forbidden, because he thereby increases its value and provides a direct benefit.

If the property of each is forbidden to the other, however, it is forbidden in every manner, as in the following seif. The Ateret Zekenim also writes this.
And they permit, etc. The Bach rules according to the first opinion. In every matter that the Sages permitted one to do for a person prohibited from benefit, as discussed from the beginning of this siman, the permission applies only when the one who imposed the prohibition acts on his own initiative, or when the prohibited person says, “Whoever wishes to do such and such may come and do it.” But he may not explicitly appoint him as his agent, nor say, “Whoever hears my voice should come and teach me,” because he is then acting as his agent. See below, seif 8.
In every matter of a mitzvah. This implies specifically a mitzvah matter. Rabbenu Yerucham writes likewise, and the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite him. The Ateret Zekenim explains that since it is a mitzvah, he is not considered to derive benefit, because mitzvot were not given for physical benefit.
