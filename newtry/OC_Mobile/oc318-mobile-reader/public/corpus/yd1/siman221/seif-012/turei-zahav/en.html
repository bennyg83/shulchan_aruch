He is permitted to benefit from its flame. It appears that the law here is the same as regarding idolatry in siman 142, seif 1, according to what we wrote there.
He may slaughter a healthy animal for him. The reason given is that slaughtering is considered destructive, as stated regarding slaughtering with a knife of idolatry in siman 10, where it is not considered benefit. Although I am not worthy to disagree with the author of Ohel Moed, who originated this ruling as explained in the Beit Yosef, I nevertheless say that one should not rely upon this ruling. It is obvious that the owner receives great benefit when the other person slaughters his animal and permits it for eating. Every day we hire a shochet to slaughter. Only regarding idolatry do we say that the prohibition of idolatry does not forbid using the knife for slaughter. There is explicit proof from Tosafos in the chapter “One who weaves,” Shabbos 106a, s.v. “except one who wounds,” who wrote: Although every slaughterer is destructive, as stated in the first chapter of Chullin that one may slaughter with a knife of idolatry, R’ Yehuda nevertheless agrees regarding Shabbos that he is liable and it is not considered destructive. Only regarding idolatry is it considered destructive, because the verse states, “Nothing of the banned property shall cling to your hand,” and he is not considered to have benefited since the damage is greater than the improvement, end quote. Thus, it is explicit that we do not derive the law of Shabbos from idolatry in this matter. The same applies to vows, since the Torah revealed this specifically regarding idolatry and nowhere else. Although Tosafos in the first chapter of Chullin, 8a, wrote that specifically regarding Shabbos it is considered benefit because the Torah prohibited intentional creative labor and the slaughterer intended this benefit, there is no difficulty. On the contrary, this proves our point, for regarding vows too we follow the intent of the one who made the vow, and here he intends the benefit. Furthermore, vows present an even stronger kal vachomer. At the beginning of the chapter “There is no difference between one prohibited by a vow,” R’ Eliezer holds that even ordinary concessions are forbidden to one prohibited by a vow, such as when a seller ordinarily adds one or two extra units for a buyer. Regarding one prohibited from benefit, we are strict even concerning slight benefit, as the Rosh and Ran wrote. For this reason, at the beginning of this siman we forbid even walking through another’s property, although people are not ordinarily particular about it, as the Ran wrote there. All the more so must we forbid this substantial benefit, where he renders the animal fit and available for eating when it could not otherwise have been eaten. There is further proof from the Raavad in chapter 6 of Hilchos Shevuos. He wrote: If the mohel or shochet swore, by consent of the public, not to benefit from the townspeople, he should circumcise and slaughter for them without payment, for what mitzvah would be served by releasing his vow? If he needs the income from his work and cannot circumcise and slaughter while occupied with his livelihood, they may release him. But if he swore that they should not benefit from him, he can circumcise and teach their children without payment, end quote. He began twice with circumcision and slaughter, yet at the end did not say that he may slaughter for them without payment. This is because slaughtering is forbidden, since he is not personally commanded to do it for them. It therefore appears clear that had the Rama seen the sources we cited, he would not have established this leniency as practical halacha. In my opinion, it is completely forbidden.
