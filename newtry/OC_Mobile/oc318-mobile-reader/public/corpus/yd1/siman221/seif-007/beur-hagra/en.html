One who rents out, etc. Tosafot in Nedarim write this in the name of Rabbeinu Tam and derive it from the preceding seif. They reject that proof, because there the owner did not expressly prohibit the property, and therefore did not intend to include what he had already rented out. This differs from a case in which he expressly prohibits it, as stated in Arakhin 21a: “How can he reside there,” etc. This explains the language here and in the preceding seif, “but if he specified,” etc. Tosafot challenge this from Ketubot 59b, where the Gemara states that specifically “when I redeem it, it shall become consecrated.” They answer that one may consecrate only the value exceeding the rent. Similarly, in Arakhin the tenant may not reside there because of the consecrated share, whereas the case in Ketubot concerns the portion corresponding to the debt. Tosafot give this answer in Arakhin and Ketubot, and the Rosh and Ran write likewise in Nedarim. Tosafot further answer in Nedarim that only full consecration can prohibit the value exceeding the debt, unlike a konam. They give the same answer in Ketubot, and on this basis the discussion there is resolved. Ran also cites Rabbeinu Tam as distinguishing between consecration, or a general konam that resembles consecration, and a private konam. Only consecration displaces a lien, whereas with ordinary konamot the Sages strengthened the lien, as stated in Ketubot. See Tosafot there, s.v. “Konamot,” and the two ensuing questions. The Rivash, siman 355, writes likewise and explains that Rabbeinu Tam follows his own view that even monetary consecration displaces a lien, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 33b, s.v. “because.” All the later authorities reject this and rule like Rashi, that only intrinsic sanctity displaces a lien, as explained in Choshen Mishpat at the end of siman 117. Tosafot in Nedarim offer another distinction, between a creditor, whose property will not ultimately return to the debtor, and a landlord, whose property will ultimately return to him. They write similarly in Bava Kamma 51b, although in Arakhin they reject this reasoning. The Rosh in Nedarim combines two answers: the case in Ketubot concerns a creditor, while the case in Nedarim is permitted not because the owner failed to prohibit expressly, but because it is a konam. In Bava Kamma, however, he distinguishes Arakhin from Ketubot on the basis of creditor versus landlord, and explains Nedarim by the fact that the owner did not expressly prohibit the property. He thereby rejects the view of the Ritzba, who agrees with Rabbeinu Tam. He then presents Tosafot’s first answer, distinguishing the amount corresponding to the rent from the excess value. Arakhin asks how the tenant may reside there because of the consecrated share. The Rosh concludes that when consecration takes effect upon the land itself, the owner can prohibit his remaining share; but when he merely prohibits benefit, the prohibition does not take effect upon the land itself. This resolves the case in Nedarim. By contrast, two partners can prohibit one another, because each is benefiting from the other’s share, as explained there. This is the meaning of the alternative view stated here, that even if he expressly prohibited it, etc. Tosafot in Nedarim also distinguish between a case in which the tenant prepaid the rent and one in which he did not. They write likewise in Arakhin, citing proof from the Tosefta in the fifth chapter of Bava Metzia and from the Yerushalmi in the fourth chapter of Pesachim. Ran cites this in the name of other authorities. The Rivash explains that the Yerushalmi distinguishes as follows: if the rent was prepaid, the tenant resides there without further payment; if it was not prepaid, he must pay the consecrated estate. Ran explains that Arakhin concerns one who rented an unspecified house, unlike the case in Ketubot, because monetary consecration does not displace a lien. He adds that the case in Nedarim remains difficult, since konamot possess intrinsic sanctity, as stated in Ketubot. He therefore cites Rabbeinu Tam’s distinction between consecration or a general konam and a private konam, and the distinction between prepaid and unpaid rent. He objects that all these approaches are challenged by the Mishnah teaching that partners may prohibit one another, although that is a private konam and resembles prepaid rent. He therefore concludes like Tosafot’s first answer: where the landlord did not expressly prohibit the rented property, he did not intend to include it. Others answer that the tenancy in that case was not a full tenancy, because the occupant received the property in exchange for paying its tax, etc. Ran himself rules that a landlord can prohibit the property to the tenant even if the tenant prepaid, because konamot displace liens, whether the prohibition is general or directed specifically at the tenant. He cannot, however, prohibit it to a third party, because that third party receives benefit from the tenant rather than from the landlord. The Rivash records all of Ran’s reasoning and agrees with it.
And the tenant, etc. The Tosefta in the eighth chapter of Bava Metzia states: If the tenant arose and consecrated it, it remains consecrated until it leaves his possession, etc. Tosafot cite this in Arakhin there. See there.
