With the language of hekdesh. This implies that regarding hekdesh everyone agrees that the owner can sanctify it. The Beit Yosef likewise cites no dissenting view. Rabbenu Yerucham, however, writes in netiv 1, part 1, in the name of the Rashba and Rabbenu Yonah that whether the property is rented or mortgaged, the owner cannot sanctify it even if its value exceeds the debt. This requires careful consideration. The limitation applies only where he rented this specific house; if he rented an unspecified house, he may sanctify one house and provide another. The Ran in chapter HaShutafin and the Rivash in responsum 355 write similarly, and the Rivash states that the later authorities agreed. The Yerushalmi in chapter Makom SheNahagu supports this. It states that if one rented a house to another and then sanctified it, the tenant continues living there and pays the rent to hekdesh. This applies when he had not prepaid the rent. If he prepaid, he continues living there without further payment. Tosafot and the Rosh understood the Yerushalmi to distinguish between prepaid and unpaid rent regarding the owner's power to sanctify the house. The Rosh therefore questioned why this should depend on prepayment, since rental of land is acquired by a document and possession, with money merely completing the transaction. Their interpretation is difficult. If the house became hekdesh when rent was not prepaid, how could the tenant continue living there, as the Gemara in Arakhin asks regarding misuse of hekdesh? Also, why does the Yerushalmi say that when rent was prepaid he lives there without payment? Perhaps they had another version of the Yerushalmi, as Tosafot in Arakhin cite a different formulation. Nevertheless, even their version can be explained as I have stated. The Semag cites the version described above and also distinguishes between prepaid and unpaid rent regarding whether the sanctification takes effect, but this is incorrect. Rather, both clauses of the Yerushalmi concern a house already acquired by the tenant. Its purpose is to reject the assumption that even when rent was prepaid the tenant must pay rent to hekdesh because hekdesh removes liens. That is not so, because the owner cannot sanctify the house so as to remove the tenant's lien. When rent was not prepaid, the tenant pays the rent to hekdesh, because although the owner cannot sanctify the house to remove the tenant's lien, he can sanctify the rental payments owed to him. When rent was prepaid, the tenant lives there without further payment, because the rent was already paid and the owner cannot undo the transaction. This is explicit in Rivash, responsum 355. He writes that if the owner rented this specific house, the tenant does not pay rent to hekdesh and does not violate misuse by living there, because the sanctification takes effect only after the rental term. Monetary hekdesh does not remove the tenant's lien, even if rent was not prepaid, since the tenant has a lien on that house. If rent was prepaid, he lives there without payment; if it was not prepaid, he pays the rent to hekdesh, because when the body of the house was sanctified, its rental income was included. Tosafot in Arakhin and the Rivash, citing the Tosefta, likewise write that the landlord cannot sanctify the tenant's present use. The Gemara in Arakhin can be understood as referring to an unspecified house, as Rabbenu Yerucham, the Ran, and the Rivash write. It may also refer to a case where the landlord retains a proprietary interest. The Gemara inferred from the phrase “and he sanctified it” that the sanctification took effect, and therefore asked how the tenant could live there. The Rama writes below in siman 228, seif 7, regarding collateral, that some say the owner cannot sanctify even the surplus value. This is based on Rabbenu Yerucham cited above. It is therefore surprising that here he rules without qualification that the owner can sanctify the property. This requires study.
