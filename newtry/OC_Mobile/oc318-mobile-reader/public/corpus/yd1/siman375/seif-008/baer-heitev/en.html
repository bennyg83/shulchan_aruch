And some say. The Shach wrote at great length about these laws, and the practical conclusion of his words is as follows. One whose relative died and who was informed in his own place counts for himself from when he learned of it; even if he afterward comes to the place of the dead, to the other relatives who already began mourning, he does not shorten his mourning because of that. Likewise, if he came to the relatives and they are not in the place where the dead died or the place of burial, each counts for himself from when he learned. But if he did not know his relative died until he came to the relatives who are in the place of the dead or the place of burial, then if he came from a nearby place, a journey of ten parsot, even if he arrived on the seventh day, if he found comforters with the head of the household, even if they had begun to rise, since he still found comforters and they were practicing some mourning, he counts with them; since the elder counts with them, he too counts with them. But if he did not find the head of the household with them, he counts for himself. Likewise, the head of the household who comes from a nearby place counts for himself. If at the time of burial some relatives went to the cemetery, they count with those in the house, whether adults or minors, whether the cemetery is near or far, whether they came within three days or on the seventh day, provided they came and found comforters with them. But if they did not find comforters on the seventh day, those who went to the cemetery count for themselves from the closure of the golel. So too, one who comes from another place and finds that the elder went to the cemetery: once the elder returns to them, even on the seventh day, and counts with them, this newcomer is drawn after them too, and all count one count, provided he came from a nearby place. This is specifically where some remained in the house; if none remained in the house, he counts for himself unless he came within three days. If the elder did not return within seven days, so that the elder counts for himself from the closure of the golel, then the one who comes from a nearby place also does not count with those in the house from when the dead left; rather, he counts for himself from when he learned. All this concerns where the elder went to the cemetery in a place where he does not live. But if he went to the place where he lives and remained there, even those left here who had turned their faces from the dead follow him and count from closure of the golel. This is specifically where he could come there within three days; but if he cannot come there within three days, those here count immediately, and the elder at the cemetery counts from closure of the golel. If those in the house and the elder began their mourning count equally, whether from when the dead left or from closure of the golel, then one who comes from a nearby place counts with them in every case, even if he comes on the seventh day while they are still practicing some mourning, since the elder returned before the comforters stood up and counts with them. But if they began their mourning equally and afterward the elder went to the cemetery to attend to the needs of the dead, to make a marker or structure for the grave, and did not return at all after the relative arrived, or returned after the comforters stood up, then the law is as follows: if the relative came within three days, he counts with them; if he came after three days, he counts for himself. If the elder went for another matter not needed for the dead, even if the relative came within three days he counts for himself, since the elder did not return home. If the relative came from a distant place, in every case he counts for himself. Anyone whose law is to count with them continues to count with them even if he afterward returns home. End quote.
