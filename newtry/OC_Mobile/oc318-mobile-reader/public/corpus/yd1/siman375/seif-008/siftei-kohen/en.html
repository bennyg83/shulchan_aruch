And some say, etc. In truth, the Rav followed what the Beit Yosef wrote, that the Rambam's view, copied here by the Mechaber, is like the Rif, who omitted the law of the head of the household going to the cemetery, etc. According to that reading, the head of the household who went to the cemetery counts only from the closing of the golel, and even if he returned to the household on day three while they count from when they turned their faces back, one who comes from a nearby place counts with the head of the household because he is drawn after him and not after the household. The Darkhei Moshe brings this and writes: I say that the Rosh's view is the view of our French teachers and Rabbeinu Chananel, as is clear from the Ramban's words brought at length by the Beit Yosef. Therefore it appears that we rule like them, for the halachah follows the lenient view in mourning. Furthermore, the Tosafists, Rabbeinu Chananel, and the Rosh are many; the Rosh is also later and agreed with them. Therefore the halachah follows them. Based on this the Rav wrote here, "and some say," etc., and "one may be lenient like this view," etc. But in truth, the Beit Yosef's words do not appear primary, as I wrote in my book. Rather, the primary view is as the Ramban wrote according to the Rif, brought by the Beit Yosef: even if the head of the household came back to the household within seven days, he counts with them and not from the closing of the golel, whether he came from a near or far place. Likewise, even a minor who went to the cemetery and returned within seven days counts with them. Likewise, one who comes from another place and finds that the head of the household went to the cemetery, once the head returns, even on day seven, and counts with them, this person too is drawn after him and all are found to count one count, provided this person came from a nearby place. If so, this is also the Rambam's view, for he followed the path of the Rif his teacher; see there. The Semag, p. 246c, writes like the Rambam, and Rabbeinu Yerucham, netiv 28 part 1, appears to rule like the Ramban. The Rosh's view seems to me that he explains "if he came within three" as referring to the other person who came, and according to him the Talmud does not mention whether the head of the household returned home or not. Rather, whenever the head went to the cemetery and another came within three days, he counts with them; after three, he counts for himself. But if the head did not go to the cemetery, then even if the other comes on day seven, he counts with them like Rabbi Shimon. The Tur, however, understands the Rosh as explaining "if he came within three" about the head of the household. According to that, the Rosh's phrase "and one of the brothers came within three" must mean that if he came after three while the head was at home, it is obvious and teaches nothing that he counts with them when the head returned within three; but this is forced. Also according to that, the Rosh's phrase that Rabbi Yochanan distinguishes here between within three and after three, where the head of the household went to the cemetery, would mean that regarding the head of the household he distinguishes whether he returned within three or not; the language does not imply this, and the main point is missing. The view of Rabbeinu Chananel, Ritz Giat, and our French teachers brought by the Ramban is that everything depends on whether the head counts with them or not. If the head counts with them, then one who comes from a nearby place counts with them; if the head counts for himself, then the newcomer counts for himself. Therefore the Ramban had to explain their words as where the head went to the cemetery before the closing of the golel, for otherwise one would not find a case where the head counts for himself. According to their distinction between within three and after three, the law is as follows: if the head returned within three, in which case he counts with them and not from the closing of the golel, the newcomer also counts with them. If he returned after three, in which case according to them he counts for himself, the newcomer also counts for himself and not with them or with the head, but by himself. But if the head too returned from the dead before the closing of the golel and began immediately to count with the rest of the household, even if he then goes to the cemetery and returns after three days, he is already counting with them; since he counts with them, the newcomer too counts with them in every case, provided he comes from a nearby place. But according to the Rosh, it does not depend on whether the head counts with them or not; everything depends on whether the newcomer came within three. Even if the head's mourning began equally with the rest of the household, there is a distinction between within three and after three. Therefore the Rosh wrote that the head went to the cemetery to tend to the needs of the dead, to make a monument or build something within the grave, meaning that from the beginning their mourning began equally, but he went to the cemetery after burial for the need of a monument and the like; then there is a distinction between within three and after three. Therefore the Tur wrote, "and my father the Rosh z"l wrote like the first view, that he is drawn after them only if the head returned within three days." He did not write briefly, "my father z"l wrote like the first view, that he is drawn after them only if he came within three," because according to the Rosh's words, the distinction between closing of the golel or not was not mentioned. Likewise regarding the beginning of mourning it appears the Rosh disagrees, for after explaining our Talmudic sugya he brought the Yerushalmi: one teaching says those here count from when the dead leaves, and those there count from the closing of the golel; another says both count from the closing of the golel. Rabbi Simon in the name of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi says everything follows the great one: if the great one was here, both count from when the dead leaves; if he was there, both count from when the golel is closed; if there is no great one, those here count from when the dead leaves, and those there count from when the golel is closed. The Rosh wrote that this Yerushalmi is not about our sugya but about the beginning of mourning, like what we say in our sugya that Rava told the people of Mechoza: since you do not follow the bier, once you turn your faces back from the gate, begin counting. The Yerushalmi teaches that there is a distinction when there is a head. This means that our Talmud speaks where mourning began equally, such as where all went to the cemetery at burial or the head also remained at home in a way that all began counting from the closing of the golel, not from when the dead left. If afterward the head went to the cemetery for a monument and the like, there is a distinction between within three and after three. According to Rabbeinu Tam and those with him, and according to the Rif as understood by the Ramban, one must say the Yerushalmi disagrees with our Talmud, and so it is explicit in Torat HaAdam. According to this, where the head was present at burial, meaning at the closing of the golel, and the others remained at home, all always count from the closing of the golel; no distinction of within three or after three is mentioned here. Whether the head returned home or not, all begin from the closing of the golel. Therefore the Tur carefully wrote that the Rosh wrote like the first view specifically that he is not drawn after them unless the head returned within three, meaning only in that point he agreed with the first view, unlike the Perishah in seif 27, who mixed the Ramban's words with the view of the Rosh and Tur and was therefore troubled by what the Tur wrote above, that everything follows the head regarding the beginning of mourning. According to what I wrote, there is no difficulty: there it speaks regarding the beginning of mourning, and here it speaks where both began equally. This is clear. In any case, one can also answer that what was written above, that both count from the closing of the golel, applies where the head of the household lives in the place where the dead will be buried, and so the Mordechai distinguishes. According to this, the Mechaber's words must be explained this way too, for he copied the Rambam's words implying, at least according to either explanation, that those who remained here count from when the dead left and not from the closing of the golel, even though the head was at the cemetery at the closing of the golel; yet above in seif 2 he wrote that if the head of the family goes with him, even those here count only from burial. Rather, there it speaks where the head lives in the place of burial. This "lives there" means that since he lives there and remains there, they follow him; but if he does not remain there, what difference does his residence make, since ultimately he is now with them? It appears even the Ramban, who wrote that the Yerushalmi speaks in every case, even where the head returned to them, and therefore had to say it disagrees with our Talmud, would concede this distinction. For in the Mordechai itself one must say so: first he wrote that the Yerushalmi disagrees with our Talmud, and afterward he wrote that according to this it does not disagree with our Talmud. The first statement that it disagrees means where the head returned home, for the Yerushalmi does not distinguish and implies that in every case we follow the head. The later statement that it does not disagree means where he remained in the place where he lives. Regarding the law, the primary view appears to be that of the Rif, Rambam, Ramban, Semag, and Rabbeinu Yerucham mentioned above, for they are many and great, and they are lenient in mourning; Rabbeinu Yerucham is also later. Maharshal wrote that even the Ramban concedes where some did not remain at home, that the newcomer counts for himself, and the later authorities bring this. It implies from his words there that he concedes to the Rosh; if so, if the newcomer comes within three, at least he counts with them even if some did not remain at home. Examine carefully. However, where the head began counting with them equally and afterward went to the cemetery for a monument and the like, although according to the Ramban he counts with them in every case, that is where the head returned to them within seven days. But where he did not return at all, his words do not mention this. It appears we rule like the Rosh in this: if one came from a nearby place within three days, he counts with them, for it is as though he was at home; if he came after three, he counts for himself, since the head is not there. This is specifically where the head went to tend to the needs of the dead; but if he went for another matter, in every case the newcomer counts for himself, since the head did not return within seven. Every "head" in this matter means like the head in seif 2 above. So it is explicitly implied from the poskim. However, where one's wife died and brothers and other relatives came, the husband is called the head of the household, because he is obligated in her burial and in tending to her more than the brother or father. We do not follow the one who is more distressed, because minds differ. So wrote the Rashba in responsum siman 478.
And one who comes within three counts with them. I do not know why he required that he come within three, for according to this view of the Tur, all that is required is that the head return within three; even if the relative came on day seven, he counts with them. Perhaps he mentioned within three as a novelty, that even if he came while the head of the household was not yet home, and certainly if he came after three while the head had returned within three. This is similar to how I explained above the Rosh's phrase, "and one of the brothers came within three," according to the Tur. Perhaps even according to the first view, if he came after three while the head had returned home, he counts with them, since he came to them and the head is there. Therefore he mentioned within three because according to the first view he would count for himself. Alternatively, because the Rav leaned toward the Rosh's view as later, as he wrote in the Darkhei Moshe, and I brought above, but he was uncertain whether the Rosh's distinction of within three applies to the head, as the Tur understood, or to the relative who comes, as I explained, he goes here and there stringently. Examine carefully. From all this: one whose relative died and who learned of it in his own place counts for himself from when he heard. Even if he later comes to the place of the dead, to the other relatives who had already begun mourning, he does not shorten his mourning because of this. Likewise, if he came to the relatives and they are neither in the place where the dead died nor in the burial place, each counts for himself from when he heard. But if he did not know that his relative died until he came to the relatives who are at the place of death or the burial place, and he was in a nearby place, a walk of ten parsahs, even if he came on day seven, if he found comforters with the head of the household, even if they had already stirred to stand, since he found comforters, meaning that they were still observing some mourning, he counts with them, because the head counts with them. But if he did not find the head of the household with them, he counts for himself; and the head of the household who comes from a nearby place also counts for himself. If at the time of burial some relatives went to the cemetery, they count with those at home, whether adults or minors, whether the cemetery is near or far, whether they came within three or on day seven, provided they came and found comforters. But if they did not find comforters on day seven, those who went to the cemetery count for themselves from the closing of the golel. Likewise, one who comes from another place and finds that the head went to the cemetery: once the head returned to them, even on day seven, and counts with them, this person too is drawn after them, and all count one count, provided this person came from a nearby place. This is specifically where some remained at home; but if none remained at home, he counts for himself unless he came within three. If the head did not return within seven, so that the head counts for himself from the closing of the golel, then the one coming from a nearby place also does not count with those at home from when the dead left, but counts for himself from when he heard. All this applies when the head went to the cemetery in a place where he does not live. But if he went to the place where he lives and remained there, even those who remained here and turned their faces back from the dead follow him and count from the closing of the golel. This is specifically if he can come there within three days; but if he cannot come there within three, those here count immediately, and the head at the cemetery counts from the closing of the golel. If those at home and the head began counting mourning equally, all from when the dead left or all from the closing of the golel, then one who comes from a nearby place counts with them in every case, even if he came on day seven while they still observed some mourning, as long as the head returned before the comforters stood up and counts with them. But if they began mourning equally and the head afterward went to the cemetery to tend to the needs of the dead, such as making a monument or building for the grave, and he did not return at all before the relative came, or returned after the comforters stood up, the law is as follows: if the relative came within three, he counts with them; if after three, he counts for himself. If the head went for another matter not related to the needs of the dead, even if the relative came within three, he counts for himself, since the head did not return home. If the relative came from a distant place, in every case he counts for himself. Anyone whose law is to count with them continues to count with them even if he later returns home.
