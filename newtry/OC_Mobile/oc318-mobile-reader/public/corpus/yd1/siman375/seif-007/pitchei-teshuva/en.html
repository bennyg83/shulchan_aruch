See Responsa Chinuch Beit Yehudah, siman 91, concerning a man who disappeared and whose fate was unknown. He ruled that the sons should not recite Kaddish for him, because there is concern for a resulting mistake just as there is regarding formal mourning. Another scholar disagreed and distinguished Kaddish from mourning, but the author responded and upheld his ruling, with the agreement of several great authorities. Nevertheless, he writes that they should bring merit to the presumed deceased in ways that will not create a mistaken legal inference, such as leading Birkat HaMazon, receiving Maftir, and occasionally leading prayers when this does not displace other mourners. If the orphan is a minor, other measures can be taken, such as giving charity on the father's behalf or hiring someone to study Torah for him. See there. Responsa Shevut Yaakov II, siman 114, raises a difficulty from Ketubot 62b, where Rabbi Yannai said to overturn the bed and begin mourning even though such an inference would certainly not have been enough to permit the wife to remarry. He distinguishes between a missing husband, where mourning is not undertaken without evidence sufficient to permit remarriage, and a husband who went to a nearby place where the truth can be discovered during the mourning period, in which case one need not prevent the family from mourning because the matter will soon become known. See there further. Responsa Knesset Yechezkel, end of siman 53, writes that although Shulchan Aruch says the sons and wife are not allowed to mourn, his own practice was that once it became publicly known that halachic inquiries had been sent to rabbis, he permitted the sons to recite Kaddish in a private minyan at home, because this publicity avoids the concern that someone will later testify simply that the man died. He likewise did not object if the wife and sons wore ordinary weekday clothes during the period, rather than formal mourning clothes. See there. Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 344, discusses an unmarried youth who drowned in water without visible boundaries, whether mourning is observed since there is no concern of mistakenly permitting an agunah. Rabbi Moshe ben Chaviv, in Ezrat Nashim to Even HaEzer 17, seif katan 60, regards it as obvious that no mourning is observed even for an unmarried man, lest one create a secondary decree. This also appears to be the implication of the Shach here. Shevut Yaakov I, siman 103, strongly maintains that one does mourn for an unmarried man and challenges the Shach. Since their dispute is not about the inherent law of mourning, but only whether to enact a secondary safeguard, we do not simply apply the rule that the law follows the lenient opinion in mourning. Rather, the one who is lenient regarding the decree and therefore stringent to observe mourning should not be discouraged, for he has a major authority upon whom to rely. See there. Responsa Mishkenot Yaakov, siman 69, concerning a son reciting Kaddish where the father's death had not been proven strongly enough to permit the wife to remarry, argues at length that the Shach and some later responsa overlooked a major dispute among the early authorities. Most early authorities maintain that where the majority indicates death, mourning is observed. He also notes a tension within Shulchan Aruch itself from siman 339:2, concerning one who is told that his relative was seen dying. Shevut Yaakov resolves that case by distinguishing a dying person only four days away, but the sources indicate that the early authorities truly disagreed. Responsa Maharam bar Baruch cites Rabbi Shimshon ben Avraham explicitly ruling that those drowned in water without visible boundaries are mourned once search efforts have been abandoned, even though the wife remains forbidden to remarry. Raavan says similarly, with a separate dispute only regarding aninut and the prohibition of meat and wine until the search is abandoned. Since Rashba, Raavan, Maharam, Tur, and Mordechai all stand together in requiring mourning based on a majority, there is substantial basis to require mourning even in water without visible boundaries, particularly where a body was found and recognized by visual identification and clothing signs, even if the full evidentiary standard for remarriage has not yet been established. This is all the more so regarding Kaddish, which is not identical to mourning, as Shevut Yaakov explains.
