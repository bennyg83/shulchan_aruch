See Be'er Heitev s.k. 7, that this speaks in a case where his wife is permitted to remarry, etc. See Chinnukh Beit Yehudah responsa siman 91 regarding one who was lost, may the Merciful One protect us, and it was not known what happened to him. He ruled that his sons should not say kaddish for him, because here too there is concern for a stumbling block, as with mourning. One sage disagreed and distinguished between mourning and saying kaddish, but he z"l answered again to uphold his words, and several great authorities agreed with him. He wrote that nevertheless they should bring satisfaction to the soul of the dead in a way that will not lead to a stumbling block, such as leading birkat hamazon, reading the haftarah, and praying occasionally in a place where he does not displace other mourners. If the orphan is a minor, other actions should be done, such as giving charity on his behalf and hiring someone to learn on his behalf; see there. See Shevut Yaakov II siman 114, who asked from Ketubot 62, where Rabbi Yannai said to overturn the bed, even though certainly on such an unproven assessment they would not permit his wife to remarry. He therefore distinguishes: specifically where her husband was lost, they do not mourn unless there is clear testimony that permits his wife to remarry. But where her husband went to a nearby place and the matter can be clarified within the days of mourning, one does not object if they mourn for him, because it is a matter likely to become known; see there further. See Knesset Yechezkel, end of siman 53, who wrote: although it is explained in the Shulchan Aruch that we do not allow his sons and wife to mourn, I practice that since it is already public that responsa are being sent to rabbis, I permit his sons to say kaddish in the home and make a minyan there, for this creates publicity and we are not concerned that someone will come and testify merely that so-and-so died. I also do not object to his wife and sons wearing weekday clothes during the time of mourning, though not actual mourning clothes; see there. See Chatam Sofer responsa siman 344 regarding an unmarried young man who drowned in waters without end, whether they mourn for him, since there is no concern of permitting an agunah. He wrote that this matter is disputed. The Gaon Maharam Ben Chaviv in Ezrat Nashim, Even HaEzer siman 17 s.k. 60, considered it obvious that they should not mourn even for a bachelor, because of decree upon decree; so too is the implication of the Shach here. But Shevut Yaakov I siman 103 considered it very obvious that for a bachelor they do mourn, and he responded to the Shach. Since there is a dispute, if they were disagreeing about a law of mourning, whether it is proper to mourn in such a case, we would say that the halachah follows the lenient view in mourning. But their dispute is only whether we decree a decree upon a decree. Apart from that, it is obvious that one should mourn based on such a majority, for even over a doubtful stillborn it would be appropriate to mourn, except because of concern that this would permit a forbidden woman, etc. Therefore one who is lenient regarding decree upon decree and stringent to mourn should not be rebuked; may blessing come upon him, and he has upon whom to rely, namely the great authority Shevut Yaakov. See there. See Mishkenot Yaakov responsa siman 69, where the question concerned a son's reciting kaddish for his father whose death was not clarified enough to permit his wife. He discusses at length that the Shach and some later responsa did not notice that there is a great dispute in the core law of mourning, and most early poskim disagree and hold that whenever according to the majority he is dead, one mourns for him. The ruling of the Shulchan Aruch itself also requires study from what is written in siman 339 seif 2, that if they told someone, "we saw your relative goses," etc. Shevut Yaakov forced a resolution of the contradiction by distinguishing that with a goses who is only four days' travel from his wife, we are not concerned, etc. But in truth these are differing opinions; according to Maharam, the Rosh, and the Tur, even in waters without end we follow the majority, etc. Within the case of waters without end itself there is an explicit dispute, for in Maharam bar Barukh's responsa he brings the responsum of Rabbi Shimon ben Avraham, who wrote explicitly that those who drown in waters without end are mourned from the moment they despair of searching, even though his wife is forbidden to remarry. So too Sefer HaRaavan wrote. Only regarding aninut does he bring a dispute there whether they are forbidden in meat and wine as long as they have not despaired of searching. According to this, since the Rashba, Raavan, Maharam, Tur, and Mordechai all stand in one view, obligating mourning based on majority, there is certainly room to obligate mourning also in waters without end, especially where a body was found and recognized by visual identification and clothing signs, though all the testimony had not yet been fully established for permitting remarriage. All the more so regarding reciting kaddish, which is not so comparable to mourning, as explained in Shevut Yaakov responsa; see there.
