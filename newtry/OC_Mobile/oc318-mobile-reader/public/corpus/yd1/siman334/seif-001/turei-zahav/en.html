Even if there is concern that this will cause him to abandon observance. This ruling greatly astonishes me. How can we be concerned with the prohibition he has committed and thereby bring him, Heaven forbid, to abandon observance, a path from which those who enter may not return? The source is Terumat HaDeshen, responsum 138, citing a collection that inferred from the account that Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi, at his death, prophetically stated that Rav Acha bar Yoshiyah had excommunicated men who enclosed fish on Shabbat, and they apostatized because of the ban. The collection argued that this was related to teach that even if the sages foresee that excommunication may cause a person to abandon observance, they must not refrain from acting according to halachah. The citation to the chapter HaNose is an error; the passage appears in Kiddushin 72. In my opinion there is no proof from there. Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi was warning his listeners of disqualified families and corrupt places in Babylonia. He also reported that in one place people had departed from the service of the Omnipresent that very day after Rav Acha excommunicated them, and therefore people should distance themselves from them. One cannot object that this incident was already public knowledge, unlike the other hidden matters. On the contrary, Rabbi’s intent was to substantiate the prophetic character of his other statements. His knowledge in Eretz Yisrael of what had occurred that day in Babylonia could only have come through prophecy, demonstrating that the spirit of Hashem also spoke through him concerning the other matters. I can bring proof to the contrary from Kiddushin 20. One sold to serve idolatry, meaning to cut wood and perform other services for it but not to worship it as a deity, must be redeemed. The school of Rabbi Yishmael teaches that although he went and became an attendant of idolatry, one might have thought to push away the falling stone and deny him redemption; Scripture therefore says, “he shall have redemption.” If the Torah commands us actively to redeem one who has already become such an attendant, all the more must we at least refrain from taking action that may bring one who has not yet begun idolatrous service into it. It is inconceivable to accept such a ruling. Further proof appears in responsum 5 of Mahari Mintz, who permitted a nursing woman to remarry because otherwise two Jewish souls might abandon observance, citing Gittin 38 regarding a woman with whom men were committing transgressions and Abaye’s statement that, were it not for Shmuel’s ruling that one who frees his slave violates a positive commandment, he would have acted to stop the sin. It is therefore clear that one may not take an action carrying such a risk. This gloss should not be followed in practice, although it comes from the holy source of the Agudah, and indeed it does not appear in our text of the Agudah.
