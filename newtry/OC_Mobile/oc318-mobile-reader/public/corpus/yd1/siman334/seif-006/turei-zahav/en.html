And to expel his children from school, and so forth. The Ran writes this in the name of Rav Paltai Gaon. The Maharshal objects: Heaven forbid that children be deprived of Torah study, upon whose breath the world endures. Circumcision is different, because the primary obligation rests upon the father and it is his honor; similarly, burial of his dead rests upon him and delay is a dishonor to him. He may therefore be compelled until he accepts correction, after which the circumcision or burial is performed. Children’s Torah study, however, can never be replaced, and concerning it Scripture says, “If you forsake Me for one day, I will forsake you for two.” Likewise, if the husband sinned, what did his wife do to deserve expulsion from the synagogue? The Maharshal therefore could not believe these two rulings came from the Gaon. Yet according to his reasoning he should have objected even more strongly to withholding burial. If the offender sinned, what did the deceased do to deserve degradation, which the Tur calls a disgrace to all the living? It appears to me that the Gaon referred only to young children who do not yet possess independent merit but depend upon their father’s merit. Their participation may be withheld, just as burial may be delayed. He did not mean older children who are themselves subject to reward and punishment. For the same reason he permitted expelling the young children from school. Regarding the wife, however, there is indeed no reason to disgrace her, for she rises with her husband but does not descend with him.
