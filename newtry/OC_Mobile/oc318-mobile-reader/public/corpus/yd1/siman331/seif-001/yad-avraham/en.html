(Siman 331, Shulchan Aruch seif 13) Every human food that is guarded and grows from the ground is obligated in terumah. See the Taz, who asked that this implies obligation by Torah law, whereas in Perek Keitzad Mevarchin 36 it says explicitly that even tree maaser in the Land itself is rabbinic. In this he followed the Kesef Mishneh in chapter 2 of Hilkhot Terumot, who asked this question; the Penei Yehoshua and Tzelach also pressed themselves in resolving the sugya in Berakhot. What the Kesef Mishneh himself answered at the beginning of Hilkhot Maaser Sheni escaped them: there the case concerns inferior fruits, like the caper-bush, and with inferior fruits even the Rambam agrees that they are not obligated in terumot and maasrot by Torah law. In Sefer Ateret Rosh he wrote this distinction on his own, without mentioning that the Kesef Mishneh preceded him. From the words of Tosafot in the first chapter of Chullin 7, Bekhorot 54, and the Rash there, it is clear that the text in Berakhot explicitly reads “maaser of caper.” In Tanchuma, Parashat Bereishit, it is clear that the caper is an inferior fruit, for it lists there several harsh things in the world, etc., including mustard and caper. With this one can resolve the question of the Rash at the beginning of Maaserot from Nedarim 54, where it discusses the verse in Divrei HaYamim regarding Chizkiyah: “And when the matter spread, the children of Israel brought in abundance the first of grain, wine, oil, and all produce of the field,” which comes to include tree fruits and vegetables, implying that they are not obligated by Torah law. According to the Kesef Mishneh’s words, one may say that there too it refers to fruits of inferior trees, which according to all are not obligated by Torah law. On the contrary, there is additional support from there that they are obligated by Torah law. For if it were true that by Torah law only grain, wine, and oil are obligated in terumot and maasrot, and other tree fruits only rabbinically, then why did the verse explicitly specify honey together with grain, wine, and oil? That honey means dates, as stated in the Yerushalmi. If it were only rabbinic, Scripture should not have specified it explicitly, but should have included it generally under “all produce of the field,” like all rabbinic tree fruits, which are not specified explicitly but only included by implication. Rather, we must infer that honey too is by Torah law, like grain, wine, and oil that are listed. The reason it lists honey explicitly more than other fruits, which are also obligated by Torah law, is that it is more significant, for we rule that dates precede for a blessing because Scripture placed them earlier in the verse “a land of olive oil and honey.” In my work on the Rambam, Hilkhot Terumot, I elaborated on this.
