Many laws of charity. It appears that one may compare the maaser that people separate from monetary profit to the law of the poor man’s tithe of grain and fruit. My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that our monetary maaser has no obligation, neither from the Torah nor rabbinically. I was astonished, for at the beginning of siman 249 it is explained that it is a full obligation, as all the poskim wrote, and the Beit Yosef in the name of the Yerushalmi. Therefore we will copy the Tur’s words in brief regarding maaser ani: “If a man and woman came to ask, etc.” It is mentioned in siman 251 seif 9. When he distributes it in the field, the owner has no benefit of discretion in it; rather, the poor come and take it against his will, and even from a poor Jew it is removed from his hand. But what is distributed in the house has owner’s discretion, and he gives it to whichever poor person he wishes. In Perek HaZeroa, Chullin 131, it is explicit in a baraita that maaser distributed in his house has owner’s discretion, and even from a poor Jew it is removed from his hand; the Gemara derives this there by a gezerah shavah from leket. Although the Tur brought this only regarding what is distributed in the field, certainly there is no distinction, for it is derived by a gezerah shavah, and both are subject to that gezerah shavah. So wrote the Beit Yosef. One should not ask from what Tosafot wrote there, that with something in which the owner has discretion, even if a poor person came and took it, it is removed from his hand; how then do we say that maaser ani is removed from the poor person against his will? This is not difficult: we compel him to give maaser ani, but the choice is in his hand to give it to whichever poor person he wishes. If a poor person came and took it without the knowledge of this poor owner of the field, he can remove it from him and give it to another poor person. All this appears obvious to me. I was astonished at my teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, who wrote here against the Beit Yosef that he was not precise, based on the words of Tosafot that I mentioned; in passing he did not examine the Gemara carefully in this matter. From this it appears to me that there is support for the practice of compelling a groom, when he receives his dowry, to give maaser, as we find here that it is removed from his hand. But they distribute the maaser to the relatives of the groom and bride, for they take precedence over other poor people in this. We also find this with maaser ani, as the Beit Yosef wrote in siman 251 in the name of the Mordechai, that his relatives have a right in this. I saw some say that one separates maaser only from the dowry that the bride gives to the groom, but not from what the groom’s father gives his son, because his father already gave maaser. I was astonished: what basis is there for this? Is there an obligation on the money here, such that you could say the money was already exempted from maaser? It is an obligation upon the person, that he is obligated to give from what God, blessed be He, gave him. What difference is there whether his father or his father-in-law gave it to him? The one who says this certainly has a confused view. A father and son, two brothers, or partners, where one of them was poor, the other gives him his maaser ani. This is also explained regarding charity in siman 251 seif 12. One may not pay a debt with it, nor pay back a favor with it. The Beit Yosef explained that if his fellow did him a favor in a voluntary matter, it is forbidden to repay his favor with maaser ani. But one may pay from it for an act of kindness, and he must inform him that it is from maaser ani; this means, for example, for the mourner’s meal of consolation. One may not redeem captives with it, nor make shoshvinut with it, and one may not give from it charity. The Beit Yosef explains “charity” as charity assessed upon him by the townspeople, in which he has already become obligated, so it is like paying his debt. It appears to me that this law as well, that one does not give maaser ani for redeeming captives, refers to where there is already an obligation to redeem him, because they assessed it upon him, and he wants to exempt himself with maaser ani. But if he is not otherwise obligated to redeem him, certainly the one who separated may give his maaser ani for this, for there is no greater charity than this, since captivity includes sword and famine, as stated in the first chapter of Bava Batra. So it appears to me. They give it to the city scholar with discretion, as fruit by way of gift and honor. All this appears also to apply to the maaser that people separate from the profit that God, blessed be He, gave human beings. Above in siman 251 the Beit Yosef also wrote in the name of the Mordechai that maaser ani is comparable to one who separates charity.
