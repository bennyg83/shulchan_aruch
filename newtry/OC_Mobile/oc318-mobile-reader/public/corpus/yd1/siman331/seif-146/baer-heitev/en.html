Tzedakah. The Taz wrote: it appears that the tithe one separates from monetary profit should be compared to the law of poor tithe of grain and fruit. The Bach wrote that our monetary tithe is not obligatory, neither by Torah law nor rabbinically. I am astonished, for at the beginning of siman 249 it is clear that it is a full obligation, as all the poskim wrote. We will copy the Tur's words briefly regarding poor tithe: if a man and a woman came to ask, etc., as written in siman 251 seif 9. When one distributes it in the field, the owner has no discretionary benefit from it; but what is distributed in the house gives the owner discretionary benefit, and he may give it to any poor person he wishes. From this it appears to me there is support for the custom to compel a groom, when receiving his dowry, to give tithe, as we find with poor tithe that it is taken from him against his will. They distribute that tithe to relatives of the groom and bride, for they take precedence over other poor people in this. I saw some say that one separates tithe only from the dowry the bride gives the groom, but not from what the groom's father gives his son. I am astonished: from where should one distinguish? Is the obligation on the money? It is a personal obligation; what difference is there whether his father or father-in-law gives it? Whoever says so certainly has a confused view. If a father and son, or two brothers and partners, one of whom is poor, the other may give him his poor tithe. If two poor people received a field as sharecroppers, this one separates poor tithe from his portion and gives it to his fellow, and the reverse. One may not pay a loan with it, nor repay favors with it, such as where his fellow did him a voluntary favor; it is forbidden to repay him with poor tithe. But one may pay with it for a matter of kindness, such as the mourner's meal of recovery, provided he informs him that it is poor tithe. One may not redeem captives with it where he is already otherwise obligated, such as where the community assessed him to redeem the captive; but if he is not otherwise obligated to redeem him, he may certainly give his poor tithe for this, for there is no greater tzedakah than captivity, which contains all misfortunes. One may not give from it toward tzedakah already assessed upon him by the townspeople, for that is paying his debt. One may give it to the chaver ir who occupies himself with communal needs and support him; one sends him the poor tithe respectfully as a gift, even though one does not send him charity from the charity fund, because it is degrading for the townspeople to support their sage from charity. But poor tithe sent from the threshing floor is not recognizable as poor tithe, and appears like an offering and honorable gift. All this appears to apply as well to the tithe separated from the profit that God, blessed be He, gives people. Above in siman 251 the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Mordechai that poor tithe resembles one who separates tzedakah. End quote from Taz and Shach.
