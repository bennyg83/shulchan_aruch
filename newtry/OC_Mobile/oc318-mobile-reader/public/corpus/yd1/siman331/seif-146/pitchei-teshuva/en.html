The laws of charity. See Baer Heitev. Regarding whether maaser kesafim is a full obligation, see Responsa Shaar Efrayim, siman 84; Chavat Yair, siman 224; Sheilat Yaavetz, part 1, siman 6; and Be'er Yaakov. It is surprising that these great authorities overlooked Responsa Maharam of Rothenburg, Prague edition, siman 74, where it is explicit, in accordance with the Bach, that maaser kesafim is neither a Torah obligation nor a rabbinic one, but only a custom. I found that Responsa Teshuvah MeAhavah, part 1, siman 87, likewise questions them at length on this point. Mishnat Chachamim, Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah, page 17, also concludes that maaser kesafim has no foundation either in Torah law or in rabbinic law. If one has adopted the practice, however, it becomes like a mitzvah custom that may not be abandoned except for a great need. See Responsa Makom Shmuel, siman 10, concerning a father of the bride who obligated himself in the customary tenaim to pay the tithe from his own pocket, but later wished to give the entire tithe to poor relatives from the bride's side. The question was whether he had the right of discretionary benefit like one who tithes his own property on behalf of another. The responsum concludes that the bride's father has no such right here; the tithe should be divided among poor relatives of both sides, those of the groom and those of the bride.
