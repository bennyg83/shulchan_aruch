And once he performed meshichah, he acquired it. For a non-Jew acquires either through meshichah or through money. Therefore, one must receive the money, for he then acquires it through payment.
He shakes the vessel. This follows the view of the Rambam, for this entire seif is his wording, and he follows his own position stated in siman 124, seif 17, concerning one who held an open vessel, etc. I wrote there, however, that we do not rule accordingly.
For as soon as it enters the airspace of the vessel, etc. The Gemara states concerning this that were we to hold that nitzok constitutes a connection, we would say that while the wine is still in the airspace of the vessel it becomes forbidden through nitzok, because the stream touches at its lower end the residual wine below, and the entire stream becomes forbidden. According to us, since we indeed rule that nitzok constitutes a connection, as written in siman 126, in a case involving only a minor loss we should rule stringently. One cannot answer that this seif deals with a substantial loss, for it should then have distinguished accordingly. Furthermore, when one pours from a vessel while measuring, the amount involved is certainly not so great. It appears to me that according to our ruling that when forbidden wine becomes mixed with other wine one may sell the entire mixture except for the value of the forbidden wine within it, one may be lenient here concerning nitzok even l'chatchilah. The Beit Yosef gave a different answer; see there.
And likewise if the non-Jew measured it, etc. So too wrote the Tur. His words are to be explained as follows. The Mishnah states: If he fixed the price and then measured, it is permitted; if he measured before fixing the price, it is forbidden. The first clause has two explanations. Rashi explains that the Jew measured the wine into the non-Jew's vessel, as the Tur discusses at length in setting out these distinctions. Rabbeinu Chananel and Rabbeinu Tam explain that even if the non-Jew measured it, it is permitted, because the case is one in which the non-Jew had already performed meshichah and acquired it before measuring. Therefore, this too is permitted. The Tur accordingly records this explanation as well, that whenever the non-Jew first acquired the wine, it is permitted even if he measured it. Nevertheless, the Tur apparently reasoned on his own that l'chatchilah it is forbidden, because the non-Jew is likely to touch the wine while occupied with its measurement. The Tur had to state this here, although elsewhere he does not state an initial prohibition merely because an act is performed through the force of a non-Jew. Elsewhere the wine is in any event forbidden for drinking, and therefore one certainly would not arrange such a procedure l'chatchilah. Here, however, the wine already belongs to the non-Jew. The Tur therefore teaches that even so one should not do this l'chatchilah, because the non-Jew is likely to touch it. Otherwise, one may end up receiving payment for yayin nesech, since the non-Jew did not first perform a valid meshichah. I have not found a source for this ruling, and it therefore appears that the Tur wrote it from his own reasoning. The Tur then explains the latter clause of the Mishnah, that if he measured before fixing the price it is forbidden. This applies whether the Jew measured it or the non-Jew measured it and performed meshichah. In either case, it has not yet left the Jew's ownership, because the price was not fixed and the purchaser does not yet rely upon their reaching agreement. It is therefore likely that the non-Jew touched the wine. This is what the Tur means when he writes that although there is here only an admixture of ordinary non-Jewish wine, it is forbidden. One might otherwise have said that where residual wine remained at the bottom of the non-Jew's vessel, that which was permitted in the first clause is forbidden here because he had not yet acquired the wine, since the price was not fixed first, but that where no residual wine remained at all it would be permitted here as well. This cannot be correct. Why, then, does the latter clause of the Mishnah state without qualification that its proceeds are forbidden? The prohibition caused by residual wine requires only that one sell the mixture except for the value of the yayin nesech within it. Why would the Mishnah say that all the proceeds are forbidden? Furthermore, the word forbidden implies even b'dieved, whereas because of residual wine it would be permitted b'dieved, as stated above. The Tur therefore explains that the prohibition is because the non-Jew may touch the wine after meshichah. Accordingly, it is forbidden even if no residual wine remained at all. I have seen one who forced an unnecessary explanation here. One might ask that in the first clause as well, where the non-Jew had not lifted the wine beforehand to acquire it, the Tur writes that he is likely to touch it, yet he forbids it only l'chatchilah. It appears that in the first clause the non-Jew did not perform a proper meshichah, and merely measuring the wine does not make him as accustomed to handling it as in the latter clause, where he drew it in order to acquire it. There he is more accustomed to it and is more likely to touch it, and therefore it is forbidden even b'dieved. This appears to me to be the correct explanation of the Tur, unlike the Beit Yosef, who explains that the case of measuring before fixing the price refers specifically to a non-Jew who measured it. The Darkei Moshe already challenged this at length and wrote that whether the non-Jew measured it or the Jew measured it, it is forbidden, because the primary concern is that the non-Jew may touch the wine after acquiring it through meshichah. When the Tur wrote above that nevertheless it is permitted b'dieved, that was because the non-Jew did not acquire it through meshichah, but only through the Jew granting him use of the domain or saying to him, Let your vessel acquire it for you. He is therefore not as accustomed to handling the wine as when he acquired it through meshichah. The Beit Yosef asked that at the beginning of the siman the Tur wrote that one may take payment from the very non-Jew who rendered the wine forbidden. This is no difficulty. Here the case is different, because the Jew gave him permission to touch it by delivering it into his hand, unlike the case above.
