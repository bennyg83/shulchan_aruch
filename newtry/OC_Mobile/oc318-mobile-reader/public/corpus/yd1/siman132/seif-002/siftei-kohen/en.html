The Darkei Moshe proves this from the Tur and disputes the Beis Yosef. So also write the Derishah and the Bach. The Beis Yosef writes that if the Jew guarded it extremely carefully and knew with certainty that the non-Jew did not touch it, its payment is permitted; the Darkei Moshe cites this.

For then he necessarily shakes it. This is difficult for me. At the end of the sugya the Gemara asks, “When it reaches the airspace, etc.” This necessarily means that the earlier answer, “If he pours into a Jew's vessel, indeed so; rather, the case is where he pours into a non-Jew's vessel,” refers to a vessel held by the non-Jew, for otherwise he would not acquire it when it enters the vessel's airspace. This also appears from the later answer, “If the non-Jew holds the vessel in his hand, indeed so, etc.” If so, how could the Gemara have understood that the case was merely one where he poured into a non-Jew's vessel? Even if he poured into a Jew's vessel held by the non-Jew, it should be forbidden because the non-Jew necessarily shakes it. This requires study.
He must take the money. A non-Jew acquires both through meshichah and through payment. So writes the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 1 of Hilchos Zechiyah U'Matanah, as cited by the Beis Yosef. See also Choshen Mishpat siman 304, se'if 10, and what I wrote there.
And afterward he should measure. The Rambam concludes: “If he measured and did not take the money, even though the price had been fixed, its payment is forbidden, because from the moment it reaches the vessel it becomes forbidden as ordinary non-Jewish wine.” The Mechaber omitted this because he holds that the non-Jew acquires it immediately upon its entering the vessel, and it becomes forbidden only because of nitzok. We do not say that nitzok involving ordinary non-Jewish wine prohibits benefit, but only drinking. So writes the Bedek HaBayis: “Nevertheless, one must wonder why he wrote that its payment is forbidden, since according to what is written in siman 126, nitzok prohibits only drinking.” Even so, regarding the requirement to take the money first, he rules like the Rambam, who holds that at least initially this is forbidden. See also s.k. 24, where I discuss, “Nevertheless, it is difficult regarding the Rama, etc.”
All of it is permitted for benefit. If there is sixty times the wine residue, all of it is permitted even for drinking, as stated below in siman 134, se'if 2, in the gloss. So writes the Rosh in the final chapter of Avodah Zarah, as cited by the Bach.
Except for the value of the small amount of wine. This is not precise, and the Tur likewise does not conclude this way. The reason is that the wine residue belongs to the non-Jew and is also worth nothing. The Rama teaches that where it has some value and belongs to the Jew, that value is forbidden.
Even initially, it is not forbidden, etc. The Perishah, se'if 13, writes that this means he initially acted deliberately so that the wine residue would become nullified and thereby be permitted after the fact. Therefore, even after the fact it is forbidden, as explained above in siman 99, that all prohibitions which one deliberately increased the permitted substance to nullify remain forbidden. This does not appear correct. Moreover, deliberately nullifying a prohibition does not apply here, since his intent was certainly not to nullify the wine residue. It is also astonishing, for if so, why does the Gemara ask that this does not follow Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel? Even according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, such a case would be forbidden even after the fact because he deliberately nullified a prohibition. The words of the Tur are identical to those of the Rama; see there. Rather, after the fact it is permitted in every case. This is clear.
The seller should say to him, “Let your vessels acquire it for you.” The Beis Yosef writes in the name of the Tashbetz that the same applies if the Jew rents the non-Jew the place where the wine stands. It is permitted, but the Jew must remove it from there before it becomes forbidden, so that he does not receive rental payment for ordinary non-Jewish wine. The Darkei Moshe cites this. See also what the Beis Yosef writes below in the name of Orchos Chaim. See further Choshen Mishpat siman 200 regarding additional forms of acquisition, from which one may derive the law here.

So writes the Rashba in Toras HaBayis, as cited by the Beis Yosef in siman 134, folio 138, at the beginning of the fourth column. So also writes the Ran, as cited by the Beis Yosef in siman 126.
For as soon as it entered the airspace, etc. The Beis Yosef asks that all this is said in the Gemara only according to the opinion that nitzok is not considered a connection. According to our ruling above in siman 126 that nitzok is a connection, however, even if the non-Jew holds the vessel in his hand or it stands in his courtyard, the wine should always be forbidden, since before entering the vessel's airspace it already became forbidden through nitzok. He answers that even the opinion which holds that nitzok is a connection concedes that when one pours into ordinary non-Jewish wine, nitzok prohibits only drinking, not benefit. The Darkei Moshe cites this. The Bach objects that this interpretation is incorrect according to the Tur and the Rashba, as he explained at length in siman 126. Yet he himself requires that very correction. It is clear that the Rashba in Toras HaBayis, folio 151, third column, holds that regarding ordinary non-Jewish wine, nitzok is considered a connection only to prohibit drinking, not benefit. The Beis Yosef cites this at the end of siman 126, and the Bach himself writes so there. The Rashba himself also explicitly explains our case as the Beis Yosef does. Nevertheless, it is difficult to explain the Tur this way. The Bach answers that the Tur follows the conclusion in siman 126 that where there is substantial loss, nitzok may be permitted, and therefore here he permits the wine even for drinking after the fact, since prohibiting all the wine measured for the non-Jew even for benefit would presumably cause substantial loss. This is very puzzling. There is no loss at all, since after the fact it may be sold to the non-Jew. Just as he originally intended to sell it to the non-Jew, he may sell it now. How, then, could one permit it for drinking? Although we said above in siman 126, se'if 2, that even where it can be sold to a non-Jew it is considered a substantial loss, that is because there he must sell it somewhat cheaply, as is explicit there. Here, however, there is no loss at all, since he can sell it now just as he originally intended, particularly since the price had been fixed, for otherwise the non-Jew's vessels or domain would not acquire it. It is also astonishing that the Bach describes this as a substantial loss because all the wine measured would be forbidden for benefit, for it is certainly permitted for benefit, as the Tur and the Rama write. Moreover, our case concerns a method by which it is permitted to sell the wine initially. It appears to me that the Tur's reasoning is as follows. Where an actual mixture is permitted after the fact, logic dictates that nitzok should be permitted even initially. Similarly, the Rosh and the Tur write above at the beginning of siman 125 that if a non-Jew pours wine from a vessel, although the wine that leaves the vessel is forbidden because of his force, the wine remaining inside is not forbidden through nitzok, since the wine outside is prohibited only because of his force and not because of actual contact. All the more so here, where the mixture is entirely permitted after the fact. Even one who prohibits nitzok there may do so only because the wine that left remains forbidden after the fact, unlike here. When the Gemara says that according to the opinion that nitzok is a connection the wine is forbidden, it is discussing the view that even after the fact wine mixed with wine is forbidden in any amount, and may not be sold to a non-Jew except after deducting the value of the forbidden wine. We, however, rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that after the fact the mixture is permitted. Therefore, nitzok should not prohibit it here. These words are clear. Nevertheless, the Rama remains difficult. He rules like the Tur and Rashba that when the non-Jew holds the vessel in his hand, it is permitted even initially, yet he also records without qualification the Rambam and Mechaber's ruling that when the non-Jew holds it in the air, it is forbidden. One must say that the Rama explains the Mechaber's prohibition where the non-Jew holds and shakes the vessel as follows: before the wine reaches the vessel, the non-Jew, through his shaking, causes it to touch the vessel's rim, and the entire flow is immediately forbidden. But if he holds the vessel gently in the air so that the wine is poured into its center, only the first drop at the bottom becomes forbidden, and the rest is not prohibited through nitzok. This may be because nitzok involving ordinary non-Jewish wine does not prohibit benefit, as the Rashba and Beis Yosef hold, or because, since an actual mixture is permitted after the fact, nitzok does not prohibit it. This explanation is strained. Alternatively, the Rama explains that the Rambam and Mechaber's phrase “When does this apply” refers back to the immediately preceding case in which the price had not been fixed. They mean that the wine is forbidden only because the non-Jew might touch it, implying that if the Jew guarded it from touch it is permitted, as explained above in s.k. 8 regarding wine measured into a Jew's vessel. But when it is poured into a non-Jew's vessel, or into a Jew's vessel held and shaken by the non-Jew, it is forbidden immediately, even before actual touch, and the Jew must first take the money. In such a case, the non-Jew does not acquire the wine merely when it enters the airspace through lifting, because neither lifting nor any meshichah is effective until the price has first been fixed, as stated by the Tur and the poskim and explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 200, se'if 7. According to this, the Beis Yosef's objection in Bedek HaBayis against the Rambam, cited above in s.k. 18, is resolved. In any event, one may also say that the Rambam follows the Raavad, cited by the Rashba in Toras HaBayis and by the Beis Yosef at the end of siman 126, who holds that nitzok involving ordinary non-Jewish wine prohibits all of it even for benefit.
And he first lifted it. If he lifts it while measuring, he does not acquire it, since that lifting is for measurement rather than acquisition. Rashba, as cited by the later authorities.
