Once he set the price, his mind relied, etc. There is no concern that he will touch it before meshikhah, because since it has not entered the non-Jew's possession, presumably the Jew is careful that he not touch it.
Its money is forbidden. This implies even if the Jew measured it, for nevertheless, once it entered the non-Jew's possession, the Jew is not careful about the non-Jew's touch. So the Darkhei Moshe proved from the Tur and objected to the Beit Yosef, and so wrote the Derishah and Bach. The Beit Yosef wrote that if the Jew was extremely careful and knew with certainty that the non-Jew did not touch it, its money is permitted; the Darkhei Moshe brings this.
His mind did not rely. It was not acquired for him through this meshikhah, since the price had not yet been set. This is the Tur.
And the wine becomes forbidden, etc. And the money is forbidden. But if he took the money beforehand in a way that the wine was acquired immediately by the non-Jew, its money is permitted, as below.
And this is like selling ordinary non-Jewish wine. The Beit Yosef asked: to that same non-Jew it should be permitted, as above at the beginning of the siman, and he left it requiring study; the Darkhei Moshe and Derishah copied this. In my humble opinion, here, since they were engaged in the sale before it became forbidden, it is worse, for it appears that he takes the money because of the sale and not because of his loss. The Atteret Zahav and the Bach in Kuntres Acharon answered differently.
When is this said? Where he set the price before he drew it, it is permitted. See above s.k. 24.
Residual wine. Or a vessel wet enough to moisten another, as Rabbeinu Yerucham wrote, netiv 17 part 3.
He shakes the vessel. Then it is forbidden because of the non-Jew's shaking, as above siman 124 s.k. 35 and siman 125 s.k. 17. According to the Rav there, it is not forbidden by shaking without lifting; see there.
Or he was holding it in his hand, etc. We do not say that as soon as it entered the airspace of the vessel he acquired it. Since the first drop fell in and became forbidden because it fell into a vessel in a non-Jew's hand, all the wine in the upper vessel becomes forbidden because of nitzok. This is the Beit Yosef.
For then he necessarily shakes it. This is difficult to me from the Talmud's question at the end of the sugya: when it reaches the airspace, etc. He must have understood that the earlier answer, "if he pours into a Jew's vessel, indeed; it is needed where he pours into a non-Jew's vessel," meant that the non-Jew is holding it in his hand, for otherwise he would not acquire it when it reaches the airspace. So too it seems from the later answer: if the non-Jew holds the vessel in his hand, indeed, etc. If so, how did he understand that it was necessary to say he pours into a non-Jew's vessel? Even if he pours into a Jew's vessel and the non-Jew holds it, it is forbidden because he necessarily shakes it. This requires study. He must take the money. For a non-Jew acquires both by meshikhah and by money. So wrote the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 1 of Hilkhot Zekhiyah U'Matanah; the Beit Yosef brings this. See below siman 304 seif 10 and what I wrote there.
And afterward he should measure. The Rambam concludes: if he measured and did not take money, even though he had set the price, the money is forbidden, for from the moment it reaches the vessel it becomes forbidden as ordinary non-Jewish wine. The Mechaber omitted this because he holds that since immediately upon reaching the vessel he acquired it, and it is forbidden only because of nitzok, and we do not say nitzok to ordinary non-Jewish wine except to forbid drinking. So he wrote in Bedek HaBayit: nevertheless, one should wonder why he wrote that its money is forbidden, according to what is written in siman 126 that nitzok forbids only drinking. Even so, regarding the need to take the money first, he ruled like the Rambam, who holds that ab initio it is at least forbidden. See above s.k. 24 regarding "nevertheless, it is difficult against the Rav," etc. All of it is permitted in benefit. If there is sixty against the residual wine, all of it is permitted even for drinking, as below siman 134 seif 2 in the gloss. So wrote the Rosh in the final chapter of Avodah Zarah, and the Bach brings this.
Except for the value of a little wine. This is not precise, and the Tur too did not conclude this way. The reason is that the residual wine belongs to the non-Jew and is worth nothing. The Rav teaches that where it is worth something and belongs to a Jew, it is forbidden.
And even ab initio it is not forbidden, etc. The Perishah in seif 13 wrote that this refers to where he initially acted intentionally so that the residual wine would become nullified and be permitted to him after the fact; therefore even after the fact it is forbidden, as above in siman 99, that all prohibitions to which one intentionally added are forbidden. This does not sound correct. Moreover, here the rule of not nullifying a prohibition ab initio certainly does not apply, for surely his intent was not to nullify the residual wine. It is also astonishing: if so, what does the Talmud ask, "according to whom, not according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel?" Even according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, in such a case it would be forbidden even after the fact because he nullified a prohibition. The Tur's words are the Rav's words; see there. Rather, certainly after the fact it is permitted in every case, and this is clear. And the seller says to him: let your vessel acquire for you. The Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Tashbetz that the same applies if the Jew rented him his place; it is permitted, and he should remove it from there before it becomes forbidden, so that he not take rental payment for yayin nesech. The Darkhei Moshe brings this. See further what the Beit Yosef writes below in the name of Orchot Chayim, and see Choshen Mishpat siman 200 for other methods of acquisition; from there you may learn here. It is permitted. If because of the drop of wine, he derives no benefit from it, since it is worth nothing; moreover, it belongs to the non-Jew. So wrote the Rashba in Torat HaBayit, brought by the Beit Yosef in siman 134, p. 138 at the beginning of column 4, and so wrote the Ran, brought by the Beit Yosef in siman 126.
For immediately when it comes into the airspace, etc. The Beit Yosef asked that all this was said in the Talmud according to the view that nitzok is not connection; but according to what we rule above in siman 126, that nitzok is connection, even if the non-Jew holds the vessel in his hand or it stands in his courtyard, it should always be forbidden, for before it enters the airspace it is forbidden because of nitzok. He answered that even the view that nitzok is connection concedes that if one pours into ordinary non-Jewish wine, it is forbidden only for drinking. The Darkhei Moshe brings this. The Bach wrote against him harshly, saying this explanation has already been shown false according to the Tur and Rashba. But the matter is clear that the Rashba in Torat HaBayit holds that with ordinary non-Jewish wine we do not say nitzok is connection except to forbid drinking, not benefit, and the Beit Yosef brings him at the end of siman 126. The Bach himself wrote so there, and the Rashba himself explains our case explicitly as the Beit Yosef did. The Tur's words, however, are forced according to this. The Bach's own answer, that the Tur wrote this according to the conclusion in siman 126 that in great loss one may permit nitzok, and therefore here he permits even drinking after the fact because presumably great loss exists when all the wine measured to the non-Jew would be forbidden in benefit, is very astonishing to me. There is no loss at all, for after the fact he may sell it to the non-Jew; just as he intended to sell it to the non-Jew at first, so he sells it now. How could one think to permit it for drinking? Although above in siman 126 seif 2 we say that even where it can be sold to a non-Jew it is considered great loss, that is because he must lower the price somewhat, as is explicit there. Here there is no loss, for just as he wanted to sell it ab initio, so he sells it now, especially since the measure's price had been set. Also, what he wrote that there is certainly great loss if all the wine he measured becomes forbidden in benefit is difficult, for it is certainly permitted in benefit, as the Tur and Rav wrote, and the case here permits selling it in this manner ab initio. What seems correct to me is that the Tur holds that logic dictates that wherever the mixture itself would be permitted after the fact, one may permit nitzok even ab initio. The Rosh and Tur wrote similarly above at the beginning of siman 125: if a non-Jew poured wine from a vessel, even though what went outside is forbidden because of his force, what remains inside is not forbidden through nitzok, since the outside wine is forbidden only because of his force and not through actual touch. All the more so here, where after the fact it is entirely permitted. Perhaps even one who holds there that it is forbidden because of nitzok holds so only because what went outside is at least forbidden after the fact; but here it is different. What the Talmud says, that according to the view that nitzok is connection it is forbidden, is because the Talmud there is speaking explicitly where even after the fact it is forbidden and not like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel; rather, wine in wine forbids in any amount, even from selling it to a non-Jew except for the value of the forbidden portion. But for us, who rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that after the fact it is permitted, the prohibition of nitzok does not apply here. These are clear words and healing for the eyes. Nevertheless, it is difficult against the Rav, who wrote like the Tur and Rashba that when the non-Jew holds the vessel in his hand it is permitted even ab initio, while also stating plainly like the Rambam and Mechaber that if the non-Jew holds it in the air it is forbidden. One must say that the Rav explains that the Mechaber forbids where he holds it in his hand in the air and shakes it because before it reaches the vessel, through the shaking it touches the edge of the vessel and all becomes immediately forbidden through shaking. But where he holds it calmly in the air, in a way that one pours into the center of the vessel and only the first drop at the bottom becomes forbidden, it is not forbidden through nitzok, either because nitzok to ordinary non-Jewish wine is permitted in benefit like the Rashba and Beit Yosef, or because since even an actual mixture is permitted after the fact, it is not forbidden through nitzok. This is forced. Alternatively, the Rav explains that the Rambam and Mechaber's "when is this said" refers to the nearby case where the price had not been set: only there is it forbidden merely because he touches it, implying that if one was careful that he did not touch it, it is permitted, as I wrote above in s.k. 8, when he measured into a Jew's vessel. But into a non-Jew's vessel, or into a Jew's vessel in the non-Jew's hand where the non-Jew shakes it, it is immediately forbidden before he touches it, and he must take the money first. We do not then say that he acquired it as soon as it reached the airspace, because lifting and any meshikhah do not help until the price is set first, as stated in the Tur and poskim and explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 200 seif 7. According to this, the Beit Yosef's question in Bedek HaBayit against the Rambam is resolved. In any case, one may also say that the Rambam holds like the Raavad brought by the Rashba in Torat HaBayit and by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 126, that nitzok to ordinary non-Jewish wine forbids all of it in benefit. And he lifted it first. But if he lifts it while measuring, he does not acquire, for that lifting is not for acquisition but for measuring. The Rashba wrote this, and the later authorities bring it.
And they are not particular, etc. Nevertheless, ab initio one should be careful, as I wrote in siman 124 s.k. 71 and siman 128 s.k. 4, that where there is no loss, even nowadays one should not permit benefit.
