Specifically when he said to him: Let this dinar remain in your possession, etc. The Rashba proves this in Toras HaBayis HaAruch. He asks: What difference does it make that the Jew advanced him a dinar? Once the Jew gave it to him, the non-Jew acquired it, for ordinarily money is given to be spent. At the time the money was given there was no prohibition, since the workers had not yet received yayin nesech. When they later received it, the dinar was no longer the Jew's. This should resemble one who gave a lamb as a harlot's fee and only afterward had relations with her, in which case the fee is permitted. The Rashba therefore explains that the case is specifically as stated here, that the Jew said the dinar should remain in the non-Jew's possession. The Tur writes concerning this: This does not appear correct, for once he advanced the dinar, the wine was immediately acquired for him, and the non-Jew gave it to the workers as his agent, end quote. The Beit Yosef writes that the Tur undoubtedly did not see the Rashba's words in Toras HaBayis HaAruch, but only the abbreviated version, and therefore disagreed. Had he seen the longer work, he would not have disagreed, for how is this different from the case of the harlot's fee cited by the Rashba? In my humble opinion, however, the Tur's words are correct. Although money does not effect acquisition, that applies to a non-Jew according to the Tur's ruling earlier in this siman. Money paid by one Jew to another effects acquisition by Torah law, as stated in the chapter HaZahav. If so, when a Jew buys from a non-Jew, all the more so he acquires with money, as stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 126, that if a Jew can transfer a debt owed by another Jew through the presence of all three parties, he can certainly do so when the debtor is a non-Jew. The same applies here. Since he advanced the dinar for wine, he acquired with his money the corresponding amount of wine. One might ask why this acquisition matters, since the Jew does not personally benefit from the wine. The answer is that the non-Jew gives the wine to the Jew's workers as the agent of the Jewish owner. This does not apply to a harlot's fee, where one cannot say that the giver acquired anything through the fee. The fee therefore has no connection to the later relations, and it is permitted there. This appears to me to resolve the Tur's words. The Rashba, however, did not consider the Jew to have purchased yayin nesech from the non-Jew, because the Jew merely stated generally that he would provide the workers with food and drink, and did not mention yayin nesech.
It is permitted to give money to his servant to purchase yayin nesech for them, as the text should read. The Kol Bo explains that since he says to purchase it for them, the wine does not belong to him.
