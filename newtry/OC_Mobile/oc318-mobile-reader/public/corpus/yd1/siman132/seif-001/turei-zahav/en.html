It is permitted to take its value from that non-Jew. For this is not a sale. Rather, he says to him: You spilled my wine and caused me a monetary loss, and he takes from him the value of kosher wine. Although the non-Jew would not pay unless the Jew gave him the wine, nevertheless it is not forbidden, for he owes him as compensation for the damage and not as payment for a sale. So wrote the Beit Yosef in the name of the Ran.
The money is forbidden. Meaning, it is forbidden even to others, because the wine had already become forbidden before the sale.
And if the non-Jew first took the wine, etc. So too wrote the Tur. The source is the statement at the end of the chapter HaSocher, 62b, that the household of R' Yannai lent produce of Shemittah to poor people, and they repaid them in the eighth year. We do not say that the poor people committed a prohibition, because when the repayment was received the forbidden produce was no longer in existence. This resembles what we learned concerning the fee of a harlot: if he gave it after relations, the fee is permitted for an offering, because it is then merely a gift. Tosafos derived there that the same applies to a sale to a non-Jew on credit. Once the non-Jew performs meshichah, he acquires the item immediately, and the money he later gives is merely a gift. The Rosh, however, wrote there that the money is permitted only to another person, but not to the seller himself, because he benefits from money received for idolatry. He derived this from the law of one who sold orlah and betrothed a woman with its proceeds: she is betrothed, but the money is forbidden to the seller himself. Thus, the permission for others is an explicit Talmudic ruling from the case of Shemittah produce mentioned above. Only the prohibition for the seller himself is derived from the sale of orlah. The Beit Yosef overlooked here the source permitting the money to others and derived it instead by his own reasoning from the law that a woman betrothed with the proceeds of orlah is betrothed. He therefore asked that perhaps orlah is different because its prohibition does not transfer to its proceeds. Accordingly, he wrote here, There are those who say, etc., that it is permitted to others, referring to the view of Rabbeinu Yerucham cited by the Beit Yosef. This is imprecise, for the permission to others is an explicit Talmudic ruling, as stated. The phrase, There are those who say, should instead have been written concerning the prohibition to the seller himself, which is the view of Rabbeinu Yerucham cited by the Beit Yosef and also the view of the Rosh mentioned above.
