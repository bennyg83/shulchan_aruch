And all that did not strike it — they are not concerned. And it implies that if it struck, they are certainly concerned; and this deals when it is silent and they are trembling; therefore even if it did not strike, the chickens would be forbidden if they were not domesticated. But from the Rashba's words that the Tur brings, it implies specifically when it pursues and struck — they are concerned, since the blow is from its anger; and it deals when both are silent — therefore for a blow alone they are not concerned. Nevertheless it is fitting to be concerned about them not to eat them; and likewise when it enters among them and it is silent and they are trembling — it is fitting to be concerned about them not to eat them. But in these two cases it is permitted to sell them to a gentile, since by law it is permitted. But when it and they are silent and we did not see that it struck them — there is no need to be concerned at all. And even though in the case of a bear that leaped on an ox Terumat HaDeshen wrote that where we see it struck, it is forbidden even when both are silent — there it is different, for it is not domesticated with animals but with people; but a cat is domesticated with birds. With this it is settled that they do not contradict each other what is written here in the Shulchan Aruch with what is written in seif 11, etc.; end of the Bach's words, and his words are not compelling. In any case there is no contradiction in the Shulchan Aruch, for here it wrote "and all that did not strike it — they are not concerned," which implies that for a blow alone they are concerned, as in seif 11; therefore one should not be lenient for a blow alone. Nevertheless there is doubt for practical law whether to sell it to a gentile, since in Semak and other poskim and Orach Chaim they permit a cat when there are no rescuers — even though we do not hold like them — nevertheless it is possible to rely on them to sell to a gentile due to a double safek, as below in this s.s.
