And not even gone. I don't want to go:
Lest she go, etc. The Prishah copied in the name of the Maharshal: This is said specifically regarding vows, which are lenient, where we say that people are not careful with a condition. Regarding gittin, however, the Rosh ruled at the beginning of the chapter “One who divorces” that whenever a condition is within her power to fulfill, she may marry immediately, and we are not concerned that she may violate the condition, end quote. See Even HaEzer, siman 143. It appears to me that the cases are not comparable at all. There, if she violates the condition she will also become forbidden to her husband from that point onward, and she is therefore frightened to transgress. Here, however, there is no prohibition from that point onward, meaning after Pesach, but only regarding what occurred before Pesach. Because of that, fear will not rise upon her, for what has passed has passed.
The Tur cited here in the name of the Rambam: “Even though Pesach has passed,” etc., and wrote concerning it, “I do not understand,” etc. In my humble opinion, the Rambam’s words can be explained comfortably. The one making the vow used the additional expression, “until the festival,” even though after Pesach there is no prohibition at all, as the Tur asks. Why, then, did he mention “until the festival”? There are two possible answers. First, he intended that she not benefit until Pesach, lest she later transgress, which is indeed the true law. Alternatively, what he said, “until the festival,” was a retraction. Initially he intended to forbid benefit only until Pesach, and therefore said, “The benefit you receive from me is a konam until Pesach if you go to your father’s house.” At first he did not intend to add anything further. He then reconsidered and said, “until the festival,” meaning that everything should extend until the festival, both the benefit and the going. We find this concept of retraction also in siman 222. The Rambam therefore wrote that if she benefited and went before Pesach, she receives lashes, for in that case the prohibition is certain. After Pesach has passed, however, there remains a doubtful prohibition, for perhaps there was a retraction here. Unspecified vows are interpreted stringently, but lashes are not administered in a case of doubt. So it appears correct to me.
Furthermore, it appears to me that the Rambam’s reasoning may be explained through what the Rambam wrote, as cited in the Shulchan Aruch, siman 237, at the end of seif 1: Although the others did not accept the oath, if they fulfilled his words they are praiseworthy for not causing him to become accustomed to making an oath in vain. So too here, since in any event he intended in his oath that it last until the festival, even though there is no prohibition of benefit if she goes, one should nevertheless be careful to fulfill as much of the oath as possible. The first explanation, however, appears primary.
