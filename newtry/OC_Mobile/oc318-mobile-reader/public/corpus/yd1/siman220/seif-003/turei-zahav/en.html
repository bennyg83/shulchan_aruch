In the middle of the week. This is not precise, as he writes afterward, “or before it.” It comes only to exclude one who is standing on Shabbos, for then he is forbidden only until Motzei Shabbos, as in seif 6 regarding one standing on 29 Elul. So wrote the Ran.
Regarding what is written, “this week,” in the Tur, Rambam, Ran, and Rosh the text reads, “this Shabbos.” The author of the Shulchan Aruch holds that the day of Shabbos is also included in the term “week,” for the word “week” denotes seven, meaning all seven days of the week together with Shabbos. Nevertheless, whenever he is standing in the middle of the week, he forbade himself only until the completion of the seven days of that week. It appears to me that if he says in German, “die Woche,” the day of Shabbos is not included in the prohibition. That expression is equivalent to “the weekdays” in the Holy Tongue, for when people wish to say Shabbos and weekday they say in German, “Shabbos und Woche.” Shabbos is therefore entirely excluded. This differs from the expression “this week,” because the word “week” includes Shabbos as the completion of the week. The expression “weekday,” however, means until Shabbos, for “Woche” is the opposite of Shabbos. Nevertheless, it appears to me that if he uses weekday language and says that he will not eat meat for five or six weeks, his intention is numerical, and the Shabbosos are certainly included. Since he stated it numerically, it is as though he said five or six times seven, and Shabbos is included automatically. When, however, he says, “die Woche,” his intention is not the number of days, but rather the entire period during which the days are weekdays and not Shabbos. I believe this is the common custom in such usage. All the more so, if he is standing on Shabbos and says that a certain species shall be forbidden to him “die Woche,” he certainly did not intend that day but the coming week. So it appears to me as practical halacha, following the principle that with vows we follow ordinary speech.
And afterward he is permitted, etc. The distinction between “this week” and an unspecified “week” is substantial, and everyone knows the difference. This is unlike seif 1, regarding the distinction between “one day” and “today.” Even there the Gemara says that “today” may be confused with “one day,” and one might permit himself at nightfall even when he said “one day.” “One day,” however, will not be confused with “today” so as to raise a concern that even when he said “today” he might permit himself in the middle of the day. It is therefore clear that in all the distinctions found in this siman concerning “until Pesach” or “until Pesach will be,” no release is required. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote later from his own reasoning that release is required lest people err. This is not implied by the poskim, who did not mention it there at all.
