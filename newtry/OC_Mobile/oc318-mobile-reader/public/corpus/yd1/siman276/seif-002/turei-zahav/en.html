He must say that he is writing for the sanctity of the Name, etc. The Tur writes here that he must think that he is writing for the sanctity of the Name. The Beit Yosef understood that the Rosh and Tur require verbalization and therefore wrote here that he must say it. In truth, according to the Rosh and Tur, thought suffices for the Divine Names after the scribe has verbally declared at the beginning that he is writing the Torah scroll for its sanctity. The Rosh states explicitly that verbalization is needed at the beginning of the writing, but at the time of writing the Names he must think of the sanctity of the Name. Nevertheless, the Shulchan Aruch ruled correctly that explicit speech is required even for each Name, because the Mordechai cites Rabbeinu Yaakov of Orleans, R' Elchanan, and Rashi that verbal articulation is required, and Rabbeinu Shimshon distinguishes between the rest of the scroll, where an ordinary intention to write a Torah scroll suffices, and the Names, where explicit verbal sanctification is required. His proof from one who intended to write Yehudah but omitted the dalet is sound. The Baraisa teaches specifically regarding the Name that he must pass the pen over it and sanctify it, showing that the Divine Names require a higher level of explicit intent than ordinary words. Therefore, although the Shulchan Aruch attributed this to the Tur, the practical ruling itself is correct: a Divine Name written without explicit verbal sanctification is invalid.
Like one who consecrates blemished animals. This implies that if a Divine Name was written by mistake in a place where no Name belongs, but was written with sacred intent, it remains sacred and requires genizah. It is no less than a Name written upon the handles of utensils. A Name written without intent, however, has no sanctity.
