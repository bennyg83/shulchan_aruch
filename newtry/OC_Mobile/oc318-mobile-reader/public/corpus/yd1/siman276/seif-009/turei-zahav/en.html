The letters attached after them. Letters preceding a Divine Name, such as the lamed in “to Hashem” or the vav in “and Hashem,” may be erased because they have no sanctity. Letters attached after the Name possess sanctity because the Name sanctifies what follows it. Therefore, if the final letters of a form such as “your God” were omitted, they may be suspended above the line. This is not considered suspending part of the Divine Name itself, since those letters are not intrinsically part of the Name but merely acquire sanctity through attachment. Even one who is stringent initially should not invalidate the scroll after the fact.
Eloah, Elohim. The Tur also lists the form “Elohei,” and some Rambam texts do likewise. The yud in “Elohei” is part of the root and is sacred. A responsum in Masas Binyamin discusses a scribe who wrote “Elohenu” without the yud. Some wished to erase the final nun-vav and rewrite the yud and those letters, arguing that the incomplete Name had not yet sanctified what followed. Others required removal of the entire sheet. Masas Binyamin preferred suspending the yud, reasoning that it might not be a root letter and that suspension is only rabbinically restricted. In my opinion this is incorrect. The yud is not merely a sign of construct state. It indicates the plural form of sovereignty, as in “Adonei Yosef” and “Elohei hashamayim,” and is therefore an essential part of the Divine Name. The Yerushalmi's discussion of appended forms does not prove otherwise, for it refers to a differently vocalized form meaning “my God,” not to the plural construct “Elohei.” Nor may the nun-vav be erased. The preceding letters already form the sacred Name “Eloah,” and they sanctify meaningful letters attached to them even though the yud was accidentally omitted. The rule that letters following a Name may lack sanctity applies only where they form no meaningful attachment and are plainly an accidental addition. Accordingly, the preferable repair is to remove the entire sheet. Where that is very difficult, there is support from Maseches Sofrim and responsa for cutting out the mistaken Name and rewriting it. If the word “Eloah” was written without the vav in a place where our scrolls have it full, it likewise requires correction; one cannot dismiss the omission merely because the word is sometimes written defectively elsewhere.
The second method mentioned there, to scrape away the final nun-vav because the Name was not written correctly when the yud was omitted, also appears incorrect to me. The letters alef-lamed-hei written before the nun-vav already form a sacred Divine Name in their own right. Why should that sanctity not extend to letters attached afterward, like the suffixes of other Divine Names? Although the word contains an error because the yud was omitted, the sanctity of the Name that was actually written remains and extends to the attached letters. The Baraisa itself proves this: according to the 'others,' the final kaf-mem of 'Eloheichem' may not be erased. Any contemplated erasure necessarily concerns a word written incorrectly, for otherwise even an ordinary Torah letter could not simply be erased. Yet they still say the suffix may not be erased because the Divine Name sanctified it. The same applies when the yud was omitted from Eloheinu. Indeed, wherever a sacred Name is written, even on the handle of a utensil that must be placed in genizah, an attached suffix such as the nun-vav of Eloheinu shares its sanctity and may not be erased.
The third method, removing the entire sheet, is certainly the preferable approach. One should not cut Divine Names out of a Torah scroll, as the Beit Yosef cites from the Rosh and his son R' Yehudah; it is better to remove the sheet. Nevertheless, since scribes have long practiced cutting out a Divine Name written in error, and this has support in Maseches Soferim, chapter 5, where the wording concerning two sacred Names can be read as 'he cuts away' the latter, if replacing the sheet is not readily possible one may rely upon this practice. The cited responsum also discusses the verse 'Vayitosh Eloah asahu,' where Eloah was written without a vav, and says no correction is necessary because the word is sometimes written full and sometimes deficient. This is astonishing. In our authoritative text that verse is written with a vav, and the fact that the word occurs defectively elsewhere does not permit changing the received spelling. Since the vav here is intrinsically part of the Divine Name, one cannot simply suspend it after the fact; the Name should be removed in the permitted manner and rewritten correctly.
