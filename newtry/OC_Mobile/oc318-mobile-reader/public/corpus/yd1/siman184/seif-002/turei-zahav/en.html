One onah. This is the explicit conclusion at the end of the chapter HaIshah. The Beit Yosef cited the Or Zarua, who ruled that if she is accustomed to see during the day, he must separate from her throughout that entire day and the preceding night. The Beit Yosef wrote concerning this that one need not be concerned for it, but should follow what the Tur and the poskim wrote, that the prohibition applies only during the day itself, as the Shulchan Aruch writes here. For the Gemara explicitly rules that the halacha follows R' Yehuda, who holds this way. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, justified this ruling of the Or Zarua by saying that although the halacha follows R' Yehuda, it is nevertheless proper to be stringent in this matter. This is astonishing, for he would be stringent regarding something that the Gemara explicitly permits, especially since vesatos are rabbinic.
Furthermore, the Beit Yosef cited the Hagahos Maimoniyos, who wrote in the name of Aviassaf that this onah is a day or a night during Nisan and Tishrei, when the day and night are equal; but during Tammuz and Teves, one requires half a day and half a night. He concluded, "This is astonishing"; end quote. The Beit Yosef wrote concerning this: "It appears that his reason is that he understood the measure of an onah to be 12 hours, from the 24 hours comprising the day and night. Nevertheless, it appears that one need not be concerned for this, since the poskim did not mention it. Moreover, the language of the Gemara implies that it is particular only about the day or the night, and the chapter HaIshah mentioned hours only because of R' Yose's dispute there"; end quote. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote concerning this that the author of the Hagahos Maimoniyos and the Beit Yosef overlooked the explicit statement at the beginning of the chapter Tinokes that regarding a niddah as well, an onah is specifically 12 hours. That is, a day or a night during Nisan and Tishrei, and half a day and half a night during Teves and Tammuz; and this is how we rule; end quote. It is difficult for me, for if the matter depends on hours, the prohibition of the onah would automatically apply during the period in which she is accustomed to see, whether during the day or at night. One could then say 12 hours only if she saw during Nisan or Tishrei in the middle of the day or in the middle of the night. But if she sees at the end of the day during Nisan or Tishrei, how would one calculate the 12 hours? If they are calculated from the hour at which she is accustomed to see and onward for 12 hours, she would also be forbidden during the following night, yet permitted before that hour on the very same day. This certainly is not so, for she is forbidden throughout the entire day according to R' Yehuda in the Gemara and according to the poskim. If they are calculated backward for 12 hours, what would one do when she sees at the beginning of the day? According to this, she would be permitted after that hour on the very same day, and Heaven forbid that one say so. Likewise, if you would say that one calculates six hours backward and six hours forward, she would not be forbidden throughout the entire day when she sees at the beginning of the day. Rather, it must be that the matter depends on the day and the night. The Sages established the measure of the prohibition here as either a day or a night, whatever its length may be, whether short or long, both before the hour at which she is accustomed to see and afterward. They applied a uniform measure and called this the measure of an onah, because in most places we reckon the night as 12 hours, and likewise the day. This has no connection to the passage at the beginning of the chapter Tinokes, Niddah 65, which depends only on hours. There the discussion concerns a young girl who saw blood in her father's house and later married, who is permitted to have relations after the first intercourse because we attribute the blood to her virginity, for a day or a night during Nisan and Tishrei, and for half a night and half a day during Teves and Tammuz, according to one answer there. In that case it is impossible to make the matter depend on the actual night, as it does here, because there the permission is based on a fixed period during which the blood after the first intercourse is attributed to virgin blood, and it does not depend on day or night. Thus, if he first had relations with her at the end of the night, she would otherwise receive only a brief period, while one with whom he first had relations at the beginning of the night would be permitted for a long period afterward. Therefore, they said there that the matter depends on 12 hours, and every such virgin is permitted for 12 hours after her first intercourse. This is unlike the present case of seeing niddah blood, which is linked to a time, either the night or the day, according to her veset. The Sages established the measure of the prohibition as requiring him to separate from her throughout that entire day or that entire night during which she is accustomed to see, both before and after the accustomed hour. The measure is therefore equal for all women, and she is permitted before that night or day and after it. I am astonished that the Beit Yosef did not raise this difficulty against Aviassaf's position. It appears possible that Aviassaf himself indeed held that one always calculates six hours before the hour at which she is accustomed to see and six hours afterward. Accordingly, if she is accustomed to see at the beginning of the day, she would indeed be permitted at the end of the day after six hours. We, however, cannot say this, for we have a settled halacha that she is forbidden throughout the entire day, even when it is a long day. This is clear and straightforward.
But not from other forms of closeness. The Beit Yosef wrote that even embracing and kissing are permitted, and we are not concerned that this may lead to relations, since relations at that time are forbidden only rabbinically. This is unlike the Terumas HaDeshen, who wrote that embracing and kissing are forbidden. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that one who is stringent in this matter is deserving of blessing. It appears to me, however, that according to the Rosh this is forbidden as a matter of law. The proof is from siman 342, where the Rosh wrote regarding mourning that one must be stringent concerning embracing and kissing, because they lead to desire. The same applies here, and all the more so, since his desire is greater when he is departing on a journey.
