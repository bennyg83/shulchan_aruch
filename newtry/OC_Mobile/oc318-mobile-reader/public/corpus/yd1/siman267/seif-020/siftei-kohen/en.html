To his slave, etc. The Gemara in Kesubos, chapter Af Al Pi, states that this applies only to a Canaanite slave, not to a Hebrew slave. Although Hebrew servitude does not apply today, this remains relevant where a person owns another's labor, as above, seif 14: he may not say, “Work for me, but I will not support you.”
But I will not support you. Rather, the slave must beg from door to door or be supported by charity, for Israel is commanded to sustain the poor among them.
But slaves of nichsei melog. If his wife brought them into the marriage, he must support them, because she brought them on that understanding; otherwise they might die or flee, and the husband does not bear financial responsibility for them. This is the Rambam's language. Raavad writes that this ruling is unclear, because the husband can say, “I will not use their labor and I will not support them.” Bach explains that Rambam certainly agrees where he expressly says this, since perhaps they will not flee and can support themselves from their own labor. Rambam speaks only where he says, “Work for me, but I will not support you,” for then he causes them to flee. Kesef Mishneh already preceded him in this explanation. See there.
