That he write. The Bach discussed this at length and concluded that we do not force his master to write a bill of manumission; and so ruled the Taz; see there.
On his own. The Shach wrote: at first glance it implies that even if there are no witnesses at all, he becomes obligated through his admission alone. Although there are no expert courts nowadays, nevertheless his admission is an admission, for the admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses. However, he is not exempt as one who admits to a fine, since there are no courts nowadays; and so wrote the Levush and the Bach. But this is surprising, for in the Talmud in Perek Merubeh it is explicitly implied that wherever he is not exempt because of admitting to a fine, his admission is also not an admission; see there, where he discusses proofs at length. He concludes that one must say that what the Rav wrote, "he admitted on his own," means this: if he admitted on his own before witnesses came, so that you should not say it speaks where he did not admit at all. This is primary. I later found explicitly at the end of Responsa She'erit Yosef that wherever one is not exempt because of admitting to a fine, his admission also, etc.; end quote.
