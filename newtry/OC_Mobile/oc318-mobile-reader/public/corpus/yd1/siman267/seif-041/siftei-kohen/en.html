And the same applies if the master admitted on his own, etc. At first glance this implies that even without witnesses the slave goes free solely through the master's admission. This is difficult, for the Gemara, Bava Kamma 75a, and the Rosh and Ramban indicate that where an admission does not exempt from a fine, it also does not create liability. Rambam likewise writes that one who admits to a fine outside beit din and is later contradicted by witnesses pays because of the witnesses, not because of his admission. Although a litigant's admission is generally equivalent to one hundred witnesses in monetary law, fines are different, since they are not adjudicated today. Rambam's ruling at the end of chapter 5 of Hilchot Avadim should therefore be understood to mean that without witnesses the master's admission does not free the slave. The Rama's phrase, “and the same applies if the master admitted on his own,” means that he admitted before the witnesses came, not that no witnesses ever came. This is the primary view. I later found that Rabbi Yosef Kohen of Krakow writes explicitly in his commentary to Tur, Choshen Mishpat, siman 1, that wherever an admission does not exempt from a fine, it likewise does not create liability. If the slave protests, he does not thereby go free. This is unlike release through money above, seif 26, where some hold that he goes free even against his will. The master is pleased to receive the money and knowingly transfers ownership, whereas a document functions differently. Alternatively, just as a slave is acquired with money, he is released with money, since the same form of currency serves both purposes. A deed of acquisition and a deed of release, however, use different legal formulations.
