One may fix a price with gleaners according to the gleaners' market price. Even Rambam, who was stringent above regarding old produce when there was no established price at all, agrees here that each class has its own established price. Above, both were the same type of produce and were ultimately expected to reach the same price. Here, however, the distinction is that the gleaner's grain is a mixture of rye and wheat, which is effectively a different type from the householder's grain, and they will never have the same price. Each therefore has its own permanent established market price, and one may contract with each according to its own price.
But he may not fix a price with a householder. The Gemara explains that a gleaner, if he has no produce, can borrow from another gleaner, whereas if a householder has none, it is beneath his dignity to borrow from a gleaner.
