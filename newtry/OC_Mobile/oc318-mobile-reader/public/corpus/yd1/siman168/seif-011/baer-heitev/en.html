Permitted. Even if the lender knew that it belonged to a Jew, for he transferred it to the gentile and he acquired it by meshichah. Shach.
To acquire. The Shach wrote: but if the borrowing gentile says to the lending Jew, "give it to so-and-so the gentile, so that he will borrow for me upon it from a Jew with interest, and when you deliver it into his hand I will immediately be obligated to return the collateral to you," it is permitted. The Beit Yosef brought in the name of the Mordechai in this language: if the gentile said to Reuven, "place collateral in my stead," and the Jew took his collateral and said to Shimon, "this is the gentile's collateral," or said to him, "I transfer it to you in his place," and Shimon agreed, he may receive interest from the Jew who gives the collateral, for he is Shimon's agent to lend to the gentile, and so too when he transfers collateral to him. If Reuven denies it, Shimon swears that it is as he says and is exempt; end quote. The Beit Yosef wrote regarding the phrase "this is the gentile's collateral" that the permissibility applies even though the collateral belongs to a Jew, because collateral has the law of a guarantor, for he does not sell it unless he first demands payment from him, etc. The Taz wrote: I am astonished that a holy mouth would say this, for certainly the lender's main reliance is on the collateral. What does it help that he first demands payment from him? In any event, payment comes from the Jew. This is not comparable to a Jewish guarantor in the gentile's hand, for there, if he demands from the gentile first, payment will not come from the Jew; unlike here. He discussed this at length to explain the Mordechai differently and disagreed with the Beit Yosef and the Bach; see there. (In Nekudat HaKesef he wrote that this is not difficult at all, for since he admitted that the collateral belongs to the gentile, his admission is like one hundred witnesses, and the gentile acquired it through his admission; it is comparable to below seif 13, see there.) Is not. The Bach wrote that even if he is not coercive, since he lent it to him temporarily to return it, he is not a robber and must return it to him.
Coercive. The Taz wrote: but if he is not coercive, only one cannot force him to redeem the collateral, yet if he came to redeem it he would need to give him the interest too. Although he is coercive regarding this, nevertheless when he comes to redeem it, he must give him the interest as well. One should not say: since he is a robber of the first Jew, how may the second Jew take interest from the first, when the gentile has no status of borrower at all? This is not difficult at all, for at the time the Jew lent on the collateral, he did not have the status of a robber; only now the matter became spoiled. Therefore he must pay the second Jew what the gentile became obligated to him until now, both principal and interest. From the time he became a robber onward, if it then remained in the hand of the second Jew, it is forbidden to take any interest from the first Jew. The Levush also permitted in this case to take the interest even after he became a robber with respect to the lender, because he can say to him, "he is a robber toward you, not toward me." This is not clear, for nevertheless he is his robber and must return it to him while it is intact. Also what Rama wrote, that if the gentile is coercive and if he came to redeem it, etc., appears to speak where he was presumed coercive even at first; but if his presumption worsened after the loan, he pays him the interest until that time, as I wrote nearby; end quote.
And interest. The Hagahot Ashiri wrote: even if the Jew delayed in redeeming it, he must give him all the interest, because until the lender receives his money it is considered sold to him.
