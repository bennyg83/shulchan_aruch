It is permitted for the Jew, etc. In this case it is permitted even if he knows that the Jew gave him this collateral, since the non-Jew borrows for his own needs. This is so even according to Rashi’s opinion cited above.
But he cannot acquire for the non-Jew,. etc. Beit Yosef brought in Mordechai's name in Rabeinu Yehudah's language: if the non-Jew told Reuven place collateral in my place and the Jew took his collateral and told Shimon this is the non-Jew's collateral or said I transfer to you in his place and Shimon agreed ; permitted to receive interest from the pledging Jew who is Shimon's agent to lend to the non-Jew; and likewise when he transfers collateral; and if Reuven denies, Shimon swears and is exempt ; end quote. Beit Yosef on what he said "this is the non-Jew's collateral" ; the permission is even though collateral is the Jew's because collateral has guarantor law, for he does not sell unless he first demands payment, etc. I wondered how a holy mouth could say this, for certainly the lender's primary reliance is on the Jew's collateral which truly belongs to the Jew ; what does demanding the Jew first help; payment comes from the Jew either way; unlike a Jew guarantor for a non-Jew where if the non-Jew demands first payment does not come from the Jew ; unlike here. But it appears to me this is not Mordechai's path; so it states there and in Hagahos Ashiri and Rosh's responsum klal 108 siman 5: a non-Jew told Reuven lend me money on my collateral and Reuven went and borrowed from Shimon on the non-Jew's collateral; or the non-Jew asked him place collateral in my place and he told Shimon this is the non-Jew's collateral or said I transfer to you in his place ; permitted for Shimon to take interest from Reuven who is Shimon's agent to lend to the non-Jew; and likewise when he transferred collateral ; end quote. Beit Yosef and my teacher z"l understood Mordechai's statement "this is the non-Jew's collateral" refers to what precedes ; the non-Jew asked him place collateral in his place ; and on this Mordechai gave two divisions; and they labored to find a reason for permission when the Jew left his collateral in his place ; heaven forbid to say this is a general permission, for it is not like a guarantor ; here is prohibition even if the lender thinks collateral is the non-Jew's he automatically has payment in hand without coercion even through the agent on the non-Jew who will say behold you have collateral needing only warning; and all the more truly it belongs to the Jew and the agent transgresses "before the blind." My teacher z"l also wrote even without collateral permitted, etc. ; he was not precise, for without collateral it is worse since lender's primary reliance is on the non-Jew ; end quote; it appears complete prohibition. And what Ran wrote even without collateral permitted ; means non-Jewish collateral; rather it refers to Mordechai's first statement: non-Jew told Reuven lend on my collateral and Shimon did so ; Mordechai says two paths: first borrowed on non-Jew's collateral and says this is the non-Jew's collateral without lying; second says I transfer, etc. ; in both permitted; therefore concludes permitted to take from Reuven who is Shimon's agent ; meaning the first division; but the second has no relevance; and on the second path concludes "and likewise when he transferred collateral" ; this is clear as the sun for they did not examine properly in their haste. And on Mordechai transferring for the non-Jew Beit Yosef wondered ; we hold there is no acquisition for a non-Jew in perek HaRibbis (71a); therefore he explained at least prohibition for the agent only permission for the lender ; very strained; if so poskim would not be silent and why not mention at least prohibition for the agent lest he do so with a Jew. My teacher z"l wrote possibly to resolve: meaning he transfers in a way the non-Jew acquires ; such as through admission he admits it belongs to the non-Jew like the case of Isur the convert, etc. ; not clear to me; that admission case where he admitted the money is not his does not apply here at all, for certainly regarding money we say a person is believed about his liability and admission of litigant is like hundred witnesses even though we know he lies, as Shulchan Aruch Choshen Mishpat siman 113 seif 2; but this certainly does not help to remove prohibition through his lie ; consider if a Jew borrows from his fellow, gives ribbis, and says I admit this money belongs to a non-Jew ; can permission come through this famous lie. I again saw in Maimoniyos Mishpatim siman 31 regarding selling a non-Jew's debt ; admission does not help in the name of Baal HaItur; the initial challenge does not exist per my view here ; not that the non-Jew acquired this collateral and the lender acquired from the non-Jew; certainly no acquisition for a non-Jew; rather here the pledgor gives the lender a path of sale for this collateral saying I transfer this thing to be yours on this right that if so-and-so non-Jew gives you principal and fixed interest you return the collateral ; this is exactly as one sells to his fellow for a non-Jew and stipulates conditions; likewise here; and there is no loan on the collateral ; the pledgor is the lender's borrower and collateral is security. So it appears to me.
To allow him to redeem it, etc. For he is like a robber who took an item from one person and gave it to another; as long as the owner has not despaired of recovering it and the item remains intact, the second person must return it to him against his will. The same applies here, as the Mordechai wrote. Regarding the interest, however, there is a distinction. If the non-Jew was such a coercive person that even had he come to redeem the collateral he would not have been required to pay interest, the Jew must likewise permit the first Jew to redeem it without interest, as the Rama subsequently writes. If, however, he is not coercive in that respect, but merely cannot be compelled to redeem the collateral, while had he come to redeem it he would have been required to pay interest, then even though he is coercive regarding the collateral, the first Jew must pay the second Jew the interest when redeeming it. One should not object that since the non-Jew robbed the first Jew, how may the second Jew take interest from the first, when the non-Jew no longer bears the status of a borrower? There is no difficulty. When the second Jew lent upon the collateral, the non-Jew did not yet bear the status of a robber, for he had permission to borrow upon it and to pay interest. Only afterward did his conduct deteriorate and he refused to redeem it. Therefore, the first Jew must pay the second Jew whatever the non-Jew had become obligated to him until that time, both principal and interest. From the time the non-Jew became a robber onward, however, if the collateral remained in the second Jew’s possession, he may not take any further interest from the first Jew. The Levush also permitted taking the interest from him in this case, reasoning that the first Jew can be told, “He is a robber toward you, but not toward me, for he would have paid me the interest.” According to this reasoning, the first Jew would have to pay all the interest even after the non-Jew became a robber toward the owner. This does not appear correct, for in any event he is the owner’s robber and the item must be returned to him so long as it remains intact. So it appears in my humble opinion.
And if the non-Jew is coercive, and had he come to redeem it, etc. It appears that this means that he was already presumed to be coercive from the outset. If his status deteriorated only after the loan, he must pay the interest until that time, as I wrote immediately above.
And that which was written, that he must give the lender the principal and interest, is because the lender lent nothing to the Jew. The Hagahos Ashri writes that even if the Jew delayed redeeming it, he must give him all the interest. This also appears to be the conclusion of the poskim cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 172, that even when the Jewish purchaser is required to give him the money, there is no ribbis, because until the lender receives his money the collateral is regarded as sold to him.
