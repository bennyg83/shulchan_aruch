Permissibly. Taz wrote: this too discusses where he specifies the permission — how it was — and not by default. And even for one who does not wish to interpret the matters thus, and says it suffices to say "I took it permissibly" without specification — nevertheless it appears that as a practical matter we must conduct ourselves thus now, for we see that many transgress the ribbit prohibition and do not consider it anything, and they do not care to act permissibly at all; therefore the judge must in any case investigate him how it was done permissibly, and if it is not clear to him he must administer an oath — end quote.
A pledge. The Shach writes that this implies that even if he claims, "you lent me one hundred for two hundred upon the pledge with fixed ribit," and the other answers, "I made a permitted arrangement with you," he is exempt without an oath. All the more so if he claims, "I left you a pledge for ribit," and the other claims that from the outset it was done permissibly. What was written above in seif 18, where he claims, "I pledged you a pledge with fixed ribit," and the other answers, "you told me it belonged to a non-Jew," while he denies it, so that the lender must swear, can be distinguished: here it is permitted because we say a person does not abandon what is permitted and eat what is forbidden, and we assume he certainly acted permissibly. There, however, he did nothing permissibly except that he says, "I relied on you when you said it belonged to a non-Jew"; the main permission depends on the borrower's statement, and the borrower denies it, so he must swear. If the lender comes to collect, the borrower swears and is exempt. This requires study, for here he rules that the borrower swears although he makes himself and the lender wicked, while below siman 177 seif 12 he writes that even though he could have exempted himself by saying "I paid" or "this never occurred," and would have sworn and been exempt, he is not believed even with an oath, since a person cannot make himself wicked. The words contradict each other and require study. The lender. This is unlike above at the end of siman 162, for there one cannot say he does not abandon the permitted and eat the forbidden; here there is certainly a prohibition, while there it never occurred to him that the wheat would rise in price such that he should be careful to have wheat at the time of the loan. The Sema in Choshen Mishpat siman 82 s.k. 33 writes that specifically here, where he admits there is ribit but says it was done permissibly, he swears and collects while holding a sacred object, like anyone who swears on a document and collects. But if the lender stands by his document and says, "there was no such thing; there is no ribit in it at all," he need not swear before receiving the money while holding a sacred object. Rather, the borrower pays, and afterward may sue him, and he swears a hesset oath. See responsa Masat Binyamin siman 67 for laws relevant here. Thus the Shach. The Taz writes that the Rama decided this from his own view, that with a document he swears and collects. In Nekudot HaKesef he challenges him; see there.
