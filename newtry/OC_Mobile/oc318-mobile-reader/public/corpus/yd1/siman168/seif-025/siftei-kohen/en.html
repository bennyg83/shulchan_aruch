Except through a non-Jew. In a manner that is not forbidden, and by the way explained above; and the same if he claims "I took profit through merchandise" and other similar permits; and so it is written in a responsum siman 132 folio 64 side 2 — see there.
Exempt even etc. Specifically here where it has not become clear that he took interest; but if it became clear that he took interest — there is no permit, and if he claims the money belonged to orphans or charity, the lender is not believed; as stated in siman 160 s.k. 32.
Such as where he had collateral, etc. This implies that even if he claims, "you lent me one hundred for two hundred on the collateral with fixed ribbit," and the other answers, "I acted permissibly with you," he is exempt without an oath. So too it implies in Beit Yosef, and all the more if he claims, "I left you collateral for ribbit," and the other claims it was done permissibly from the outset. As to what Rosh and Tur wrote above in seif 18 s.k. 54, that where one claims, "I pawned you collateral with fixed ribbit," and the other answers, "you told me it belonged to a non-Jew," and he denies it, the lender must swear, etc., one can distinguish: here it is permitted because of the principle that one does not leave the permitted and eat the prohibited, and we say certainly he did permissibly whatever he could do. But there he did nothing permissibly; he merely says, "I relied on you because you said it belonged to a non-Jew." If so, the main permission rests on the borrower's statement, and the borrower denies him; therefore he must swear. So it appears to me. He swears and is exempt. This requires study, for Mordechai wrote without qualification that the lender is believed without an oath, implying even with an oral loan. Beit Yosef explained that this is specifically where he has a document or collateral in hand; but if it was an oral loan, it is obvious he is not believed to collect even with an oath, end quote. I do not know why this is obvious, for one can say that because a person does not render himself wicked, he is not believed. So wrote Semag in the name of R. Yaakov, that if he denies and says, "it is a loan with ribbit," even though had he wished he could exempt himself by saying "I repaid" or "there never was such a matter," he is not believed, because a person does not render himself wicked, end quote; Beit Yosef brings it below in siman 177 and established it in Shulchan Aruch there seif 12. Necessarily it speaks of an oral loan, for otherwise there is no migo. Although Shulchan Aruch there did not explain that it speaks of an oral loan, since his words there are taken from Semag it implies that he speaks like Semag. Furthermore, from where did Beit Yosef know to explain Mordechai's words unlike their plain meaning and unlike Semag in the case where he speaks? So too Mahari Weil siman 37 wrote without qualification that the borrower is not believed because a person does not render himself wicked. Bach followed Beit Yosef and explained that it speaks with a document or collateral, but this is not implied in his language and it is forced to distinguish. Furthermore, in Darkei Moshe seif 6 regarding the above law in seif 13, "according to your first words," etc., he brought Mordechai's words and wrote: so ruled Semag, that even where there is a migo the borrower is not believed, for a person does not render himself wicked, end quote. This requires study. Ateret Zekenim also wrote here that the borrower swears and is exempt, even though he renders himself and the lender wicked; the presumption of money is stronger, to follow it through an oath, end quote. But below in siman 177 seif 12 he wrote that even though had he wished he could exempt himself by saying "I repaid" or "there never was such a matter" and would swear and be exempt, he is not believed even with an oath, because a person does not render himself wicked, end quote. The words contradict one another and require study. And if there is a document, etc. This is not comparable to above at the end of siman 162, for there one cannot say he does not leave the permitted and eat the prohibited, since here there is certainly prohibition; but there it did not enter his mind that the wheat would become expensive so that he should be precise to have wheat at the time of the loan. So responsum Rosh, kelal 108 end of siman 7, explicitly distinguishes. He swears and collects. Sema in Choshen Mishpat there s.k. 33 wrote that specifically here, where he admits it contains ribbit but says it was done permissibly, he swears and collects while holding a sacred object, like the law of anyone who swears on a document and collects. But if the lender stands on his document and says, "there never was such a matter, there is no ribbit in it at all," he need not swear before receiving the money while holding a sacred object; rather, the borrower pays and afterward can sue him, and he swears a heset oath. So wrote Bach there, though at the end of this siman he confused the matters and contradicted his own words in Choshen Mishpat; see there. Nevertheless, this law that the Rav wrote, "he swears and collects," is marked in all editions of Shulchan Aruch as Beit Yosef's reasoning from the implication of Mordechai, and so wrote Darkei Moshe. But in Beit Yosef he wrote explicitly the opposite according to Mordechai: where he has collateral or a document, he collects without an oath when he claims, "I acted permissibly with you"; see there. This requires study. See Ateret Zekenim, responsa Masat Binyamin siman 67, and Beit Yosef for further laws relevant here.
