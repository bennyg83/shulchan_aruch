My collateral. Ran implies that even without collateral the arrangement is permitted where the lender bears no responsibility against the Jewish agent, such as where the specific non-Jewish borrower is identified or the lender is placed directly with him. If the agent says, 'Whatever you take from the non-Jew, give me,' it is permitted. The agent may even redeem the collateral with his own money and pay the lender principal and interest, because Torah interest is forbidden only when passing from borrower to lender, and the agent is not the borrower.
That he say. Shach initially implies that failure to formulate the arrangement properly forbids interest. Bach limits this to an initial precaution against a possible Torah prohibition; after the fact, ordinary intent is presumed permissive. An owner's declaration that the collateral belongs to the non-Jew or is transferred in his place may suffice. If disputed, the lender may swear and retain the collateral until interest is paid.
Gave. Where the lender gave money to the agent to lend to a non-Jew and obtain collateral, the lender must accept responsibility for the collateral. If the agent independently brought the collateral before becoming the lender's agent, he need not return it to the non-Jew merely so that the lender can accept responsibility.
I believe you. No oath is required. Shach.
To swear. An Ashkenazic responsum limits the rule to where the collateral was delivered at the time of the loan. Maharshal holds that if witnesses prove it belonged to the Jew, interest is forbidden. If a wife pledged her husband's property and it is proven his, it must be returned. If he cannot prove ownership but claims the lender knows it is his, the lender must swear lack of knowledge.
Became known. If the lender clearly learns that the collateral belongs to a Jew, he may not collect interest even if beit din cannot compel him because he claims to trust only the borrower. Shach.
And one who schemes. Meaning one who borrows under a non-Jew's name while claiming the collateral is his. Rashba.
The collateral. The same rule applies without collateral where it is known that the loan was truly to a non-Jew. If the lender asserts certainty and the recipient denies it, the recipient takes a hesset oath and is exempt. Shach.
Wicked. Shach cites Rashba that he is called wicked only if his conduct removes him from the principle, 'The remnant of Israel shall not commit injustice.' Rama's earlier case was more severe because he initially stated explicitly that the loan was to a non-Jew.
