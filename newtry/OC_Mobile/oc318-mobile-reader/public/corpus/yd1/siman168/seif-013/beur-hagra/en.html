A non-Jew, etc. The Tosefta, chapter 5, teaches: if a Jew said to a non-Jew, "Here is your wage; go lend my money with interest," it is forbidden. If a non-Jew said to a Jew, "Here is your wage; lend my money with interest," it is permitted, though forbidden because of marit ayin. Money belonging to a Jew and deposited with a non-Jew may be lent with interest; money belonging to a non-Jew and deposited with a Jew may not be lent with interest. If a Jew became an administrator or steward for a non-Jew, it is permitted to borrow from him with interest. If a non-Jew became an administrator or steward for a Jew, it is forbidden to borrow from him with interest. This is the rule: whenever responsibility rests upon the Jew, it is forbidden; whenever responsibility rests upon the non-Jew, it is permitted. The same appears in the Yerushalmi. In the first and last cases, involving wages, administrator, and steward, responsibility remains upon the owner. In the middle cases of deposited money, responsibility rests upon the custodian. In the final case, because the arrangement is publicly known, there is not even a concern of marit ayin. See the end of this siman below. Just as a Jew who became administrator or steward for a non-Jew may lend to a Jew, and thereby act as the Jewish borrower's agent to borrow from the non-Jew, since the responsibility does not rest upon him, so too he may act as the Jewish lender's agent to lend to the non-Jew. This is the meaning of "provided he says," etc., and "likewise," etc. See Mordechai, simanim 447 and 448, and the Rosh's rulings, siman 56. In a responsum of the Rosh, and in the Tur in the name of the Ramah, it is written that the agent need accept responsibility only for the money, not for the collateral. The Rosh disagrees and requires him to accept responsibility for the collateral as well. See there.
And if the non-Jew gave, etc. It is obvious that the Jew need not accept responsibility for events that occurred before the loan. See the Darkei Moshe.
And if he did, etc., he should say, etc. Although a single witness is ordinarily believed regarding prohibitions, here he is not believed because his statements contradict one another. A stronger case is stated in Gittin 54b: Rava said, such as where he encountered him, etc. All the more so here, where he now states the opposite. Rashba. See the Beit Yosef, folio 131c.
But he must be concerned, etc. For the non-Jew is believed insofar as his statement imposes a loss upon himself.
And even if, etc. Mordechai and the other poskim write in the name of the Riva that since the other party is holding the collateral, the claimant is not believed through an oath to extract it from his possession. This is the meaning of the Rama's words, "All this does not discuss," etc. According to this, however, the Shulchan Aruch's statement that even witnesses testify, etc., is difficult. It is certainly difficult according to the reason that a person cannot render himself wicked, for here he transgressed from the moment the interest was fixed. If so, it is obvious that witnesses are believed, and even his own oral admission should not be believed. Rather, the source here is a responsum of the Rosh, which explains that a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses, and a person is believed regarding himself, as stated in Keritot 12a and Shevuot 41b: "Go bring witnesses," etc.; "whoever says," etc. The Rama's distinction nevertheless fits, because where there was no collateral one may claim, "I was merely jesting with you," whereas that claim is unavailable where collateral was delivered. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 81, seif 4, and below at the end of siman 177.
However, if, etc. As stated in Kiddushin 66a: Rava said, "If he is trusted by you," etc. See there.
And if he suspected him, etc. As in every claim based upon uncertainty.
And one who does, etc. The Rashba writes only that he transgressed the principle, "The remnant of Israel shall not commit iniquity," etc. Here, however, the case is one in which interest was fixed, and he transgressed from the moment of the appraisal, as the Rashba and Rosh write.
