And so the custom is widespread. That is to say: so the widespread custom is like the lenient view, that even with his collateral in his possession by default — it is permitted; he returns his collateral and takes interest from this one. But if he is obligated to return it — there is no custom here, only that they customarily return it when he redeems it; and possibly if he wished not to return it the authority is in his hand per law. And the Rabbi proceeds to explain that possibly he is obligated to return it since the custom is so; and so Darkei Moshe implies — as I explained, see there.
And if they stipulated from the outset, etc.; and it is possible, etc. The Rama wrote this based on his own reasoning, following what he wrote in Darkei Moshe. I find it difficult. Since they stipulated from the beginning that the second Jew must later return the collateral, and likewise where he is obligated to return it by established custom, which is equivalent to an express stipulation, the transaction is not a genuine sale. It should therefore be forbidden for him to take ribbis from the first Jew, as Rashba writes in a responsum cited by the Rama below at the beginning of siman 174. Mordechai likewise clearly implies that the arrangement is permitted only because the second Jew is not obligated to return the collateral. He writes that presumably the parties intended to structure the transaction permissibly, and even without an explicit statement the first Jew intended to remove himself entirely from the collateral, so that the second Jew could, if he wished, refuse to return it at all. The first Jew merely relies upon him voluntarily to return it and allow him to redeem it by paying principal and ribbis. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 303, likewise writes that there is no permissible arrangement through an actual binding condition, but one can rely upon a promise and personal trust. Although Darkei Moshe writes that Terumas HaDeshen speaks specifically where the ribbis was converted into principal, this is not correct. Anyone who examines Terumas HaDeshen will see that he makes no distinction between a case where the ribbis was capitalized and one where it was not. Beit Yosef likewise writes that if the first Jew is fully secured by a binding condition, it is forbidden, as the Gemara explicitly states regarding one who sold a house and said, "When I have money, return it to me," which is forbidden. This is explained below in simanim 172 and 174. In truth, however, it appears to me that the widespread custom of lending upon collateral belonging to a non-Jew, while the Jewish lenders later return the collateral to the first Jew, rests on the fact that they do so voluntarily, as though they later sold it back to him, but they are not legally obligated to do so. Even if one assumes that they are obligated to return it, the custom can be explained on the basis of the Rosh in the chapter Kol Sha'ah, citing Baal HaItur: collateral belonging to a non-Jew that is held by a Jew with ribbis may be re-pledged by that Jew to another Jew at the same rate of ribbis even without bringing the second Jew before the non-Jew and without saying that he is selling the collateral to him. We do not say that the first Jew is thereby taking ribbis from the second Jew, because the first Jew did not acquire the collateral unless the non-Jew had said, "Acquire it from now if I do not redeem it by such-and-such a time." Beit Yosef cites this ruling, and the Rosh there appears to agree with Baal HaItur; his Kitzur Piskei HaRosh says likewise. That ruling must concern a case where the first Jew does not sell the collateral to the second Jew and the second Jew is nevertheless obligated to return it to the first Jew for redemption, either because they expressly stipulated so or because such is the local custom. If the Rosh meant that the first Jew expressly said, "I sell it to you as a complete sale," or that he simply pledged it to the second Jew in circumstances where we presume that he intended a permissible transaction and it is therefore treated as though he had expressly sold it, then even if the non-Jew had said, "Acquire it from now," the transaction should be permitted. Even if the collateral had become the first Jew's own property, he could sell it to the second Jew and later buy it back, as explained below. It follows necessarily that Baal HaItur and the Rosh discuss a case where the collateral remains merely a pledge in the second Jew's hand. Even so, it is permitted because the non-Jew did not say, "Acquire it from now if I do not redeem it by such-and-such a time," and therefore the collateral remains legally the non-Jew's property. Since the second Jewish lender primarily relies upon that collateral, the ribbis he receives is regarded as coming from the non-Jew's collateral. This also appears from Darkei Moshe, seif 92. There the Rama explains that the Rosh in the chapter Kol Sha'ah follows his own view in a responsum that the first Jew need not sell the collateral to the second Jew, but may simply deliver it to him. Therefore, according to the Rosh, if the non-Jew said, "Acquire it from now," the arrangement is forbidden. Rabbeinu Tam, however, permits it because he understands the transaction as a sale; even in that case it is permitted because there are two successive sales. This also resolves the apparent contradiction in the Rosh. In the chapter Eizehu Neshech he writes that the arrangement is permitted only where the first Jew sells the collateral to the second Jew. Beit Yosef and Bach understood this to contradict the ruling in Kol Sha'ah, but there is no contradiction. Eizehu Neshech concerns a case where the collateral has already entered the first Jew's ownership, such as where the non-Jew said, "Acquire it from now," or where it is apparent that the non-Jew intends to abandon it permanently. In that case an actual sale to the second Jew is necessary. Similarly, the Rosh writes in a responsum that a Jew may pledge to another Jew collateral that he holds from a non-Jew even without selling it to him, merely by delivering it on condition that the second Jew collect the ribbis from the non-Jew. This does not contradict his ruling in Eizehu Neshech. Since the first Jew tells the second Jew to collect the ribbis from the non-Jew, the first Jew thereby removes himself from the collateral, and therefore no express statement of sale is required. See Responsa Rosh, kelal 108, siman 4, where this is explicit, and the Tur likewise shows that this responsum does not contradict the Rosh's rulings but establishes an additional independent rule. This is unlike Beit Yosef, Bach, and other later authorities. All of this applies where the collateral has become the first Jew's property, meaning where the non-Jew said, "Acquire it from now," or it is evident that he intends to abandon it permanently, or where the local custom is that after one year the lender may sell the collateral. Otherwise, it is obvious that the arrangement is permitted in every form. Likewise, when most authorities and the Mechaber require the first Jew to say expressly, "I sell it to you," unlike Mordechai, they too speak where the collateral has already entered the first Jew's ownership. See Responsa Maharam, Prague edition, siman 728. We therefore rule in accordance with the majority of authorities, while our customary practice rests upon Baal HaItur and the Rosh in Kol Sha'ah, whose ruling is not disputed. This appears clear to me.
Even if the borrower and lender wish, etc. It appears to me that in this case the first Jew must say explicitly, "I sell it to you as a complete sale wherever it is located," according to all opinions. Otherwise, the words do not imply that he sold it, since the collateral still remains in the borrower's possession. This also appears from the Rama's wording that from that point onward the collateral must be at the risk of the second lender, etc. Likewise, according to what I wrote above, where the collateral remains in the non-Jew's possession, meaning the non-Jew did not say, "Acquire it from now," and it is therefore permitted for the first Jew to borrow from another Jew with ribbis, here too he must transfer the collateral to the second Jew wherever it is located, so that wherever it is, it becomes the second Jew's collateral. So it appears to me.

Although some question this, etc. This refers to Maharik, shoresh 119, whose words imply that it does not help for the first Jew to transfer to the second Jew his debt secured by the collateral. Darkei Moshe writes this, and Maharshal likewise writes it in Responsa, siman 52. It appears to me, however, that Maharik's principal intent there is only to say that a person cannot transfer a debt owed to him by a non-Jew, even through a kinyan, through agav land, or through ma'amad sheloshtan, as Mordechai writes in the chapter Mi Shemes in the name of a responsum of Maharam. It is possible that Maharik discusses a case where the debt exceeds the value of the collateral. In such a case, transferring the collateral itself does not suffice; one would have to transfer the debt represented by the collateral, and a debt owed by a non-Jew cannot be transferred through an ordinary kinyan. But Maharik certainly agrees that if one transfers the collateral itself, the recipient acquires the collateral wherever it is located. This appears clear to me. See there carefully. This also resolves the difficulty that Beit Yosef encountered in Maharik's words.

You shall release the non-Jew, etc. Responsa Maimoniyos, siman 68, writes in the name of Maharam that the arrangement is as follows: the first Jew goes to the non-Jewish debtor and guarantor and tells them, "The debt that you owe me, promise to pay to this other Jew, my fellow; when you promise him, you will be released from your obligation to me." Kol Bo writes likewise, and Beit Yosef cites both. It appears to me that the same applies even if the first Jew does not go to them, but immediately releases the non-Jew from any claim against him in every respect, leaving the second Jew to collect from the non-Jew whatever he can. This is permitted, as is implied by the Tur.
