A non-Jew who took collateral, etc. The Semak, cited by the Tur, writes that even according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak, who permits a case where the Jew gave his collateral to a non-Jew to borrow upon it because he transfers ownership to the non-Jew, that reasoning does not apply where the non-Jew seized the collateral against the Jew's will. This follows the Baal HaItur cited by the Rosh in the second chapter of Pesachim. According to the Rosh, however, who disputes the Baal HaItur, it is permitted, as explained below in seif 20. The Tur likewise writes here that the later words of his father, the Rosh, indicate that this too is permitted. The Rosh explains in Pesachim that the prohibition of interest applies between borrower and lender. Here the Jew lent nothing to another Jew, but only to the non-Jew. The dispute of Rabbeinu Yitzchak and the Ramban does not apply, because there the Jew sends the non-Jew to borrow from a Jew and permission therefore requires the presumption that he transferred ownership. Where the non-Jew borrowed for his own need, the Rosh permits it. Sefer HaTerumah, folio 65d, makes the same distinction. The Semak nevertheless holds that since the non-Jew took collateral from one Jew and immediately pledged it to another Jew, it appears that he borrowed for the first Jew's benefit, as the Beit Yosef explains, folio 130d, s.v. "And from the words of my father, the Rosh," etc. This is also the meaning of "Likewise, if the Jew," etc., for agency is irrelevant where the lender did not know. It is forbidden specifically when the Jew himself redeems the collateral, because this makes it appear that the non-Jew was his agent from the outset. This is the meaning of "If the Jew," etc. Below, however, in seif 20, the ruling is that even if the Jew redeems it, it is permitted, following the Rosh's view. The alternative explanation offered by the Beit Yosef there is difficult, because the Baal HaItur's reason is that a non-Jew does not acquire collateral from a Jew. The Rosh should therefore have answered that the Jew actually transferred ownership to him, whereas instead he challenged the Baal HaItur from the nature of the borrower and lender relationship. Furthermore, did the Jew know from the outset that the non-Jew would need to borrow upon the collateral? If one says that the Jew presumably intended to transfer it, the same should apply regarding chametz. All this follows Rashi's view in seif 6, which the poskim accepted, as explained above.
And if the responsibility, etc. In that case the collateral is merely like a guarantor. The lender must first seek payment from the non-Jew, and it is permitted. This is stated in the Tosefta and Yerushalmi cited by the Rosh in siman 53: when a Jew borrowed from a non-Jew, or a non-Jew from a Jew, a Jew may become guarantor, etc., in a case where the lender may not first collect from the guarantor, as the Raavad and Rosh explain there. See above, siman 170, and the Beit Yosef, folio 131a, s.v. "And the Ramban," etc.
