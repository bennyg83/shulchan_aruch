And there is. The Bach writes that there is no dispute here. Specifically where the non-Jew already redeemed the pledges and did not give him the ribit according to what the agent fixed, there one does not extract from the agent, since he did not explicitly become a guarantor for the ribit. But where the non-Jew redeems only from the agent, and the agent redeems from the lender and says that the non-Jew is violent and did not pay the ribit as fixed, the lender need not return the pledge unless he gives him what he fixed with him, for he can say, "I have no dealings with the non-Jew, only with you." This is specifically where it was unspecified; but if the Jew explicitly said, "you will be my agent," he must give the pledge without ribit, provided that he suffer no loss in the principal. It is clear from his words that wherever he explicitly specified the ribit, he certainly must pay it, since the body of the loan was upon the pledge. However, where he mentioned the name of the non-Jew and knew that he was violent, he may not take the ribit from him, even from the pledge, for he knew and waived it, and must return the pledge without ribit. Thus the Shach. The Taz writes that if the agent did not know this non-Jew was a coercive person and learned only afterward, he has no liability, and the sender must give him the pledge without ribit, for why should he lose when he was not negligent? But if the agent knew the non-Jew was violent, he is liable under dina degarmi, since he should have informed the lender that this non-Jew was violent. The Levush writes that this applies specifically where the agent brought the pledge with him at the time of the loan; otherwise, where the non-Jew sent him to bring the money and would afterward give him the pledge, responsibility for the pledge was never on the agent, for he was only the Jew's agent. The agent may say, "do not sell it, for you will damage me, since the non-Jew is violent," and if the lender sold it and the agent was damaged thereby, the lender must compensate him. The Taz disagrees and writes that whenever the agent knew the non-Jew was violent and did not inform the lender, he cannot extract the pledge from him without ribit, for he caused the loss to himself. He further writes that the Rosh speaks explicitly where he sent him to bring a pledge from the non-Jew on the lender's responsibility, and nevertheless ruled so. In Nekudot HaKesef he agrees with the Levush; see there. For the need. That is, he should say that he borrows for the need of the non-Jew, and should not say that he will repay principal and ribit. Although it is permitted for a Jew to be an arev kablan for a non-Jew where there is a pledge, as below siman 170 seif 2, there the beginning of the loan was to the non-Jew. However, I have seen in many places that the Jew says at the time of the loan that he will repay principal and ribit. It appears they hold that only where the pledge is not good enough for the amount of the loan, to be worth principal and ribit even if it remains a long time, does it appear that the lender relies on the Jew, and in such a case it is forbidden to say so. In such a case I have indeed seen the practice that the Jew gives the lender a document upon himself by way of heter for security. But where the pledge is good, the Jew's words neither add nor subtract, since the loan is for the need of the non-Jew, and there is no concern. Nevertheless, one should be careful in this matter. Thus the Shach.
