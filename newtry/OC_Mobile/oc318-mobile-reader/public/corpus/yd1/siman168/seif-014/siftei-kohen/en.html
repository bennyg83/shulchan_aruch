He shall not pay etc. And if he suspects he received more from the non-Jew, he places a ban on him since he does not claim it certainly; and likewise Beit Yosef; and Bach so ruled.
Except if he mentioned to him etc. And Bach wrote that even the first reasoning discusses where he mentioned the non-Jew's name to him and he recognizes him — for otherwise the lender is not obligated to give him the collateral unless he purchases it from him, as stated nearby; and likewise Darkei Moshe that Mordechai also discusses where he mentioned the non-Jew's name.
Rather, the beginning of the loan must be for the need of the non-Jew. Meaning, he should say that he borrows for the need of the non-Jew; even though it is known that he borrows for the non-Jew, he should not say that he will repay principal and ribbit. So it is explicit in responsum Rashba: even if he borrowed it for the need of the non-Jew, when Shimon said that he would repay the principal and ribbit, it emerges that Shimon is the borrower and Reuven the lender. Although the collateral belongs to the non-Jew, Reuven claims from Shimon and Shimon is liable to him; it is close in my eyes that one who speaks this way creates fixed ribbit even if the collateral belongs to the non-Jew, for it is as if Shimon borrowed from Reuven with ribbit and lent to the non-Jew, end quote. Darkei Moshe brings it. Although it is permitted for a Jew even to be a kablan guarantor for a non-Jew where there is collateral, as below in siman 170 seif 2 in the gloss, there it is different because the beginning of the loan was to the non-Jew. However, I have seen that in many places the Jew says at the time of the loan that he will repay principal and ribbit. It appears they hold that this is specifically where the collateral is not good enough to lend on for the amount of the loan, such that it would be worth the principal and ribbit that accrue even if it stands a long time; then it appears that the lender relies on the Jew, and in such a case it is forbidden to say so. In such a case I have indeed seen the practice that the Jew gives the lender a note upon himself in a permitted manner as security. But where the collateral is good, the Jew's words do not add or subtract; since he lends for the need of the non-Jew, there is no concern. Nevertheless one must be careful in the matter.
