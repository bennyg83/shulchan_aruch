It is the trickery of the wicked. To make a permit because the collateral belongs to the non-Jew — and it does not help, since the Jew fixed interest for him. And Bach wrote that it is already explained: if he said about the collaterals that they belong to the non-Jew, even though he gives interest it is permitted. And it seems to me specifically when he said to him, this collateral of the non-Jew — lend on it thus and thus, and when the non-Jew comes to redeem it take such-and-such interest — we say by default he intended for permission and he removes himself from the collateral. But if he said to him, lend me money with interest on these collaterals that belong to the non-Jew — certainly it is fixed ribbit, for he fixed interest with him whether the non-Jew gives interest or not; what difference does it make if the collateral belonged to the non-Jew, since the Jew fixed interest for him. And obviously in such a case they did not practice leniency. And even though below siman 170 we say it is permitted for a Jew to be guarantor where there is collateral — here, where the Jew lent first for his own need, it is forbidden. However, all this is when the collateral was removed to the Jew; but when it is in the non-Jew's possession and he did not say from now — in every case permitted — end quote Siftei Kohen. And Derishah wrote that if the first Jew lent on the collateral one hundred and wants to pledge it in the second Jew's hand for fifty — then the collateral is still in his possession and he returns and borrows on it for his need — forbidden. And Taz wrote he was not precise in this, for Tosafot explicitly wrote there is no distinction — see there (and Nachalat Yaakov agreed with Derishah — see there).
