He placed them upon the ground and you are discharged, etc. This is the wording of the Gemara and all the poskim. The Mechaber follows his own view in the Beit Yosef, where he writes that since Rambam and the Tur did not mention the words 'and you are discharged,' it follows that the arrangement is permitted in every manner. It appears to me, however, that the words 'and you are discharged' are essential. Moreover, even if the creditor says, 'Throw me my debt,' the money remains the borrower's responsibility until the creditor says, 'and you are discharged,' as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 120. See there. There is no proof at all from Rambam, because Rambam does not mention this law of placing the money on the ground at all. The Gemara asks, 'What does this teach? It is obvious,' and therefore establishes the case differently, namely that the person actually took and gave the money by hand. For that reason Rambam omitted the interpretation of 'place it upon the ground and be discharged,' because it was rejected as obvious. On the contrary, if Rambam held that the words 'and you are discharged' were not essential, he should have written the case in order to teach precisely that point. Nor is there proof from the Tur, because his latter clause refers back to the first clause. In the first clause, when the non-Jew tells him, 'Give it to this Jew and be discharged,' it is forbidden. The latter clause says that if instead he tells him, 'Place it upon the ground,' meaning 'place it upon the ground and be discharged,' it is permitted. Furthermore, I found an old Tur in which the words 'and be discharged' were written, and it is obvious in my opinion that the words dropped out of the other copies. I later saw that the Bach attempted to prove from the Gemara that 'and be discharged' is not essential. His words are astonishing. His argument that the Gemara did not establish the case where he merely said, 'Place it upon the ground,' proves the opposite, for such a case is obviously forbidden. His further argument from an a fortiori inference is likewise refutable: where the person actually took and gave the money by hand, the first Jew has withdrawn from the transaction and the money is in the non-Jew's possession; but where he merely placed it on the ground, perhaps the non-Jew means, 'Place it there, but it remains in your responsibility.'
