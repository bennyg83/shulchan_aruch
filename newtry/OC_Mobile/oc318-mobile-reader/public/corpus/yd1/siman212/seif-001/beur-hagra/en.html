One who vows, etc. — Rambam's language; and he explains it refers to actual "I vow"; and Raavad brought proof from chapter 12 of Laws of Shevuot from Yerushalmi that Rosh brings there: in Torah it is permitted like the sanctity of Torah — meaning as written, his intent is on the scrolls that were sanctified through the letters; and here there is no vowed matter in "something in which prohibition is written" like sacrifices written therein.
“For he is.” He said, "In the midst of such sacrifices," he said, "but in the midst of the Lord's words," he said:
But one who takes an oath, etc. That is the view of Raavad, that the Gemara deals only with one who vows — as above; but Rambam's view is that the same law applies to one who takes an oath — as written there; and the proof is that in the Tosefta it is taught regarding an oath. Kesef Mishneh. But in our Tosefta in chapter 1 of Nedarim it is taught regarding vows; and the view of Ran and Rosh is [that it applies] specifically to oaths; and nonetheless Beit Yosef wrote that one should not be lenient regarding an oath, for the Geonim were very stringent regarding an oath — as stated above siman 203 seif 3. And see above siman 205 seif 1 in the gloss: 'some say,' etc. — and accordingly one should not be lenient even regarding vows; and even according to the interpretation of Ran and Rosh, in distinguishing between a Torah scholar and an unlearned person, they admit there is no distinction — for the Gemara cites it regarding the vows in the mishnah, and there is only a distinction regarding 'in it' and 'what is written in it' for a Torah scholar. And nonetheless Rambam's ruling is true — for it pertains to vows, and the Yerushalmi can only be said regarding vows; but it is difficult for Rambam, since the Gemara takes the reason of scrolls and divine names — and therefore the Gemara deals with an oath and the Yerushalmi deals with vows; and likewise Rambam in Laws of Oaths wrote the language of the Gemara, and here he wrote the reason of the Yerushalmi; and it is not specifically sacrificial offerings — but the same applies to any prohibition that is vowed, such as the treasury contribution and the like; and this is what is written 'that contains a prohibition' — meaning the prohibition that is vowed; and despite all this, Ran and Raavad forced themselves to interpret even the Gemara as dealing with one who vows — as above.
