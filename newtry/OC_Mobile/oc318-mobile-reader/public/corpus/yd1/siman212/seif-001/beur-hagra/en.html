One who vows, etc. This is Rambam's language, and he explains that it refers to one who actually makes a vow. Raavad brought proof for this in chapter 12 of the Laws of Oaths from the Jerusalem Talmud cited there by Rosh: If one says, "By the Torah," it is permitted, for this refers to the sanctity of the Torah. This means, as stated above, that his intent is directed toward the parchment sheets, which became sanctified through the letters, and these are not an object whose prohibition was created by a vow. If he says, "By what is written in it," it is forbidden, like the offerings written in it.
For, etc. The Jerusalem Talmud states that the reason is the offerings, as explained above. The Babylonian Talmud, however, says that the reason is the Divine Names. This is also Raavad's version of the Jerusalem Talmud.
But one who swears, etc. This is Raavad's opinion, namely, that the Talmud discusses only one who vows, as explained above. Rambam, however, maintains that the same law applies to one who swears, as explained there. A proof is that the Tosefta teaches this law with respect to an oath. Thus writes Kesef Mishneh. In our text of the Tosefta, in the first chapter of Nedarim, it is taught with respect to vows. Ran and Rosh maintain that it applies specifically to oaths. Nevertheless, Beit Yosef wrote that one should not rule leniently regarding an oath, since the Geonim were exceedingly stringent concerning oaths, as stated above in siman 203, se'if 3. See also siman 205, se'if 1, in the gloss: "Some say," etc. According to this, one should not rule leniently even with respect to vows. Even according to Ran and Rosh's explanation distinguishing between a Torah scholar and an am ha'aretz, it must be conceded that there is no distinction in this respect, for the Talmud cites this teaching regarding the vows of the Mishnah. The distinction for a Torah scholar concerns only the phrases "by it" and "by what is written in it." Nevertheless, Rambam's ruling is correct in referring to vows, for the Jerusalem Talmud can only be understood as discussing vows. A difficulty remains according to Rambam: the Babylonian Talmud gives the reason of the parchment sheets and Divine Names, and it must therefore be discussing an oath, while the Jerusalem Talmud discusses vows. Likewise, in the Laws of Oaths Rambam wrote the language of the Babylonian Talmud, while here he wrote the reason given by the Jerusalem Talmud. The reference is not specifically to offerings, but to any object whose prohibition was created by a vow, such as the contribution to the Temple treasury and the like. This is what Siftei Kohen means by "prohibition," namely, a prohibition created by a vow. Despite all this, Ran and Raavad strained to explain the Babylonian Talmud as discussing one who vows as well, as explained above.
