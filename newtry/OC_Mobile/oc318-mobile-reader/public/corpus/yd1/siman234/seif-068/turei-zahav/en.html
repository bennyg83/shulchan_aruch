She forbade upon herself relations with every man. The Tur writes: If she said, “My relations are a konam upon the entire world,” etc. The Beit Yosef objects that it should have said the reverse, “The relations of the entire world are a konam upon me,” because “my relations are a konam” is ineffective while she is obligated to her husband. He also objects to the Tur’s conclusion that after divorce she becomes forbidden even to him, since matters between husband and wife remain permitted after divorce until she marries another. The Maharshal answers the second question: That rule applies only where the vow concerns the husband alone. Here, she forbade the entire world and he is merely included among them. His annulment therefore removes his portion only while she is married to him. Upon divorce he becomes like every other man. The Perishah and my revered father-in-law write that this also answers the first question, because the inclusive nature of the vow allows it to take effect despite her obligation. I say they were not precise. Inclusion does not help against his existing right. Nedarim 89a asks how a woman can consecrate something not yet in existence. Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua answers that she says, “My hands shall be consecrated to their Maker,” because her hands exist. The Gemara then asks how they can be consecrated when they are obligated to her husband, and answers that she says, “when I am divorced.” Her statement concerning her hands is fully inclusive, preventing everyone from benefiting from her handiwork, yet the Gemara still asks from the husband’s lien. Even according to the Beit Yosef, who explains “my hands shall be consecrated” as meaning that what she produces for him shall be prohibited like hekdesh, the Rambam’s words prove the point. The Beit Yosef cites him: If she says, “My hands shall be consecrated to their Maker,” or vows that he shall not benefit from her handiwork, he is not forbidden because her handiwork is pledged to him. Thus, even this inclusive formulation does not defeat his lien. Further proof is Nedarim 81a: If she says, “I will not have marital relations,” he should annul. The Gemara asks that if she means, “the benefit of my relations is forbidden to you,” she is obligated to him. She used an inclusive expression prohibiting her relations to the entire world, yet the vow is ineffective regarding him. Had inclusion defeated the lien, the Gemara should have answered so. It is therefore clear that inclusive language does not nullify his right. The Maharshal intentionally offered his answer only to the Beit Yosef’s second question, not the first. The Shulchan Aruch properly avoided copying the Tur’s exact wording. It is obvious that the Tur also did not mean that she necessarily said, “My relations are a konam.” He used that only as shorthand, meaning that she spoke in a manner that prohibited the benefit of all men’s relations upon herself. The Rosh and Ran explicitly describe it that way.
He annuls his portion. One should not ask that seif 65 says a husband is not included among “people.” The Gemara on 83a asks this and answers that here it is evident she intends a permission. Relations with every other man are already forbidden to her, so she necessarily intends her husband.
She is forbidden in relations with every man. This implies even her husband, as the Tur states explicitly. As I wrote nearby in the Maharshal’s name, after the get her husband becomes included among all men, and this is not like ordinary matters between husband and wife. The Mishnah’s wording also proves it: “He shall annul, and she may have relations with him, but she shall be removed from the Jews.” If this were an ordinary matter between them, “and she may have relations with him” would be superfluous, for it would already follow from “he shall annul.” Rather, the Mishnah teaches that permission exists only while she is under him. Then she remains removed from the Jews, including her former husband after divorce. For this reason, the Tur correctly writes in the law cited in seif 65 that he annuls only while she is under him, because afterward he becomes included in the prohibition with everyone else. The Perishah was not precise there, as I wrote.
