Because that is what they told him. Meaning, they explicitly identified the person, so his mistake is like an express statement. This excludes a case where they merely said, 'A woman in your house vowed,' and he assumed it was his daughter. In that case the annulment is effective under seif 33. This follows Ran, Rosh, and Rabbeinu Yerucham. Bach rules that if he actually believed it was his daughter, this too is treated as explicit. Only if they said simply that a woman in his house vowed and he annulled for whoever it was would the statement be unspecified. Since this is a Torah prohibition, one should be stringent, especially because Rambam, Ramban, and Tur appear to treat an unspecified statement like an explicit one. See below, siman 340, seif 24.
She vowed regarding figs, etc. It appears obvious that the annulment should fail if he did not specify the vow. Perishah suggests that specifying the vow is required only so that we know she did not vow in order to separate herself from a prohibition; if so, confusing figs and grapes would not matter for that concern. This is untenable, as explained above in siman 228, seif katan 24, where every vow must be specified. Furthermore, Rambam and Mechaber above in seif 25 write that a husband may annul vows even before hearing them. The matter is therefore simple. Detailed specification is required only for a sage's release, because if the sage knew the vow involved a trace of prohibition he might refuse to release it or use a different regret or opening. A husband, however, may annul even a prohibition-related vow for his wife's benefit. This is also the plain implication of Gittin 35b. No authority states that a husband must hear the detailed vow.
