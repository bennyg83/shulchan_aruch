And some say, etc. Beit Yosef and Rama abbreviated this matter greatly, and according to their formulation it is difficult, because below in seif 69 a husband may annul even when the vow depends upon an act. Rabbeinu Yerucham's full explanation is precise. Some explain that a father or husband may annul before a vow takes effect only when its condition itself involves personal affliction or a matter between husband and wife, such as, 'I will derive no benefit from my father or your father if I act according to you,' or, 'I am forbidden to all Jews if I have relations with you.' If she made a vow of affliction depend upon an unrelated matter, such as, 'Wine is forbidden to me if I go to a certain place,' he cannot annul until the vow takes effect. This differs from 'Wine is forbidden to me after thirty days,' because there it takes effect automatically. Rabbeinu Yonah writes this in the name of his teachers. Rambam, Semak, Semag, and Tur appear to disagree and allow annulment before she goes. Nevertheless, one should be stringent in a Torah prohibition. Rabbeinu Yerucham himself cites Rambam before presenting the dissenting explanation. Moreover, as written below in seif katan 84, Tur may also agree. The present example may therefore refer to travel that itself entails affliction. Ran similarly proves that advance annulment applies only where she cannot avoid the affliction or marital consequence even by observing the condition, or where the condition is something she is likely to violate and difficult to avoid. Where the condition is unrelated, can be kept, and involves neither affliction nor marital matters, he cannot annul beforehand. Although Rabbeinu Yonah may not distinguish a condition she is likely to violate, Ran's leniency may be followed, especially since Rambam and other authorities appear to permit advance annulment in all cases.
