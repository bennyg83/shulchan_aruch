The father cannot annul, etc. Although we say that when the husband dies his authority returns to the father, this case is different. Since the father already annulled his portion, the husband’s remaining portion became weakened and no longer has sufficient force to be inherited by the father.
After the day of hearing. This means that the husband died after the father’s day of hearing. If he died on the father’s day of hearing, the father’s earlier partial annulment is treated as nothing, because he can now annul the entire vow. The beginning of the annulment on that same day does not harm, for all annulments of that day are considered one. This appears to be the reasoning of the law, which the Tur cites in the Ramban’s name.
And if she returned. The Tur cites the Rambam: A betrothed maiden vowed, her father alone heard and annulled, and the betrothed man died before hearing. If she became betrothed again on that same day, even to one hundred successive men, her father and the final betrothed man may annul the vows she made before the first betrothed man, who died before hearing. It appears that “on that same day” refers to everything preceding it, meaning that the first betrothed man also died on the day the father heard. Otherwise, even if the first remained alive, he could not annul, according to the Rambam’s ruling at the beginning of the siman and seif 4, that both must hear on the same day. One should not ask why the latter betrothed man’s partnership is needed, since according to the Ramban cited nearby the father alone should suffice. This case is different. Since a later betrothed man now exists, the father must join with him. Only where no betrothed man exists on the father’s day of hearing may the father annul alone. It appears that the Rambam agrees with the Ramban, and the Tur cited the Rambam only to question his ruling, not to dispute the Ramban. It is therefore extremely difficult that the Shulchan Aruch here states in the Rambam’s name that even if the first betrothed man died after the father’s day of hearing, the father and second betrothed man may annul. Why should the second be stronger than the first? The Derishah answers that immediately upon the first man’s death, before the second betrothal, the husband’s portion returned to the father. Although the father could not annul it alone, the day of the father’s hearing now became a day of hearing for the husband’s portion as well. If she becomes betrothed again that day, the father and second man share the same day of hearing. This differs from the first man’s lifetime, when the father had no authority over his portion. I do not understand even the beginning of this reasoning. How can the first man transmit to the father a right he never possessed because he did not hear on the father’s day? How can the successor be stronger than the one through whom he comes? At most we can say that the second stands in the place of the first, as the Ran explains, but not that he is stronger. The Shulchan Aruch’s intent requires further study.
And there are those who disagree, etc. Their reason is that the husband’s portion was already weakened by the father’s annulment, so the later betrothed man’s annulment cannot help. The first opinion, the Rambam, holds that the final betrothed man stands in the place of the first as though the first were still alive and may annul together with the father. So wrote the Ran.
