And the husband did not hear. If he heard, the divorce is treated as though he confirmed the vow, because he divorced her. Seif 20 leaves uncertain whether divorce is equivalent to confirmation or to silence. In either event, it is obvious that the vow could not later be annulled.
And afterward he betrothed her. All the more so if he married her, he cannot annul a vow she had made before she left his authority, for a husband does not annul earlier vows. With a betrothed woman, we ruled above that he may annul earlier vows together with the father. Here, however, he cannot do so even together with the father, because when she made the vow she was not capable of annulment, having then been married. She can never again enter a state in which the father can join with him regarding that vow. The same applies to a vow she now makes while betrothed, for the father’s partnership is ineffective for the same reason. Once the father has no authority over an earlier vow because she had been married, he likewise has no later authority. This is clear according to the correction I made nearby. The Beit Yosef attempted to infer from the Tur’s mention of the earlier vow that a vow made now could be annulled with the father. He entered into strained explanations, saying the case concerns only delivery to the husband’s agents according to the Tur and not actual chuppah. In my view, the Tur agrees with what I have written. My revered father-in-law also explains the case as mere entry into the husband’s authority and therefore permits the father to join regarding later vows. In my opinion, delivery to the husband’s agents or bringing her into his authority entirely removes her from the father’s authority forever. Only as a stringency do we say that the husband should not annul vows made after delivery until after chuppah, as I explained above.
