Whether after hearing. The Tur rules this and adds that the Rambam says the final betrothed man cannot annul with the father vows made before the first betrothed man unless the first had not heard before his death. If he heard and died, the latter cannot annul. The Tur rejects this. His difficulty is from Shmuel on 71a, who explains the Mishnah, “the final betrothed man annuls together with the father,” to include even vows presented to the first betrothed man, meaning that he had heard them. The Beit Yosef answers that the Rambam agrees and that his condition, “since the first did not hear,” refers to the case in seif 19 involving divorce, where we are uncertain whether divorce is confirmation. This does not fully resolve the matter, for regarding death too the Rambam explicitly writes “provided the first did not hear,” as I copied in seif 16. My revered father-in-law explains that the Rambam distinguishes. If the father first annulled his portion, thereby weakening the first betrothed man’s portion, the first must not have heard. If the father did not annul first, then even if the first man heard, the father and latter man may annul. This does not appear correct. First, what source would the Rambam have for such a distinction? Furthermore, Scripture disproves it. Shmuel derives from “and if she shall be” that the latter stands in the place of the first, and from the additional word “upon her” that even vows presented to the first may be annulled by the father and latter man. If a case where the father had already annulled were not included in “upon her,” it would likewise not be included in “and if she shall be,” upon which “upon her” depends. Then the latter would not stand in the first’s place at all. It appears simple that the Rambam does not rule like Shmuel in this respect. The Gemara asks whether divorce is confirmation or silence. It does not resolve the question from the Mishnah that the latter betrothed man annuls together with the father. If divorce were confirmation, how could the latter annul what the first confirmed? The Gemara dismisses this by establishing the Mishnah where the first did not hear, and the question remains unresolved. If Shmuel’s interpretation that the Mishnah includes vows heard by the first were accepted as halacha, the question would be resolved and that dismissal would fall away. Thus, the Rambam, who rules that the question remains unresolved, does not rule like Shmuel. The Ran cites this proof for the Rashba, who holds that Shmuel resolves the question, whereas the Ramban holds it remains unresolved. Accordingly, the Rambam likewise does not follow Shmuel. This creates a difficulty for the Tur, who also holds the question unresolved yet rules like Shmuel that even vows heard by the first may be annulled by the father and latter man. It appears that Shmuel’s statement is not halacha regarding divorce, where the first must not have heard. Regarding death, however, we may rule like him even if the first heard. This may be the Tur’s view, though it requires study. One should not challenge the Rambam from his ruling that if the first betrothed man heard and annulled, then died, and afterward the father heard and she became betrothed that same day, the father and latter man may annul. There, the first heard and completed his own role by annulling. The vow is therefore not called one merely “presented” to the first, for he removed himself and did what he could. Ideally, the father alone should annul after the first’s death, because the husband’s authority returned to him. Since a latter betrothed man now exists, the father must join with him. This differs from a vow the first heard and silently left pending until death. His failure to annul resembles explicit confirmation, unless Shmuel’s derivation from “upon her” teaches otherwise, and the Rambam does not accept that derivation for the reason explained. Therefore, practical halacha should follow the Rambam stringently, that the father and latter betrothed man may annul only if the first had not heard.
