Lest he divorce her, etc. The Perishah asks why we do not say the same in seif 67, where she says, “The benefit of my relations is forbidden to you,” and no annulment is required. Why not fear that he may divorce her and the vow will take effect? The same difficulty applies in seif 73, where she vows not to wash, etc. He answers that the same law truly applies there, but those cases concern a husband unconcerned with what will happen after divorce, whereas the Tur mentions the concern here because the Gemara states it here. This does not satisfy me. Nedarim 81a states, “If she vows not to have relations, he shall annul,” and asks that if she said, “The benefit of my relations is forbidden to you,” why is annulment needed, since she is obligated to him? This implies no annulment is needed at all. It therefore appears that divorce allows the vow to take effect only concerning her handiwork. Nedarim 86a asks regarding, “My hands shall be consecrated to their Maker,” which is effective after divorce, that she has no power to cause her own divorce. Rav Ashi answers that konamos are like bodily sanctity, and bodily sanctity removes a lien. The Ran explains: If one made an ox an apotiki for a creditor and afterward consecrated it for the altar, that sanctity removes the creditor’s lien, and he collects elsewhere. The animal is fit for an offering and is not stolen, because the creditor did not own it, but merely had collection rights. Thus, sanctity removes only a lien that is merely for collection. Her handiwork likewise is not owned by the husband, but merely pledged to him. She could compensate him with money instead of performing the work, and she can say, “I will not be supported and will not work.” Hekdesh therefore removes that lien, and so does konam. Her body, however, is obligated to her husband for marital relations, washing his face, hands, and feet, duties rooted in affection from which she cannot withdraw through payment. Hekdesh or konam does not remove such an obligation. Tosafos in Gittin 40a likewise writes that if a damaging ox is consecrated, the consecration is not effective because the owner cannot discharge the lien with money, and sanctity does not remove such a lien. All the more so with bodily marital obligations that cannot be discharged monetarily. Therefore, even after divorce the vow does not take effect. Since she cannot control her divorce, it concerned something not yet in existence. The Gemara prohibits only her handiwork, where by Torah law the prohibition already begins during marriage because hekdesh removes the lien, though the Sages strengthened the husband’s right. It therefore becomes effective at least after divorce. A bodily obligation is not removed at all during marriage, and therefore also cannot become effective after divorce. This differs from seif 64 according to the Tur, where the Rosh says that after divorce she is forbidden to her husband. There she included him with all other men, and the prohibition begins now regarding everyone else. He alone is not included until after the get, as explained in seif 69. This is why the Gemara and poskim mention “lest he divorce her” only regarding her handiwork. So it appears to me.
Specifically if she said, “They shall be consecrated,” etc. The Tur writes this, because otherwise she cannot prohibit handiwork not yet in existence. In siman 239, the Beit Yosef cites the Ran that she need not use this exact phrase, but must mention her hands, which presently exist. Here the Beit Yosef cites the Rambam that even if she vows that he shall not benefit from her handiwork, the law is the same as saying, “My hands shall be consecrated to their Maker,” and gives a sound reason. He rules this way in Even HaEzer, siman 81, unlike the Rama’s ruling here.
