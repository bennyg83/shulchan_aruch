She does not return to the father’s authority. The Tur’s text reads, “except regarding vows she will make after the husband’s death,” which is extremely difficult. If she actually entered chuppah, it is obvious that she left the father’s authority entirely, as the Mishnah states that a widow from marriage must uphold her vows. Even if, as the Beit Yosef first attempted, the Tur refers only to the second case, where the father delivered her to the husband’s agents, following the Tur’s view that this is not fully equivalent to chuppah, the difficulty remains. The Tur first mentions both actual chuppah and delivery, so how could his conclusion refer only to one? Moreover, Kesubos 49a expounds, “The vow of a widow or divorced woman, everything she prohibited upon herself shall stand.” Why is this verse necessary, since she is outside both the father’s and husband’s authority? It refers to a father who delivered her to the husband’s agents and she became widowed or divorced on the road. Is she then called in her father’s house or her husband’s house? The verse teaches that once she left the father’s authority for one moment, he can never annul. Rashi explains that the Torah previously described a vow in the father’s house and in the husband’s house. The verse teaches that a woman delivered to the husband’s agents is no longer called in her father’s house but in her husband’s house. The husband’s authority does not return to the father at the husband’s death. Tosafos writes that according to Rashi the verse seems needed merely to determine whose authority she is under on the road, but this is already learned elsewhere. Rather, the verse is needed to teach that she does not return to her father after widowhood or divorce. Thus, even after death or divorce she never returns to the father’s authority. Tosafos plainly refers even to vows she makes after the death or divorce, for they use the language “return to the father.” Rashi’s phrase, “the husband’s authority does not return to the father,” concerns vows made during the husband’s lifetime, but Tosafos teaches the additional point concerning later vows. Rashi does not dispute this in law. He too says she is called in her husband’s house, treating her like a fully married woman whose husband’s authority does not transfer to her father. The verse itself says, “the vow of a widow,” implying a vow made while she is a widow. Further proof comes from Nedarim 89a: A married woman vowed, was divorced that day, and her husband took her back that day; he cannot annul. “This is the rule: whenever she entered her own authority for even one moment, he cannot annul.” The Gemara says this includes delivery by the father to the husband’s agents, where the husband cannot annul earlier vows. The Rosh explains that if she vowed, was divorced, and then taken back, there is no annulment because she left the father’s authority and the husband cannot annul earlier vows. The Mishnah thus equates delivery to actual marriage. In both, once she left the father’s authority, the father has no later authority at all. The Beit Yosef therefore correctly concludes that even for vows made after the husband’s death she does not return to the father. The Tur’s words clearly contain a scribal error. The word “even” was replaced by “except,” and the correct reading is, “even regarding vows she will make after the husband’s death.” This follows Tosafos exactly, and the Tur regularly rules like Tosafos. My revered father-in-law relied on Rashi to argue that only vows made on the road before the husband’s death do not return to the father, and he disagreed with the Shulchan Aruch. In my view, Rashi also agrees with the Shulchan Aruch, as proven, and my revered father-in-law overlooked Tosafos. We therefore have only the Shulchan Aruch’s ruling, that she has entirely left the father’s authority. The Tur himself equates delivery to actual chuppah, writing, “If he brought her in and died, or even if he did not actually bring her in but the father delivered her,” etc. My revered father-in-law explains “brought her in” merely as bringing her into his authority, not chuppah. This is incorrect. Everywhere in this siman, and in the Gemara’s phrase, “he brought her in on condition that she had no vows,” “brought her in” means chuppah. If the Tur meant merely entry into his authority, why would he then write, “or even if he did not actually bring her in but the father delivered her”? Delivery itself is entry into his authority. The correct law is therefore as the Shulchan Aruch ruled.
