And her husband annulled for her, etc. Bach writes that although he may initially annul because kiddushin takes effect, the ruling is phrased after the fact because the marriage itself was prohibited. This is difficult, for it should still say that if he married her in a prohibited union he may initially annul. Kesef Mishneh explains Rambam's after-the-fact wording to mean that because the marriage is prohibited, he should not initially annul, since at every moment he is obligated to remove her. Ateret Zekenim states this explicitly, that he should not initially annul because she remains with him unlawfully.
Even if he is obligated to provide her support. This applies in the circumstances explained in Even HaEzer, siman 55. Tur's reference to thirty days after kiddushin concerns a case where she demanded marriage immediately at the time of kiddushin. Tur did not enter into that detail here, because the main laws of support are explained there. This resolves Bach's objection.
And even vows that were upon her before betrothal. The father and the betrothed husband may now annul them jointly.
And some say, etc. Bach writes that we rule stringently. This is implied by the presentation of two opinions without resolution, and also by the fact that Rambam's view is stated anonymously while Ramban's is introduced as 'some say.' See below, seif katan 22.
The betrothed husband cannot annul. A betrothed husband can annul only in partnership with the father. This remains true even if the father dies after the betrothal, as explained below in seif 15.
