In proportion to the amount of his money. This is not comparable to lending a se'ah for a se'ah in siman 162, seif 2, where even a small amount suffices. There, we regard that one drop as though it has become conclusively acquired by the lender in exchange for the drop that he lends him. After he has permissibly borrowed one drop, he has two drops against which he may borrow two drops, and so on indefinitely. Here, however, where it is a sale, this reasoning does not apply. So writes the Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of the chapter HaRibbis in the name of the Rashba. It is implied here that what he lends him is effective as though he gives it to him as a gift. See what I wrote in siman 162, seif 2, and see what we will write, with God's help, in siman 173, seif 7.
It is permitted even if the market price has not been established. Rashi explains that since he possesses it, the buyer acquires it from now, even though he has not performed meshichah. If it rises in price, it rises in his possession, and it is not considered ribbis, since if the seller seeks to retract he is subject to the curse of mi shepara. Although the market price has not been established, the seller is merely selling it to him cheaply.
But if not, it is forbidden, etc. Rashi's wording on this matter is as follows: Nevertheless, where he does not possess it, this is not comparable to a prohibition that has come into his possession. Where the buyer gives him money, he may set a price even though the seller does not possess the produce, because the seller can purchase it with the money he received, since wheat is readily available, etc. This person, however, has nothing with which to purchase it, and it resembles ribbis. End quote. Since Rashi writes that he has nothing with which to purchase it, this implies that he does not even have money. So too, the Rosh writes explicitly that he has neither money nor wheat. The Tur also writes explicitly that he said to him, “I have no money,” etc. This is also implied by the wording of the Gemara, which states: “Give me my money, for I wish to purchase wheat with it.” The other said to him: “I have wheat that I will give you,” etc. Since he said, “I have wheat,” this implies that he was unwilling to give him money, meaning that he had no money with which to pay. Were he to have money, why would he agree to give the wheat later at the higher market price according to the present price, rather than retain the profit for himself? It must therefore be that he presently had no money. Concerning this the Gemara subsequently says that if he has no wheat it is forbidden, since he also had no money. There is a further proof. In the preceding siman, the Tur writes in the name of the Ra'avad that one may not borrow in reliance upon the market price unless the borrower has money, etc. We infer that where he has his own money, this is effective because he can purchase the item, and the same applies here. One should not ask that according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak in siman 162 it is permitted even if the borrower has no money, so why is it forbidden here when he does not possess the wheat? The Rosh answers that there he lent him produce, a se'ah for a se'ah, and it does not appear like ribbis as it does here, where he first lent him money. Thus it appears, in my humble opinion, according to the authorities we have cited, that here it is effective if he has his own money, provided that the market price has been established, just as it is effective if he possesses the wheat. The Beit Yosef, however, writes that Rashi's wording implies to him that here we specifically require that he possess wheat, and not merely money. He likewise understands this from the wording of the Remazim, which mentions only wheat. I have written what appears correct in my humble opinion as a matter of halacha, but not for practical application to rule leniently, since the Beit Yosef is stringent.
