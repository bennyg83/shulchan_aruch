And if through qua. The Lord’s command is made in the darkness:
And if there is oakum in it, etc. It appears that it is not specifically oakum — rather even if there are cracks and nothing at all seals them, it is forbidden; for the Talmud states that regarding Rav's winepress in the last chapter of Avodah Zarah, when cracks were found in it, the rabbis said wiping alone does not suffice. And it appears that this law is derived from there — the novelty taught being that even when the cracks were sealed with oakum it is not enough. And Beit Yosef wrote that the law is derived from the Tur's ruling — which is perplexing. It is not remedied by peeling and wiping. The Tur writes: requires peeling and wiping. Beit Yosef proved this is a scribal error — for the mishnah says of a wooden press according to the Sages: "he shall peel" — meaning afterward he must wipe, as the Rosh and Tur wrote — and then the mishnah says regarding the Sages' ruling: "and if of earthenware, even if he peeled the pitch it is forbidden," implying that peeling and wiping do not help — for if they did, it would be the same as wood; and Beit Yosef proved well. But to settle the Tur's view if this is not a scribal error — it seems to me the Tur holds that in truth wood and earthenware are equal when the vessel was not treaded, in that both require peeling and wiping; and Beit Yosef's difficulty from the mishnah does not arise, for one can say regarding wood that the mishnah comes only to say that in earthenware, even Rabbi agrees with the Sages that peeling and wiping are required; but for the Sages there is truly no difference between them. And the Tur too presents no difficulty — since his intention is that when not treaded, there is no difference between wood and earthenware, but when treaded there is a difference — namely the Tur wrote at the outset when treaded that earthenware requires rinsing only; and the Tur now teaches regarding earthenware that peeling and wiping alone do not help when treaded — which is what Beit Yosef intended in his note on the Tur. But the Tur did not mention that peeling alone suffices for earthenware since peeling is not effective with it, and Beit Yosef's note says: "rather only rinsing, without peeling." Peeling, filling, and rinsing, or scalding. In Derisha he raised a difficulty — from here it implies that scalding is better than rinsing, while in siman 135 at the end the Tur writes in the name of Ramban regarding earthenware: when placed for storage, scalding does not suffice — only rinsing; and Derisha resolved this by making a distinction — see there, where he greatly strained himself. And it seems to me from the outset there is no difficulty: certainly where scalding applies it is better than rinsing; but for earthenware in that passage Ramban holds that scalding does not apply at all, since the Torah testified regarding it that it never fully expels its absorbed taste — as stated regarding earthenware: "that which is cooked in it shall be broken"; and even so the Sages said rinsing helps there — it is stated there that scalding does not penetrate the entire vessel, while rinsing does penetrate it; but in other vessels whose nature allows scalding to penetrate them, it is certainly better than rinsing, since scalding penetrates with boiling liquid while rinsing penetrates only with cold. Therefore according to us Ashkenazim even with earthenware it is so — since we hold that scalding helps for earthenware in siman 135 seif 15; in this prohibition earthenware is like other vessels, and scalding is also better for it. So it seems to me to be straightforward. One can distinguish: if its use began, etc. The Tur's words here need explanation — and we will quote what is relevant: the mishnah states: "a gat of stone — wipe; of wood — peel," according to the Sages; "and of earthenware — even if he peels it is forbidden" — meaning rinsing is required, as the Tur and Shulchan Arukh wrote at the beginning of this siman with all their details. And afterward the Gemara brings that R' Abahu cites a baraita of lentils — that is, the gat, with rinsing — and Rashi's and the Tur's view is that this baraita contradicts the mishnah, and since R' Abahu brings it, it is authoritative; and we rule like him that rinsing suffices. And R' Tam disagrees with Rashi and holds we rule like the mishnah. And the Rosh in his view writes that the baraita's "lentil-gat" does not refer to the gat itself but to something else; and one can further say — even if we explain it as referring to the gat itself — there is no contradiction: the mishnah refers to when its initial use was in the hands of a gentile, and the baraita refers to when initial use was in the hands of a Jew. And Rashba in Torat HaBayit and Ran cite this distinction also in the name of Raavad — that in earthenware one distinguishes between when its initial use involved a prohibition or a permission — and they have another proof for this: from what the Gemara brings — the masher and funnel of a gentile — the specification "of a gentile" implies that specifically when its initial use was in the hands of a gentile and involved a prohibition is rinsing required; but when begun with permission, wiping suffices as Rabbi holds. And regarding the mishnah's contradiction with the baraita, they give the same answer as the Rosh. And therefore Beit Yosef correctly wrote that Rashba only distinguishes between initial use by a gentile or Jew with regard to earthenware, but with wood the law remains as the mishnah for all cases — and not as my father-in-law of blessed memory who wrote against him "God forgive him" — for Rashba is a man whose Master aided him. And the Tur cited the words of Rashi and R' Tam, and afterward wrote in the name of Raavad: "and so too wrote Raavad — that even earthenware and other vessels, if their initial use was in the hands of a Jew, wiping suffices; and of stone, rinsing suffices" — and at first glance this is puzzling: if Raavad's proof for this distinction is from the mishnah vs. baraita contradiction — distinguishing initial use by a Jew for the baraita from initial use by a gentile for the mishnah — why did he rule for stone that rinsing always suffices following the baraita, while the Tur mentions nothing about wood — though if its law changed here for initial use by a Jew, he should have mentioned it too; and if wood still requires peeling, how shall we compare it to earthenware — from our reasoning, neither should require the severe treatment the mishnah assigns? And if Raavad's proof is from the baraita of the masher and funnel — specifying "of a gentile" — from this he learned that initial use by a Jew with permission in earthenware suffices with wiping; but for stone one should say no such distinction is needed there since stone is easy to purify, and what the mishnah says about stone — "wipe" — applies always regardless of initial use; and for wood, perhaps both peeling and wiping are needed as in the mishnah — since it is uncertain what the baraita's "lentil-gat" refers to besides earthenware; but for stone it is clear that in both cases rinsing suffices. However for wood the matter is debated — and since Rashi holds everything follows the baraita, one should not diverge from his ruling and say that wood too suffices with rinsing in case of initial use by a Jew, just as Rabbi's ruling applies to both; and this is why the Tur wrote stone explicitly as clearly sufficing with rinsing, but for wood the dispute between the mishnah and baraita remains. And even though Tur HaZahav wrote this, it is according to the reading that lacks the first version — and therefore does not need Tosafot's resolution; but for us who have that reading it is difficult. And the Shulchan Arukh at the end of this siman ruled that wood and stone suffice with rinsing when their initial use was in the hands of a Jew; and my father-in-law of blessed memory disputed the Shulchan Arukh, holding that wood requires wiping — but according to what I wrote, the Shulchan Arukh's words are correct.
