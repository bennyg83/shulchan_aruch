Intends. If the persecutor intends only his own benefit, one may not be stringent and surrender his life; doing so is considered self-injury, and he must transgress rather than be killed. Rabbeinu Yerucham. Bach writes that even publicly one may not surrender his life in such a case, while Perishah implies that publicly he may. If the needs of the time require it, all agree he may sanctify Hashem's Name. Beit Yosef writes that a great, pious, God-fearing person who sees the generation lax in a matter may surrender his life even for a minor mitzvah so that the people learn to love and fear Hashem. Bach follows Rambam that one who was required to transgress rather than die, but chose death, is culpable for his own life. Other authorities disagree. Terumat HaDeshen writes that ordinarily life-endangering doubt is treated leniently, though sanctification of Hashem's Name may differ. The ruling depends upon the circumstances and the person's intent. Hagahot Semak regards those martyrs who killed themselves because they feared they could not withstand the trial as fully righteous, citing Shaul; others forbid suicide. See Orach Chaim.
A negative commandment. For a positive commandment, even one that will pass, one need not spend more than one-fifth of his property, as Rama writes in Orach Chaim 656. Shach. Shach further questions whether danger to a limb is treated like monetary loss or danger to life; he inclines to leniency.
In the presence of. This does not mean literally before them, but that they know of the transgression. This is proven by the Gemara's discussion of Esther. Shach.
A shoelace. Meaning a Jewish custom in footwear differing from non-Jewish practice, where the difference expresses Jewish identity, such as Jews wearing black straps and non-Jews red ones. Beit Yosef writes that Rambam interprets the law specifically where the act itself violates the prohibition against wearing non-Jewish dress. This rule applies both publicly and during persecution. Bach rules that during persecution one must die rather than transgress even if the persecutor seeks only personal benefit, unlike the case of public coercion. Shach and Taz disagree and invoke the principle that life-endangering doubt is treated leniently. A decree applying throughout the kingdom, with Jews included incidentally, is not considered a time of persecution.
To throw him. In the case of idolatry, this includes forcing a person's body to bow before an idol. Rosh.
To be killed. Rabbeinu Yerucham limits this to a public setting. Shach disagrees and rules that even publicly he need not be killed.
To transgress. This implies that for a rabbinic sexual prohibition all agree he should transgress rather than be killed. Shach.
Before. Such as being forced to lend an object for idolatrous worship or a similar indirect act.
Sexual prohibitions. The discussion concerns an unmarried woman; a married woman involves actual forbidden relations. Accordingly, Esther must have been unmarried. With an unmarried woman, even if she actively brings the act upon herself, this is not included in the cardinal prohibition. A Jewish man who relations with a non-Jewish woman is included in forbidden relations and must die rather than transgress, because zealots may strike him and he is treated like one liable to karet. If saving his life depends upon his independently committing one transgression, and this will bring rescue, it may be permitted. Shach and Taz discuss whether this applies only publicly or even privately. See Even HaEzer, simanim 15 and 16.
Liable. Rambam writes that one should not initially instruct the group to surrender the designated person. Taz explains that the matter is left to the populace. Although Sheva ben Bichri was not liable to death under ordinary Torah law but under royal law for rebellion against David, he could be surrendered when specifically demanded. Likewise, one who dangerously rebels against the government, counterfeits, or engages in other acts endangering Jews may be surrendered, even if not specifically named, because he is like a pursuer. Where surrender is forbidden, one may not surrender him for death, torture, or even monetary punishment, since once in non-Jewish hands they may show no mercy. Darkei Moshe writes that every alternative must first be exhausted.
All of them. Beit Yosef cites Ran that if the persecutors designate one woman, she may be surrendered. Taz writes that this depends on the dispute regarding whether the designated person is independently liable. If they say, 'Give us this woman to violate, or we will kill all of you,' the designation certainly helps, because she performs no act and is not herself commanded to die rather than transgress. She may therefore be surrendered.
