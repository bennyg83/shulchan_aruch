He is permitted. The Bach rules like Rambam that whenever the law is that one must transgress rather than be killed, if he instead allows himself to be killed he is culpable for his own life. The Beit Yosef agrees, and this also appears to be Ramban's view in Toras HaAdam. Hagahos Ashri cites Mahari'ach as uncertain whether a person may choose martyrdom where the law is to transgress. Terumas HaDeshen writes that although danger to life is ordinarily treated leniently in a dispute among the authorities, perhaps sanctification of Hashem's Name is different, since the Torah itself did not insist upon preservation of life in that context. He therefore concludes that instruction depends upon the circumstances and the person's intent. Hagahos Semak writes that the holy people who killed themselves because they did not trust themselves to withstand coercion are complete martyrs, with Shaul as a proof. Orchos Chaim, cited by the Beit Yosef, records a dissenting view that one may not kill oneself.
If the non-Jew intends, etc. But if his purpose is only his own benefit, it is forbidden for the Jew to be stringent; he is considered as injuring himself and must transgress rather than be killed. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that this applies even publicly, although the Prisha indicates that in public one may choose martyrdom. It appears that where the needs of the generation require such conduct, all agree that a great and pious person may give his life, as the Beit Yosef writes even according to Rambam: if he sees the generation breaking through a prohibition, he may sanctify Hashem's Name even over a minor commandment so that the people learn to fear and love Hashem.
And he shall not transgress a negative commandment. But for a positive commandment, even a fleeting commandment, one need not spend more than a fifth, as the Rav wrote in Orach Chaim end of siman 656 — see there. And if there is danger of limb loss, requires study whether it is comparable to money or to life — see Rivash siman 326 and Orach Chaim siman 328 seif 17; and it appears we are lenient.
In the presence of ten Israelites. Specifically; and it does not mean literally before them but that they know of the transgression; and so is proven from the Gemara and poskim regarding "but was not Esther in public" — see there.
A shoe-strap. Rashi explained that the gentile's way was to tie it in such manner while Israel's way was to tie it differently — such as when there is a Jewish side to the matter and Israel's way is to be modest — even this change where there is no commandment here but merely a custom, he sanctifies God's name before his companions; and the Rif explained that in that time the gentiles' sandal straps were red while Israel made them black so as not to wear gentile garments; and Maharik wrote this is as Rashi said — there is a Jewish side to the matter; and Beit Yosef wrote that the Rambam explains specifically in such a case of not wearing gentile garments, which is a negative commandment — see there.
Upon Israel alone. But if the decree is upon the entire state of his kingdom — even if Israelites are included — it is not called a time of persecution.
Even regarding a shoe-strap, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that this also applies to the preceding law of public coercion, so that public coercion and a time of persecution have the same rule. The Bach distinguishes them, but Rashi does not appear to agree, and Hagahos Ashri leaves the matter unresolved. Therefore, because this is a danger-to-life question, one should be lenient.
Or where they wish to throw him down, etc. In a case of idolatry, this includes where they bend his body so that he appears to bow to an idol. Rosh.
He need not be killed. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes: unless it is public. This is difficult, because Tosafos and the Rosh explain regarding Esther that where she is passive, the act is not regarded as a forbidden-relations transgression requiring martyrdom. According to Abaye's answer, passivity exempts her even publicly. It is possible Rabbeinu Yerucham understood Rava to disagree with Abaye, but there is no clear proof of that. The Rif cites both answers, implying both are true in law, and this also appears from the Ran. The Rama likewise seems to follow this approach.
Only an ordinary negative prohibition, etc. The Gemara at the end of Sanhedrin relates the man who became dangerously lovesick for a woman. The Sages ruled that he must die rather than have relations with her, rather than have her stand naked before him, and even rather than speak with her from behind a fence. These latter acts are not actual forbidden relations; rather, they violate 'Do not come near to uncover nakedness.' The Ran and Nimukei Yosef therefore understand that even conduct of affection that falls under this prohibition is included in the rule of martyrdom. This accords with Rambam, Semag, Sefer HaChinuch, and Keter Torah, who hold that affectionate embracing, kissing, and similar acts with forbidden relations violate a Torah prohibition. Ramban disputes this and holds that Torah lashes apply only to intercourse or its beginning, but Megillas Esther defends Rambam. Even Rambam, however, appears to prohibit only embracing or kissing done in a sexual manner, for the Gemara records sages embracing female relatives in circumstances lacking such intent.
A non-Jew who comes upon, etc. But a Jewish man having relations with a non-Jewish woman is included in forbidden relations for purposes of martyrdom, according to Ramban, Ran, Nimukei Yosef, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and the Beit Yosef. Orchos Chaim appears to limit this to a public act, and this also follows from the Rama in Even HaEzer, because the severity stems from the rule that zealots may strike one who cohabits with a non-Jewish woman, which applies publicly. On this approach, when public he must give his life even where the coercer's purpose is personal benefit. Nimukei Yosef, however, writes that since the offender is liable to karet even if zealots do not strike him, he must give his life even in private. See further in Even HaEzer, simanim 15 and 16, for the definition of forbidden relations.
Who comes etc. Such as when she performs an act that brings the forbidden relation upon her — as the Rosh wrote at the beginning of Ketubot and Tosafot at the end of Sorer u'Moreh — for otherwise it would be derivable that she did no act, as explained.
Upon a daughter of Israel. Specifically an unmarried woman; but with a married woman it is actual gilui arayot — there; and so the Magen Avraham wrote; and even though Rabbenu Tam held even a married woman — nevertheless that is not the view of all the poskim.
Unless he is liable to death. And the Rambam wrote: nevertheless we do not instruct them so initially; and Darkei Moshe wrote in the name of Hagahot Maimoniot that one must first try all possible avenues before handing him over; and see in the Bach seif 4 who wrote several distinctions of law in this; and it appeared to me that his words are not compelling, but since he distanced his proof and wrote that in a responsum he clarified his words with clear evidence, I did not challenge him.
