And she pressed it against her thigh, etc. Here too one may say that a louse was on her thigh and was crushed when she pressed the cloth there. The alternative opinion is the Rambam, who is stringent when it was pressed against her thigh. He holds that only beneath a pillow or cushion, in the preceding seif, did the Sages rule leniently when the stain is round and no larger than a bean plus a little, as stated in the Mishnah in the chapter Kol HaYad, but not when it was pressed against her thigh. His reasoning appears to be as follows. If blood is found on the thigh, he follows his view that blood found on her flesh may not be attributed to a louse even when smaller than a bean, as stated above in seif 8 in the name of the alternative opinion, which is likewise the Rambam. If no blood is found on the thigh, that itself proves that the blood on the cloth came from her body, for had it come from a louse crushed against the thigh, blood should also have been found on the thigh. If it came from her body, however, the bloodied portion of the cloth may not have touched the thigh. The Tur writes in the name of the Rosh that he too rules that when she pressed it against her thigh she is impure even for the smallest amount, since he writes that she is impure as a niddah. The Beit Yosef wonders, because the Gemara itself uses that language. It appears that the Tur inferred this from the Rosh's need to state that she is impure as a niddah. In Talmudic times this distinction mattered for laws of ritual purity, but the Rosh generally cites only laws practiced now; what present difference is there whether she is impure as a niddah or because of a stain? He must therefore mean that she is impure even for the smallest amount.
