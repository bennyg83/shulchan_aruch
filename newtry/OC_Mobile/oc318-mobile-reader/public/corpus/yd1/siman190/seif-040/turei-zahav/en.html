She checked the garment, etc. Why was the first examination necessary, since the garment was found clean after she removed it? The Derishah leaves this requiring study. My father-in-law answers that the first examination is mentioned only by way of inference: if the garment was not found clean afterward, the earlier examination would not help. But the Tur later explicitly writes that whenever the two women are equal, both are impure. The Perishah explains that “she was found pure” refers to the woman rather than to the garment, and that because she initially checked the garment and was later found pure, we maintain her prior presumption; although the second woman was likewise found pure after removing it, we apply “here it was found, here it was.” These words are astonishing, for throughout this siman it is obvious that when they are equal we do not apply that rule. The correct explanation is straightforward. An examination after removing the garment is effective only if performed deliberately to ascertain its condition. A casual glance is ineffective, because a person does not pay attention to a matter that is not relevant to him. If she did not inspect the garment before wearing it, she has no reason to examine it carefully after removing it, for even if she finds a stain she remains pure under the preceding seif. Her later examination will therefore be casual and cannot render the second woman impure. Only when she first checked the garment does she have reason to inspect it carefully after removing it, since a newly found stain would render her impure. One cannot say that she examines it carefully because she wishes to lend it to another woman and thereby protect herself if a stain is later found, for then both women would be pure, since the garment was not examined initially and the stain may already have been there. Thus the Rashba, followed by the Tur, specifically states that she checked it first, ensuring that the later examination was deliberate. This appears entirely clear.
