Two women who handled blood, etc. Tosafos in the chapter HaRo'eh, Berachos 58, write in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that the case is where they handled it one after the other. Had the first woman asked before the second woman's case became known, we would have declared her pure because she handled blood; yet because of the second woman we now declare the first impure, although she was otherwise entirely pure. End quote. My father-in-law copied this and wrote that since they come to ask together, both are impure, and that this is implied by Tosafos. His words imply that if they ask one after the other both are pure. This does not appear correct. Had that been so, the Gemara or the poskim should have stated the distinction explicitly, as they did in the first chapter of Pesachim concerning a mouse with chametz in its mouth, a ruling cited in Orach Chaim in the laws of Pesach. Moreover, Tosafos could only have declared the first woman pure if she came before anything was known concerning the second; once both cases are known, both are certainly impure even if they ask one after the other. I found the same in Toras HaBayis HaAroch concerning three women, as cited below in seif 49. The reason is that this case differs from the chametz case. There, when there is doubt whether the mouse entered another house, the house remains permitted; the Sages were stringent only when two houses are involved and one of them must contain chametz, yet they were lenient when the owners ask one after the other because an ordinary doubt is resolved leniently. The same applies to two paths, where each person may be maintained in his original state of purity when he asks separately. Here, however, when a stain cannot be attributed to any cause, the doubt itself renders her impure. The only basis for leniency is a reasonable attribution of the stain to something else. When two women are involved and there is one extra sela-sized amount of blood, there is no greater reason to attribute the impurity to one than to the other, so it is as though there were nothing to which the blood could be attributed. The Derishah also writes that there is no distinction between asking together and asking one after the other, although his reason, that this rule applies only to rabbinic matters whereas this is actual blood and a Torah matter, is incorrect. The principle is used even in Torah matters, as in the cases of two paths and permitting a woman to her husband, because each is maintained in her prior presumption of permission. His further suggestion, that perhaps half a sela of bird blood reached each woman and the remaining half sela came from each woman's source, is also incorrect. If that were so, the Gemara would not have needed to say that there is one extra sela and ask why it should be attributed to one more than the other; it could simply have ruled both impure because each has only half a sela from the bird. Rather, whenever there is a cause before us to which a stain may be attributed, we attribute the entire stain to it and do not say that only part came from that cause and the remainder from the woman. The difficulty therefore returns, why is this unlike chametz? The correct answer is as stated: here it is as though there is no cause of attribution at all. Therefore, even when they ask one after the other, both are impure.
