Rema wrote in his responsum siman 96: he was asked about a stain found on her garment that was larger than a gris, but the woman had reason to attribute it to something else — namely that she suffered from eruptions and wounds oozing blood; and this woman, at the time she found the stain, was in the habit of seeing white and greenish appearances that are pure, and all her garment was soiled with those greenish and white stains; and at the head of one of those stains there was found the reddish appearance around the white stain, in the manner of a liquid spreading on a garment, so that it appeared to the eye that the reddish appearance was the edge of the white stain, and all constituted one stain. He responded: even though we rule leniently in the matter of stains, nevertheless in this case it appears she is tamei — since there is proof that it came from her together with the white stain, for Terumat HaDeshen siman 247 wrote that white and greenish appearances also come from the womb; and if so, since it is known that the womb was open to emit the white appearance, it follows certainly that the reddish appearance also came from there, since it all appears to be one stain. And even if there is only doubt whether all of it is one stain or not, it seems we go to stringency; and the proof is from chapter HaRo'ah Ketam: Rava said — "if one substance is found on her, she may attribute multiple substances to it"; Rashi explains: if something resembling a stain is found on her, such as a collyrium or sap, and afterward she found a stain on her garment, even if its appearance does not resemble that substance, she may attribute it to it; we challenge: "if she handled redness, she may not attribute black to it"; answer: "handling is different" — for since she handled red, how can we attribute black to it? But if a red substance is found on her she may attribute black to it — for we say, just as this substance came upon her without her knowing, so too another substance came upon her without her knowing. An alternative version: handling one substance allows attributing multiple substances to it; we challenge: "if she handled redness, she may not attribute black to it"; answer: this only applies when she handled a chicken that has multiple kinds of blood. We see that if she handled one substance she may attribute multiple kinds of blood to it — for we say, just as this came from elsewhere, so too did others — and we go leniently even though this substance is not identical; all the more so we go stringently to attribute one substance to another, to say just as the white appearance came from the womb, so too the reddish appearance. And from this reason one should not be stringent here according to the first version of Rava, which attributes different substances to each other leniently — all the more so we say so stringently. And even according to the second version, which only attributes multiple kinds where she handled a chicken — nevertheless in our case it resembles handling a chicken, for the womb also has many kinds of blood. End of quote [abbreviated]. And although the words of Rema are cherished and we drink his words thirstily in most places, nevertheless these particular words are not acceptable to me, as I will explain. What he wrote — to be stringent here by saying that just as the white appearance came from the womb so too the reddish appearance, his reasoning being that the womb emits many kinds of blood like a chicken — would mean we should say this even if the reddish appearance were found far from the white appearance: for just as we say regarding one who handled a chicken that wherever she finds a stain on her garment she may attribute it to the chicken's blood — so too here, and this certainly cannot be — for if so, any woman who emitted a pure appearance should make every stain of hers tamei even when there is something to attribute it to. And the Talmud and decisors made no such distinction. And there is an explicit proof from chapter HaIshah She'hayeta Osah Tzerakheiha: mishnah — three women who were sleeping in one bed and blood was found under one of them — all of them are tamei; if one of them examined herself and was found tamei, she is tamei and all the others are pure. And we say on this: Rav said — this applies specifically when she examined herself within the time of her established period for the discovery of blood; therefore we establish this blood as hers and the others are pure. Here before us: the principal reason the others are pure is through the presumption of the one who examined herself and was found tamei — but without this there is no presumption. Yet according to Rema, even if she did not find herself tamei but only found a white or greenish appearance, we should say she has been established as having her womb open — and just as the white came from her, so too the blood found in the bed; whereas the others who have no open womb — and this contradicts the mishnah. And from this another difficulty arises: even if you want to say he was only stringent where the reddish appearance and the white are found specifically in one place — but in separate places there is no such presumption — for in this mishnah we go after the presumption from her finding herself tamei, and stains found in a different place — we would not say; and if without finding herself tamei but only finding a white stain we were to say she has a presumption, even stains found in other places on the garment should be hers — according to his reasoning. And this is certainly not so. And an explicit proof from the mishnah in chapter HaRo'ah Ketam: an incident of a woman who came before R' Akiva; he said to her: "perhaps you had a wound?" She said: "yes, and it healed." He said: "perhaps it could reopen and emit blood?" R' Akiva declared her pure — and if a woman is in the habit of emitting a white appearance, R' Akiva should have asked her whether she sees a white appearance — for we say: just as one who handles a chicken can attribute various bloods to it, so too here. And if you say one need not investigate something that leads to leniency: this certainly contradicts Rema's reasoning that where something appears as one stain we go stringently — similarly here, R' Akiva would have investigated something that leads to stringency; but for him the case is obvious that one may attribute a stain to a wound or ulcer wherever there is something to attribute it to — and then we go leniently, not stringently. And this proves that the decree of the Sages on stains goes leniently — as we say one cannot attribute it to anything unless specifically to something one handled; but when it can be attributed to a wound or a lesion, we always go leniently. And this is what is clarified at the end of chapter HaRo'ah Ketam: "Ravina said — not to be lenient about biblical law but to be stringent about biblical law; and stains themselves are rabbinic" — Rashi explains: the Sages decreed stringency about stains regarding biblical law, so as not to be lenient about niddah itself; however, stains themselves are rabbinic, and we go leniently with them and attribute them to anything — end of quote. And for this reason one should not be stringent, even according to the first version of Rava — that we attribute one substance to another leniently — all the more so we would do so stringently; for the whole point of not going stringently is because the decree of the Sages about stains was only as stringency on biblical law, not to extend further than biblical law, and their decree was to be lenient about attribution to anything possible; but where there is nothing to attribute — there we certainly go stringently. Only in this specific case that Rema was asked about — where the reddish appearance appears to the eye as the edge of the white stain and they all appear as one stain — there she is certainly tamei; but otherwise, merely that the white appearance was found on the garment and the reddish was found elsewhere — one cannot say from this alone that the womb was opened to redness since it was opened to white. And in my humble view, even in that same case Rema was asked about: if the white stain covers over the reddish appearance and it is possible to attribute the redness to something else — she is certainly pure; but if it is not possible to attribute the red to anything else and it is clearly visible as being at the edge of the white — then she is tamei. So it seems to me.
