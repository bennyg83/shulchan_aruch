It appears that only concerning a checked cloth, where the Gemara says that she is impure as a niddah, do we regard the finding as an actual sighting for every purpose. But an unexamined cloth, although it also renders her forbidden as stated in seif 36, does not render her a niddah; she is only impure because of doubt, like an ordinary stain. It is therefore not treated as an actual sighting for establishing a fixed period. This also supports what I wrote in seif 38, that blood on an unexamined cloth is not Torah-level impurity even when she wiped herself. The Maharshal copied a responsum of Maharam: “Concerning a woman afflicted with boils, it is obvious that her stains are pure. Even if she did not examine herself at the time of her fixed period and later examined and found stains on her sheet or garment, she is pure; all the more so during her clean days, when we attribute leniently.” End quote. Her status during the first three clean days will be explained, with God's help, in siman 196, seif 10.
The Rama wrote in responsum 96 concerning a stain larger than a bean plus a little found on a woman's garment, where she had a possible attribution because she suffered from scabs and blood-filled wounds. At the time she found the stain she commonly discharged permitted white and green appearances, and her garment was soiled with such marks. At the edge of one of them a red appearance surrounded the white mark like moisture spreading through cloth, making it appear that the redness was the border of the white mark and that both formed one stain. The Rama ruled that although stains are generally treated leniently, she was impure, because the appearance indicated that the red came from her together with the white. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 247, states that white and green appearances also come from the source. Once the source had opened to discharge the white, the adjoining red presumably emerged from there as well. Even if it were only doubtful whether they formed one stain, the doubt should be resolved stringently. He supported this from the Gemara's rule that when one staining substance is found on her, she may attribute several colors to it. Rashi explains that if something capable of producing a stain, such as eye salve or resin, was found on her, and she later found a stain on her garment, she may attribute it to that substance even when the colors differ. The Gemara's second version limits this to handling a chicken, which contains several kinds of blood. The Rama reasoned that if different colors are associated leniently, all the more should they be associated stringently; and the source resembles a chicken because many types of blood emerge from it. End of his words in brief. Although the Rama's words are precious to us and we generally drink them thirstily, this ruling is not accepted by us. If the opening of the source for a white appearance established that any red stain came from it, then even red found far from the white should be impure, just as blood from a handled chicken may be attributed wherever it is found on the garment. That is certainly false, for it would mean that whenever a woman discharged a permitted appearance, every later stain would be impure even where there is another valid attribution, a distinction never made by the Gemara or the poskim. The Mishnah concerning three women sleeping in one bed proves the contrary. When blood is found beneath one, all are impure; if one immediately examines herself and is found impure, she alone is impure and the others are pure. Their purity rests on the presumption created by her actual impure finding. According to the Rama, even if she found only a white or green appearance, we should say that her source was open and attribute the bed's blood to her rather than to the others, yet the Mishnah says no such thing. Thus a permitted appearance creates no presumption regarding an impure one. The Mishnah concerning the woman who asked R' Akiva about a stain provides another proof. He asked whether she had a wound that could reopen and bleed, and purified her on that basis. Had a customary white discharge prevented attribution to a wound, he should also have asked whether she saw a white or other permitted appearance. One cannot say that such investigation was unnecessary, for the Rama's logic is that a rule used leniently should all the more be used stringently. Rather, where a stain can be attributed to a wound or boils we rule leniently. Even an actual sighting may be attributed to a wound in that place, and certainly a stain on a garment may be. The Gemara's statement that one may repeat an experiment stringently, cited above in seif 32, applies only where the blood cannot be attributed to anything other than the woman and the sole basis for permission is that she was not known to have passed that place. There the Sages required reenactment because stains were decreed stringently when no attribution exists. Ravina states at the end of the chapter HaRo'eh Kesem that the decree was made to reinforce Torah law, but stains themselves are rabbinic and are treated leniently by attributing them to any plausible cause. For the same reason, even according to Rava's first version, where one found substance allows attribution of several colors, we may not reverse that leniency into a stringency. The Sages specifically established leniency in attribution whenever possible. The Rama's comparison to a chicken is also difficult. A chicken actually contains red slaughter blood, black limb blood, and saffron-colored intestinal blood, as the Tur explains. Therefore one who handled a chicken may attribute all three colors to it. But if she handled only saffron-colored intestinal blood, she certainly could not attribute red blood to it. Why, then, should a woman who discharged a permitted color be presumed also to have discharged an impure color? Were that so, every permitted appearance would itself render her impure, lest an impure drop was covered by it, as we fear after first marital relations that semen may cover a drop of blood. There, however, the Beit Yosef explains that most virgins have virginal blood. No comparable presumption exists here. Opening of the source for a permitted appearance establishes nothing concerning a red appearance. The red therefore remains an ordinary stain and is treated leniently when there is another cause to which it may be attributed. Only in the precise case asked of the Rama, where the eye could see that the red was the edge of the permitted mark, spreading around it like a line, is she certainly impure. Otherwise, if the permitted appearance merely lies over or passes across the red, and the red can be attributed to another cause, it retains the law of an ordinary stain. We say that the red was already there from another source, and she is pure. This appears correct in my humble opinion.
