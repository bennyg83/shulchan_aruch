If she killed a bedbug. This is called a wanze in the vernacular.
Or she smelled its odor, etc. Without this, we do not attribute the stain to a bedbug. The Rosh, as cited by the Beit Yosef, writes that ordinarily we do not attribute it to a bedbug because bedbugs are uncommon. This implies that in places where they are common, we do attribute stains to them, just as we do to a louse. The Rashba states this explicitly, as cited by the Beit Yosef, ruling that in such a place she may attribute a stain to a bedbug up to the size of a lupine bean. The Beit Yosef, however, interpreted the Rosh's words differently, explaining that “uncommon” means that it is uncommon for the bedbug to be so near her body without her smelling it. He later wrote that this interpretation can be rejected. He also cited the Maggid Mishneh, who brought the Rashba's words and wrote that the Ramban and Rambam did not distinguish between places where bedbugs are common and places where they are not. It is possible that they hold that even where they are common, we do not attribute the stain to them, since they have an odor, and we do not say that perhaps she smelled it without noticing. As a matter of practical halacha, since the Rashba explicitly rules leniently, the plain meaning of the Rosh agrees, and the Darkei Moshe likewise cites Rabbeinu Yerucham that where bedbugs are common stains are attributed to them, and since in matters of stains we follow the lenient view, it appears that where bedbugs are very common one may attribute stains to them up to the size of a lupine bean. A lupine is a round legume about the width of a small coin. The Beit Yosef explains that where a stain may be attributed to a bedbug, there is no distinction whether its length equals its width.
