And if she knows she was upside-down. That is, her feet were raised upward and her head was downward. Therefore she is impure wherever the stain is found. From the language of the author and the Tur who wrote "wherever," it implies that even if found on her thigh and on the outer side of her feet she is also impure. But in truth this goes against reason — for what of it that she was upside-down; how is it possible for the blood to reach the outer side? The nature of blood is to flow downward when she is upside-down, as the Rashba himself wrote, as explained in the Beis Yosef. And in the Talmud, the chapter of "One who sees a stain," it only states that even above the belt she is impure. I subsequently found in the source of the law in the language of the Rashba in Toras HaBayis, section seven, gate four, where he wrote: "When is this said? When she was not upside-down. But if she is certain that she was upside-down and raised her feet upward and her head downward, then above becomes like below, and she is concerned about a stain found on her body — whether on her back, whether on her stomach, whether on her garment." Thus far his words. It is clearly evident from his words that only above becomes like below, and she is only concerned regarding areas opposite the source — on her back and on her stomach; but from the sides toward the outer area, there is no concern. According to this, the words of the author and the Tur are to be explained similarly, and what is stated "even above the belt," etc. — it is the explanation of "wherever." This is straightforward. This is also clear from the language of the Raavad in Sefer Baal HaNefesh page 59 — see there.
