To protect her from stains. Rambam wrote, 'Therefore the Sages instituted colored garments.' The Kesef Mishneh and Beit Yosef objected that the Gemara concludes that they did not institute such a requirement, but merely permitted her to wear colored garments. It may be explained that Rambam was troubled by the following. Why did they permit colored garments during the campaign of Vespasian in order to render a woman pure for her husband? The Gemara states that from the day the Beis HaMikdash was destroyed it would have been proper not to marry at all, except that one does not impose a decree upon the community unless most of the community can endure it. This implies that, were it not for that consideration, they would not have been concerned for the resulting neglect of procreation. Why, then, was a decree that otherwise ought to have been enacted set aside because of a limited concern for neglect of procreation? It must be that an earlier enactment already required women to have colored garments. Therefore, even during the campaign of Vespasian, they did not wish to uproot that earlier enactment entirely. It was sufficient to leave the matter optional, so that a woman was not required to wear colored garments. Originally, however, the enactment specifically favored colored garments. They later left the choice to the woman, but they did not wish entirely to abolish their own enactment and prohibit what they had previously instituted. The Gemara therefore asks, 'Who instituted this?' meaning that everyday practice shows women also wearing uncolored garments. The Gemara answers that this was not the later enactment. Rather, after the earlier enactment they no longer prohibited uncolored garments because of stains, although the original enactment had indeed been to wear colored garments specifically. This resolves the matter. See also Mordechai to Yevamos, who infers from the Gemara cited above that nowadays one may not divorce a wife merely because he has no children, even after ten years.
