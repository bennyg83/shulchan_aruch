That does not receive impurity. Sidrei Taharah innovates that if the nonreceptive object rests upon an object that does receive impurity and becomes impure by carrying, the woman may also become impure. Authorities debate attached structures and objects receiving only rabbinic impurity.
They did not decree upon it. Noda BiYehudah and others debate whether modern paper receives stain impurity. Some distinguish rag-based pressed paper from ancient leaf or bark paper. Chatam Sofer concludes that even modern paper does not receive impurity because its fibers are transformed, though he is less willing to be lenient for an actual examination cloth.
A fixed privy that does not receive impurity. Noda BiYehudah permits a stain on a seat fixed into the ground, even if the board was once a detached utensil, because attachment or a large opening removes its susceptibility. He sets out four categories of attached utensils.
A colored garment. Meil Tzedakah rules that all colors protect. Stained portions on the colored area do not count toward the required size, though separate white portions connected by one stain may combine. Chavot Daat, Tiferet Tzvi, and Teshuvah MeAhavah are more stringent.
She is pure. Dagul MeRevavah notes that Rabbeinu Simchah, Raavan, and Ramban appear stringent regarding colored garments. Teshuvah MeAhavah nevertheless follows the great majority who are lenient, while being stringent where half the stain is on white and half on color. Chatam Sofer accepts Rama's leniency, especially for an outer colored garment rather than one touching the body.
A woman should wear colored garments. Amudei Kesef suggests this is only as a regular practice, not as a device specifically during niddah or clean days, especially the first three clean days. See Adnei Paz.
