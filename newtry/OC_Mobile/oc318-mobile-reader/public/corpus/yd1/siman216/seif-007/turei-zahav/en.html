Even though it left his possession, etc. The Tur writes: “For example, if he specified, ‘during my lifetime and after my death, your entering my house is a konam,’ if he died or sold it to another, it is permitted,” end quote. This follows the Rosh, as the Beit Yosef cited. The Beit Yosef wondered about the latter clause, which permits it. If he did not specify, “during my lifetime and after my death,” then even if he said, “this house,” it is permitted according to the Rosh. If he did specify it, why should it be permitted? The Prishah cited the Maharshal that the latter clause concerns one who said, “during my lifetime and after my death.” My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, likewise wrote this and explained that it is nevertheless permitted because he first said, “my house,” and the later phrase, “during my lifetime and after my death,” is ineffective. Although above, when the one who made the vow said, “this house of yours,” we ruled stringently because the two expressions contradict one another, that case is different. There, the one making the vow says it, and it is possible that he will never enter that house. Here, however, the one imposing the prohibition says to Shimon, “your entering my house is forbidden,” and what does he care once it has left his possession? It is therefore permitted after the death of the one who imposed the prohibition, even though he said, “during my lifetime,” etc. The Rivash wrote this reasoning in siman 357, end quote. I saw, however, that the Rivash there distinguished between one who vows and one who imposes a prohibition only where he did not say, “during my lifetime and after my death.” The same appears in the Ran on 46, s.v. “Avimi inquired,” and it is straightforward. Where he explicitly says, “during my lifetime and after my death,” I do not know even the beginning of a basis for this distinction. There are no contradictory expressions, for he means: “You shall never enter the house that is presently mine, even after my death.” It is extremely surprising, for the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write here shortly that one who says to his son, “Your benefiting from me is a konam during my lifetime and after my death,” does not inherit him. There too he first said, “your benefiting from me,” which is exactly like, “my house,” and nevertheless the later phrase, “during my lifetime,” etc., is effective. The Gemara likewise asks regarding one who says, “My loaf is forbidden upon you,” and then gives it to him as a gift. Did he say, “my loaf,” meaning that it is forbidden only while it is in his possession, or did he say, “upon you,” thereby making it like hekdesh? The Beit Yosef cites this. Thus, although the phrase, “my loaf,” indicates specifically while it remains in his possession, the phrase, “upon you,” nevertheless forbids it, and all the more so where he subsequently says, “during my lifetime,” etc. It appears to me that the words of the Rosh and Tur should be understood as follows. The reason he must say, “during my lifetime and after my death,” even though he said, “this house that you enter is a konam,” is that a person ordinarily does not consider forbidding his house after his death. Ordinarily he is particular only that he not see his enemy in his house. Therefore, above, where one says, “My entering this house is a konam,” it remains forbidden even if the owner dies, and he need not say, “during my lifetime and after my death.” It is obvious that one who forbids himself intends all the days of his life, and since he said, “this house,” he evidently has some aversion to the house itself. Here, however, where he forbids his property to another, even though he said, “this,” his intention is that he not see his enemy benefiting from his property. After death, it is the way of the world to forgive all enemies. He must therefore say, “during my lifetime and after my death.” According to this, it further appears that although the Rosh and Tur required him to say, “during my lifetime,” etc., this applies only with respect to after death, for the reason I wrote. After a sale, however, it is certainly forbidden even if he did not specify, “after I sell it,” for the same reason remains, namely, that he does not want to see his enemy benefit from this house. This is clear, for if the issue were also sale, what did he accomplish by saying, “during my lifetime and after my death”? He should instead have said, “whether it is in my possession or in another’s possession.” One cannot answer that “during my lifetime” indicates this, for that phrase is necessary to prevent immediate permission during his life if he said only, “after my death.” It is therefore clear that no qualification is needed except concerning death, but not sale. This is also implied in siman 226, where the Tur writes that if he said, “this courtyard,” coercion is ineffective because it remains forbidden even after he sells it. This resolves the Beit Yosef’s questions. The latter clause in the Tur, “your entering my house is a konam,” concerns one who did not specify, and it teaches that with respect to sale it is permitted when he says, “my house,” and forbidden only when he says, “this house.” Regarding sale, it makes no difference whether he says, “during my lifetime and after my death.” The Tur mentioned death incidentally together with sale. This appears correct according to the Tur. As practical halacha, however, the Shulchan Aruch already ruled that here one need not say, “during my lifetime and after my death.” It therefore did not mention those words, but ruled that whenever he says, “this house,” it remains forbidden even if he dies or sells it, for by saying, “this,” he forbade it forever.

Accordingly, even if both are alive, it suffices that he act with the permission of one of them. The mention that one of them died reflects only the particular incident that occurred.
