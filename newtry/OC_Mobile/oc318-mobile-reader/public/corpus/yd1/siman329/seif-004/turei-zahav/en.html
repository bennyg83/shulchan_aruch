If his intent is to bake, etc. The Rashbam proved this in chapter 1 of Challah from what we say in chapter Keitzad Mevarkhim: bread made for kutach [a condiment] is exempt [from challah] — and similarly as written in seif 7 nearby, that his actions demonstrate his intent. In the Yerushalmi it states that bread made for kutach is obligated, lest he change his mind and make a small flat-cake from it. Therefore it seems to me that the case in chapter Keitzad Mevarkhim is where he took a little from the dough and baked it in fire — for we are concerned he may change his mind and take the full measure. For the same reason we say in seif 7: if he made [it in the shape of] thick loaves (ke'avin) it is obligated — because [people] are likely to change their mind regarding it.
And [if] he baked even a little, etc. At first glance this implies he had not yet separated challah from this dough until after baking the small portion he took from it — and so wrote the Beit Yosef, saying: "It seems this is specifically when he baked that small portion; but if he did not bake it because he intended to bake it, it does not become obligated — for even where he did bake it, it is only obligated by rabbinic decree, and we should not extend the ruling to obligate him merely because he intended to bake a small amount that does not meet the challah measure." Therefore he wrote here "and he baked it," etc. And so too the Mordechai in chapter Kol Sha'ah states: "R. Shimshon returned and said: since his intent was to make doughnuts (sufganin) and cook them in water, it is exempt — unless she made a small flat-cake from it and baked it on coals, since we are concerned she may change her mind and make a white flat-cake from it meeting the obligated amount." I said: to avoid this disagreement, one should make a small flat-cake and bake it, then take challah from it and from the dough together — for one may take challah from baked [bread] on behalf of dough and from dough on behalf of baked [bread]. End quote. However the Asher Ri (Rosh) in chapter Kol Sha'ah wrote: "Rashbam was accustomed, when kneading dough in his home to cook in water, to command that a little be baked from it, and through this the dough became obligated in challah before taking from it the small portion — for we are concerned she may change her mind." End quote — this implies challah separation here should be before taking from it the small portion to bake. For this reason my father-in-law z"l objected to the Beit Yosef, who wrote here according to the Tur's view that baking is specifically required and that having the intent to bake does not suffice. And so too is ruled here in the Shulchan Aruch — but this is not the view of the Rosh, who disagrees with the Mordechai.
The Nekudat HaKesef comments: I say that according to my father-in-law's understanding, one should indeed wonder at the Beit Yosef all the more — for in the abridged rulings of the Rosh that the Tur compiled, it is written explicitly: "However, if his intent is to bake a small flat-cake from it, it became obligated in challah." End quote — it is explicit that intent alone suffices. But I say that at first glance one should wonder at the Rosh and the Tur regarding this — that they said intent suffices — since they wish to follow also the view of Rashbam, which is the primary ruling, and Rashbam wrote explicitly at the beginning of Challah several times that the case is where he already baked the small portion — see there. Rather it seems to me there is no disagreement at all: certainly if at the time of kneading the dough he intended to cook it in water and also bake a little from it, the whole became obligated immediately — and this is clear from the Rosh's language, where he wrote that Maharam was accustomed to command that a little be baked when they kneaded the dough, and through this it became obligated immediately. In this manner is also the view of the Tur. The reason: since at the time of kneading the concern was immediate — lest she change her mind and take the full challah measure from it. The Mordechai is speaking of a case where at the time of kneading and rolling her intent was to cook the entire [dough] in water, but afterwards she separated a little to bake — in that case it does not suffice until it is actually baked, even though when one changes her mind to make [the dough into] bread it becomes obligated in challah immediately after the change of mind, as implied at the end of seif 3 in the language of the Mishnah. That is only when she changed her mind to make all of it into bread — unlike here, where all of it will remain for cooking in water, and she only takes some for baking. The primary obligation on the entire dough [comes] by rabbinic decree lest she change her mind — and this does not suffice to obligate it immediately once she has set aside some for baking, since this is not so evident, since the rest of the dough will be cooked. Rather, we require that she actually bake it. The Beit Yosef and the Shulchan Aruch require actual baking specifically — and the Beit Yosef's words were directed at the Tur's words, and the Tur wrote: "Such was the custom of Maharam in his home — when they made a dough to cook in water, to bake a little from it, and through this the whole became obligated in challah." End quote. This language implies he actually baked it first, and through this the dough became obligated — since he did not write "he commanded to bake," which would imply the obligation came through the command to bake even before baking. Rather he wrote that Maharam was accustomed to bake, etc. — implying he actually baked it first, and through that the dough became obligated. This is in line with what I wrote: there were two practices for Maharam — when he commanded to bake [at the time of kneading], intent alone sufficed and it became obligated immediately, as the Rosh states. But if he had not commanded at the time of kneading — so that at the time of kneading the intent was to cook everything in water — and afterwards he set aside a little, then it is only effective once it is actually baked. And so is the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch here, and this seems correct to me in this distinction.
However I have a difficulty: why did Maharam need the remedy of baking a portion? For even without it the dough is obligated in challah — as Rashbam and the Rosh wrote in chapter Kol Sha'ah that there is proof from chapter Keitzad Mevarkhim: if he intended to cook the dough in water it is exempt — from the ruling that bread made for kutach is exempt [from challah], as stated there. The Tosafot there explain: a dough made for kutach — one does not bake it in an oven but dries it in the sun — and even though from the time of kneading the challah obligation applies, since he kneaded it with intent to bake it in the sun, it is exempt because it will ultimately be withdrawn from the category of bread. And the ruling distinguishing between thick loaves and thin student's loaves is because with thick loaves it resembles other dough not made for kutach — it may be confused with regular dough. But thin student's loaves — their actions demonstrate [their purpose] and they are not likely to be confused. And in the Yerushalmi it states: kutach dough is obligated in challah lest she change her mind and make a white flat-cake from it. And lest the Yerushalmi conflict with ours, one may say the Yerushalmi speaks of a case where she took a little from the dough to bake it in fire — for we are concerned she may change her mind and take the full measure; and this is also why the Gemara distinguishes between thick loaves and thin loaves — for with thick loaves she is likely to change her mind, but with thin loaves it is not her way to change her mind. Now regarding Maharam's dough made in his home to cook in water or fry in oil — certainly they made it in the ordinary manner of all dough-making, and thus there is concern she may change her mind to bake it — making it like kutach dough [in the shape of] thick loaves, which is obligated even without baking a portion. It seems to me that when they made a dough to cook in water, they typically added spices or egg-water not in the manner of dough for baking but in a different manner — therefore there is no concern unless she took a little from it to bake, making it like the Yerushalmi case. This is my understanding of the matter.
