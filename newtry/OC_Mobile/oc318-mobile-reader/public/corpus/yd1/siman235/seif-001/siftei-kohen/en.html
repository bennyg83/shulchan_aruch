He said, “Your marital relations are forbidden to me.” But if he said, “The benefit of my marital relations is forbidden to you,” the prohibition does not take effect at all, since he is obligated to her, as stated above in siman 234, seif 67. The responsa of the Rashba, siman 734, states that Rav Hai Gaon wrote in a responsum that even if one swore that he would divorce his wife, it is not an oath, just as if he swore not to repay a creditor, his words have no effect. End quote. Yet it appears to me that the responsa of Maharam, Prague edition, and the Mordechai in the chapter Shevuos Shetayim cite a responsum of Rav Hai Gaon that even release from the oath does not help and he must divorce her. Although we do not rule this way, but rather permit release from all oaths, as stated above in siman 230, it is nevertheless clear from his words that this is an oath. This is also explicit in the words of the Rambam, chapter 6 of Hilchos Shevuos, halachah 11, and the other poskim regarding one who swore to divorce his wife, where an opening for release is found on account of the honor of his children; see there. The same is explicit in the responsa of Maharam Mintz, siman 18, question 2, in Piskei Mahari, siman 58, and in other responsa of the later authorities.
He must remain married to her for seven days. This is because a woman who is a niddah ordinarily remains without marital relations for seven days.
Or for longer, or if he prohibited her without specifying a period, etc. He should not divorce her immediately, for in the meantime he may find an opening through which his vow can be released. It appears that all these cases concern only where they remain bound by their vows, but they may certainly obtain release through a sage, as stated above in siman 234, seif 23 and seif 49.
After seven days he must divorce her, etc. This applies when he prohibited her without specifying a period, or explicitly for more than seven days. If he prohibited her only for seven days, he must remain married to her.
