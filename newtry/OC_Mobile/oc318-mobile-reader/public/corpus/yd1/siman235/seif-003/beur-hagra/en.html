If he imposed a vow upon her, etc., or, etc. This is the Mishnah there, following the first tanna, and so rules the Tur. Rabbeinu Chananel, Rambam, and Rashba rule like Rabbi Yose, because the Amoraim discuss his view there on 71b. Rashba writes: “Rabbeinu Chananel of blessed memory wrote that the halachah follows Rabbi Yochanan, while some say that the halachah follows Rav Chisda, and this latter view appears more correct.” See the Rosh, chapter 3 of Bava Kamma, and Choshen Mishpat, siman 413, seif 1. Rambam rules like Shmuel because his view is the intermediate position. See the Rif and Rosh, chapter 22 of Shabbos, and below, siman 316, seif katan 9; also see Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer, beginning of siman 74. However, the words of the Rif and Rosh here imply the Tur's view, for they omitted the entire discussion of the Gemara. See the Maggid Mishneh and the Ran.
And he made it contingent, etc., or, etc. See the discussion there on 71a to 71b, from “We learned: one who imposes a vow,” etc., until “it is he who hates me.” See Tosafot there, s.v. “Granted,” and s.v. “that she vowed,” etc. The Ran explains the Gemara's question more fully. When it asks, “We learned,” etc., it cannot mean that he simply prohibited her from tasting, etc., because he has no power to do so, as stated there on 59a and elsewhere. Rather, he must have forbidden his property to her if she tastes, etc. If so, she should taste and then wait thirty days. Although with respect to tasting fruit she cannot wait at all, with respect to deriving benefit from him she can wait thirty days, as in the first clause. This is also stated in the Yerushalmi: “One who imposes a vow upon his wife that she not taste,” but can a person impose a vow upon his wife? Rather, he says to her, “If you eat from a certain item, my property shall be forbidden to you.” She may eat from that item and thereby become forbidden to benefit from his property. The case is where his wife vowed and her husband heard and did not annul it. This accords with the answer of our Gemara. Tosafot, s.v. “Granted,” explain that he made the prohibition contingent upon marital relations. The Gemara asks that she should eat and then wait seven days. There is no practical difference between these explanations, except that Tosafot interpret the case in the manner of our Gemara's later explanation, namely that he made it contingent, etc., while the Ran interprets it in accordance with the Yerushalmi. The Gemara answers that the case is where she herself vowed, etc., without making it contingent at all, so that she cannot wait at all, as explained above. One may ask what she gains through divorce. The answer is that even so she may say, “I do not want a husband who upholds my vows.” Tosafot, s.v. “that she vowed,” explain that she vowed for as long as she remains married to him. Yet in the conclusion the Gemara states: “Rather, the case is where he made it contingent,” etc. However, here, where she vowed, etc., even if she made the vow contingent upon marital relations or upon benefiting from his property, he must nevertheless divorce her immediately. Although this answer was stated specifically according to Rabbi Yose and following Rav Huna's interpretation, see Tosafot there, s.v. “This is the correct text: Rabbi Yose says,” etc., and we do not rule that way, as stated in siman 234, seif 59, we still require this answer even according to the Sages. The final clause, concerning a condition that she tell a certain person, etc., involves neither personal affliction nor matters between husband and wife, so he cannot annul it. It must therefore be understood in this manner. The Tur writes that the same applies to the entire Mishnah there, including the cases where she may not go to her father's house, to a house of mourning, or to a wedding feast. None of these vows involves personal affliction or matters between husband and wife, and he cannot annul them. See Even HaEzer there, seif 4. The governing rule is therefore as follows: when he imposed the vow by making it contingent upon marital relations, she waits seven days. When she herself vowed and he remained silent or upheld the vow, whether or not she made it contingent upon marital relations, he must divorce her immediately. We rule like Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehudah, that he is deemed to have caused the separation, and in accordance with the anonymous Mishnah here. On this basis the entire siman is explained.
