Or a piece of meat that has not been deveined. This refers to meat that has already been cut into small pieces. But whenever the deveining is visibly recognizable, he is certainly believed, for we can see that it was deveined. We need not be concerned that perhaps it was deveined by someone who is not expert in the procedure, for all those who commonly engage in deveining are assumed to be expert, as the Rama wrote in siman 65. Although he wrote there that it is proper to be stringent, that applies only l'chatchila. Nor need one be concerned that perhaps the deveining was not completed. Only where the person left, and it is possible that he intended to return and complete the deveining, are we concerned for this possibility, as stated in siman 65. Here, however, there is no basis to assume such a defect, as I explained. Nevertheless, there is also a remedy for this: He may give or sell the meat to the witness who testifies concerning it. The witness will then be believed regarding it because it is his, as stated below, and afterward he may give it back to the original owner. We are not concerned about this subsequent transfer. I have written all this according to the Rama's view, who added the case of a piece of meat that has not been deveined. In truth, however, this case is not found in the Mordechai in the chapter HaIshah Rabbah, nor in the Rosh in the chapter HaNizakin. Only tevel is mentioned at the beginning of the chapter HaIshah Rabbah, while the case of meat appears only in the Ran in the chapter HaIshah Rabbah. I am astonished that such a statement issued from a holy mouth. Only with regard to tevel and similar matters, where the entire quantity was definitely forbidden for consumption before it was corrected and no portion of the heap was permitted before that correction, do we say that, since a definite prohibition was established, one witness is not believed to remove it from its presumptive status and permit it. Meat that has not been deveined, however, was never established as inherently forbidden. It is forbidden only because of the sinews or forbidden fats that may be present within it, and thus its prohibition is based on uncertainty. It is therefore certainly comparable to the ruling stated afterward: If there are two pieces before us, one permitted and one forbidden, one witness is believed to say, “This is the permitted one.” It is obvious that this rule applies even where the permitted and forbidden portions are attached to one another. This is precisely the law concerning meat that has not been deveined. It is forbidden because of the uncertainty that one may encounter a prohibited sinew or fat. Whatever becomes permitted after the correction was intrinsically permitted even before the correction, only its identity had not been clarified. Therefore it is inapplicable to say that a definite prohibition had been established in this piece. An additional proof may be brought from the Rosh in the chapter Gid HaNasheh, who wrote: “An entire thigh stands with a presumption of permissibility except for the sciatic nerve, etc.,” end quote. The same is further evident from the Ran in the chapter HaNizakin, who wrote that if kosher and non-kosher meat were present in the butcher shops and one witness said, “This piece is kosher,” he is believed. Although a prohibition had been established somewhere in the group, it had not been established with regard to this particular piece, and therefore he is believed, end quote. The same applies here. The meat itself was not established as forbidden; it was forbidden only because of a prohibited substance mixed within and attached to it. It is therefore obvious that one witness is believed to say that this is the permitted portion and that the prohibited substance is not present in it, since the prohibition was never definite. This also appears from Tosafos in the third chapter of Kiddushin, 65b, s.v. nitme'u, who wrote: “One witness is believed specifically to permit, such as by saying, ‘This meat was deveined,’ or, ‘This piece is permitted fat,’ etc.” This necessarily applies even where it is not within his power to correct the matter, as in the case of permitted fat. One must therefore say that these cases are regarded as having had no original presumption, neither of permissibility nor of prohibition, and for that reason the witness is believed in every circumstance to permit them. Indeed, daily practice is to believe any person who says, “This meat was deveined,” and people eat it on the basis of his statement, even where the person holding the meat does not himself know how to devein it. According to this gloss, we would have to forbid it, since the matter is not within his power and he should therefore not be believed. Rather, the correct explanation is certainly as I have written.
The sage Ploni permitted it for me. The Darkei Moshe wrote as follows: An incident came before us concerning Reuven, who prepared butter for Shimon. Shimon sold a large quantity of butter as kosher, saying that Reuven had prepared it and had given all of it to him as kosher. Reuven said that he had given only part of it to him as kosher, while regarding the remainder he had explicitly told him that it was forbidden. Shimon contradicted him. We were lenient in the matter, since many authorities permit butter made by non-Jews, and in some places the custom is to permit it. With regard to other prohibitions, however, it appears to me that the food is forbidden, for this resembles the case where one says, “Ploni corrected my heap of produce,” and Ploni responds, “I did not correct it,” in which case it is forbidden. One cannot distinguish by saying that the latter ruling applies only where a prior prohibition had been established, for if so the authority should have counted 15 laws rather than 14. Rather, no such distinction is made, end quote from the Darkei Moshe. The Derishah copied the language of the Darkei Moshe, who arranged 14 laws based on the Mordechai and the Rosh, and also copied this incident as though it were from the Mordechai and the Rosh. In truth, however, it is the Darkei Moshe's own ruling. In my humble opinion, it appears that one should not be lenient in this matter. We stated in seif 1 that, regarding something that had already been in his possession, if he made the statement the first time, he can no longer contradict it. Here this is indeed the first statement. All the more so in this case, Shimon is certainly speaking falsely, since he attributes the preparation to Reuven and Reuven contradicts him. As the Rosh wrote in the chapter HaNizakin, the principle that the Torah believed one witness like two does not apply here, because Shimon himself admits that Ploni prepared it, and that person contradicts him. Consequently, all that remains is a mixture of butter made by a non-Jew with butter made by a Jew. Although in siman 115, seif 1, the Rama wrote that a mixture of such butter is permitted, that applies only where the permitted butter constitutes the majority, as we demonstrated there. Here the case does not clearly involve a majority of permitted butter, for if it did, the butter would be permitted even if Shimon admitted that Reuven's account was correct. Nevertheless, it appears that Shimon must be regarded as negligent, for he mixed prohibited food into permitted food and now lies about the matter. Therefore one should be stringent with him and forbid all of his butter. This is worse than intentionally nullifying a prohibition l'chatchila, which is discussed in siman 99, seif 5. This appears correct in my humble opinion. Afterward, the responsa of the Rama were printed, and in siman 66 he discussed the law of this butter and noted the difficulty from the words of the Rosh in the chapter HaNizakin. He answered that the case there is different because the heap stood with a presumption of being forbidden as tevel, whereas where no prohibition had been established, the witness is believed. This is exceedingly astonishing. Since this person has no credibility once the Ploni whom he names contradicts him, what difference does it make that no prohibition had previously been established? Ultimately, the butter owner's credibility has been lost, and the butter is like that of a person upon whom one cannot rely. Moreover, the Rosh mentioned other laws there and explicitly distinguished between cases where a prohibition had been established and cases where it had not, yet regarding this law he made no such distinction. The Rama further brought proof from the chapter HaCholetz: A person said, “I converted privately, by myself,” etc., until they said to him, “According to your own words, you are a non-Jew, and you are not believed to disqualify your children.” We thus see that, although the children's presumption of Jewish fitness seemingly derives only from their father, nevertheless, since according to his own statement he is a non-Jew, he is not believed to disqualify them. The same should apply to this butter. Since according to Reuven's words he is merely an outsider and not the custodian of this butter, he should not be believed where the owner contradicts him, end quote. I am astonished that such a statement issued from a holy mouth, for Rashi explicitly explained in the chapter HaCholetz, Yevamos 46b: “There is no testimony by a non-Jew; since your children are presumed fit, you are not believed to disqualify them,” end quote. We learn from this that the children possessed a presumption of fitness independently of their father. Therefore it appears to me that the butter here is equivalent to butter found in the possession of someone whose reliability cannot be trusted. My brother, the gaon, our teacher R' Yitzchak HaLevi, of blessed memory, ruled in a responsum concerning butter that had been sent through a non-Jew without a seal that it is permitted b'dieved, and we are not concerned that perhaps the non-Jew exchanged it for his own.
