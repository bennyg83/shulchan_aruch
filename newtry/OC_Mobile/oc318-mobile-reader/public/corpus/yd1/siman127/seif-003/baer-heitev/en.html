To permit. Meaning, one witness may be believed in prohibitions to permit without the owner's silence being treated as admission. To impose a prohibition, however, one never finds that the witness is believed without the owner's silence. If neither prohibition nor permission was established, the item is already forbidden because of doubt without the witness. If permission was established, one witness cannot prohibit it, as stated in seif 1. Shach.
And I will show you. Taz explains that the owner must go with him. If he goes and does not find what the witness claimed, there is no prohibition. Shach writes that even if the owner or another witness contradicts him, he is believed where the matter can be clarified. This is proven by the Gemara and Tosafot.
To prohibit it. The Rosh and Mordechai read, 'even to permit it,' which is the greater novelty. Without the witness it is already forbidden because of doubt, yet his testimony establishes definite prohibition, relevant to a compounded doubt. He is also believed without the owner's silence where the owner is absent or says, 'I do not know.' Shach.
Deveined. Taz explains that the meat had already been cut into small pieces. If the deveining is still recognizable, the witness is certainly believed, and we do not suspect that an unqualified person performed it, since those commonly engaged in deveining are expert. One solution is to give or sell the meat to the witness, who is then believed concerning his own property, after which he may return it. Taz nevertheless challenges Rama: only where an entire item was certainly forbidden, such as untithed produce, does an established prohibition prevent one witness from permitting it. Meat before deveining was not intrinsically forbidden; only forbidden fats or sinews were mixed within it. A witness should therefore be believed to identify the permitted meat. Taz cites daily practice of relying on any person who says meat was deveined, even where the present holder cannot devein it. Nekudot HaKesef defends Rama and rejects these proofs.
Permitted. Although a prohibition was established in that location, no prohibition was established in this particular piece. Shach. A person is believed concerning his own property even where prohibition was established, because he can correct it. The Rosh. Taz cites a case where Reuven prepared butter for Shimon. Shimon sold much butter as kosher, claiming Reuven had delivered all of it as kosher; Reuven said only part was kosher and that he expressly identified the rest as forbidden. Darkei Moshe ruled leniently because many permit non-Jewish butter and some communities follow that practice, but would prohibit other items. Taz would prohibit all Shimon's butter because he was negligent in mixing forbidden and permitted butter and is no longer trustworthy. Nekudot HaKesef follows Darkei Moshe. Taz also cites his brother's ruling that butter sent through a non-Jew without a seal is permitted after the fact, without concern for substitution.
Believed. Even concerning a Torah prohibition, provided she can correct it or the item belongs to her. Otherwise she is no better than one witness who cannot permit against an established prohibition. Shach.
Aspects. Women are not believed concerning matters involving significant exertion, because women are considered lax in such work. Shach records a tradition that women are not believed concerning deveining the hindquarters. The Mechaber's statement that women are believed concerning deveining refers to ordinary deveining of the forequarters. See siman 84:11.
A minor. Shach writes that this is relevant where the witness says, 'You knew facts supporting this,' where another person contradicts him while the owners are absent, where the matter can be verified, where he says, 'Come and I will show you a non-Jew libating your wine,' where neither prohibition nor permission was established, or where one of two pieces is forbidden and the other permitted. In such cases an adult witness may be believed without the owner's silence, but a minor is not. Where the matter is in the minor's control, however, the minor is believed even to remove it from its prior status, whether to permit or prohibit. See Orach Chaim, end of siman 436, and above, end of siman 48.
