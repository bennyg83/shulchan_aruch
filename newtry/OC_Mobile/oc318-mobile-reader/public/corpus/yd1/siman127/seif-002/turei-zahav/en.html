Where the worker erred, so that he does not lose his wages. This does not mean specifically all his wages. The same law applies if, because of his error, he thinks that he will lose only part of his wages, even a small sum. This is proven from the Gemara in the chapter HaNizakin, concerning the scribe who erred and thought that he would not be paid for all the Divine Names he had written in the Sefer Torah. He was not concerned about this loss, and nevertheless he spoke falsely. See siman 281 regarding what I wrote there. A difficulty arises for me from what is stated here: Since he is believed to cause himself the loss of his wages, he is also believed to invalidate the Sefer Torah. How is this different from the chapter HaOmer in Kiddushin, 65b, where the Gemara says: “As for her, were she not certain of the matter, she would not have made the statement and thereby forbidden herself; shall he then also become forbidden to her relatives? It therefore teaches us otherwise.” Why do we not say there as well that, since she renders herself forbidden, she should also be believed regarding others, namely her relatives, just as here? One cannot answer that the case there is different because he contradicts her and says, “I did not betroth you,” whereas here he says, “I do not know,” for we rule that a claim of “I do not know” in this context is equivalent to a contradiction. It appears that the answer is that the case there is different. Although her relatives are uncertain whether she was betrothed, they remain permitted because she had a prior presumption of being unmarried; therefore we maintain her according to that presumption and regard her as not betrothed. A Sefer Torah, by contrast, had no prior presumption clearly establishing its permissibility. This appears to me to be the correct distinction, namely, whether or not there is a prior presumption.
