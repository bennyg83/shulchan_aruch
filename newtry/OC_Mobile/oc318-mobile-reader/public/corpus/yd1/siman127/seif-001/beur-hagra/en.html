If it is, and so forth. This follows Abaye's resolution, which Rava does not dispute, and the incidents there. Rashi explains that because the matter was in his control, he is believed under the principle that a craftsman is believed concerning his work. The Rosh and Mordechai explain that he is treated like an owner. They prove this from the case of sacred names already written and from cases of pure foods, even where he must pay for damage. Yevamot likewise relies on his ability to repair the matter.
Even, and so forth. Although the halachah follows Rava, the Rosh reconciles the incidents that appear to follow Abaye.
Even, and so forth. When the owner remains silent, the witness is believed even if the matter was never in his control, as in Kiddushin 65b and Gittin.
But if, and so forth. Where it had once been in his control, for otherwise he is never believed.
Within the time required for speech. Otherwise the owner has already admitted the claim. See Bava Metzia 6a and Bava Batra 138a.
Or he says, and so forth. From the sacrificial case there, where the owner cannot contradict him.
But if he remained silent, and so forth. Even where it was never in the witness's control.
We instruct him, and so forth. The Tur. See the gloss below concerning an owner's inward disbelief. Tosafot says that if the witness is trusted like two witnesses, even denial does not defeat him. See Shach, seif katan 15.
Then according to all opinions, and so forth. This is an independent rule, comparable to an ox said to have been used for bestiality, which remains permitted for ordinary use but not for the altar.
Some say, and so forth. Kiddushin and Rabbeinu Nissim in Ketubot discuss contradiction by the owner. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 82, seif 12, and siman 46, seif 37. The first opinion treats silence in the owner's presence as admission. See Shach.
Provided he says, and so forth. Tosafot and the other authorities, based on Bava Metzia 37b and Mordechai.
If he remained silent, and so forth. Also from Bava Metzia. This is the approach of Tosafot, Rosh, Mordechai, and their followers. Ramban, Rashba, and Ritva challenge it and hold that a person is generally believed when the owner is silent or says he does not know, but never when explicitly contradicted. Rabbeinu Nissim summarizes their position. He nevertheless favors Tosafot: silence counts as admission because the Torah grants credibility to the witness where the owner does not deny him. Thus a disqualified witness is not believed. This also explains Rava's rule that one witness is ineffective in matters of forbidden relations.
If not, and so forth. As in Ketubot 22a. See Even HaEzer, siman 152, seif 6.
Anyone believed, and so forth. A claim of credibility does not override witnesses, as taught in Bava Metzia and Bava Batra.
Nevertheless, for himself, and so forth. As in Ketubot 23b. See Tosafot, Yevamot 47a.
A butcher, and so forth. See the end of seif 1.
All those disqualified, and so forth. Rabbeinu Nissim holds they are not believed. A suspected sinner may be believed to permit another's item but not to prohibit it through testimony. Shach raises this difficulty, and it requires study.
(Supplement) All those disqualified as witnesses, and so forth. One suspected in one area is not automatically suspected in another. See siman 119, seifim 4 and 7.
