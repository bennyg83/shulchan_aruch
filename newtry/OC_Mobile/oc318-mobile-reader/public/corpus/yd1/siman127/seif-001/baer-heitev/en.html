Believed. Shach writes that he is believed even if he did not tell him the first time he encountered him, because he has a migo that he could have poured the wine for idolatrous use while it was in his control. Accordingly, one who says that another person's cooked wine had been libated before it was cooked is not believed even if the wine is presently in his possession, because he lacks that migo.
Contradicts him. Shach disagrees and holds that the witness is believed only when the owner is silent. If the owner contradicts him, he is not believed, as stated in Choshen Mishpat 108:14. Rama writes in a responsum that if the owner contradicts him by saying that the witness did not tell him this when he first encountered him, the owner is certainly believed.
Is not. It is then permitted to everyone. Darkei Moshe writes that even if the owner says, 'I do not know,' it is permitted to everyone. Mordechai writes that if he says, 'Perhaps you were not precise,' this is treated as a contradiction.
Like an admission. Even if the worker says the item was never in his control and he did not tell the owner the first time, silence is treated as admission. There is no distinction between a worker and a guardian, or between immediate and delayed reporting. It is not literally an admission; rather, his silence indicates that he recognizes grounds for the claim and relies upon the witness. Shach.
And they instruct him. Meaning, they instruct the owner before the witness speaks that when the witness arrives, he should immediately say within the time required for speech, 'I do not believe you.' They do not instruct him to contradict the witness factually, for they may not teach him to lie. Nekudot HaKesef explains that they instruct him to say, 'I do not know.'
Another. Shach disagrees and rules that there is still an initial prohibition, though after the fact one should not be stringent even for the witness himself.
Helps. Shach asks what the contradiction accomplishes, since if the owner is absent the testimony is ineffective in any case, because the witness must say, 'It was done in your presence,' or, 'You knew.' He answers that the contradiction helps even if the first witness later repeats his testimony before the owner and the owner remains silent. Since silence is effective because the owner relies upon the witness, once that witness has already been contradicted the owner no longer relies upon him. Nekudot HaKesef explains that the requirement to say, 'In your presence,' applies only when the owner is present and his silence is treated as admission. If the owners died or went overseas and one witness says that this wine was libated, he is ordinarily believed unless another witness contradicts him.
Like two. Shach explains that even if the owner generally trusts the witness like two witnesses, he may say, 'I do not believe you,' meaning that in this matter he does not trust him as two. Once he says this, we accept that he truly lacks such trust. If two witnesses arrive sequentially and the first testimony was already accepted by beit din and judgment rendered, the first is treated as two and a later single witness cannot contradict him. If they arrive together, the first is not treated as two. Where Scripture itself grants credibility to one witness, he is treated as two; where credibility arises only from reasoning or rabbinic enactment, as in ordinary prohibitions, he is not.
You knew. Shach writes that otherwise silence is not admission, because the owner may be silent simply because he does not know the facts. He notes, however, that many authorities hold that even such silence gives credibility to the witness. Regarding a worker, all agree that if the item is no longer in his control and he did not report the matter when he first encountered the owner, he is not believed unless the owner's silence reflects admission, because a worker who accepted responsibility to perform the task properly is presumed to have been careful.
Contradicted him. Shach explains that this means the owner later contradicts the witness. If he later says only, 'I do not know, and my earlier silence was not admission,' the witness remains believed according to all. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that if the witness said, 'Your wine was libated in your presence,' and the owner was silent, he cannot later say that he ignored the witness, because one witness is believed in prohibitions. In matters of sexual prohibition, where two witnesses are required, such an explanation is effective. Taz writes that if the owner says he was silent because he thought the witness knew with certainty, but later heard from others, whom he identifies, that the witness is unreliable, he is believed, as though he initially said, 'I do not know.' But he is not believed to say that he merely did not care to answer, because a person does not ordinarily ignore a claim that he is consuming a prohibition.
Who says. Shach notes that above, at the end of siman 1, the Mechaber rules without qualification that such a person is believed, and Rama concurs. Here Rama cites the ruling as 'some say' because Darkei Moshe questioned it from siman 185, where a woman who wore niddah garments cannot later offer an excuse. Perhaps the cases differ, but the matter requires study. Taz also writes in siman 1 that one should not rule this way.
Suspected. Shach writes that this applies specifically to an apostate regarding that matter. One who is merely suspected remains believed, as stated in siman 119:7. A non-Jew is not believed either to prohibit or to permit, even where he has no personal benefit from his testimony. The law of a non-Jew speaking innocently was explained in siman 98:1 and siman 313:2 in the gloss.
