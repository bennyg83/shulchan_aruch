Is believed. The Shach wrote: even if he did not tell him the first time he found it. The reason he is believed is because of a migo, since it was in his power to render it libated wine. On this basis one may judge regarding one who says to his fellow about his cooked wine (*) [but this is not so, for necessarily here it speaks in a case that is not called a case of great loss; if not, why would sixty be needed against the stream?] that it became libated before cooking: he is not believed, even if it is in his hand, since he has no migo; this is his language.
Contradicts him. The Shach disagrees with this and holds that he is believed specifically if the owners are silent, but if the owners contradict him, he is not believed, as stated in Choshen Mishpat siman 108 seif 14. The Rav wrote in Responsa siman 66 that if the owner contradicts him by saying that he did not tell him the first time he found it, certainly the owner is believed; see there.
Is not. Then it is permitted to everyone, for even if he says "I do not know," it is permitted to everyone; so wrote Darkei Moshe. The Mordechai wrote that if he says to him, "perhaps you were not precise," it is considered as though he contradicted him.
Like an admission. Meaning even if he said that it was never in his hand and he did not tell him the first time, nevertheless since silence is like admission, there is no distinction between a worker and a guardian, or between whether he told him immediately or not; silence is like admission. It is not literally like an admission, but since he is silent, it appears that he knows there are grounds for the matter and relies on the witness. So wrote Tosafot; end quote, Shach.
And we teach him. Meaning that we teach him before the witness speaks to him, that when the witness comes and tells him, he should say to him immediately, within the time of speech, "I do not believe you." We do not teach him to contradict him, for how could they teach him to lie? (In Nekudat HaKesef he explained that we teach him to say, "I do not know"; see there.)
Another. The Shach disagrees with this and rules that nevertheless there is a prohibition at the outset, but after the fact one should not be stringent for the witness himself.
Helps. The Shach wrote: the language is difficult, for what does it help? Even if he does not contradict him, his testimony is nothing, since the litigant is not before us, for he must say "in your presence" or "you knew," etc. One may say that his contradiction helps in that even if the witness later also says it before the litigant and he is silent, he is not believed, since the reason for silence, etc., is that he relies on the words of the witness, as I wrote above; therefore, if he was already contradicted, he no longer relies on him. This requires study as a matter of law; this is his language. (In Nekudat HaKesef he answers that what the Mechaber wrote, that he must say "in your presence," etc., speaks specifically when the owners are here, but only regarding silence being like admission does he say that he must say "in your presence." This is unlike where the owners died or went overseas, and one witness says this wine was libated and there is no one to contradict him; it is obvious that he is believed. Regarding that he wrote that if another contradicts him, the other is believed; see there.)
Two. The Shach wrote that this is astonishing: if he is believed by him like two, how does he fulfill his obligation before Heaven by saying "I do not believe you"? Rather, this is what it means: even if he had been trusted by him like two, he may say "I do not believe you," meaning, "for this matter you are not trusted by me like two." If the litigant says this to him, we say that he certainly speaks truth, that in his heart he does not believe him like two. We do not say that since until now he was trusted by him like two, the litigant can no longer say "I do not believe you," as though two witnesses had come and told him, "your wine was libated." Rather, since he said to him "I do not believe you," he nullified his trustworthiness. Although this is somewhat forced in the wording, I prefer this, so that we not say that Rabbeinu Tam holds that although he believes him in his heart like two, he may fabricate and say "I do not believe you." No author is found to have written this in Rabbeinu Tam's name or to hold so. But it requires study, for it implies that according to this view, even if the litigant is not there, this witness who contradicts and says "it was not libated" is of no significance; so too the Atzei Zahav explained. This law is astonishing, and the Shach disagrees with this and rules that when the litigant is not before us, even without a contradicting witness, the witness who says "it was libated" is not believed at all; all the more so if another contradicts him, it is permitted according to everyone. (I already wrote above in s.k. 7 that in Nekudat HaKesef he retracted, and therefore explained that this "there is one who wrote" refers to where the owners are before us and are silent, and there is one witness contradicting; the contradicting witness is of no significance.) However, all this is when the two witnesses came at once. But when they came one after another, since the first witness's testimony was already accepted in court and the law was decided according to his testimony, he is like two witnesses; when one witness later comes to contradict him, the words of one have no standing against two. See above siman 1, where the Mechaber ruled regarding a shochet who slaughtered improperly and the shochet contradicts him, saying that the slaughter is valid, etc. However, where they came at once, we do not say that the first is like two. Although in the incident of the shochet they came at once, there it is different, because since they believed the shochet on the basis that most who are found by slaughter are experts, it is as though his testimony had already been accepted. Likewise, wherever Scripture revealed that one witness is believed, he is like two, for the Torah believed him like two. But where Scripture did not reveal this, and he is believed only by reasoning or by rabbinic enactment, such as a piece that is doubtfully fat and doubtfully chelev and one witness said it is fat, or where one witness said to him, "your wine was libated," and he is silent, and the like, and in every place where we say one witness is believed in prohibitions, since Scripture did not reveal it, he is not considered like two. Only in testimony regarding a woman did they act leniently and believe one like two, and the words of one have no standing against two; end quote. You knew. The Shach wrote that otherwise silence is not like admission, for he is silent because he does not know the matter clearly. However, this is surprising, for several poskim ruled that even if he is silent because he does not know the matter clearly, nevertheless the witness is believed. He further discusses several proofs from the Talmud and Tosafot that prove this, and concludes: however, certainly with a worker, even the Rashba and his supporters agree that if it is not in his hand and he did not tell him the first time he found it, he is not believed if the litigant is not silent because of admission. The reason for a worker is that since he accepted responsibility to do it in a valid manner, there is a presumption that he is very careful, and therefore he is not believed. Contradicted him. It appears to me that this is specifically if he contradicts him afterward. But if afterward he says, "I do not know, and what I was silent about at first was not because I admitted," the witness is believed according to everyone. Shach. Rabbeinu Yerucham wrote: if he said to him, "in your presence it was libated," and he is silent, and afterward he retracted and said, "I was silent only because I did not take the witness's words seriously," this does not help, since one witness is believed in prohibitions. But in a matter of ervah, where fewer than two are not enough, if he says, "I was silent because I did not take the witness's words seriously," it helps. The Taz wrote as follows: it seems to me that if he said, "I was silent because I thought that since I do not know and you know clearly, but now I heard from others that you are not believed," and he verifies this from those others who told him so, he is believed in this, and it is as though he said at first, "I do not know." A proof for this is from what Rama wrote in Choshen Mishpat siman 20, that if he brought proof that he did not initially know of these witnesses, etc.; here too it is similar. But if he said, "what I was silent about at first was because I did not care to answer you," he is not believed. Even if the witness did not tell him the first time, the litigant cannot say, "since you already saw me and did not tell me anything to separate me from prohibition, and now you tell me, this is only mockery and jest, and therefore I did not care to answer you," because a person is not likely to be unconcerned when they say that he is drinking something forbidden; end quote.
Who says. The Shach wrote: above at the end of siman 1 seif 13, the Mechaber wrote plainly that he is believed, and the Rav wrote plainly like him; but here the Rav wrote it in the name of "there is one who says" because in Darkei Moshe he wrote about this: one must analyze from siman 185 regarding a niddah who wears niddah garments, where an excuse does not help; perhaps one can distinguish, and this requires study. The Taz also wrote in siman 1 that one should not rule so; see there. Who is suspected. The Shach wrote: specifically if he is an apostate with respect to that matter. But if he is only suspected, he wrote above in siman 119 seif 7 that he is believed. Although there he says, "one who is publicized for a transgression," that means he is publicized as having committed the transgression once, so that he is suspected, but is not established as an apostate. However, in siman 124 seif 9, regarding coerced apostates, they are certainly established. Perhaps, since they do so out of coercion and pressure, we judge them like those merely suspected. Regarding testimony of a gentile, the poskim wrote that he is not believed, neither to prohibit nor to permit. It appears that even if he has no benefit from his testimony, he is not believed to testify about another's property. However, where he speaks casually, its law was already explained in siman 98 seif 1 and siman 313 seif 2 in the gloss; end quote.
