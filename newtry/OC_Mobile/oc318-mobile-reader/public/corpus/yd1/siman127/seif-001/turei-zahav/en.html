Rather, I remained silent in order to collect my thoughts and consider the matter, etc. It appears to me that if he did not say this, but instead said, “I remained silent because I thought that, since I do not know and you know with certainty, I should accept your words; but now I have heard from others that you are not trustworthy,” and he substantiates this through those others who told him so, he is believed through this claim. It is as though he had said from the outset, “I do not know.” A proof for this may be brought from Choshen Mishpat siman 20, where the Rama states that if one brings proof that he was initially unaware of these witnesses, etc., he is believed; the same applies here. But if he initially said, “I remained silent because I did not consider it worthwhile to answer you,” he is not believed. Although in Choshen Mishpat siman 81 we find that one may make such a claim regarding admissions concerning money, that case is different, for he can say, “I knew the truth, that your statement that I owe you 100 would not harm me, since I did not utter anything myself.” Here, however, a person does not ordinarily remain unconcerned when he is told that he is consuming a prohibited substance. Based on this approach, it appears to me that one can resolve what the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Rashba. The distinction made here between whether the person told him the first time or not applies specifically to a worker or a custodian. An ordinary witness, however, is believed even if he did not tell him the first time he encountered him, for perhaps he did not remember the matter the first time, since responsibility for it did not rest upon him, or perhaps he did not wish to testify to him then and afterward reconsidered and told him, end quote. The Darkei Moshe challenged this: Necessarily, the ordinary witness is believed only where the owner remains silent. If so, it is obvious that there is no distinction between the first time and the second time, for in either case the owner's silence is equivalent to an admission. Granted, with a custodian one can properly distinguish, for at the first encounter he was the custodian, whereas later he was not; but where the prohibition arises because of the owner's silence, what difference is there between the first time and the second time? End quote. It appears to me, however, that there is no difficulty at all. One might have thought that the owner can say, “Since you had already seen me and did not tell me anything to separate me from a prohibition, and now you suddenly tell me, I regarded your words merely as jest and mockery, and therefore I did not trouble myself to answer you.” The Rashba therefore teaches that we do not say this. Rather, the witness is believed even in such a case, since he was not the owner's custodian. This is indeed a significant novelty.
