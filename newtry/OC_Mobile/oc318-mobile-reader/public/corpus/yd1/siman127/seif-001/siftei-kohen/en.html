If it is in his hand. The language of the Tur: such as when it was given into his hand to guard it; and he means that “in his hand” does not mean in his hand to libate it, but rather that it was given into his hand to guard it, and it is as if he were its owner over it; and not because of a migu; and so ruled the Rosh in the chapter Nezikin in distinction 11, and Mordechai at the beginning of the chapter HaIsha Rabba; but Beit Yosef did not bring the words of the Rosh and Mordechai, and on the contrary brought simply the words of Rashi, Rabbenu Tam, and the Rashba, who explained: anyone within whose hand it is to defile it; and perhaps for this reason he omitted the language of the Tur here; and likewise in Pri Chadash he explained the reason because it is within his power to diminish it; and the Rashba proved it in his chiddushim in the chapter Nezikin at the beginning of daf 79 with proofs; and it is implied in Tosafot in Kiddushin in the chapter HaOmer (daf 65 side 2) that he is believed because of a migu; and according to this one may consider: one who says to his fellow regarding his boiled wine that it was libated before the boiling is not believed) even if it is in his hand, since he has no migu.
Believed. Even if he did not tell him the first time he found it.
But if it is not in his hand. Meaning: even if it was in his hand initially, nevertheless since it is not now in his hand and he did not tell him the first time, he is not believed; but when it was never in his hand at all, even if he told him the first time, he is not believed when the owner of the wine contradicts him — except when he is silent; so it is explained in Tosafot, the Rosh, Mordechai, and their supporters; and the Mechaber abbreviated the matter.
If the owner contradicts him. And then it is permitted for everyone; and so too the rabbi wrote at the end of the seif; and even if he said “I do not know,” it is permitted for everyone — as written in Or Zarua general law 13 din 13 in the name of R. Chaim; and so too ruled Darkei Moshe.
Or he says, etc. It is written in Mordechai in the chapter HaOmer that if he says to him "perhaps you were not careful," it is considered as if he contradicts him; and Darkei Moshe brings it.
Does not know. See above s.k. 15.
And they teach him, etc. Meaning: they teach him before the witness tells him, that when the witness comes and says to him, he should say to him immediately within the time of speech "I do not believe you"; and they do not teach him to contradict him, for how would they teach him to lie.
And afterward he contradicted him. It appears to me specifically if he contradicts him afterward; but if he told him afterward "I do not know, and that I was silent at first was not because I admitted," etc. — the witness is believed according to all; and this is proven explicitly from the Gemara, from the incident of King Yannai in Kiddushin (daf 66 side 1) — see there and examine.
Some say, etc., he is believed. And above at the end of siman 1, seif 13, the Mechaber wrote simply that he is believed, and the rabbi ruled likewise; and here the rabbi wrote it in the language of “some say” because he wrote concerning it in Darkei Moshe: one must examine in siman 185 regarding a niddah who wears niddah garments, that an excuse does not help — and perhaps one may distinguish; and requires investigation — end of his words; and see above siman 185 s.k. 5, that according to Bach and the rabbi in responsum, this is resolved nicely — see there.
And all who are invalid as witnesses, etc. This law is written in the Darkei Moshe in the name of the Ran, and so it is marked in the Shulchan Aruch, but this requires study, for the Ran in Kiddushin, Perek Ha-omer, writes explicitly that those invalid for testimony are not believed regarding issurim. Yet in responsum siman 25 he rules that even one suspected in that very matter is fit to testify concerning another person's property, for example to say, "this meat was slaughtered," and the like. It appears that one must distinguish: to permit, he is certainly believed to testify, for we hold that one suspected in a matter may judge and testify about it, as above siman 119 s.k. 18. But in Perek Ha-omer the case is explicitly where he comes to prohibit another person's property, such as saying, "your wine became nesech"; there he is believed only because of the litigant's silence, since the owner relies on the words of a valid witness, as the Ran writes there. Therefore, where the witness is invalid, the owner does not rely on him, and he has no power to prohibit another person's property. Still, the Rav's words remain difficult, for the present case is "your wine became nesech," and in such a case the Ran holds that invalid witnesses are not believed. Even if you say he speaks where the witness testifies to permit, that is also difficult, for in that case the Ran holds that even a suspected person is believed, and so we rule above in siman 119 seif 7 and siman 124 seif 9; this requires study. I then went back and examined the Darkei Moshe, whose wording is: the Rashba wrote in responsum siman 544 that two brothers are only like one witness for issurim, whether to uphold a matter or for contradiction; he further wrote, the Ran, siman 430, that even one invalid for monetary testimony is believed to testify about issurim if he is not suspected in that matter. It appears that "he further wrote, the Ran" is a scribal error, for otherwise the reference to siman 430 does not fit, and it should have said simply, "the Ran wrote"; rather it must read, "the Rashba further wrote," for the Rashba wrote in responsum siman 430 that all those invalid for testimony are valid regarding issurim, and even a mumar for one matter is valid for another matter in which he is not involved; see there. This is not as marked in the Shulchan Aruch as Ran. From the Rashba there it also sounds that he speaks of testimony to permit, but when testifying to prohibit it appears that he agrees with the Ran that invalid witnesses are not believed, as implied in the Gemara, Perek Ha-omer, and as I wrote in s.k. 9 in the name of Tosafot and the Mordechai; so too it is implied in the Rashba responsa at the end of siman 376. Still this requires study, for it seems that the Rashba in responsum siman 430 speaks specifically of a mumar, meaning one habituated to the matter, whereas regarding a merely suspected person he wrote in responsum siman 64 that even one suspected in that very matter is believed to testify about another person's property. So too I wrote above, siman 2 s.k. 20, in the name of the Torat Chayyim and the Kesef Mishneh, to distinguish between a mumar and one merely suspected; see there. One may answer that when the Rav says "unless he is suspected in that matter," he means a mumar in that matter, while siman 119 seif 7 speaks of one merely suspected and not a mumar. Although there it says "one who is publicly known for a transgression," that means he is publicly known to have committed the transgression once, so he is suspected but not established as a mumar. However, in siman 124 seif 9 regarding coerced apostates, they are certainly habituated; perhaps since they act under coercion and pressure we judge them like merely suspected persons. Examine carefully. And regarding testimony of non-Jews, the poskim write in many places that a non-Jew is not believed, neither to prohibit nor to permit; see above siman 118 s.k. 37, siman 122 s.k. 3, the end of siman 129, and siman 130 at the end of s.k. 8 in the gloss. It appears that even if he derives no benefit from his testimony, he is not believed to testify about another person's property. Although the Sefer Mitzvot Gadol, negative commandment 137, writes in the name of Rav Hai Gaon that one does not rely on the words of a non-Jew, neither to prohibit nor to permit, and for prohibition brings the final chapter of Yevamot, where a non-Jew was selling fruit in the market and said, "these fruits are orlah, they are from Azekah," and he said nothing, for he intended only to enhance his sale, this does not mean that only where he benefits from his testimony, as there, he is not believed, but otherwise he would be believed to prohibit. For he states the general rule that one never relies on non-Jews. Rather, the reason "he intended only to enhance his sale" is not exclusive; the same applies wherever some pretext can be found. So it is implied in responsa Maharil at the beginning of siman 38, and in the Gemara in the last chapter of Yevamot. Alternatively, only there was that reason needed because the fruits were his, and so it is implied in Torat Chattat, klal 32 din 9. I later found this explicitly in responsa Rashba siman 118; see there. Now the proof from Yevamot is understandable, even though those are the words of Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel and Rabbi disputes him in the Tosefta, saying that he is believed to be stringent but not lenient, and we hold that the halachah follows Rabbi against his colleague. Rather, as the Rosh writes in Perek Elu Megalchin in the name of Maharam, Rabbi disputes only because the fruits are his, but in an ordinary case he agrees with Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. It is also clear there in the Rosh that according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, in an ordinary case a non-Jew is not believed even without the reason that he intended only to enhance his sale, unlike the view of Maharshal that I brought above in siman 122 s.k. 4. Take this rule in hand: always remove the testimony of a non-Jew as though it did not exist; his words neither raise nor lower the status of another person's property. So we say below in siman 190 s.k. 63. However, where he speaks incidentally, mesiach lefi tumo, its law has already been explained in siman 98 s.k. 2 and siman 313 s.k. 4.
