If it is in his hand. The language of the Tur: such as when it was given into his hand to guard it; and he means that “in his hand” does not mean in his hand to libate it, but rather that it was given into his hand to guard it, and it is as if he were its owner over it; and not because of a migu; and so ruled the Rosh in the chapter Nezikin in distinction 11, and Mordechai at the beginning of the chapter HaIsha Rabba; but Beit Yosef did not bring the words of the Rosh and Mordechai, and on the contrary brought simply the words of Rashi, Rabbenu Tam, and the Rashba, who explained: anyone within whose hand it is to defile it; and perhaps for this reason he omitted the language of the Tur here; and likewise in Pri Chadash he explained the reason because it is within his power to diminish it; and the Rashba proved it in his chiddushim in the chapter Nezikin at the beginning of daf 79 with proofs; and it is implied in Tosafot in Kiddushin in the chapter HaOmer (daf 65 side 2) that he is believed because of a migu; and according to this one may consider: one who says to his fellow regarding his boiled wine that it was libated before the boiling is not believed) even if it is in his hand, since he has no migu.
Believed. Even if he did not tell him the first time he found it.
If he told him the first time, etc., even if the owner contradicts him. This is indeed the view of Tosafos, the Rosh, Mordechai, and many other poskim. It is difficult, however, because the general rule is that one witness does not rise against a man except for an oath. Although one witness is believed in matters of prohibition, that is where the owner does not contradict him, or where the witness has a migo. Where neither applies, it is not reasonable that one witness should be believed to contradict the owner and cause him monetary loss. Even where there is a migo, there is some difficulty, for the Rosh and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in Choshen Mishpat, siman 108, seif 14, rule that he is not believed. Similar questions were raised by Tosafos, the Rosh, and other poskim regarding Rashi's explanation of a seller who says to whom he sold when the object is no longer in his possession, even though it had previously been in his possession. There is some room to distinguish; see there. The clearest approach to the Gemara is that of the Raavad and Rashba, who explain the passage in Gittin concerning one who finds him and tells him immediately as a case where the owner remains silent, and they distinguish a worker, whose credibility is weaker than that of an ordinary witness. The Ran in Gittin writes likewise, as do the later authorities cited by the Ran in Kiddushin. This is also Rambam's view at the end of Hilchos Pesulei HaMukdashin and at the end of Hilchos Metamei Mishkav uMoshav: he is believed specifically when the owner is silent. See there carefully.
Believed. The Rama writes in responsum siman 66 that if the owner contradicts him and says that the witness did not tell him the first time, the owner is certainly believed. See there further regarding these laws.
But if it is not in his hand. Meaning: even if it was in his hand initially, nevertheless since it is not now in his hand and he did not tell him the first time, he is not believed; but when it was never in his hand at all, even if he told him the first time, he is not believed when the owner of the wine contradicts him — except when he is silent; so it is explained in Tosafot, the Rosh, Mordechai, and their supporters; and the Mechaber abbreviated the matter.
If the owner contradicts him. And then it is permitted for everyone; and so too the rabbi wrote at the end of the seif; and even if he said “I do not know,” it is permitted for everyone — as written in Or Zarua general law 13 din 13 in the name of R. Chaim; and so too ruled Darkei Moshe.

Does not know. See above s.k. 15.
But if he remained silent, etc. Meaning, even where this is another person who never had the item in his possession and did not tell him the first time, nevertheless silence is always treated as admission. The Tur writes that another person who does not have it in his possession, or a custodian who did not tell the owner the first time and tells him only afterward, is not believed specifically where the owners contradict him or say, 'I do not know'; but if they remain silent, silence is like admission. The Bach infers from the Tur's wording that even a custodian who did not tell him immediately is believed if the owner remains silent. This is correct. The Rashba's contrary distinction follows his own view that the witness is believed even where the owner's silence is not an admission, but the Tur holds that the credibility here rests on the owner's silence. Therefore there is no distinction between a custodian and another person: whenever the owner remains silent in circumstances where his silence is an admission, the witness is believed. This is also clear from Tosafos, the Rosh, and Mordechai.
Silence is like admission. It is not literally an admission, for then the ruling would be obvious and there would be no need for the witness's credibility. Moreover, Kiddushin 66a proves that if the witness was a robber, he is not believed even when the owner remains silent. Rather, the silence indicates that the owner recognizes some basis for the matter and relies upon the witness. Tosafos at the beginning of Yevamos, Tosafos in Kiddushin, s.v. Rava, and Mordechai write this. The Ran's strained explanation of the phrase 'silence is like admission' therefore requires study, for these words of Tosafos escaped his attention.
And they teach him, etc. Meaning: they teach him before the witness tells him, that when the witness comes and says to him, he should say to him immediately within the time of speech "I do not believe you"; and they do not teach him to contradict him, for how would they teach him to lie.
Although another witness contradicts him, it does not help. Darkei Moshe derives this from Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 11, though I did not find it in the version before me. Issur VeHeter, kelal 13, din 13, writes so in the name of Semag. The rule nevertheless requires study. Abaye derives the credibility of one witness where the owner remains silent from the Mishnah in Kerisos: if one witness says, 'You ate forbidden fat,' and the person says, 'I did not eat,' he is exempt, implying that had he remained silent the witness would be believed. Yet the beginning of that same Mishnah states that if one witness says he ate and another says he did not eat, he brings an asham talui. This necessarily concerns a case where he remains silent; otherwise the ruling would be obvious. Thus even after his silence, the contradicting witness is effective to remove the certainty created by the first witness. Even according to the approach of Tosafos, the Rosh, the Tur, and their school that silence shows some basis for the matter, the owner may be relying on the contradicting witness, or may recognize some basis for both possibilities. One cannot dismiss this as unique to forbidden fat, because even if that category is more susceptible to doubt, the sources cited by Semag and Issur VeHeter state that the contradicting witness does not permit the wine even for himself. If the matter were merely doubtful, there would be strong grounds to permit it to that witness himself, just as in certain cases of conflicting testimony concerning a married woman the woman need not leave the witness who testified that her husband died. It therefore appears that according to Semag and Issur VeHeter there remains at least a prohibition l'chatchilah, while b'diavad one should not be stringent for the contradicting witness himself.
It helps. The wording is difficult: what does his contradiction accomplish when, even without it, the first witness's testimony has no force while the owner is absent, since according to this approach he must say, 'It was libated in your presence,' or 'You knew grounds for the matter'? It can be answered that the contradiction helps in that even if the first witness later repeats the statement in the owner's presence and the owner remains silent, he is no longer believed. Since the silence is not a literal admission but only shows that the owner recognizes some basis for the witness's words and relies on him, once that witness has already been contradicted the owner no longer relies on him. This remains difficult as a matter of practical law.
And there is one who writes, etc. The Rama's words require extensive study. They imply that according to the cited view, identified as Rashba in responsum siman 545, even when the owners are absent the second witness who says that the wine was not libated has no effect. The Levush explains that since the Sages believed the witness who says it was libated, he is like two witnesses, so one witness cannot contradict him. This cannot be correct. Rashba's actual responsum says that one witness in a matter of prohibition is nothing when contradicted by the owner, even if the owner is not before him, because one witness cannot forbid another person's property against that person's denial. The phrase 'one witness under contradiction is nothing' refers to the witness whose testimony is contradicted, not to the contradicting witness. This is the meaning of the Gemara in Kiddushin and the understanding of the Ran, Maharik, Terumas HaDeshen, and other poskim. Moreover, according to the Rama's own ruling that the witness who says the wine was libated is believed only through the owner's silence, when the owner is absent the witness cannot be believed at all, even without any contradicting witness. Issur VeHeter also states explicitly that when the owner is absent anyone may contradict the witness, because the required admission through silence is lacking. The better reading of the Rama is therefore that the phrase 'there is one who writes that one witness under contradiction is nothing' refers back to the earlier case where the owner is present but silent: once a second witness contradicts the first, the first witness is no longer effective. This can be inferred from Rashba's general formulation and from the Ran, who likewise treats a single witness under contradiction as ineffective even where the interested party remains silent. The case of forbidden fat, where conflicting witnesses can create an asham talui, is different because even a person whose own heart troubles him brings an asham talui. The practical conclusion is that where one witness says, 'Your wine was libated,' and another contradicts him while the owner remains silent, according to Semag and Issur VeHeter the wine is doubtful and the contradicting witness himself should not drink it l'chatchilah; according to the Ran, and apparently Rashba, the first witness is entirely neutralized and the wine is permitted. Where the owner is absent, under the Tur's approach the first witness is not believed in any event, and the second witness certainly prevents his testimony from creating a prohibition. There is also a strong reason to retain the original presumption of permissibility when the witnesses are balanced. If the witnesses came one after the other, however, and the first witness's testimony had already been accepted and acted upon, then according to Tosafos and many poskim his testimony can acquire the force of two and a later single witness cannot overturn it. The Ritva distinguishes ordinary prohibitions from matters such as marital status where the Sages explicitly believed one witness as two. Maharshal and Bach follow that distinction, while Maharik, the Mechaber, the Rosh, Mordechai, and apparently the Rama extend the rule more broadly. In any event, where the two witnesses come simultaneously, even Maharik's school does not treat the first as two; the special case of a shochet is different because the presumption that those engaged in slaughter are competent supports his testimony.
Even though in his heart he believes him like two witnesses, etc. This appears to mean that although his conscience troubles him and he is convinced that the witness would not have said the wine was libated without clear knowledge, nevertheless if he expressly says, 'I do not believe you,' we do not impose the prohibition. The Levush appears to understand it this way, and Mordechai can be read that way. In my humble opinion, however, Rabbeinu Tam never intended that a person who truly believes the witness like two should lie and say that he does not believe him. Rather, Rabbeinu Tam means that even if the witness had previously enjoyed such credibility with him, he may now say, 'I do not believe you,' meaning: in this matter I do not accord you the credibility of two. If the owner says this, we accept that he truly does not believe him like two and do not say that his prior trust permanently gave the witness the status of two. R. Meir of Rothenburg and Raavyah disagree in any event, and the Bach rules stringently where he truly believes the witness in his heart.
Provided that he says to him, 'It was libated in your presence,' or says, 'You knew grounds for the matter,' etc. Otherwise silence is not like admission, because the owner may simply remain silent because he does not know the facts. This is the approach of Tosafos, the Rosh, and Mordechai, cited by Beit Yosef. Rashba, the Ran, Ritva, Nimukei Yosef, Raavan, and other authorities disagree and hold that even silence resulting from lack of knowledge can allow one witness to establish the prohibition. Rambam also appears to follow this view. In my work I have shown that this approach is supported by the incidents of the blind man and King Yannai in Kiddushin, by the discussion at the beginning of Yevamos concerning one witness in prohibitions, by the opening sugyos of Gittin and Yevamos, by the law of a mikveh cited by Beit Yosef in siman 201, and by the Mishnah in Kerisos. Those sources indicate at minimum that the witness's credibility in ordinary prohibitions where no prior prohibition is established does not depend upon a formal admission by silence, and there is substantial basis to extend this even where a prior status exists. Nevertheless, Semak, Sefer HaTerumah, Hagahos Maimoniyos, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Agudah, and Issur VeHeter follow the Tur's approach. All agree, however, that a worker whose task was to keep the item permitted has weaker credibility after it is no longer in his possession and after he failed to report the problem at the first opportunity, because we presume that a worker entrusted to perform the task properly was careful.

What I remained silent for, etc., was only in order to consider the matter, etc. Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise writes this at the end of netiv 15. Knesses HaGedolah, part 2, however, writes that if the witness said, 'It was libated in your presence,' and the owner remained silent and later said, 'I was silent only because I paid no attention to the witness's words,' this does not help, since one witness is believed in matters of prohibition. And all those disqualified from testimony, etc. Darkei Moshe attributes this rule to the Ran, but this requires study. The Ran in Kiddushin explicitly writes that persons disqualified from testimony are not believed in matters of prohibition, while in responsum siman 25 he rules that even one suspected concerning a particular prohibition may testify regarding another person's property, such as to say that meat was properly slaughtered. It appears that one must distinguish: to permit, he is certainly believed, since one suspected regarding a matter may judge and testify concerning it for others, as explained above in siman 119, seif katan 18. The discussion in Kiddushin concerns one who comes to prohibit another person's property, such as by saying, 'Your wine was libated.' There he is believed only together with the owner's silence, because the owner relies on the statement of a qualified witness; if the witness is disqualified, the owner does not rely on him. Yet this remains difficult for the Rama, because the present discussion concerns one who says that another's wine was libated. Upon reexamining Darkei Moshe, I found that it cites Rashba in responsum siman 544 that two brothers count only as one witness regarding prohibitions, whether to establish a matter or to contradict it, and then cites another responsum that all those disqualified for monetary testimony are nevertheless qualified in matters of prohibition if they are not suspected regarding that particular matter. The attribution to the Ran appears to be a scribal error; it should read Rashba. Rashba's responsum siman 430 states that those disqualified from testimony are qualified regarding prohibitions, and even an apostate regarding one matter is qualified regarding another. Nevertheless, Rashba appears there to discuss testimony that permits; where the witness comes to prohibit another person's property he may agree with the Ran that a disqualified witness is not believed. There is also room to distinguish an apostate who is habitually transgressive from one merely suspected because of a single act. Regarding testimony of non-Jews, the poskim write in many places that a non-Jew is not believed either to prohibit or to permit. Even where he has no personal benefit from the testimony, his statement concerning another person's property is disregarded. Rav Hai Gaon explains the rule through the case in Yevamos of a non-Jew selling fruit and declaring it orlah or produce of Azekah: his statement has no effect, for he intends only to enhance his merchandise. This rationale is not limited to cases of direct benefit; whenever there is some plausible explanation for his statement, we disregard it. The general principle is that testimony of a non-Jew is treated as though it were absent and neither establishes nor removes a prohibition concerning another person's property. Where he speaks incidentally, however, the applicable rules were explained in siman 98, seif katan 2, and siman 313, seif katan 4.
