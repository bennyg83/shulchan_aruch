The Shach, s.k. 26, writes that if she feels that this blood comes from the wound, she may attribute it to the wound, for the concern that she would never become impure does not apply: if she feels otherwise she will become impure. He further argues that this proof would also be difficult according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, and also according to the conclusion there that the dispute concerns whether vesetot are rabbinic. Therefore he decides that if she feels this blood is from the wound, she is pure. He also writes that what the Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Hagahat Perishah wrote, that the Rambam in chapter 1 of Hilchot Issurei Biah disagrees, is not so, for the Rambam merely copied the language of the Shas. In truth, although his interpretation is forced, for if the Rambam spoke specifically where she now feels it comes from the wound, he should have explained that if she does not feel so she is impure, nevertheless one may say that the Rambam holds there is no distinction at all between whether she says she feels it comes from the wound or not. The Rambam wrote regarding Issurei Biah that even if her whole body trembled she may attribute it to urine; likewise here he may hold that even if she says it certainly came from the makor, we do not heed this, but say she erred, as the Rashba writes: are there eyes in that place to know whether her sensation came from the wound? Therefore he may purify her forever by attributing it to the wound. Still, this is no proof. As to the law itself, although I am unworthy to raise my head against the light of Israel, the gaon Shach, and Heaven forbid that I should wish to overturn his words, especially since the Shach's words are the words of Tosafot on Niddah 16, nevertheless I come only to discuss humbly. In my weak opinion there is room to question this permission. What compelled me, besides the fact that the Rama here rules simply without distinction, is that in siman 188 regarding forms like tiny creatures he rules otherwise; the matter therefore requires explanation.
And in another way one may answer the Shach's question: if so, this proof would also be difficult according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. According to the Shach's approach, the same question, "she will never become impure," would apply to the Rashba, who purifies forever, and to the Rambam as understood by the Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Perishah. One may say the opposite. Certainly, if she saw without any sensation at all, then by Torah law she is completely permitted, especially where there is a wound. Even if one does not accept what I proved in siman 184, that there is no chazakah that all who see always see with sensation except where there is doubt of the sensation of a cloth or organ, nevertheless here, since she has a wound that emits blood, that chazakah certainly does not apply. Since vesetot are rabbinic, we do not say she felt and did not notice, and this is a rabbinic doubt against the majority that women see with sensation, so one may be lenient. But where she felt and saw, and she attributes both the sighting and the sensation to the wound, from where should we be lenient in a Torah-level doubt of sensation, perhaps only because she erred? In that case perhaps the Rashba and Rambam agree that she is impure. They speak only where there was no sensation at all, and then they can properly purify forever. The Rama's question, that she would never become impure, is not difficult, because if she feels, they would agree she is impure. According to this, the Shach's question from Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel is also not difficult: the dispute speaks without sensation, as no sensation is mentioned at all. Indeed, it must be without sensation, for if it were with sensation as the Shach understands, how could Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel say we are not concerned at all? One can find a case where she feels it certainly comes from the makor, where she is obviously impure. Rather, the matter does not speak of sensation at all. Tosafot there, however, do not accept this distinction and write explicitly like the Shach. Still, according to the above one can align the Rashba's words with this explanation: since it is only a doubt whether the blood is from the wound, where there is no sensation and vesetot are rabbinic, we may attribute it to the wound; but with sensation it is a Torah doubt.
But according to what I wrote above, that the dispute of Rabbi and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel concerns only niddah impurity, while regarding a ketem all agree, one may say the same in the Rambam. When he says she is pure, that means from niddah impurity, but regarding ketem she is certainly impure, for we have found no one who disputes this according to what we wrote above. See the Rambam in Hilchot Mishkav UMoshav chapter 3, where he writes that all these impurities are only rabbinic and one does not burn kodashim for them. It is also explained there several times that the makor itself is impure, but this is only the impurity of a ketem. If so, he properly writes here that the woman is pure and the blood is pure, as he wrote in Hilchot Mishkav UMoshav, meaning pure from niddah impurity; but both are impure with the impurity of a ketem. This also resolves what otherwise requires attention in the Rambam: he writes that vesetot are rabbinic, as in Hilchot Mishkav UMoshav, and several times in Hilchot Issurei Biah rules leniently for that reason, while in the immediately preceding halachah about examination he writes that she is pure, and the Maggid Mishneh gives the reason that vesetot are rabbinic, yet the Rambam does not mention it there in the same wording. His intent is as we wrote: there it is explained that blood that comes through the makor is impure only as a ketem, and so too regarding the woman he holds she is pure from niddah status. He wrote the matter briefly and relied on what he had explained in Hilchot Mishkav UMoshav. According to our words, the Rambam and all the poskim, were it not for the Tosafot mentioned above, would agree in law with the Rama that she is impure at the time of her veset and that there is no distinction whether she feels it came from the wound or not. Nevertheless, who am I, the small one, to stand in the king's palace and raise my head against the light of Israel, the gaon Shach, from whose waters we drink thirstily, especially since all his words are the words of Tosafot? I have only spoken to defend the gaon Rama and remove the Shach's complaint from him.
