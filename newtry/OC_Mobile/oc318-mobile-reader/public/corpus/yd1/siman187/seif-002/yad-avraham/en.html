If she has no fixed veset and it is uncertain whether the blood came from the source or from the sides, we attribute it to the wound by means of a double uncertainty: perhaps it came from the sides, and even if it came from the source, perhaps it came from the wound, although she does not know that her wound discharges blood. It appears that in this case the Rama permits her entirely, so that she does not require seven clean days. This is more favorable than the case of a woman with a fixed veset who knows that the blood came from the source, where the Shach permits only with respect to not establishing her as one who sees blood due to relations, but not to render her entirely pure. Here, the double uncertainty is stronger, since a double uncertainty is permitted in all prohibitions. This is also implied by the Taz at the beginning of siman 190. The later authorities raised many objections to the Rama, but in my opinion his words are correct, as I shall explain. The Shach objects that the Hagahot Maimoniyot and Sefer HaTerumah imply that even if she did not sense that the blood fell from the womb, but found blood when she examined that place, she is impure if she does not know that the wound discharges blood. This is no difficulty in light of the Minchat Yaakov at the beginning of siman 190, who explains that the possibility that the blood came from the sides is treated as an uncertainty, despite the rule that most blood comes from the source, because that majority is weakened by the fact that most women do not see blood from the source due to relations. Accordingly, the Rama permitted only one who sees blood due to relations. When she examines herself at another time and finds blood, one cannot be lenient on the possibility that it came from the sides, since most blood comes from the source. Nor, according to the Minchat Yaakov, does the existence of a wound itself weaken that majority, for then both uncertainties would have the same basis, as he writes there. There is therefore no contradiction between the Hagahot Maimoniyot, which discusses an examination not made during relations, and the Rama, who discusses seeing blood due to relations. More fundamentally, the Shach's objection is resolved by the Taz at the beginning of siman 190, who explains that the Rama's uncertainty whether the blood came from the sides means that perhaps the wound itself is on the sides. This possibility is treated as a genuine uncertainty despite the rule that most blood comes from the source, because that rule applies to a woman without a wound. Here, since she has a wound and it may be on the sides, the majority is weakened. Thus, the Hagahot Maimoniyot and Sefer HaTerumah may concern a wound known to be in the source itself. In such a case, the Rama agrees that we do not attribute the blood to the sides, because where there is no wound on the sides, most blood certainly does not come from there. When the uncertainty is whether a wound exists on the sides, however, one may be lenient, and perhaps the Hagahot Maimoniyot and Sefer HaTerumah agree. This also resolves the Chavot Daat's objection from the Mishnah that blood found in the corridor is presumed to have come from the source. Noda BiYehudah, siman 55, already answered that in Shevuot the Mishnah is established as referring to a time near her veset. According to the foregoing, the distinction is simpler, since here the uncertainty is whether the wound is on the sides. The Minchat Yaakov objects to the Taz that if the uncertainty regarding the sides also arises from the wound, both uncertainties have one basis, since neither permits more than the other. The Sidrei Taharah answers that the possibility of blood from the sides is more permissive, because then even the blood itself is pure, whereas if it came from a wound in the source, the blood is impure, since the Rambam rules that the source renders what is in its place impure. At first glance, this is difficult, because the Sidrei Taharah himself writes in siman 188 that only blood formed in the source is impure, whereas blood from a wound in the source is pure, as the Rambam himself writes in chapter 8 of Hilchot Issurei Biah. If so, blood from a wound in the source is no more impure than blood from a wound on the sides, and neither uncertainty permits more than the other. Perhaps he follows the view of responsa Shemen Rokeach, siman 41, that when the Rambam says blood from a wound in the source is pure, he means only that after it separates it does not have the impurity of menstrual blood, but while it remains in the source it renders the woman impure until evening. According to this, the double uncertainty is indeed based on two distinct grounds, because the possibility that the blood came from the sides permits more: then the blood is pure even while in its place, unlike blood from a wound in the source. The Sidrei Taharah, however, was overly brief where elaboration was required. This differs from Noda BiYehudah, first edition, Yoreh Deah, siman 41, and elsewhere, who equates blood from a wound in the source with blood from the sides for purposes of purifying both the woman and the blood. See Beit Ephraim, siman 50. I have a further defense of the Rama, that the double uncertainty indeed rests on two different grounds with respect to rendering something susceptible to impurity. We rule that blood from an actual wound renders susceptible, whereas blood from the sides not caused by an actual wound does not. Mahari'l Rokeach writes this, as cited at the end of Sefer Sidrei Taharah. Although I wrote that blood from the sides also arises because of a “wound,” and the Rosh likewise uses that term, it need not mean an actual wound. Rather, ordinarily there is no blood on the sides naturally, but some small abrasion or pustule discharges blood. The Rambam writes explicitly in chapter 10 of Hilchot Tumat Ochalin that blood from boils and pustules does not render susceptible, and the same is evident from the commentary of the Bartenura to chapter 6 of Machshirin. Thus, the double uncertainty rests upon two distinct grounds. Accordingly, Rabbi Baruch of Prague's objection to Mahari'l Rokeach, that blood from the sides also renders susceptible because it comes from an injury and is therefore blood of a wound, is not compelling. It may come from an ordinary boil or pustule, whose blood does not render susceptible. I later saw that the Noda BiYehudah, second edition, Yoreh Deah, siman 107, was asked about this matter. He first answered like Rabbi Baruch, and then wrote that even blood from boils and pustules renders susceptible, as does all blood drawn not for healing. He explained the Rambam's statement that blood from boils and pustules does not render susceptible as referring to blood that has become putrid or emerges mixed with putrid mucus. He understood the Mishnah in Machshirin, which states that putrid mucus, excrement, and blood emerging with them neither convey impurity nor render susceptible, as applying because they have become putrid. This is difficult. If so, why did the Raavad not object to the Rambam according to his own view that food or liquid that spoiled and is no longer fit for human consumption still receives impurity as long as it is fit for a dog, a rule he derives from Torat Kohanim? Moreover, according to Noda BiYehudah, the Mishnah in Machshirin would contradict Torat Kohanim, and the Raavad would be ruling like Torat Kohanim against our Mishnah. It is strained to say that the Mishnah concerns blood so putrid that even a dog would not consume it. Rather, the Mishnah's reason is not that the blood became putrid, but that blood emerging with mucus and excrement is not called blood of a wound and was not drawn for drinking. It therefore does not render susceptible, because blood is considered a liquid only when drawn for drinking, or when it is actual blood from a wound, which resembles the blood of the slain. Blood from boils and pustules is not included in “the slain.” Noda BiYehudah's proofs can all be rejected. He infers from the Mishnah's wording “blood that emerges with them does not render susceptible” that blood by itself would render susceptible, for otherwise it should have said “blood that emerges from there.” One may answer that this wording was chosen because of Rabbi Eliezer, who disputes the latter clause. It teaches that in the first clause he agrees that when the blood emerges with them it does not render susceptible because it is putrid, but the first Tanna's underlying reason is not that. Similarly, the first Tanna's phrase “bloodletting for healing does not render susceptible” does not imply that all bloodletting not for healing does render susceptible, as Noda BiYehudah writes. It was formulated because of Rabbi Eliezer, who disputes and says that even blood drawn for healing is impure and renders susceptible. According to the first Tanna, however, even blood drawn not for healing does not render susceptible unless it was drawn for drinking, as the Mishnah states earlier, or unless it is actual blood of a wound. Blood from a boil is not included. Noda BiYehudah further proves that all blood emerging from a person renders susceptible when not for healing, even if not from a wound, from the Rambam's ruling that blood emerging from the urethral opening or dripping from the mouth is impure and included in blood of his wound. This is not comparable. Blood from the urethral opening or mouth is known to arise only from a wound, as stated in Gittin regarding blood emerging from the mouth. It is therefore included in “blood of the slain,” as Tosafot in Chullin, page 35b, explain that a person's wound blood renders susceptible because “what difference is there between killing him entirely and killing him partially?” If the blood does not arise from a wound that can be called partial killing, it does not render susceptible even if drawn not for healing, such as blood from boils and pustules. According to the explanation I have given of the Rama, that this is properly considered a double uncertainty resting on two different grounds, in accordance with Mahari'l Rokeach, one can also answer the objection raised at the end of Torat Yekutiel from the beginning of the second chapter of Niddah, page 20, concerning one who miscarries a piece of tissue. According to the foregoing there is no comparison. There, the uncertainties certainly share one basis, since if the blood came from the piece it likewise does not render susceptible, just like blood from the sides. Blood contained in a miscarried piece is no more significant than blood of an eight-month stillborn, which does not render susceptible. It is therefore not comparable to blood from a wound in the source, which does render susceptible. According to what I have written, one may also permit the case discussed in responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 78, of a woman who developed a wound in the rectal area, known as hemorrhoids, because of a difficult birth, found blood, and during her examination touched the wound. Here too there is a double uncertainty based on two grounds: perhaps the blood came from the sides because of an ordinary boil or pustule, which does not render susceptible, and perhaps it came from the actual wound, which does render susceptible. This differs from Rabbi Akiva Eiger's conclusion there that a wound in the rectal area is equivalent to a wound on the sides and therefore there is no double uncertainty. The Shach further objects to the Rama that this is not a double uncertainty, since one may immediately say, “Perhaps it is menstrual blood,” and such a case is not considered a double uncertainty, as he writes in the laws of double uncertainty, siman 10, rulings 11 and 13. This has already been answered by the Minchat Yaakov in siman 190, seif katan 37, and in Kuntres HaSfeikot: here the uncertainties need not be reversible. Responsa Shemen Rokeach, part 1, siman 40, and Noda BiYehudah, siman 41, write likewise.
