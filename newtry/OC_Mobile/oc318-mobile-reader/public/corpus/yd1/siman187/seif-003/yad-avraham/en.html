And regarding stains, she may attribute them to the wound in every circumstance. It appears that the same applies to a flow seen from her body without sensation, which is only rabbinically prohibited, even when not during relations. This too creates a double uncertainty, and the possibility that the blood came from the sides is properly treated as an uncertainty. Although most blood comes from the source, since most women sense when they see blood, that majority is weakened. The Minchat Yaakov writes similarly there regarding ordinary stains. In the ordinary case we are stringent for the reason given by the Kesef Mishneh, that there is only one uncertainty, namely, perhaps the blood came from the upper chamber. One cannot add the possibility that it came from elsewhere, since there is nothing upon which to attribute it. Where there is a wound, however, there is a double uncertainty, and one may permit her even to her husband. I later saw that Noda BiYehudah, siman 71, writes this explicitly: even during the time of her veset, if she does not know that the wound discharges blood, but the wound is in the source and she has no sensation, the double uncertainty permits her, and she remains pure until she senses blood from the source. See Sefer Nachalat Azriel, who permits a woman whose examination produces tiny red grains resembling sand, while during her veset she sees actual blood. She feels no pain, but recently developed pain in her kidneys and back. This applies where she does not sense the discharge of the grains. Even if she senses something, where a pure-colored discharge also emerges, she attributes the sensation to the pure discharge and the red particles to the wound in her kidneys. Moreover, the grains cannot be properly tested, and perhaps they would not dissolve in water. Furthermore, with blood of such an unusual appearance we do not apply the rule that most blood comes from the source.
