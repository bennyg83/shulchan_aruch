(In the gloss there) And regarding stains, she may attribute them to the wound in every circumstance. It appears that the same applies to blood seen from her body without sensation, which is only rabbinically impure, even when not during intercourse. There is a valid double doubt: perhaps the blood came from the sides. Although most blood comes from the uterus, most women feel the onset of uterine bleeding, so the majority is weakened. The Minchat Yaakov writes similarly concerning ordinary stains. There we are stringent because, as the Kesef Mishneh explains, there is only one doubt, perhaps it came from the upper chamber; one cannot add that perhaps it came from elsewhere when there is nothing to which it can be attributed. Where a wound exists, however, there is a double doubt, and she may be permitted even to her husband. I later found this stated explicitly in Noda BiYehudah, responsum 71: even during her expected period, when she does not know that the wound bleeds, if the wound is in the uterus and she feels no bleeding, the double doubt permits her, and she remains pure until she senses uterine blood. See also Nachalat Azriel, which permits a woman whose examination produces tiny red granules like sand, while during her period she sees ordinary blood. She feels no pain from the granules, though recently she developed pain in her kidneys and back. This applies when she does not sense the granules emerging. Even if she feels something, where a pure-colored discharge also emerges she may attribute the sensation to that pure discharge and the red material to a kidney wound. Furthermore, the granules cannot be properly examined and may not dissolve in water. With blood of such an unusual character, the rule that most blood comes from the uterus was not stated.
