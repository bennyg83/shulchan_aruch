Like a synagogue, etc. Ramban and Ran wrote that one should wonder at Rambam, for his words contradict each other — for at the beginning of the chapter of partners, Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and the Sages dispute regarding partners who vowed benefit from each other: Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov holds that both can enter the shared courtyard, for each one enters his own portion; and the Sages forbid. Rabbah said: the dispute is regarding a courtyard that has a law of division, but regarding one that has no law of division, all agree it is permitted. Rav Yosef said to him: but behold a synagogue, which is like a courtyard that has no law of division — and yet we learned "both are forbidden" — like whom does the Mishnah follow? Rather, Rav Yosef said: the dispute is regarding a matter that has no law of division, but regarding one that has a law of division, all agree it is forbidden. Ran (48b) wrote: the Mishnah that teaches they are forbidden in a synagogue follows the Sages, not Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, as we say above that a synagogue is like a courtyard without a law of division; therefore since the halachah was decided like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov who permits when there is no law of division, the Mishnah saying both are forbidden in a synagogue does not stand. One should wonder at Rambam, who ruled both like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and like the Mishnah — and likewise Ramban challenged: how does one transfer a document to two parties — end quote. Beit Yosef brought this challenge. If so, one should wonder also at Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch, who all wrote like Rambam — that is, here they wrote they are forbidden in a synagogue, and in siman 226 they wrote both are permitted in a matter without a law of division — yet a synagogue has no law of division as mentioned. Heaven forbid to say these great authorities, including Rambam, erred in this. My father-in-law z"l resolved this challenge, saying it was difficult for them — halachah against halachah, the halachah being like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and the halachah being like the anonymous Mishnah that forbids in a synagogue — therefore they resolved that a synagogue is different: it is forbidden even without a law of division, since there is a remedy whereby they transfer their portion to another and can afterwards return to use it, and this does not appear as a ruse since many use it there — unlike a courtyard of two partners, where giving one's portion to another and using it appears as a ruse. And Rav Yosef's challenge from the synagogue was because he did not hold this distinction, but we follow the conclusion with this distinction — end quote. But this is not precise at all: is it that in every matter without a law of division one cannot say that one party acquires another's portion? Certainly in a partners' courtyard too it does not appear as a ruse — for what is it different from siman 221 seif 9, where we say one gives a gift to another, etc., and we are not concerned it appears as a ruse, and he himself may benefit from it afterwards since it has left his domain — for if not, the Talmud and poskim should have written that he himself may not benefit from it afterwards on account of the ruse, which would be a great novelty; but certainly not, and as Ran wrote explicitly that in every case it helps to give one's portion to another, and there is no distinction between a synagogue and other matters without a law of division. Further — to what does the "conclusion" refer? We have before us only an anonymous Mishnah that forbids in a synagogue — who gave us authority to dispute Rav Yosef? And did Rav Yosef not see the Mishnah as it is? Furthermore — if we say Rav Yosef's challenge is resolved, then Rabbah's statement remains, that regarding a matter with a law of division there is a dispute and the halachah is like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov who permits — and why do we rule in siman 226 they are forbidden regarding a matter with a law of division? Rather, perforce no one disputes Rav Yosef, and the challenge returns to its place.
What seems correct to me is that the Talmudic sugya is well explained according to Rambam and the other great authorities mentioned — and this is by being precise in the Talmud's language: "behold a synagogue, which is like a courtyard that has no law of division" — it does not say it has no law of division, but rather there appear to be three categories. First: there is a complete law of division, like a courtyard that has enough for each party — it is reasonable they are forbidden. Second: there is no law of division at all, i.e., a courtyard that lacks four cubits — it is impossible to say they are forbidden. Third: an intermediate case, i.e., a synagogue and the like, where partial division is possible — this one withdraws from his section and that one from his section and can pray in his portion — only there is no way to divide it since many enter there; and likewise the ark. And because of this it then says "and the books" in the plural — meaning there are two Torah scrolls, so there is enough for division; but if there were only one scroll it would be a matter without division and it would be permitted. Thus some aspects are called "having a law of division" and some "not having a law of division" since there is no way to actually divide it. Now it is well: when Rabbah initially said the dispute is regarding a matter with a law of division — that refers to complete division, in the courtyard mentioned in the Mishnah in the dispute of Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and the Sages — there specifically the Sages hold it is forbidden; but in the intermediate matter, i.e., a synagogue, all agree it is permitted. And on this Rav Yosef challenges well: but we learned both are forbidden in a synagogue, and that is like no one. Rav Yosef answers: thus he says — they dispute regarding a matter with absolutely no law of division, i.e., the courtyard in the Mishnah's dispute; and according to this, the intermediate matter, i.e., a synagogue, falls under the category of a matter with a law of division, and all agree it is forbidden. Accordingly the Mishnah that both are forbidden in a synagogue holds for everyone, and therefore they ruled it as halachah. So it seems correct to me to resolve clearly and properly, though Ramban and Ran left it as a challenge — nonetheless Rambam, Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch are not in error, heaven forbid, especially in a matter explicit in the Talmud, and it is fitting to advocate for them as much as we can, especially in an abundant matter, with Heaven's help, as written.
