Even by way of breaking. I hope I see here that his words are wondrous — they follow Ran, chapter 1 of Chullin; and thus: and there is in this a leniency — that if he slaughtered one animal and touched its joint-bone and a flaw was found, his shechitah is valid, for when he broke bones it is similar; and it is reasonable that we cannot rely on this leniency, since the halachah was not decided like Rav Chisda except when he broke — one may not be lenient thus unless he broke the bone entirely; but not by touch — and so is Ramban's view; and others are further stringent to say that even if he broke the joint-bone itself, his shechitah is invalid; and it is possible to explain "he broke bones" — the joint-bone is soft; and furthermore, even other bones that if he broke them like the joint-bone bone we do not attribute — specifically breaking they say; but cutting by holacha u'va as he does with the joint-bone — not; and so is Rashba's view — end of his words. Behold the rabbi, author of Shulchan Aruch, understood what Ran wrote and "others are stringent" — even if he broke the joint-bone itself means even by way of breaking, namely by striking, not by holacha u'va; and perforce he ruled here: even by way of breaking it is forbidden. The examiner will see with his eyes that Ran did not intend this, for he had not yet mentioned anything of the distinction between striking or holacha u'va until he wrote afterward; and furthermore, "even if he broke other," etc. — he then invented this distinction; and since way of breaking is permitted even according to the third view, all the more according to the second; rather simply what Ran said first, that even if he broke the joint-bone itself, means by holacha u'va. Rather he comes to exclude mere touch, where at first he did no breaking in his cutting; but even if he did actual breaking in his cutting and something was missing in the place of the cut — nevertheless we do not attribute, for two reasons: one, that it is soft and that cut does not spoil the knife; and furthermore, even if it were hard like other bones, we do not attribute, since the cutting was done by holacha u'va — the knife is not spoiled thereby, even with other bones; but never if he struck forcefully on the joint-bone, not by holacha u'va — it is obvious we attribute, and it is included in "he broke bones in it," meaning all bones; and why did they not say except the joint-bone? Rather it is clear they did not exclude except that which is by holacha u'va as his way of cutting the joint-bone after shechitah; but they do not exclude striking at all in any bone. And clear proof further: for Ran wrote that so Rashba wrote; and behold Rashba himself wrote in his long Terumat HaDeshen, end of gate 2: "because the way of the joint-bone is not made to be flawed by holacha u'va," etc. — it implies; but not by holacha u'va we attribute even in the joint-bone, which is made to flaw. And furthermore, what is worse than falling on hard ground, that he permits here when it fell on its point — all the more on the joint-bone. And furthermore, we find in Mordechai in the name of Rivak, who was accustomed to strike the knife on wood before hiding it so that afterward he could attribute — behold our eyes see that certainly the joint-bone is harder than wood; and what reason not to attribute if it is by striking that he struck, not by holacha u'va — as above, so it is correct; and so is Tur's conclusion in the name of HaItur, who wrote: since the way of shechitah is flawed — it is not explained "he broke bones"; it implies: if not by way of shechitah but by way of striking, we properly attribute — end of his words; it appears to me the rabbi's words in Shulchan Aruch are wondrous in this.
