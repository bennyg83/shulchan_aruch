(Seif 18 in gloss) In some places they practice that a person does not slaughter and check for himself. It seems this is one who slaughters for himself and not to sell to others — and this is only from custom's power; but when he slaughters for himself and sells to others — by law forbidden, for suspected to declare treifah fit, as explained plainly in Rambam chapter 8 of Laws of Me'ilah, laws 7, 83, 91; and Tur and Shulchan Aruch, end of siman 65; and he learned this from Talmud Avodah Zarah (daf 38): one does not buy meat in Syria except from an expert; and Rashi there: they are suspected, for they are not careful before skin; but they themselves are not suspected to eat prohibition, as Rama siman 119 in gloss; and it is surprising on one of the acharonim who wrote the opposite: the gloss here deals with one who slaughters for himself and sells to another — this alone is forbidden from custom's power; and I did not grasp his final view; for we cannot say he meant only one established in fitness — if so, there is also no custom.
And in the Taz he challenged that it implies that by law one may slaughter and examine for himself. Why should this be so? There is an established prohibition, and by law he should be forbidden to slaughter, as we learned at the end of the second chapter of Negaim: a person may inspect all firstborns except his own firstborns. He answered that by law he is permitted only to slaughter, because most who are involved in slaughter are experts, but if an actual ruling occurs to him he should not rule. This is difficult, for that case of all firstborns was also established as speaking of an expert at the end of the fourth chapter of Bekhorot. What the Mordechai answered in the first chapter of Chullin in the name of R. Baruch was from the case in Yevamot 71, that he is not believed even for the world where prohibition was established; but for being believed for himself, this reason alone is necessarily insufficient, as with a firstborn, which has an established prohibition as the Taz wrote in Orach Chayyim siman 498. Rather, it appears that slaughter is like maaser, where we say there that one may inspect his own maasrot because, if he wished, he could have put a blemish into the whole flock at the outset. So too here, it was in his power at the outset to slaughter properly, and in such a case, even where prohibition was established, he is believed regarding his own, as explained in siman 127 in the gloss. If so, he may also rule about a matter where it was in his power to be careful in the slaughter. But where it was not in his power to be careful, he may not rule, and expertise does not help one rule about a matter where prohibition was established. It implies that with treifot, where prohibition was not established because once slaughtered it stands in a presumption of permissibility, he may rule even though it was not in his power. So too he may inspect his wife's blood, as Tosafot wrote at the end of the first chapter of Niddah and as brought by the Shach at the end of siman 188, because niddah does not have an established prohibition, as Tosafot wrote in Yevamot chapter 8 and at the beginning of Gittin. The Rash in Negaim also distinguished this way. See responsa Chavot Yair siman 121. In Torat Yekutiel he is stringent that the slaughterer should not rule on any matter where a defect was established before us, because we suspect he may have permitted improperly for the butchers, since kosher meat is more expensive. But this is difficult from what they say there in Bekhorot regarding one who asks about his taharot, because they are fit for him in his days of impurity. Tosafot already asked there that with other prohibitions and permissions, shall he not be believed? See there in Bekhor Shor, who answered that it speaks where prohibition was established. With this, what was written in Sefer Torat Moshe, Hilchot Niddah, to bring proof that one may not rule for himself is rejected. What the Tevuot Shor wrote here to settle the Taz, that he means slaughter, where prohibition was established and it was not in his power, does not help; for if so, in such a case what does the principle that most involved in slaughter are experts accomplish, as in the case of firstborns?
And see responsa Mayim Amukim of R. Eliyahu Mizrachi, siman 24, regarding a butcher from whose hand treifah emerged, whether he may slaughter and examine for himself, or where he takes more wages from kosher animals than from treifot, so that there is monetary desire in the matter. It is not enough for him to return to fitness by accepting the words of the chaverim; rather, he must repent in a manner relating to monetary desire, as he sinned, for we suspect those who did this acted intentionally for money. This must be in a way that has no evasion, such as causing treifah to emerge from his hand, or returning a lost object in a place where they do not recognize him. What the Rashba and the Rosh wrote, that repentance through acceptance of the words of the chaverim helps, refers to slaughterers whom the community hires for the year and who have no benefit from kosher animals; these are not suspected as intentional sinners, for a person does not sin for no benefit to himself, but only as negligent, because he was not careful about causing others to stumble. Their repentance is light, through accepting the words of the chaverim, without punishment, only that he accept upon himself before three to be careful. Even with those butchers, if treifah emerged twice or three times, we consider them intentional and they require severe repentance; accepting the words of the chaverim does not suffice. If there was monetary desire in the matter, even the first time it emerged from his hand he is considered intentional and requires severe repentance, as stated. Regarding removal from office, we remove him even the first time, and he does not have the power to say, "I was inadvertent."
