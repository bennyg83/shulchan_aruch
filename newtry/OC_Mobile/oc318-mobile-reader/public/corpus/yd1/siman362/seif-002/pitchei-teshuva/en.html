To bury him in the ground. See Chatam Sofer responsa siman 332, where he was asked by a rabbi regarding the cemetery in his city. They were accustomed to bury with the head to the north and the feet to the south, and the entrance gate to the cemetery was on the south. Because the place had become crowded and full, they wanted to change and begin another row from east to west, with the head to the east and the feet to the west. Is there any concern in changing from the usual practice? In Bava Batra 102 it is clear that they were not particular about directions, for they dug burial niches along the length and width of the cave; but he found at the end of Sefer HaChayim that one should bury from north to south, without giving any reason. He answered: it is also wondrous in my eyes where the practice of fixing boundaries and directions in burial came from. In the holy community of Pressburg they bury with the head to the west and the feet to the east. Nevertheless, leave Israel to their practice, etc. People commonly say that the feet are buried toward the entrance and exit gate, to allude to faith in the resurrection of the dead, that the deceased will rise from his grave and leave through the gate, etc. Now, when we who live in Europe want to travel to Eretz Yisrael, there are two routes before us: either to travel from north to south until the Mediterranean Sea and from there turn east toward Eretz Yisrael, or to travel from west to east to Constantinople and from there turn south and come to Eretz Yisrael. Therefore it is proper to bury either with his head to the north and feet to the south, as written in Sefer HaChayim, or with his head to the west and feet to the east. According to this, there is no proof from the Talmud, which speaks in Eretz Yisrael, where the dead rise and stand in Eretz Yisrael, and therefore there is no concern about directions. Outside the Land it is different. Therefore, if the people of his city want to lay the head to the west and feet to the east, it is equivalent to head north and feet south. There is no concern because of changing the usual practice, as is proven at the end of Nazir, etc. How good it would be, if possible, to open another gate on the side facing the feet of the buried, to fulfill the above custom. If even this is impossible, to bury with the head to the west is plainly permitted, for we do not judge what is possible from what is impossible; even more than this is found in the Shach s.k. 4; see there.
