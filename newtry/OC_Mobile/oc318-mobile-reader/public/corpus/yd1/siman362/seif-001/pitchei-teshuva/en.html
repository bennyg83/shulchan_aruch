And if he was not buried in the ground, one violates, etc. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov II, siman 101, concerning a person who developed a wound in his leg and required amputation. He was asked whether the severed limb must be buried. Although Ketubot 22b appears to imply that it should be buried, this may not stem from an actual burial obligation. One who is concerned for danger to himself may perhaps not be obligated to bury it. Nevertheless, it must be placed in a room into which kohanim will not enter, because a limb severed from a living person conveys tumah. See there and what I wrote in siman 369, seif katan 3.
To bury him in the ground. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 332, concerning a cemetery where the custom was to bury the dead with the head to the north and feet to the south, while the entrance gate was on the south. Because the cemetery had become crowded, they wished to begin a new row from east to west, with the head to the east and feet to the west, and asked whether changing the customary orientation was objectionable. Bava Batra 102 indicates that no particular orientation was required, since burial niches were dug lengthwise and crosswise within the cave. The rabbi had found, however, at the end of Sefer HaChaim, an instruction to bury north to south without explanation. Chatam Sofer replied that he too wondered where the custom of fixing directions for burial originated. In Pressburg the dead were buried with the head to the west and feet to the east. Nevertheless, one should not lightly dismiss Jewish custom. A common explanation is that the feet are placed toward the entrance and exit gate to express belief in resurrection, for the deceased will one day rise and walk out through the gate. For those of us living in Europe and journeying toward Eretz Yisrael, there are two main routes: one travels from north toward the south until reaching the Mediterranean and then turns east toward Eretz Yisrael; or one travels west to east toward Constantinople and then turns south toward Eretz Yisrael. It is therefore appropriate either to bury with the head north and feet south, as Sefer HaChaim writes, or with the head west and feet east. There is no proof from the Talmudic passage, which concerns Eretz Yisrael, where the dead rise and stand in Eretz Yisrael and there is therefore no concern about direction, unlike outside Eretz Yisrael. Thus, if the townspeople wish to lay the head west and feet east, that is comparable to head north and feet south. Changing the local custom itself is not objectionable, as is evident from the end of Nazir. If possible, it would be best to open another gate opposite the feet of those buried, thereby maintaining the custom. If even this is impossible, it is certainly permitted to bury with the head west. We do not judge what is impossible by what is possible. See also the stronger point in Shach, seif katan 4.
