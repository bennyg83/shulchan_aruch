And he did not bury him in the ground, he transgresses etc. See in the responsa Shevut Yaakov volume 2 siman 101, who was asked about one who developed a wound in his foot and they needed to cut off his foot: whether he is obligated to bury that limb or not. And he answered that even though in the Talmud, Kesubos daf 22b, it implies that one should bury it, one can say this is not from the side of the obligation to bury; and one who is concerned for himself because of danger, it is possible that he is not obligated to bury. Nevertheless one must place it in one room so that kohanim should not enter there, for a limb from a living person conveys tumah; see there. And see what is written in siman 369 seif katan 3.
To bury him in the ground. Chatam Sofer, siman 332, was asked about a cemetery where the custom was to bury with the head to the north and feet to the south, while the gate was on the south. Because of lack of space, the community wished to begin a new row from east to west, with the head east and feet west. The question was whether changing the established orientation mattered. He writes that he too found no clear source requiring a fixed compass direction. In Pressburg they buried with the head west and feet east. Nevertheless, established Jewish customs should not lightly be dismissed. A popular explanation was that the feet face the cemetery gate as an expression of belief in techiyat hameitim, so that the deceased will rise and walk out through the gate. For Jews in Europe traveling to Eretz Yisrael, one can travel either south and then east or east and then south. Therefore, placing the head north and feet south, or the head west and feet east, are both meaningful orientations. The Talmudic burial caves in Eretz Yisrael do not prove otherwise because resurrection itself occurs there and no comparable travel-direction symbolism applies. Thus, changing from north-south to west-east is permitted. If possible, it would be proper to make an additional gate opposite the feet of the newly buried to preserve the symbolism. If not, there is no prohibition, because one does not judge what is impossible as though it were possible.
