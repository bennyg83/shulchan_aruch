I am. A modern deco with you when I eat:
To speak. The Shach writes that it appears a gloss of the Rav is missing here, namely one opinion that holds that whenever he did not complete his words it is not a vow at all, since it can mean this or that and is not a clear yad. So too the Taz writes, and it is clear from the Rav's words in seif 3, where he wrote, "and it has already been explained," etc. The Bach is very stringent, holding that unclear yadot are yadot, and even if he did not complete his words he is forbidden even to eat with him, but this is not primary in the Shas and poskim. If he said, "that I eat yours," he said nothing. If they were urging him to eat, it is a proof, meaning, "what I eat of yours shall be konam," and it is forbidden. If he said, "I am vowed from you," or other such expressions, and said only "if I eat" but did not say "yours," one authority rules it forbidden and another rules that since he did not say "yours," it is nothing. If he said, "that I do not eat," even if he said, "I am vowed from you" or other such expressions, it is nothing; all the more so if he said, "that I will not eat yours," even if he said, "I am vowed from you." In any case, all agree that "that I do not eat yours" alone, or "that I will not eat yours" alone, means nothing, for whenever he prohibits himself upon the object it is not a yad for nedarim. Even with actual nedarim, if he said, "it is upon me that I will not eat this loaf like a korban," it is nothing. The Rosh likewise wrote that if he said, "I vow that I will not eat with you," it is nothing, because it is language of a shevuah and not of a neder. However, since people commonly vow in this language, one should not be lenient, and it requires release so that people not treat vows lightly. Thus Rabbenu Yerucham. According to this, what the Rav wrote, "and some say that even if he said that I," is not precise at all; on the contrary, there it is more forbidden according to all. Thus his language.
