It is the language of an oath. For the language of a vow is that he forbids the object upon himself, whereas the language of an oath is that he forbids himself from the object.
And there is one who says, etc. The Tur wrote in the name of the Rambam: “One who says, ‘The fruits of so-and-so are forbidden upon me,’ in any language by which he forbids them upon himself, they are forbidden to him, even though there is no oath at all, no mention of the Name, and no substitute Name. Concerning this it is stated, ‘to bind a binding prohibition upon his soul,’ meaning that he may forbid permitted things upon himself. Likewise, if he says, ‘This is an issur upon me,’ they are forbidden,” end quote. The words “in any language” mean that it need not specifically be in the Holy Tongue, as the Beit Yosef wrote, and the Tur also explained it this way. Nevertheless, from the Rambam’s words, “even though there is no oath,” the Tur inferred that he expressed the vow in the language of an oath, though not as an actual oath, and even so it is forbidden. One must understand why the Rambam concluded, “Likewise, if he says, ‘This is an issur upon me,’” etc., since this appears entirely superfluous. It seems that the Rambam wrote this as an explanation of the verse, “to bind a binding prohibition upon his soul,” which would appear to mean that he forbids himself through something already forbidden, namely, that he associates his vow with a prohibited object, just as the verse first says, “When a man makes a vow.” Therefore, the Rambam explains that the verse is divided into two clauses. “To bind” means that he forbids a permitted object upon himself in the language of a vow, and this may be in any language. The word “issur” stated by the verse means that he says, “This loaf is an issur upon me.”
