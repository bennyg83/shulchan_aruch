And in siman 311 there he was asked about a woman in the province of Hagar who had a goat about to give birth for the first time and she sold it to a non-Jewish woman for half a gold coin and told her to take it to her home. The non-Jewish woman did not want to lead it through the street because of the evil eye and told the woman to leave it in her house. Meanwhile it gave birth to a male — is there concern about firstborn sanctity? He responded that one cannot be lenient at all: even though according to Rashi money acquires from a non-Jew, and according to the Rambam in chapter 1 of Laws of Zekhiyah one acquires either with money or with pulling, and according to other authorities as well, whatever acquires according to their law works to exempt from firstborn sanctity — as stated in the Magen Avraham siman 448, unlike the responsum of Maharam Mintz, and the law is with the Magen Avraham — nevertheless it appears to me that this applies only when full payment was given. But in this case, where she gave only half a gold coin for a goat worth much more, not only by their law does this not acquire — as testified also in responsum of the aforementioned authority (see there further in siman 318) — but even according to authorities who hold a non-Jew acquires with money and that giving a perutah acquires everything — that applies where the price was properly agreed and afterward he gives a perutah as a form of acquisition, not as a deposit — the rest of the money remains his debt, and it all acquires. But to give half a gold for a goat worth many gold coins — this is merely an asmakhta and neither party intends to convey or acquire. Proof: Tosafot in Avodah Zarah 71a s.v. Rav Ashi (cited in Baer Heitev se'if katan 3) — it is apparent there that if not that we are confident the Israelite intends to fully convey in order to be exempt from the firstborn, there would be a void transaction (bitul mekach). Accordingly it works to sell just one limb cheaply, for even if the non-Jew takes that limb for himself in a small amount, the Israelite profits greatly from the calf that is exempt from the firstborn — much more than the value of the limb. But where the entire goat was sold, etc. (See Orach Chaim siman 448 se'if 3 where it says "or sell it to him as a complete sale for a small amount" and see the Olas Tamid there — this can be resolved.) Furthermore, I am uncertain — since she said she should take it to her home, she did not intend to convey except through pulling, and since she did not pull, she did not acquire. See Choshen Mishpat siman 250 se'if 17. The truth is: one can argue and say — since the non-Jewish woman told the Jewish woman to hold the goat in her home, it is as if she asked her to lend her a place for keeping the goat, and she acquired the goat through the loan of the place. As the Tur writes, this is the clearest method. But this is not so — for with what did she acquire the room, without chazakah, without money, without a document, and she also did not mention "agav" in her acquisition? Even though the authorities write regarding one who conveys a share of the fetus that her words are interpreted as conveying in an effective manner — that she conveys the cow for its fetus — the Tzantz already wrote that Maharai said this only there and not in a case like ours. Therefore it appears to me that in this case it is at least a doubtful firstborn.

And in responsum Chatam Sofer simanim 316–317 he writes that the aforementioned authorities overlooked an explicit Tosefta in chapter 2 of Bekhorot regarding wild animals, consecrated animals, and animals of a convert who died without heirs — they are exempt from the firstborn. The reason this enactment was not mentioned, nor that we decree on account of deception as with chametz, is that renouncing chametz differs from ordinary renunciation — chametz does not require proper renunciation resembling the Sabbatical year, requiring the animal to be taken out of one's home or the stable doors opened and declaring "whoever wishes to take may come and take," making it truly ownerless so that anyone can take possession. But chametz need not be such — since it is forbidden in benefit and is not in one's possession, and the Torah treats it as if it were in one's possession, a mere mental nullification suffices, and one is not required to actually take it outside. Therefore deception applies here, since who can penetrate the depths of one's heart. But with the firstborn, the Sages were careful and stated in the Tosefta the above — "the cattle of the wilderness, etc." — and the same for one who renounces his animal and takes it outside; this is like "cattle of the wilderness." In this there is no room to decree on account of deception, for one cannot be suspect since it can be verified. This is why it is not mentioned — not because it was not enacted, but because no decree was needed. See there.
And there in responsum Chatam Sofer siman 317 he elaborates on the case of a man who sold a firstborn animal to a non-Jewish minor of nine years with money and situmta — whether the acquisition of a non-Jewish minor takes effect to exempt from firstborn sanctity. He concludes: certainly in all laws that apply to Noahides there are no age thresholds of Israel, except for dedications and vows which were extended from "a man, a man" — specifically one who has brought two hairs at the appropriate age, and at the end of tractate Nazir the near-adult is excluded from "a man." But for all their laws and acquisitions no age thresholds were given to them, and from when they come to mature understanding — that they are told [a reason] and understand — they are considered adults. Therefore in this case, if the minor had not reached the maturity of Palgutha D'Reuven (partial reasoning) there is no sale here at all. But if he has already reached intellectual maturity, he is considered adult and his purchase is a valid purchase from the Torah. And there further in siman 318 he was asked about one who sold a firstborn animal to a non-Jew who was his household servant, receiving some money, the rest standing as a loan, without pulling — and the servant fed and watered it, and it then gave birth to a male. The questioner held that by reason of transfer (mesirah) the servant did not acquire — even though mesirah does not require hand to hand, nevertheless here there are two reasons it should not acquire. First, it was in the possession of the one conveying it — and even though mesirah acquires even in the public domain, in the seller's possession it does not work. Furthermore, since both the buyer and seller thought the money had already effected acquisition, the subsequent mesirah is like "one who cultivates in the property of a convert [who died without heirs], thinking it is his" — which does not acquire. And here there is no "daat makneh" (consent of the conveyor) since even the conveyor thought the money had already acquired. However, since money was given — and according to many authorities this takes effect — it is possible to be lenient to inflict a blemish on it. He elaborates and his view inclines to fully permit the firstborn — for besides monetary acquisition, which according to most early authorities takes effect, there was also here mesirah acquisition, by which large animals are acquired, and the fact that it was in the seller's possession does not harm it according to the Ran in chapter 1 of Kiddushin, from whose words it appears that where mesirah takes effect, and only the location in the seller's house prevents it — if one says "acquire it" it is as if he lent him the place, etc., and hence in our case where he certainly lent him the place and was very pleased that he acquire the animal, the location does not impede the mesirah acquisition. Also, the lack of intent to acquire from both parties does not harm, according to the Maharit part 1 siman 150 — only chazakah or meshikhah acquisition requires intent, but where it is in one's hand, like hagbahah and similar, it is no worse than one's courtyard. The Shakh siman 275 se'if katan 3 who disputed him had Tosafot in Bava Batra 54 hidden from him (the Netivot HaMishpat there also wondered at him for this). Therefore it appears that here by mesirah he made a complete acquisition. He then raises doubt about this and concludes it is difficult to permit, but to inflict a blemish via an Israelite — God forbid to permit.
