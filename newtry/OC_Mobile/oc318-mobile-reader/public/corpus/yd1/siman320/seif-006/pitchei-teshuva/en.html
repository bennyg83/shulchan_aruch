See the Baer Heitev s.k. 7 [and what he writes that it implies that even after the fact, if he performed only one acquisition, etc., and also what he writes afterward — that the same applies if he purchased a cow, etc., and thus if the Israelite performed pulling or gave money etc. — all this is the language of the Siftei Kohen. But in the Turei Zahav s.k. 8 he wrote in the name of Resp. Rema §87 that this is specifically required ab initio — that one needs both money and pulling; but after the fact we certainly rule like R. Tam, and therefore if the Israelite did not pull the cow, it is obvious that the cow still stands in the domain of the non-Jew]. Regarding the question of after the fact, whether we rule like R. Tam, see Resp. Sha'ar Efraim §79, and Resp. Ḥinnukh Beit Yehudah §§84, 87, and 88, and Resp. Naḥalat Shiv'ah §31, who agreed with the opinion of Rema z"l that after the fact we rule like R. Tam — that pulling effects acquisition and not money. Many later and earlier authorities dispute this, as written in Resp. Tzemaḥ Tzedek §62. See Resp. of my grandfather Panim Me'irot vol. 2 §52 and Resp. Me'ir Netivim §39 and see the book Be'er Ya'akov at length on this matter. See Resp. of our teacher R. Akiva Eger §37, regarding a case where one had a pregnant cow, a doubtful firstborn, and sold her to a non-Jew shepherd for money alone. The shepherd afterward led her to the field to graze with the rest of the animals, as was his custom, without any intent of acquisitional pulling, and afterward she gave birth. He wrote that there is no concern of firstborn sanctity on the offspring — for one reason: the acquisition by pulling, for even though it was without intent, since there is the consent of another who transfers ownership, it takes effect even entirely without intent [see Resp. Ḥatam Sofer §313 where he elaborated on this point, and see there also §317 on this matter]. And further, by reason of a double doubt (sefeik sefeik): perhaps she had already given birth, and perhaps the law is that a non-Jew acquires through money alone. Although this alone is not sufficient to rely upon — since it is against the presumption that she had not yet given birth — unless she was found to be lactating, in which case the majority removes the presumption — nevertheless it constitutes a supporting consideration. And there are further supporting considerations; see there at great length. See the Mishneh la-Melekh, ch. 4 of Hilkhot Bekhorot, who wrote that if he sold to a non-Jew for money alone and afterward the owners inflicted a blemish upon it, it is possible to be lenient that one may slaughter it on account of this blemish — since the ruling that one may not slaughter it on account of this blemish is only rabbinic, and so it is a doubtful rabbinic prohibition, for perhaps we rule that a non-Jew acquires through money alone. But he ultimately concluded that one should not be lenient, since the doubt arose while it was under a biblical prohibition, and then the matter evolved into a rabbinic prohibition — in such a case we do not say "a doubtful rabbinic prohibition is resolved leniently," as Maharival vol. 3 §100 wrote. See there, and see Resp. of our teacher R. Akiva Eger §64 what he wrote on this. See Resp. Maharam Ziskin §7 in the name of the Gaon Maharsak, regarding a Jew who purchased a lamb from a non-Jew outside the city and gave the non-Jew money through mere visual contact, without pulling or lifting the lamb, and left it in the non-Jew's house; afterward the Jew sold the lamb to another non-Jew, also for money alone without pulling; and afterward the lamb gave birth in the non-Jew's house where it stood. He ruled that there is not even a doubt of firstborn here — for either way: if money does not effect acquisition for a non-Jew, then the Israelite never acquired it at all; and if money does effect acquisition for a non-Jew, as Rashi holds, then when the Jew sold the lamb to the non-Jew the latter also acquired it — for one cannot say that we rule one way here and the other way there to be stringent, since it involves one animal, and it would be like a fool walking in darkness. He further wrote that even had he not sold the animal to the non-Jew at all, if he had not paid the full purchase price, there is reason to argue leniently that it is considered as if the non-Jew's hand is in the middle [yad nokhri ba-emtza], and it is exempt from the firstborn obligation; see there. See Resp. Ḥinnukh Beit Yehudah §81, regarding a firstborn animal that the wife of a Torah scholar sold to a non-Jew for a perutah, etc. He wrote that one should be in doubt whether her sale is valid, since a woman does not have the power to sell her husband's property unless we say that the husband implicitly consents — and if she transacts business within the house, it is certainly not a concern. See the Peri Megadim in Oraḥ Ḥayyim at the end of §434, who wrote that even though one may act on another's behalf in his absence, we nevertheless require specifically that he instructed him, though an indication of intent suffices; see there. See Even ha-Ezer at the end of §86, and the Beit Shemu'el there, regarding a woman who sold movable property — in what circumstances the sale is valid — and draw an inference therefrom to here. I have subsequently seen in Resp. of my grandfather Panim Me'irot vol. 2 at the end of §52, who wrote plainly regarding a firstborn case where the mother of a Torah scholar sold his pregnant cow to a non-Jew without his knowledge — that it is a valid sale, since one may act on another's behalf in his absence. Similarly with leaven, if the master of the house was not home and another person sold his leaven, he acts as his agent since it is to his benefit; see there.
