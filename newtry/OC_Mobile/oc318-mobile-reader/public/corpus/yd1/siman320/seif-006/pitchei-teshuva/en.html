To make a partnership with the non-Jew. See Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 4 of Hilchot Bechorot, who was asked about one who owned an animal expecting its first birth and wished to exempt it from firstborn sanctity by selling a share to a non-Jew for a perutah. He appointed an agent and gave him a perutah with which to make the sale. The agent left, and before it became known whether he had fulfilled his agency, the animal gave birth, while the agent was unavailable to be asked. Mishneh LaMelech discusses at length the presumption that an agent carries out his agency and concludes that it cannot be relied upon leniently here for several reasons. First, that presumption is used leniently only where failure to act immediately could cause the principal to transgress, such as the laws of Shabbat boundaries and new grain. Here, however, until the animal gives birth there is no present prohibition, so the agent may delay and say that he will perform the task tomorrow. Second, fulfillment depends on another person's consent, since a non-Jew may refuse to buy. Third, major early authorities hold that in a Torah matter this presumption is not applied leniently. Accordingly, the newborn has firstborn sanctity until it develops a blemish. He was later informed that the owners had deliberately inflicted a blemish and wished to eat the animal on the strength of it. He ruled that they accomplished nothing and may not slaughter it on that blemish. Although the rule that one who deliberately blemishes a firstborn may not slaughter it on that blemish is only a rabbinic penalty, and the presumption regarding an agent is accepted leniently in rabbinic matters, the other two reasons for stringency remain. In addition, the distinction between Torah and rabbinic law applies specifically to a rabbinic enactment with no Torah root, and there is a further ground for stringency because Rabbeinu Tam holds that money does not acquire from a non-Jew. The animal therefore remains sanctified and may not be slaughtered until it develops another blemish. The owner need not give it to a kohen, however; he may wait until another blemish appears and then eat it himself. See there at length. See also Responsa Chavat Yair, end of siman 40, who writes that some question whether, after a Jew made a partnership with a non-Jew and sold him a share in the mother or fetus, if the Jew later sells his own share to another Jew, the second buyer must again sell a share to that non-Jew or another. Chavat Yair rejects this as unfounded. The first Jew sold the second only what belonged to him; the non-Jew's share remains exactly as before, and he is now partner with the second Jew. We may presume the second buyer accepts this arrangement.
The acquisition does not take effect. See Dagul MeRevavah, who writes that according to the Rama at the end of the siman, one must explain that here the seller explicitly said he was not transferring ownership of the mother, or that the non-Jew did not pull the mother into his possession and the fetus was transferred by barter, according to authorities who hold that barter works with a non-Jew. And the acquisition should be. See Responsa Knesset Yechezkel, Orach Chaim, siman 14, who writes that a commercial handclasp alone, without any payment, is effective in a place where merchants customarily acquire through it, as stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 201, seif 2. The reason the enactment regarding firstborn animals specifies pulling and money is that those methods work everywhere, whereas a handclasp depends on local commercial practice. In countries where it is established practice, it certainly acquires, and he recalls an actual ruling permitting meat of a firstborn that had been sold by such a handclasp without payment. Levushei Serad strongly rejects relying on this to permit a prohibition involving karet, particularly because commercial custom is difficult to determine with certainty. Chok Yaakov, siman 448, seif katan 14, was unwilling to rely on this except regarding chametz, and even money together with a handclasp should not be treated leniently. See what I wrote above in siman 316, end of seif katan 2. Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 314, discusses at length whether a rabbinic mode of acquisition can remove a Torah prohibition. He concludes that situmta, a recognized commercial act, is itself a Torah acquisition, since the Sages never instituted it as a formal enactment; rather, Rava in Bava Metzia 74a taught that Torah law recognizes whatever merchants voluntarily establish as binding, like a valid monetary stipulation. This differs from modes of acquisition imposed by rabbinic enactment. Netivot HaMishpat, siman 201, however, assumes that situmta is only rabbinic. Chatam Sofer argues that they are not comparable and concludes that in the case before him there is at least a double doubt: perhaps situmta acquires by Torah law, and even if only rabbinic, perhaps a rabbinic acquisition suffices for a Torah matter. Moreover, if the handclasp is legally binding under the law of the kingdom where the question arose, that law itself is certainly recognized by Torah law in monetary matters. As an added safeguard, one should have a non-Jew cause the animal a blemish in a manner that does not cause suffering to animals. Ibid. In siman 310, Chatam Sofer was asked about one who gifted a share of an animal that had not yet given birth to a non-Jew, who pulled the animal but was not granted a place in the Jew's courtyard. Could one rely on Tosafot, Avodah Zarah 71a, that even according to the view that pulling ordinarily does not acquire from a non-Jew, where there is no money, as in a found object or gift, all agree that pulling acquires? Ramban, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia, disagrees. The questioner thought Ramban's words difficult and Tosafot decisive. Chatam Sofer explains that Ramban's words are correct, and that Tosafot too meant not an ordinary pulling in a semi-private or public area but taking the item fully into one's home, which certainly acquires according to all views, not because of courtyard law but as a direct and ancient mode of acquisition. He concludes that in the case before him he found no basis for leniency. It further appears that even if the non-Jew had pulled the animal fully into his home, there may still be difficulty where he received only a share rather than the whole animal, and this requires study. And in siman. In siman 311 he addresses a woman in Hungary who owned a goat expecting its first birth. She sold it to a non-Jewish woman for half a gold coin and told her to take it home. The buyer refused to lead it through the street because of concern about the evil eye and told the Jewish owner to leave it in her house. Before removal the goat gave birth to a male. He ruled that there is no basis for full leniency. Although according to Rashi money acquires from a non-Jew, according to the Rambam one acquires either by money or pulling, and according to other authorities local gentile law can sometimes suffice to remove firstborn sanctity, nevertheless this applies where the full agreed purchase money was paid. Here half a gold coin was given for a goat worth much more. Under their law this does not effect the purchase, and even according to the view that a perutah can acquire, that is only when the full price was fixed and the perutah was expressly given as an act of acquisition rather than earnest money, leaving the balance as a debt. Giving a small fraction for an animal worth several gold coins is merely an inconclusive undertaking, with neither party intending a complete sale. He adduces Tosafot, Avodah Zarah 71a, who indicate that absent the clear benefit of avoiding firstborn sanctity such a sale of a small limb would be void. That reasoning applies where the Jew sells only one limb cheaply, because even if he loses the limb the gain of exempting the fetus is greater, but not where he purports to sell the entire animal for a trifling sum. He further questions whether, since she told the buyer to take the animal home, she intended transfer only through pulling, so that without pulling no acquisition occurred. One might argue that when the non-Jewish woman asked the Jew to keep the goat in her house, she effectively requested the loan of a place to stand it, and could acquire the goat together with the borrowed place. But this fails, since she did not acquire the space by possession, money, or document, and no acquisition by way of the property was mentioned. Thus at minimum the newborn remains a doubtful firstborn. He also discusses how Yaakov acquired Lavan's future offspring, citing later explanations that wages for labor can acquire something not yet in existence, and that before the giving of the Torah such an object may have been transferable. See Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 23, who holds that even where a non-Jew did not acquire according to strict law, if he is powerful and the Jew cannot recover the property from him, this is treated as full acquisition and the animal is exempt from firstborn sanctity. Chok Yaakov cites this. Chatam Sofer, siman 315, disputes it, rejects the proofs, and concludes that it should not be relied upon; see also end of siman 316. Responsa Olat HaGolah, siman 25, assumes that by Torah law declaring an animal ownerless would exempt it from firstborn sanctity, but the Sages did not institute this as a standard solution because of concern for evasion, as with chametz. Turei Even, Rosh Hashanah 13a, proves that ownerlessness is ineffective. Chatam Sofer, simanim 316 and 317, writes that those authorities overlooked an explicit Tosefta, Bechorot chapter 2: an animal of the wilderness, an animal of consecrated property, and the animal of a convert who died without heirs are exempt from firstborn sanctity. He explains that this does establish true ownerlessness as effective, but not as a practical device, because genuine ownerlessness must resemble Shemittah: the animal must be removed from one's domain or the doors opened so that anyone may take it. Chametz is different. Since it is already forbidden in benefit and effectively outside one's ownership, the Torah treats it as though still in his possession for liability, so sincere inward nullification can suffice, and there is therefore room for deceptive intent. By contrast, genuine ownerlessness of an animal leaves no useful device, because strangers can simply take it. Ibid. In siman 317 Chatam Sofer discusses a firstborn animal sold to a nine-year-old non-Jewish boy through money and situmta. He concludes that for laws applicable to descendants of Noach there is no fixed age corresponding to Jewish adulthood, except in areas specifically derived from Scripture such as valuations and vows. Once a non-Jewish minor has sufficient understanding to comprehend a transaction, he is legally an adult for his civil dealings. Therefore, if this child had not reached real understanding, the sale is nothing; if he had, his acquisition could be valid by Torah law. Had there been money together with pulling or acquisition of a place, the sale would be effective. Since there was only money and situmta, he remains uncertain whether merchants generally intend their handclasp to bind transactions with a young boy not ordinarily engaged in commerce, and civil law also treated such a child as a minor. He concludes that if another great authority agreed, he would combine the opinions that money alone acquires with the possibility that situmta works here, and then have an unrelated non-Jewish minor cause a blemish that permits slaughter nowadays. See there further. At the end of siman 316 he cites Tzemach Tzedek, siman 64, that if a non-Jew gave two gold coins to a Jew to buy a cow for him, and the Jew bought it and it gave birth while in the Jew's possession, the calf is exempt from firstborn sanctity. He further discusses a cow in labor on Shabbat that the Jew gifted to a non-Jew by handing over the stable key, relying on commercial custom that movable property is acquired by delivery of a key. He concludes that there is no basis for leniency, because even if Ramban writes that situmta helps for firstborn animals, that is in genuine commercial buying and selling, not in a merely formal device designed only to remove sanctity, and especially not a gift by handing over a key, for which no such merchant custom exists. He also considers a case where the non-Jew paid part of the price for a cow standing in a stable rented by the Jew from that same non-Jew. Could the non-Jew's own courtyard acquire the cow for her in addition to the money? He cites Machatzit HaShekel, siman 448, who, because agency does not apply to a non-Jew, recommends transferring chametz to an unmarried non-Jewish woman so that her courtyard may acquire it as an extension of her hand when she stands beside it. Chatam Sofer questions whether a non-Jew has acquisition by courtyard even as an extension of the hand and concludes that this is not sufficiently clear. In his case, moreover, the woman did not stand beside the courtyard.

In siman 318 Chatam Sofer discusses a man who sold his cow expecting its first birth to the non-Jewish servant in his household, received part of the purchase price, and left the balance as a loan. The servant did not formally pull the cow, but fed and watered it, and it later gave birth to a male. The questioner argued that mere delivery did not acquire: even if delivery need not be directly hand to hand, here there were two reasons it should fail. First, the animal remained in the seller's domain, and even if delivery can acquire in a public domain it is ineffective in the seller's domain. Second, buyer and seller both believed that the earlier payment had already effected the purchase, so the later acts resembled one who works the property of a deceased convert while mistakenly believing it is his own, which does not acquire. Nor was there another person's conscious intent to transfer at that later moment, since the seller too thought the money had already completed the sale. Nevertheless, since money alone effects acquisition according to many authorities, the questioner thought one might at least permit causing a blemish. Chatam Sofer discusses the case at length and initially inclines to permit the firstborn entirely. Besides the monetary acquisition, which is effective according to most major early authorities, there was also acquisition by delivery, the proper mode for large livestock. The fact that the cow remained in the seller's domain need not prevent acquisition, based on the Ran in the first chapter of Kiddushin, whose words imply that where delivery is intrinsically effective and only the place is a problem, the seller's statement "acquire" can amount to lending the buyer the place. Here the seller certainly wished the servant to acquire and effectively lent him the space. Nor does the parties' lack of fresh acquisition-intent necessarily invalidate the act. Maharit, part 1, siman 150, distinguishes between possession or pulling, which require intent, and an item physically held in one's control, such as lifting, which can acquire even without explicit intent and is no worse than one's courtyard. Shach, siman 275, seif katan 3, challenges Maharit, but apparently overlooked Tosafot in Bava Batra 54, as Netivot HaMishpat also notes. Thus Chatam Sofer initially concludes that delivery may have effected a complete acquisition, but afterward raises doubts and finds it difficult to permit the animal entirely. He absolutely rejects having a Jew cause a blemish, but permits having an unrelated non-Jewish minor cause it, since this will not be confused with permission for an adult Jew.
He should acquire for him. See Levushei Serad, who writes that this means acquisition together with land, not acquisition by courtyard. He records a case where a Jew's cow was in a village in a non-Jew's house while the non-Jew himself was in the city. The Jew sold the cow to him and received money, and afterward it gave birth to a male. He ruled that the calf was a doubtful firstborn, like any case of money alone. Although one might think the non-Jew's courtyard also acquired the cow, this is ineffective because a non-Jew has no agency, so his courtyard cannot acquire for him through agency. This is certainly so where he was not standing beside the courtyard. Levushei Serad adds that even if the sale occurred in the village while the non-Jew stood in his house, it would still not help, based on Shach in Choshen Mishpat, siman 243, seif katan 10: only a woman's courtyard is sometimes treated as an extension of her hand, whereas a man's courtyard does not have that status. Accordingly, a non-Jew's courtyard never acquires through the doctrine of "hand." This is unlike Responsa Even Shoham, siman 45, which implies that standing beside the courtyard would suffice. According to Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 316, cited above, however, it appears that where this is truly the non-Jew's own courtyard it may acquire even if he does not stand beside it, and this requires study. Levushei Serad further writes that if the Jew told him, "Go, pull it and acquire it," this should work even while the non-Jew was in the city, because pulling may be performed outside the seller's presence when authorized, as stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 197. Since the cow was in the non-Jew's house and he fed and watered it, it is virtually impossible that he did not move it. If there remains factual doubt whether he actually pulled it and the non-Jew says that he did, the matter requires deliberation.
That he receive money. See Baer Heitev, seif katan 7. Its statements that even after the fact a single mode of acquisition is insufficient, and that the same applies when a Jew buys a cow so that if he either pulls it or pays money, are both from the Shach. Taz, seif katan 8, however, cites Responsa Rama, siman 87, that only initially do we require both money and pulling. After the fact we certainly rule like Rabbeinu Tam that pulling, not money, acquires. Thus, if the Jew did not pull the cow, it unquestionably remains in the non-Jew's ownership. See Responsa Shaar Efrayim, siman 79; Chinuch Beit Yehudah, simanim 84, 87, and 88; and Nachalat Shivah, siman 31, who agree with the Rama that after the fact the law follows Rabbeinu Tam, that pulling acquires and money does not. Many later and early authorities disagree, as Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 62, notes. See also Panim Meirot, part 2, siman 52; Meir Netivim, siman 39; and Be'er Yaakov at length. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 37, discusses one who owned a pregnant cow that was only doubtfully expecting its first birth and sold it to the non-Jewish shepherd for money alone. The shepherd later led it to pasture with the other animals as usual, without specific intent to perform acquisition by pulling, and it then gave birth. Rabbi Akiva Eiger rules that the offspring has no firstborn concern. One reason is that the pulling itself can acquire even without the buyer's specific intent where another person is consciously transferring ownership. Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 313, discusses this principle at length, as does siman 317. There is also a double doubt: perhaps the cow had already given birth previously, and perhaps the law is that a non-Jew acquires by money. The second doubt alone is insufficient against the established presumption that it had not given birth, unless the cow had been producing milk, in which case the majority consideration weakens that presumption, but it can at least serve as an additional factor. Rabbi Akiva Eiger adds other supporting considerations. Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 4 of Hilchot Bechorot, writes that if one sold to a non-Jew for money alone and, after birth, the owners deliberately blemished the animal, one might have thought to permit slaughter on that blemish because the penalty against slaughtering on a deliberately caused blemish is rabbinic, creating a rabbinic doubt whether money alone had already transferred the animal to the non-Jew. He ultimately rejects this leniency because the original doubt arose while a Torah prohibition was in force, and only afterward did the matter devolve into a rabbinic prohibition; in such a case one does not apply the rule of leniency in rabbinic doubt. See Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 64. Responsa Maharam Ziskind, siman 7, citing Rabbi Shmuel Kaidanover, discusses a Jew who bought a sheep from a non-Jew outside the city, paid money merely upon viewing it, did not pull or lift it, and left it in the non-Jew's house. He later sold the same sheep to another non-Jew, again for money alone and without pulling, and the sheep then gave birth while still in the first non-Jew's house. He rules that there is not even a doubtful firstborn: if money does not acquire from a non-Jew, the Jew never acquired the sheep in the first place; if money does acquire, then the later non-Jew also acquired it from the Jew. One should not irrationally adopt opposite rulings in the same animal merely to be stringent both ways. He further writes that even if the Jew had not resold the animal, where he had not paid the entire purchase price there is room for leniency because the non-Jew retains a financial hold in the animal, exempting it from firstborn sanctity. Chinuch Beit Yehudah, siman 81, discusses a case where the wife of the owner sold the firstborn interest to a non-Jew for a perutah. He questions whether her sale is valid because a wife generally has no authority to sell her husband's property, unless we say it is self-evident that the husband desires this beneficial act. If she regularly conducts household business, there is certainly no concern. Pri Megadim, Orach Chaim, end of siman 434, writes that although one may confer a benefit on a person outside his presence, one ordinarily needs some instruction from him, though a clear expression of his wishes can suffice. See Even HaEzer, end of siman 86, and Beit Shmuel there, regarding when a wife's sale of movable property is binding. Panim Meirot, part 2, end of siman 52, however, writes simply that if the owner's mother sold his pregnant cow to a non-Jew without his knowledge, the sale is valid because one may confer a benefit on a person outside his presence. He applies the same principle to chametz: if the owner is absent and another person sells his chametz, that person is treated as his agent because the sale is clearly to his benefit.
