(Siman 320 in Shulchan Aruch seif 6) And the kinyan should be that he receives a perutah from the gentile and transfers the place to him. Responsa Emunat Shmuel siman 45 justifies the poor custom of relying ab initio on money, because under their laws money effects acquisition and he cannot retract. In addition, several poskim hold this way, and there he resolves the words of Mishnat Binyamin cited in Orach Chaim siman 448. In Responsa Shaar Ephraim siman 79 he wrote to be lenient in a doubtful bekhor that was sold even by money alone, because the Rambam wrote that outside the Land, bekhor is rabbinic. The Chinukh in Parashat Bo wrote likewise, and see Hagahot Mordechai at the end of Chullin, that bekhor nowadays is rabbinic. If so, it is a rabbinic doubt, all the more so when it is milked or gives milk. One should not say that this is an established prohibition, by placing the mother in her presumption that she had not given birth; on the contrary, we follow more strongly the presumption of permissibility of the offspring itself before it was born than the presumption of the mother, as written in Responsa Zikhron Yosef, Yoreh Deah siman 19; see there, simanim 20 and 21. See Ketzot HaChoshen siman 204, that if the gentile owes the Jew, it is like money and meshikhah alone suffices.
Question. Reuven was owed twenty rubles by a non-Jew. The non-Jew said he could not pay, but offered him a cow that had never calved and would certainly calve that winter, at a price of fifteen rubles, with the rest of the debt remaining. Reuven saw the cow and agreed to receive it at that price, but only on condition that it remain by the non-Jew at his feeding expense until it calved, after which Reuven would take it home. Later Reuven decided to take it before birth so it would calve in his house. Without the non-Jew's knowledge he tied a rope to its horns and led it from the cowshed into the courtyard, but it broke away and returned to the cowshed, and he left it there. Later Reuven and Avraham were in the village. Avraham saw that the cow was close to calving and advised Reuven to take it home. Reuven hired a non-Jew to bring the cow to his house, telling him to go to the owner and take it; it is unknown whether this was with the owner's knowledge. On the road Reuven and Avraham met the non-Jew leading the cow. Avraham warned Reuven that he might forget to sell it properly according to Torah law to exempt it from bekhorah, and advised him to sell it to the non-Jew who was leading it. Avraham arranged the sale; the non-Jew gave Reuven earnest money called auf gab, and Avraham became guarantor for the balance, since he owed that buyer at least the amount of the sale, but no kinyan sudar was made. When the cow arrived at Reuven's house, Reuven paid the non-Jew for leading it. Later it gave birth to a male calf. They slaughtered it without asking a chacham, gave the hide to the lessee, salted and cooked the meat, ate one meal, and only afterward learned that a question had to be asked. The hide was already mixed with many hides and could not be identified. The question is the law of the remaining meat, the salting, cooking, and eating utensils, and the hides. Thus far the questioner's language.
