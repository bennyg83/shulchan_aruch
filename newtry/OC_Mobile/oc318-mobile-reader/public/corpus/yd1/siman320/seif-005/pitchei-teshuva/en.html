He also wrote that a case came before him involving a cow belonging to an Israelite that was in a village with a non-Jew in the non-Jew's house; the non-Jew was in the city and sold the cow to an Israelite, who received money for it, and afterward she gave birth to a male — and he ruled it is a doubtful firstborn, following the law of monetary acquisition alone. Although at first glance there is also here an acquisition by courtyard, nevertheless it is of no effect, for regarding a non-Jew, who has no agency, his courtyard does not acquire on his behalf — not only in this case where the non-Jew was not standing beside his courtyard, but even if the sale had taken place in the village in the non-Jew's house where the non-Jew was standing in his house, it would also be of no effect. As the Siftei Kohen explained in Ḥoshen Mishpat §243 s.k. 10, only a wife's courtyard acquires because it is derived from her hand, etc. (see above s.k. 4 in the name of Resp. Ḥatam Sofer). Consequently, the same applies to a non-Jew — his courtyard never acquires, because a man's courtyard has no legal status of "hand" whatsoever. This is contrary to what seems to emerge from Resp. Even Shoham §45, that if he was standing beside it, it would be effective — and that is not correct. (According to Resp. Ḥatam Sofer §316 cited above, it seems that here even when he is not standing beside it, it is effective since it is his actual courtyard; this requires further study.) He further wrote that if the non-Jew said "Go and take possession and acquire," even if the non-Jew was in the city it seems to me it would be effective, since in such a case pulling is effective even when not in front of the seller, as explained in Ḥoshen Mishpat §197. And since the cow is in the non-Jew's house and he feeds and waters her, it is impossible that he did not pull her. And here, where there is doubt whether he pulled her and the non-Jew says he did — the matter requires deliberation; see there.
