To partner with a non-Jew. See the Mishneh LaMelekh, chapter 4 of Laws of Bekhorot, where he was asked about one who had a firstborn animal and wished to exempt it from the firstborn by selling a share of it to a non-Jew for a perutah. He made an agent and gave him a perutah so that the agent would sell it to the non-Jew. The agent went but we do not know whether he fulfilled the agency, and meanwhile the animal gave birth and the agent is unavailable to ask. He elaborates at length on the presumption that an agent fulfills his agency, and concludes that in the case before him it is impossible to be lenient for several reasons — we do not apply the presumption that an agent fulfills his agency for leniency, except in a case where if the agent did not act immediately the sender would come into transgression, as in the case of Shabbat boundaries and the case of chadash — but here, as long as it has not yet given birth there is nothing at stake, and the agent procrastinates saying "tomorrow I will fulfill my agency." Furthermore, here it depends on another person's consent, since the non-Jew may not want to purchase. Furthermore, the opinion of the great early authorities is that in matters of Torah law we do not apply this presumption for leniency. Therefore in this case, this firstborn retains its sanctity until a blemish falls upon it. He was subsequently informed that the owners had deliberately inflicted a blemish on it and wished to slaughter it on that blemish. He wrote that they accomplished nothing and it is forbidden to slaughter the firstborn on this blemish, even though we rule that one who deliberately inflicts a blemish on a firstborn may not slaughter it — this is only a rabbinic penalty, as stated above in siman 313 — and in rabbinic matters we do apply this presumption even for leniency. Nevertheless, one should be strict for two additional reasons: and furthermore, the distinction here between Torah law and rabbinic enactment only applies where the matter has no basis in Torah law. And there is also a subsidiary reason for stringency: since it is the opinion of Rabbeinu Tam that money does not effect acquisition with a non-Jew — therefore in this case the firstborn retains its sanctity and cannot be slaughtered until another blemish falls upon it. However, it is not obligated to be given to the kohen — he may wait until another blemish develops and eat it with that blemish. See there at great length. See also the responsum Chavot Ya'ir at the end of siman 40, who writes that some question whether even if an Israelite created a partnership with a non-Jew and sold him a share of the cow or fetus — if the same Israelite subsequently sells it to another Israelite, the buyer also needs to perform the same correction and sell again to that non-Jew or to another non-Jew. This is mistaken: the first seller sold to the second only what belonged to him, and the non-Jew's share remains as it was from the first, so the second is a partner with [the non-Jew], and we can be confident the second is also pleased with this arrangement. See there.
