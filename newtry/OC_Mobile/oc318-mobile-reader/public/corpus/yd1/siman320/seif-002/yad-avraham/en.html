Question. Reuven was owed twenty rubles by a non-Jew. The non-Jew said he could not pay, but offered him a cow that had never calved and would certainly calve that winter, at a price of fifteen rubles, with the rest of the debt remaining. Reuven saw the cow and agreed to receive it at that price, but only on condition that it remain by the non-Jew at his feeding expense until it calved, after which Reuven would take it home. Later Reuven decided to take it before birth so it would calve in his house. Without the non-Jew's knowledge he tied a rope to its horns and led it from the cowshed into the courtyard, but it broke away and returned to the cowshed, and he left it there. Later Reuven and Avraham were in the village. Avraham saw that the cow was close to calving and advised Reuven to take it home. Reuven hired a non-Jew to bring the cow to his house, telling him to go to the owner and take it; it is unknown whether this was with the owner's knowledge. On the road Reuven and Avraham met the non-Jew leading the cow. Avraham warned Reuven that he might forget to sell it properly according to Torah law to exempt it from bekhorah, and advised him to sell it to the non-Jew who was leading it. Avraham arranged the sale; the non-Jew gave Reuven earnest money called auf gab, and Avraham became guarantor for the balance, since he owed that buyer at least the amount of the sale, but no kinyan sudar was made. When the cow arrived at Reuven's house, Reuven paid the non-Jew for leading it. Later it gave birth to a male calf. They slaughtered it without asking a chacham, gave the hide to the lessee, salted and cooked the meat, ate one meal, and only afterward learned that a question had to be asked. The hide was already mixed with many hides and could not be identified. The question is the law of the remaining meat, the salting, cooking, and eating utensils, and the hides. Thus far the questioner's language.
Answer. At the beginning of the second chapter of Bekhorot the sugya implies that according to R. Yochanan, who holds money effects acquisition for a Jew by Torah law, we expound that with one's fellow acquisition is by money, but with a non-Jew by meshikhah. According to Rabbenu Tam and many authorities, the halachah is that with a non-Jew meshikhah acquires and money alone does not, and the later authorities accepted this as primary bedieved regarding a Jew selling to a non-Jew. The same should apply in reverse: if a Jew bought from a non-Jew with money only and without meshikhah, he did not acquire. In our case Reuven never drew the cow into his own domain or a simta with a valid meshikhah. His earlier leading it in the non-Jew's courtyard, and the later taking by a non-Jewish messenger, even if known to the owner, were not valid meshikhah or mesirah. Moreover, there is no agency for a non-Jew. Thus according to Rabbenu Tam's view there is certainly room to permit. Even according to Rashi, the Rosh, the Tur, and the Rambam, who are concerned that money acquires with a non-Jew, Reuven still did not acquire here, because he paid no ready money; the whole arrangement was against a debt. A loan does not acquire even where money acquires, as is clear in several places in Choshen Mishpat, and certainly it should not acquire from a non-Jew. Even according to the Rambam, who treats a loan as money for some sales among Jews, one may say that this is only among Jews, not with a non-Jew, and this also resolves the comparison to the acquisition of Ephron's field.
And further Reuven did not clearly intend to acquire through that debt. All kinyanim require the buyer's intent to acquire through the act. The Rambam's case of acquiring by a loan is where the buyer says, "sell me these movables for the debt you owe me," which shows present intent to acquire by that debt. Here the non-Jew initiated the offer, and Reuven only said he would accept the cow, language that sounds future-oriented, especially since he said it should remain by the non-Jew until birth. Perhaps he meant to receive it only later. Nor was there mesirah, because even those who allow mesirah for a large animal require the seller to say, "go, take possession and acquire," and here the owner said nothing; the first attempt was without his knowledge, and the later taking by a non-Jewish messenger cannot create agency for a non-Jew. Therefore, from many sides, Reuven never acquired the cow from the non-Jew.
Apart from this, even if one says Reuven did acquire by money, he transferred it onward to the second non-Jew by that same kind of acquisition. In any event it is not a firstborn: if money acquires with a non-Jew, the second non-Jew acquired from Reuven by the money he gave; and if money does not acquire, then Reuven never owned it and it remained in the domain of the original non-Jew. One should not distinguish between a Jew acquiring from a non-Jew and a non-Jew acquiring from a Jew by money, for the Shach in Choshen Mishpat rejected that distinction and the Rambam in Hilchot Bekhorot also rules without it. Even though the buyer gave only earnest money, Avraham became guarantor for the rest, and since the guarantee was at the time of sale it needs no kinyan. Moreover, Avraham already owed the buyer, creating a form of maamad sheloshtan. The seller's mind therefore relied on the payment, and it is a valid money acquisition if money acquisition is effective here at all.
What emerges for halachah is that there is strong ground to rule that the calf was not a bekhor at all. Even if one treated it as a doubtful bekhor, the salting and cooking utensils that were not ben yoman when the owners learned that the matter required a chacham, and the mixture of hides, may be permitted. By Torah law one in two is nullified in a mixture of the same species, so the remaining concern is rabbinic. Although the matter began as a Torah issue and became rabbinic only through later circumstances, since the doubt was not known until it had already become rabbinic, the Peri Megadim permits in a case of loss, and Levushei Serad writes likewise. Even if it was known before the mixture, where the mixture is one in two and therefore rabbinic we may rely on the poskim who hold that money does not acquire with a non-Jew and that it was never a bekhor at all, as is found in the gloss in siman 66 regarding blood in eggs mixed one in two with the same species. See Levushei Serad, who discusses the details at length.
