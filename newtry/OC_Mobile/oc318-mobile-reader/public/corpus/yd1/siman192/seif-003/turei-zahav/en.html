If they postponed the marriage, etc. The Mordechai writes there as follows: “If they postponed the marriage, for example because the father of the groom and the father of the bride could not reach an agreement, whether because of the dowry or the wedding expenses, then even if she already observed seven clean days, she must again observe seven clean days once they reach an agreement and resolve the matter together and intend to proceed with the marriage,” etc. End quote. This language implies that the marriage was postponed without setting another wedding date. Rather, the parties separated from one another in a manner in which they did not know when the marriage would occur. This was with the consent of both sides, as implied by the expression “they postponed the marriage,” meaning that it was definitively deferred from taking place at that time. It also implies that both sides agreed to this. Therefore, she certainly did not maintain proper awareness during her examinations. We learn from this that if either of these two conditions is absent, the law is different. Thus, if the wedding was not definitively postponed, but they failed to reach agreement regarding payment of the dowry and therefore the marriage did not take place that day, while they still anticipated reaching an agreement and holding the feast the next day, this is not considered a postponement. The same applies even if the groom firmly declares that he does not wish to marry her because her dowry was not paid, while the bride's side pursues him through legal claims and compromise in order to persuade him until he agrees. Even if this continues for one or two days, it is not called a postponement of the marriage, for throughout that time the bride relies upon the expectation that the groom will be persuaded. Were you to say that even in such a case new desire arises when the uncertainty leaves her mind and the matter becomes certain, then even on the wedding day itself there are many occasions when the groom is obstinate and explicitly says that he will not marry her until several hours later, when peace is restored between them. Would the bride then need to observe seven clean days anew? This has neither been heard nor appears correct. On the contrary, such cases occur every day and no one is concerned for this. Therefore, what difference is there whether the dispute lasts that day or continues for one or two days? The bride certainly does not remove the marriage from her mind and relies upon the expectation that the marriage will take place after they reach an agreement. The Levush wrote: “It appears to me that even if at the outset, after they had prepared for the chuppah, they postponed it from the original day but immediately stipulated and set another specific day, even only one or two days later, she must observe seven clean days anew. Since she diverted her mind from the original time, the desire is aroused a second time shortly before the later date.” End quote. His words do not appear correct at all. Even Maharik, who is stringent as cited by the Beit Yosef, was stringent only because perhaps she saw blood without realizing it once the marriage had been postponed, etc. That concern does not apply here, for she certainly knows the exact wedding date and continues to examine herself. The same is true in the case I described, where a dispute regarding payment of the dowry continues between them and the bride's side is occupied with compromise and reconciliation. She certainly remains aware that this is the ordinary course of such matters. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, likewise disagreed with the Levush where they set another date, and ruled that another seven clean days are unnecessary unless they jointly agreed not to marry one another at all. The general rule is that there is concern only when both sides agree that the wedding will not take place at the designated time and they set no other date. In that case, even if the matter remains unresolved only one or two days and they then reconcile and decide to hold the wedding immediately, she must observe seven clean days anew. This is the case discussed by the Mordechai and the Shulchan Aruch here. It further appears to me that if the groom did not arrive on the designated wedding day because of an unavoidable circumstance that befell him on the road, and only after that day sent a messenger to the bride explaining why he did not come either yesterday or today, the bride requires seven clean days anew. Since he failed to arrive on the designated day, she certainly became uncertain, for all roads are presumed dangerous, and perhaps some danger would prevent him from coming at all. When she subsequently learns that he is coming, this certainly creates new desire. I saw in the responsa Masas Binyamin that in such a case she does not require another seven clean days, because she assumes that he was delayed by circumstances beyond his control and will come when the impediment passes. I have written what appears correct to me. She certainly worries either that he is in danger or that he no longer desires her, and that this is why he failed to appear on the wedding day. Therefore, when this doubt leaves her mind, new desire arises. So it appears in my humble opinion.
The Levush writes that if the groom was exchanged, meaning that the first groom refused to bring her into the chuppah on the wedding day and the bride agreed that another man should marry her, and he married her immediately, she requires seven clean days anew, for new desire has arisen, etc. This is so obvious that it need not have been written. It appears to me, however, that if after agreeing to the second groom the first groom returned and reconciled with her, she likewise requires seven clean days anew, since another man intervened between them. So it appears to me. If the act has already occurred and he had relations with her in violation of the prohibition, see what I wrote at the end of this siman.
