If the wedding was postponed for some reason. This implies specifically that the wedding itself was postponed. If the wedding was not postponed, but there was merely a dispute between them concerning the dowry, there is no new desire. Likewise, if they postponed the first date and, during that same meeting, immediately agreed upon a second date, there is no new desire. So wrote the Bach, and this is the conclusion of the Taz and Shach, unlike the Levush. The Levush further wrote that if, because of the dispute, the match was broken off, and during that same meeting the bride agreed to marry another man and entered the chuppah with him that very day, it is obvious that this is a new demand. Even if she examined herself and found herself pure, she must count seven clean days anew. All the later authorities agree. If the wedding was fixed for Rosh Chodesh and she counted seven clean days, but when Rosh Chodesh arrived the groom did not come, and on the following day a messenger came from the groom reporting that an unavoidable circumstance had delayed him on the road and that he would arrive one or two days later, and he did so, with the chuppah taking place two or three days after Rosh Chodesh, the Taz holds that seven clean days must be counted anew. The responsa Masas Binyamin, siman 42, however, rules plainly that she does not require seven clean days anew, and the Shach follows him. His words are reasonable, because the basic ruling requiring seven clean days anew is a very great stringency, as Masas Binyamin explains at length in refuting the Mahari'l. See there, and see Shach, s.k. 7. Nevertheless, it appears to me that she must examine herself when the messenger arrives and find herself pure.
To return and count seven clean days. Even if she immersed, she requires seven clean days and a second immersion. So wrote the Shach in the name of the Mordechai. If she transgressed and had relations, she must wait six complete onos, then count one clean day and immerse, as explained below at the end of siman 196. We do not say that she must separate from him for two days in order to distinguish between seed conceived in sanctity and seed conceived without sanctity, because the prohibition here is only rabbinic. So wrote the Taz at the end of this siman and at the end of siman 176; see there. In truth, even apart from this, it appears that such a distinction does not apply here, because the child of a niddah is fully Jewish, as explained in Even HaEzer, siman 7. The Shach likewise wrote this in Nekudos HaKesef at the end of siman 196. I also see some proof from the Mishnah in the fourth chapter of Yevamos, folio 33: two brothers betrothed two women, and when they entered the chuppah the women were exchanged. They are liable for adultery; if the men were brothers, also for a brother's wife; and if either woman was a niddah, also for niddah. They separate them for three months lest they are pregnant and their children be mamzerim, for a distinction is required between valid seed and invalid seed, so that the pregnancies not be attributed to their husbands. If a distinction were also required merely because the women had relations while niddos, the Mishnah would have required it even without the exchange. Rather, it is clear that no such distinction is required in the case of niddah. This is also implied by all the halachic authorities, who mention such a distinction only regarding a male and female convert, where there are several practical halachic consequences, as explained in the laws of converts.
