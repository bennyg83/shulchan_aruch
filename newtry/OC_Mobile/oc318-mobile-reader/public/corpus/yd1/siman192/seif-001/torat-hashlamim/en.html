Lest she saw because of desire. See the Taz, who challenges this from Tamar, who told Yehudah, "I am pure," and from Ruth, who entered Boaz's bed after immersing without counting seven clean days. He resolves the difficulty in several ways. According to what I wrote above in siman 183, s.k. 4, that the reason an unmarried niddah requires seven clean days is that nowadays the Sages treated all women as though they were major zavos, there is therefore no distinction between an unmarried woman and a married woman regarding the requirement of seven clean days. According to Torah law, however, an unmarried woman does not require seven clean days; only a married woman requires them, because her husband is accustomed to her and may become repelled by her, so she must wait seven days. Tamar and Ruth, who were unmarried, were therefore permitted to immerse immediately at that time, since the Sages had not yet treated all women as major zavos. This is straightforward.
And she does not require a hefsek taharah. Nowadays the custom is to perform a hefsek taharah even for a virgin and to examine throughout all seven clean days. Maharshal likewise wrote this, as cited by the Perishah, Bach, and Shach. After the fact, however, one examination suffices, as the Rama wrote.
Only once during the seven days. Although we rule below in siman 196, se'if 4, that it does not suffice unless she examined herself on the first day and on the seventh day, that case is different because she certainly saw blood. Here, however, she did not certainly see blood, and therefore one examination is more effective. So wrote the Lechem Chamudos, folio 299b, and the Shach. It appears obvious to me that all this refers to a minor who has never seen blood, concerning whom there is only a concern that she may have seen because of desire. An adult woman who has menstruated, however, even if she did not see now but menstruated many days ago, is presumably still impure as a full niddah, since, being unmarried, she presumably did not count seven clean days. She therefore requires seven clean days like a full niddah, for there is no distinction between an unmarried woman and a married woman, as explained above in siman 183. It is possible that for this reason the custom cited in s.k. 2 in the name of Maharshal became widespread. In Talmudic times, however, even regarding an adult woman there was only a concern that she might have seen because of desire, for in respect of definite niddah status she would presumably have purified herself even while unmarried, because of matters of ritual purity. This is readily understood.
