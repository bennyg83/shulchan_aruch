Seven days. The Tur cites this in the name of the Rashba, as follows: “This does not refer to a case in which she was asked to marry after 12 months, for the appointed time is distant and she does not experience desire because of it,” etc. It is clear that the Rashba, who holds this view, does not accept the ruling cited by the Rama below that if relations do not take place she must examine herself every day. The Rashba wrote in Toras HaBayis HaAruch that even during the seven clean days she does not require examinations, and certainly not afterward. Rather, he holds that she need only observe seven clean days with the presumption that she is pure; he does not hold that additional desire arises afterward. Therefore, he says that since she must in any event count seven clean days from the proposal before marrying, it does not help for her to observe seven clean days immediately if she will marry only after 12 months. At present she has no desire at all. Her desire arises only when she prepares herself for the chuppah, so what benefit would there be in the seven clean days observed now? Rather, the count begins when they prepare the beer for the chuppah, or when they inform her. From that point the seven clean days are effective even if much time remains until the chuppah, because after she observed seven clean days following the beginning of the proposal, when the primary desire occurred, she is thereafter like all other women and need not examine herself at all. The Derishah found it difficult why the Rashba did not explain that she will again experience desire later, shortly before the wedding, but instead explained that the time is distant and she does not experience desire because of it. He explains that the practical difference is where she was asked to marry after 12 months and began counting seven clean days, but on the third or fourth day of her count they agreed to bring her into the chuppah immediately. She must then count seven clean days anew, not from the original proposal, since there was no desire at the time of that proposal. Accordingly, the Rashba concludes that the count begins when they place the barley in water to prepare the wedding beer, etc. Even if it is customary to hold the chuppah, for example, four weeks later, the count nevertheless begins then, for if in the interim they agree to hold the chuppah immediately, she need not count another seven clean days. End quote. The Derishah understood that she must in every case observe seven clean days specifically close to and before the chuppah. He therefore explained that the Rashba means that one does not count from the proposal when the wedding will be after 12 months. That is, if she now counts seven clean days and during those seven days they decide to hold the wedding immediately, those seven clean days are ineffective, since the desire arose only when they decided to bring her into the chuppah. If, however, they began preparing the beer and she counted seven clean days, while several weeks still remained until the wedding, and they then decided to hold it after those seven clean days, she need not count another seven clean days. According to his explanation, however, if they did not advance the wedding, she would require a second set of seven clean days before the wedding. He was not precise in this interpretation. According to the Rashba, once she has observed seven clean days from the time of the desire, she requires nothing further and is like all other women. Other poskim were stringent that she examine herself from the beginning of the seven clean days until relations take place, and this is a l'chatchilah requirement, as the Rama wrote.
