Permitted. Shach writes that the restriction was stated only regarding a Jew who prepares wine grown in a non-Jew's vineyard, where the non-Jew invested little effort and expense and is therefore less concerned if the Jew discovers tampering. When he transports wine over a long distance with great effort and expense, and also purchased it, he fears losing his investment and does not falsify the seals. The Rosh and Tur. Bach writes that where the accepted practice is to permit such wine with one seal, that practice may be followed.
In partnership. The case is where the Jew paid his share to the other non-Jew, or the latter wrote, 'I have received payment,' so that when the Jew comes he cannot withhold the wine. It is then treated as Jewish wine. Shach.
We are concerned. This differs from wine that a Jew prepared for a non-Jew, which is not permitted merely through a key and seal. Here the Jew owns a share, so the non-Jew fears being caught as a thief. Since nowadays one seal suffices, the wine is permitted even when the key is not in the Jew's possession. Rama requires the key in the Jew's hand because that permits even initially. Shach understands that even if the wine is stored in the house of the non-Jewish partner, it is permitted, because the Jew's share makes it like Jewish wine stored in a non-Jew's home, which nowadays is permitted after the fact with one seal. Taz likewise permits with one seal in a major loss, but writes that Rama's text contains a printing error. It should read: 'A Jew and a non-Jew who own wine in partnership, and the Jew prepared it and they placed it in the house of another non-Jew.' According to the printed wording, the seller has no reason to fear and an actual guard should be required. Moreover, the plural expression 'they prepared it' is difficult if it refers to a non-Jew. Taz therefore requires the emendation.
