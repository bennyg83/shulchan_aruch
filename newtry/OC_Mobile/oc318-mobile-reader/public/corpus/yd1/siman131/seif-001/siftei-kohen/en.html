And he did not pay him money. Meaning he did not pay him anything; but if he paid him somewhat such that the Jew has a portion in the wine, it appears from Rashba's responsum that I brought at the end of this siman that it is like a Jew's wine resting in a gentile's possession, which is permitted with a key and seal; and for us, b'di'eved with one seal — see there.
And the Jew lives in the courtyard. For it is considered like a guard, for whom going in and out suffices — so wrote the poskim.
If a Jew lives there. Even another Jew — Beit Yosef and Perishah; but Rashba holds that even if this Jew alone lives in the city, he is not afraid to enter there and falsify — and we specifically require many Jews living there since he fears one of them may see him; and likewise the Ran at the end of the chapter R. Yishmael; and one should be stringent where there is no loss; but for benefit it appears permitted even where there is no loss — since even certain touch by gentiles nowadays is permitted in benefit in a case of loss, as above beginning of siman 123; and further, just as we rely below on sfeik-sfeika and its supporters regarding havla’ah ve-chatumah to permit in benefit — so too here one may rely on the permitting poskim; examine.
Permitted, etc., with a key and seal. Rashba and the Ran in the name of the Raavad hold that where the non-Jew does not live in the courtyard, the wine is permitted even without any seal, since the courtyard is open to the public domain. It appears that all authorities agree with this in practice. Rabbeinu Yerucham's words on this matter are confused and appear to contain printing errors; the words of Toras Chaim at the end of the chapter Rabbi Yishmael are likewise difficult.
If there is, etc. Rashi holds that even then it is forbidden, because so long as the non-Jew has not written to the Jew, 'I have received payment,' in a manner that prevents him from withholding the wine, he thinks: if the Jew sees me, I lose nothing, for I can say that the wine is still mine. Bartenura likewise rules this way. Although his explanation of the Mishnah is not compelling, the law itself may be correct. The Mishnah that permits when the entrance is open may concern a case where the non-Jew does not live there, as Rashba and the Ran in the name of the Raavad explain. If the non-Jew actually lives there, what does an open entrance accomplish when he himself sits at that entrance? Rashba accordingly rules that where the non-Jew lives in the courtyard and the Jew lives elsewhere, even two seals do not suffice unless the Jew actually sits and guards. One should therefore not be lenient except in a case of loss and under the conditions explained in seif katan 3.
And b'di'eved, etc. Meaning: for us it is permitted b'di'eved with one seal, except that l'chatchilah two seals are required; but for the Mechaber, even b'di'eved two seals are required — as above siman 130; and here it deals explicitly even with b'di'eved.
Even with one seal. And with key and seal it is permitted in benefit for us even when not open to the public domain and no Jew lives in the city — as below at the end of the seif in Hagah; and likewise for drinking in a case of great loss.
Even though Jews live in the city. And even many Jews.
And goes out and enters. Even though he stayed a long time — as long as he did not inform him he is sailing, as above beginning of siman 129.
With key and seal. And for us it is permitted with one seal b'di'eved — so proven in the poskim.
Peddlers, etc. Even at night he is afraid lest they regard him as a thief. Nevertheless, on Shabbos and Yom Tov, when he does not fear the peddlers, it appears that guards are required. Rabbeinu Yerucham, cited by Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, writes that the rule stated above in siman 129, seif 6, that we are not presumed in their eyes to observe Shabbos applies where a Jew would otherwise suffer loss; peddlers, however, do not circulate in their towns on those days, so he does not fear them.
And it has already been explained, etc. This is the Raavad's position, cited by Rashba and the Ran. His words there show that an entrance open to the public domain also does not help at night; the Jew must sleep inside. The Rama does not reproduce the Raavad's further limitation that where there is only a window, only the wine visible through the window is permitted. This implies that on that point the Rama follows Rashba and the Ran, who permit all the wine because this case differs from siman 128, seif 3: there the non-Jew certainly entered, whereas here he is afraid and does not enter the house containing the wine. The Rama also follows Beit Yosef's explanation that Rashba does not dispute the Raavad's distinction between day and night.
And if he wrote, etc., 'I have received payment,' etc. Specifically where he wrote that he had received payment or converted the price into a loan against himself. Otherwise, even if the Jew purchased the wine from him before witnesses in accordance with the manner of non-Jewish sales, such that neither party could retract, the wine is forbidden. Beit Yosef writes this in the name of a responsum of Rashba. The Ran writes a similar ruling in a responsum cited by Beit Yosef at the end of this siman.
Permitted in every manner. Even with gentile wine in a gentile's possession where he lives, and the Jew does not live there, and the entire city is gentile — because havla'ah ve-chatumah is like a guard there; and it is implied from the words of Sefer HaTerumah and his supporters that key and seal is considered havla'ah ve-chatumah, which is like a guard.
To permit it for benefit, etc. The Bach challenges the Rama: since we rule that any contact by a contemporary non-Jew, even deliberate contact, and ordinary non-Jewish wine are forbidden only for drinking, then here, where there is merely a doubt whether contact occurred, one should rely on Sefer HaTerumah and permit even drinking. This is not a valid objection. In many places the Rama writes in the same manner and sometimes simply follows the Mechaber's Talmudic rule, with practical relevance even nowadays outside a case of loss. Moreover, even today the Rama did not wish to rely upon Sefer HaTerumah to permit drinking, though he permits benefit, as the Ran writes. The principle stated above in siman 124, seif katan 71, that whatever the Talmud permits for benefit may nowadays be permitted even for drinking applies where the Talmudic law clearly permits benefit, or where most poskim agree. Here the permissibility for benefit rests on reliance upon Sefer HaTerumah, while most poskim disagree. More fundamentally, above we descend one level because contemporary non-Jews are not regular idolaters: where deliberate contact would have prohibited benefit in Talmudic law but the actual contact is of a lesser form that permitted benefit, nowadays it may permit drinking. Here, however, if we knew that actual contact occurred, all would prohibit benefit under Talmudic law. We permit benefit only because Sefer HaTerumah, or the Raavad regarding doubtful contact, allows us to assume that no contact occurred. The contemporary status of non-Jews therefore does not itself justify drinking. In a case of great loss, however, one may perhaps permit drinking in accordance with the Bach, especially since Semag, Hagahos Maimoniyos, Semak, Kol Bo, and other poskim agree with Sefer HaTerumah. Know also that throughout this siman and simanim 129 and 130, whenever we are concerned that a non-Jew may have touched the wine, that concern exists specifically where he may have touched it to drink, exchange it, derive some other benefit, or incidentally handled it. Where none of these concerns exists, we do not presume that he touched it merely to libate it, and in a case of loss it is permitted for drinking because contemporary non-Jews are not regular idolaters, as explained above in siman 128, seif 4.
