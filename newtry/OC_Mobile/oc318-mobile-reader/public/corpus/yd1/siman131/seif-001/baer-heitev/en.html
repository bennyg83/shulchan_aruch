Paid. Shach writes that this means he paid him nothing. If he paid part, so that the Jew owns a share in the wine, Rashba implies that it is treated as Jewish wine deposited with a non-Jew, which is permitted with a key and seal, and nowadays after the fact with one seal.
And the Jew. He is regarded like a guard, and therefore entering and leaving intermittently suffices. The authorities write this. Darkei Moshe limits this to wine stored inside a house. If it is in a courtyard, a guard must be stationed there, because the non-Jew has an interest both in the wine and the courtyard and therefore does not fear touching it. Taz writes that he does not understand this, for in a courtyard the non-Jew should fear discovery even more than in a house, since he also has an interest in the house.
A Jew. Even another Jew. Beit Yosef and Perishah. Rashba, however, holds that if only this one Jew lives in the city, the non-Jew does not fear entering and falsifying the seals. Many Jews must live there, so that he fears one of them may see him. Ran agrees. One should be stringent where there is no loss, though the wine appears permitted for benefit even without loss. If the non-Jew does not live in the courtyard, no seal is needed at all when it opens to the public domain. Shach.
A key. Rashi holds that even then the wine is forbidden. Until the non-Jew writes, 'I have received payment,' in a manner preventing him from withholding the wine, he thinks that if the Jew sees him he will lose nothing, for he can claim the wine is his. An open doorway likewise does not help where the non-Jew sits inside that entrance. Even two seals do not permit unless a guard is actually present. Shach therefore concludes that one should be lenient only in a case of loss.
After the fact. Shach writes that with a key and seal the wine is permitted for benefit nowadays even if the premises do not open to the public domain and no Jew lives in the city. In a major loss it is permitted even for drinking. Taz writes that a key alone likewise suffices. See simanim 118 and 130 regarding Shach and Nekudot HaKesef.
Lives. Even if many Jews live in the city, because the non-Jew does not fear touching wine that belongs to him. This differs from Jewish wine kept in a non-Jew's possession, which is permitted with a key and seal, or after the fact with one seal, because there he fears more. Intermittent entry and exit nevertheless helps here even after a long interval, provided the Jew did not inform him that he was departing, as stated at the beginning of siman 129. Shach.
And a seal. Nowadays one seal is sufficient after the fact, as appears from the authorities. Shach.
Merchants. Even at night he fears that they will consider him a thief. Nevertheless, on Shabbat and Yom Tov, when he does not fear merchants, guards appear necessary. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe write that the rule in siman 129:6, that non-Jews do not presume us to observe Shabbat, applies where a Jewish financial loss is involved, which does not apply to merchants.
A window. Taz writes that all the wine in the room is then permitted. Although in siman 128:3 only the area opposite openings through which one can see is permitted, that case differs because the non-Jew certainly entered and closed the door behind him. Here we rely upon the presumption that he did not enter at all. Therefore there is no distinction between the area opposite the opening and the sides. Rashba.
At night. Even a doorway open to the public domain does not help at night. The Jew must sleep inside. Shach.
Withholds. Beit Yosef cites a responsum of Rashba that this applies specifically where the non-Jew wrote, 'I have received payment,' or converted the debt into a loan. Otherwise, even if he purchased it from him before witnesses through a form of non-Jewish acquisition from which neither party can retract, the wine is forbidden.
Circumstance. Even non-Jewish wine in the possession of a non-Jew who lives there, where no Jew lives there and the entire city is non-Jewish, is permitted because a key and seal are equivalent to a guard. Bach challenges Rama for not writing that nowadays it is permitted even for drinking. Shach responds that Rama often states the Talmudic rule, which remains relevant because outside a case of loss the wine is ordinarily forbidden for benefit, yet here it is permitted. Furthermore, Rama may have declined to permit drinking even nowadays because if actual touch were known, the wine would be forbidden for benefit under Talmudic law. In a major loss, however, drinking may perhaps be permitted. Shach adds that throughout this siman and simanim 129 and 130, concern for non-Jewish touch applies only where there is reason to suspect that he touched to drink, exchange, gain another benefit, or while occupied with some task. Otherwise we do not suspect libation, and nowadays the wine is permitted for drinking in a case of loss, because contemporary non-Jews are not considered idolaters, as stated in siman 128:4 in the gloss. Rama relied upon that earlier ruling. Taz writes that one should not permit drinking at all, though one may buy the wine initially for resale and profit.
