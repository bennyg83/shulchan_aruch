For a seah. The prohibition is rabbinic and therefore cannot be recovered through beit din. Taz writes that where the arrangement is structured as a sale, one sells a seah now and receives a seah at a fixed later date, it resembles a transaction close to both profit and loss. Since even as a loan the prohibition is only rabbinic, as a sale it is fully permitted.
In circulation. This excludes a coin no longer current. Even if it had circulated previously and was later invalidated, lending it for an equivalent coin is forbidden. Shach.
Currency. Taz explains that nowadays most people own movable property worth money, which they could sell cheaply if necessary. This resembles borrowing a seah against a seah when one already owns some of the same kind. Even a single gold coin can serve as security for borrowing many coins. By contrast, movable property does not permit borrowing produce against produce, because produce is ordinarily purchased only with cash, whereas movable property is commonly sold for money. Taz's reasoning would imply that cash ownership permits a seah-for-seah loan, though the authorities do not appear to agree.
