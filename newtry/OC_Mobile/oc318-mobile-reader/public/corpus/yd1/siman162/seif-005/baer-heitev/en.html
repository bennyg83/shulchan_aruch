The borrower. Shach writes that the burden of proof is on the lender only where it is known that the loan occurred while prices were low, or the date of the loan is recorded, so that a possible interest issue appears and permission must be demonstrated. If the lender claims the obligation arose through a purchase rather than a loan, and the document merely states that a certain quantity is owed at a later date, he may be believed. A clause giving the lender credibility regarding all aspects of the debt also supports him. Taz distinguishes siman 169, where a lender holding a document swears and collects because many common heterim exist; here the only heterim are ownership of the species or an established market price, which often are absent. Nekudot HaKesef gives a different explanation: the parties may simply not have anticipated a price increase and therefore did not arrange the heter.
I transferred. Taz questions why ownership cannot be transferred to the borrower outside his presence, as with a prozbul, since both laws are rabbinic. Nekudot HaKesef answers that prozbul is treated as a benefit to the borrower because it preserves access to credit; that rationale does not apply here.
Millet. The same applies to any two different species. Levush writes that although a market price for wheat alone may permit borrowing wheat, lending one species against another is worse. Taz distinguishes siman 163:2, where one may sell wheat to buy wine if the seller owns the wheat, because that is a commercial sale intended for profit. A loan may not generate profit through repayment.
