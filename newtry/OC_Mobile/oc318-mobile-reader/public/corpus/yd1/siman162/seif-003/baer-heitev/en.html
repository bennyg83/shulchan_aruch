Market price. Beit Yosef writes that it suffices for the parties to know that a market price exists, even if they do not know the precise price. Rosh appears not to require knowledge at all. Shach writes that although initially one should not lend until the price is established, after the fact, if it was established, the loan involved no prohibition and must be repaid even if they did not know. Taz explains that readily available market goods resemble the case of one who says, 'Lend me until my son arrives.' Even if the borrower has no cash, he can buy on credit or borrow from another. This applies only to produce lent against produce, not to money loans. A price established in another locality may be relied upon even if surrounding places lack a price.
Whenever he wishes. Shach writes that it is permitted even if a repayment date was fixed. Rosh writes that if the lender says he will not accept payment before that date, borrowing based on market price is forbidden. If the borrower actually owns wheat, even this condition may be permitted.
The higher price. Even if the lender does not explicitly say, 'until the price rises,' but merely refuses to accept earlier payment, the loan is forbidden although it is uncertain whether the price will rise. This resembles lending a kor of wheat until harvest. Shach adds that if the fixed date is chosen because the price may fall before then, all agree it is forbidden even without an explicit refusal to accept earlier payment.
