(Seif 3) If that commodity had an established market price known to both parties, it is permitted to borrow one se'ah in exchange for one se'ah. It appears to me that one who lends one se'ah for one se'ah in reliance on the market price must deduct the ordinary allowance for shrinkage and loss, just as one who contracts to supply produce at the market price must do, as stated in siman 175, seif 5, and in Choshen Mishpat, siman 292, seif 12, regarding a deposit. The lender must not gain anything through the transaction, all the more so than in a forward purchase, which is a sale. Certainly, borrowing one se'ah for one se'ah at the market price is not more lenient than contracting at the market price. Indeed, one authority in Perek Eizehu Neshekh, folio 73, forbids borrowing at the market price, whereas no authority disputes that one may contract at the market price, for the Mishnah states explicitly there: once the market price has been established, one may contract. Accordingly, when the Gemara concludes that borrowing is permitted, it certainly did not intend to make it more lenient. This is also explicit in the Gemara in Perek Eizehu Neshekh, folio 63, concerning one se'ah for one se'ah, where it discusses wheat spoiling in storage and requires that the lender gain nothing. The Rosh likewise wrote this at the end of Perek Eizehu Neshekh; see there. Nevertheless, if the market price has not been established, the transaction is forbidden. The same implication appears in Tosafot on folio 62 regarding produce that came into their hands as a deposit: because they gain nothing, it is treated as a deposit. The same applies here when the market price has been established and the loan is permitted, since it is treated like a deposit. He must therefore deduct the ordinary losses, as with a deposit. This appears clear to me, and the burden of proof rests upon one who would rule leniently. In Perek HaOmanin, chapter 2, where one who borrowed produce is treated as a paid custodian and may apparently gain through the transaction, the case is one in which he already possessed a small amount when he borrowed. See the Taz at the end of siman 160. It also appears to me obvious that if he lent the produce during the summer and it was returned during the rainy season, he need not deduct for shrinkage, because produce gains weight then, as stated in the cited siman of Choshen Mishpat regarding a deposit. However, where no repayment date was fixed, it appears that no deduction is required. See Rambam, at the beginning of chapter 10 of Hilkhot Malveh VeLoveh.
