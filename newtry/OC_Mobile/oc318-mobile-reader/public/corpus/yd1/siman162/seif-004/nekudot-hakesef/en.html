(Taz, seif katan 8) It is difficult for me, etc., since it is rabbinic, etc. His words are astonishing. What relevance does the fact that it is rabbinic have here? On the contrary, the permission is rabbinic, for the Sages instituted the prozbul. Here too, the prohibition against lending one seah in return for one seah is rabbinic. Regarding his difficulty, it appears to me that there is no difficulty, because that case is different, as the Ran wrote in the chapter HaSholeach: 'From here it appears to me that the rule that one may acquire a benefit for a person in his absence applies whenever the matter itself is beneficial, even though a liability greater than the benefit results from it. The acquisition is effective, for here, when one transfers to him any amount of land, he thereby incurs a liability, because no shemittah will cancel his debt, and nevertheless we say that the transfer is effective. It is possible, however, to reject this proof and say that this too is one of the leniencies of prozbul.' These are his words. He means that they enacted this specifically concerning prozbul because of the concern that lenders would otherwise refuse to lend. That concern does not apply here. Furthermore, even if one were to say that the same principle applies generally as it does to prozbul, that would be only in a case comparable to prozbul, where the resulting liability does not take effect immediately, but only later, when shemittah arrives. Here, by contrast, the liability takes effect immediately upon the transfer, because the commodity may rise in price at once. This distinction is necessary. Otherwise, how could the Ran seek to derive from prozbul that generally one may acquire a benefit for a person in his absence even though a liability greater than the benefit results? The Gemara states in the chapter HaIsh Mekadesh, Kiddushin 42a: 'From where do we know that one may acquire a benefit for a person in his absence? It is stated, one leader, one leader. But is that necessarily a benefit? There may also be a liability, because one person prefers a mountain and not a valley, while another prefers a valley and not a mountain. Rather, it accords with Rava bar Rav Huna, who said in the name of Rav Gidal in the name of Rav: Regarding orphans who come to divide their father's estate, beit din appoints a guardian for them, to impose a liability in order to secure a benefit. The verse states, one leader, one leader.' We thus see that one does not ordinarily acquire for a person in his absence where there is also a liability. The distinction must therefore be as I wrote. Furthermore, even if one says that here too the liability is considered to arise only later, the cases are still dissimilar. Here, for the permission involving ribbit, the knowledge of both parties is required, as stated above in seif 3. He wrote above that concerning the rule of 'he has it,' the knowledge of both parties is unnecessary, but his words are not compelling, as I wrote there.
