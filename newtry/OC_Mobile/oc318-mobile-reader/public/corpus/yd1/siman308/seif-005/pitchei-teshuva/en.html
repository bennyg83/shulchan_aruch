It is nullified in the majority. See the Noda BiYehudah responsum there, where he was asked about wool shorn from a bekhor that became mixed with other wool, and it is recognizable because the bekhor's wool is soft, though there is concern that one cannot be exact. He wrote that since even definite wool from an unblemished bekhor is forbidden only rabbinically, as Tosafot wrote in Chullin 36, therefore even if it fell off by itself and it was an unblemished bekhor, since the Rosh and Tur hold that a significant item is required, we listen to the lenient view. Furthermore, even according to the view that forbids in any amount, that is specifically if it was certainly mixed in; but if one removes it, we are not concerned that perhaps he did not remove all of it, as proven from above siman 302 seif 1. He further wrote that even if it were certainly mixed and he could not recognize it at all, one may permit selling it to a non-Jew except for the value of the forbidden portion, and one need not be concerned that the non-Jew will sell it back to a Jew, since this wool involves a double rabbinic prohibition: the bekhor's wool itself is only rabbinic, and the fact that it is not nullified in the majority is rabbinic. All the more so after he removed the recognizable soft wool and there is only concern that perhaps a little remained; certainly one should not be so stringent, and he may sell it to a non-Jew; see there. See Chatam Sofer responsa siman 307, where it is explained that if bekhor wool was certainly mixed in, there is no remedy by selling all of it to a non-Jew except for the value of the forbidden portion, since it is a Torah prohibition, and he did not mention there the Tosafot in Chullin cited above. Since it is a definite prohibition mixed in, we are concerned that the non-Jew may sell it back to a Jew. Nor is there a remedy by casting the benefit into the Dead Sea, for whenever the prohibition does not transfer its prohibition to its proceeds, casting away the benefit does not help, as the Shach wrote in siman 110 s.k. 2. However, he wrote that all this applies if it is clear to us that bekhor wool became mixed with the wool. In his case, no Jew saw it, and only the shepherd said so; his words are as nothing. Who says he mixed the bekhor's wool into it? Perhaps he took the wool for himself, etc. Certainly we may permit it by selling all of it to a non-Jew except for the value of the bekhor wool. As an added measure before the sale, one should also cast the value of one fleece into the sea, since the Raavad and those with him, brought in the Shach there, hold that this helps even for prohibitions that have no redemption. According to him, it would then be permitted to a Jew outright; so at least we can afterward sell it to a non-Jew except for the forbidden value, and in such a case it is permitted without hesitation. See there. I do not know why he leaned so far to stringency and caused him the loss of two fleeces, after already mentioning that the shepherd's words are as nothing; perhaps he was concerned that the owner believes him.
