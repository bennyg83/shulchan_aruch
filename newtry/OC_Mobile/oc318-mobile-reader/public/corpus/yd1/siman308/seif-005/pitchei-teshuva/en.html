It is nullified in the majority. See the same Responsa Noda BiYehudah concerning firstborn wool mixed with other wool, where the firstborn wool can be identified as softer, but there is concern that it is impossible to remove it with absolute precision. He writes that even wool certainly from an unblemished firstborn is prohibited only by rabbinic law, as Tosafot state in Chullin 36. Therefore, even if it fell off by itself from an unblemished firstborn, since the Rosh and Tur require an important amount, one may follow the lenient view. Further, even according to the authority who prohibits any quantity, that applies where it definitely became mixed. If one removes the identifiable wool, we do not suspect that some remained, as proved from siman 302, seif 1. He further writes that even if it certainly became mixed and could not be identified at all, one may sell the mixture to a non-Jew after deducting the value of the prohibited portion. We need not fear that the non-Jew will resell it to a Jew because there are two rabbinic factors: the prohibition of the firstborn wool itself is rabbinic, and the rule that it is not nullified in the majority is also rabbinic. All the more so after the identifiable soft wool has been removed and only a concern remains that a small amount may still be present, there is certainly no reason for such stringency. See there. Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 307, however, indicates that if firstborn wool was certainly mixed in, there is no remedy by selling the whole to a non-Jew after deducting the value of the prohibited material, because he treats the prohibition as Torah law and there is definite prohibited material in the mixture, creating concern that the non-Jew may resell it to a Jew. Nor is casting the value of the benefit into the Dead Sea effective where the prohibition does not transfer its sanctity to money, as Shach writes in siman 110, seif katan 2. He adds, however, that this applies only if we know with certainty that the firstborn wool became mixed. In his case no Jew saw this; only the shepherd said so, and his statement has no halachic standing. Who says he mixed the wool in rather than taking the firstborn fleece for himself? Therefore the mixture can certainly be sold to a non-Jew after deducting the value of the firstborn fleece. As an additional precaution, before the sale he should also cast into the sea the value of one fleece, because the Raavad and those who agree with him, cited by the Shach, hold that this helps even for prohibitions that cannot be redeemed. According to them, the mixture may afterward even be sold directly to a Jew. Thus at least one may sell it to a non-Jew after deducting the value of the prohibited portion. I do not know why he was so stringent as to require the owner to lose the value of two fleeces after already saying that the shepherd's words have no halachic standing. Perhaps he was concerned that the owner personally believed the shepherd. See above, siman 16, seif 11.
