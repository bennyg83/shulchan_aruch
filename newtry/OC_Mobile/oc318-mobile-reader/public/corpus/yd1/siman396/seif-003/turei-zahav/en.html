A minor who died, etc., and even if he became adult, etc. This law is a dispute between Maharam and his student the Rosh at the end of Moed Katan. Maharam holds that he is obligated to mourn after he becomes adult, observing seven and thirty from the day he became adult, and it is like one who heard a close report during a festival, who mourns after the festival seven and thirty. Although there the days of the festival count toward the thirty, that is because he practiced in them the prohibition of pressing clothes and haircutting; but here, in his minority, he practiced nothing. Although there is some room to distinguish between hearing during a festival and this case, for there the person is subject to obligation and the day is the cause, while here the person is not subject to obligation at all, and if so we might say that since at the time of death he was not fit to mourn, he is exempt forever, this is not so, for there is no disqualification regarding mitzvot, as stated in Perek Kisui HaDam, etc. The Rosh disagrees and says: I accept that reasoning, that one may distinguish between where the person is subject to obligation and the day is the cause, and a minor, who at the time when he should have mourned was exempt because of minority; the obligation of mourning has departed from him forever. It is comparable to what is said in Perek Mi SheHayah Tamei: a minor who became adult between the two Pesachs, where Rabbi Natan holds that the second is compensation for the first, and anyone who was not obligated in the first is not obligated in the second. So too, sitting for a close report for seven and thirty is not an independent mitzvah, but compensation for the mourning he should have performed immediately after burial when he knew; when he did not know, they enacted that he has compensation throughout thirty days. Where he knew and was not obligated, compensation is not relevant, just like one lame on the first day who became able-bodied on the second day of the festival, where compensation is not relevant, etc.; end quote. Rabbeinu Yerucham wrote that one should rule like Maharam, who was the Rosh’s teacher, but the Beit Yosef decided like the Rosh, because the halachah follows the lenient view in mourning. So he ruled here in the Shulchan Aruch, that he is exempt. In my youth such a case came before me, a minor who became adult within the seven days, and I examined it. I found difficulty in the words of the Rosh, who wrote that mourning is treated as compensation, for compensation is not relevant here; rather, each day of the seven is a mitzvah unto itself, since we rule here in seif 2 that if he treated part of the seven days lightly, he need not make them up. Why then did the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write “even if he became adult within seven”? I presented these matters before my teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, and he answered that in any case the Shulchan Aruch’s words contradict each other. In Perek Mi Shemeto there is also a dispute between the Rosh and Rabbeinu Yehudah, on one side, and Maharam, regarding havdalah. Rabbeinu Yehudah was an onen on motzaei Shabbat and did not make havdalah, and the next day, Sunday, after burial, he also did not want to make havdalah, since he was exempt at the time of the obligation of havdalah. This is like the lame person who became able-bodied on the second day that I mentioned above: since he was deferred, he remains deferred. Maharam disagreed there and held that on the next day he is obligated to make havdalah, for we hold that one who did not make havdalah on motzaei Shabbat continues to make havdalah all week. The Rosh wrote there that he agreed with Rabbeinu Yehudah, for when we say that one who did not make havdalah on motzaei Shabbat, etc., that is where he had no wine on motzaei Shabbat or another unavoidable circumstance that prevented him from making havdalah. But an onen, who at the moment the obligation of havdalah fell upon him was entirely exempt, is no longer obligated; it is like the lame person above; end quote. Now above in siman 341 the Shulchan Aruch ruled that he is obligated to make havdalah, like Maharam. Here he ruled that he is exempt when he became adult, like the Rosh, though the same reason underlies both disputes. There is no settlement for this. Therefore my teacher and father-in-law ruled everywhere like Maharam, who was the teacher of the Rosh, and here too he is obligated in seven and thirty, unlike the Shulchan Aruch’s ruling. So he set it in his commentary to Yoreh Deah here. I, in my poverty, was astonished and amazed at this ruling. The fathers of the world, Maharam and the Rosh, disagree in it, and I fear to insert my weak head between great mountains, the luminaries of Israel. Nevertheless, I cannot hold back words, for in my humble opinion this ruling is an explicit Talmud, neither like Maharam nor like the Rosh, with forgiveness for their honor. On this basis it will become clear that the Shulchan Aruch’s rulings are the words of the living God.
We say in Perek Nigmar HaDin, Sanhedrin 47, regarding what we say that those executed by the court were not buried in their ancestral graves: if you think that once he is executed he has atonement, let them bury him. It answers: death and burial are required. Rav Ada challenged: they would not mourn over those executed by the court, but they would be onen. If you think that once he is buried he has atonement, let them mourn. It answers: we also require decomposition of the flesh. Rav Ashi said: in truth we do not require decomposition of the flesh, but only a little pain of the grave’s beating. The reason they did not mourn is that mourning takes effect from the sealing of the golel; when does atonement occur? When he experiences a little pain of the grave. Therefore, since they were deferred, they remain deferred. Rashi explains: since they were deferred, for they were not fit to be mourned at the time mourning began, they are deferred even afterward. Although regarding one who buries his dead on a festival we learned that he counts seven days after the festival and we do not say that since they were deferred they remain deferred, there they were not entirely deferred, for we say that anything involving the public does not have the festival interrupt it, and many come to comfort him; end quote.
Behold you see an a fortiori here. In Perek Nigmar HaDin, after the sealing of the golel there was no mourning in the world, since the dead person was not fit for it because he had not yet received atonement, and one might have said that after he is in the grave a little and receives atonement, his mourning should begin, just as with other dead from the sealing of the golel. Nevertheless we do not say so; rather, we say that since he was deferred at the time mourning begins for the rest of the world, they never mourn for him afterward. All the more so here, where mourning existed in the world but the mourner did not become obligated: it is obvious that since he was deferred at the time of obligation, he is deferred forever. It is simple that the halachah follows Rav Ashi, for he is later. Furthermore, I say that even the first answer in the Gemara does not disagree with this reasoning that once deferred, it remains deferred; for if so, according to that answer too it is difficult: they should mourn for those executed by the court after decomposition, for then they certainly have atonement. Rather, it is obvious that here we say once deferred, deferred. They disagree with Rav Ashi only regarding what he said, that they have atonement quickly, once they experience a little pain of the grave; in this they disagree and hold that atonement does not come so quickly. But if it were quick, certainly they too would hold that once deferred, deferred. So it is also explained there earlier in the sugya, that according to the conclusion all agree that once deferred, deferred. The Beit Yosef too, in siman 345, wrote: “And we conclude regarding those executed by the court in Perek Nigmar HaDin that they have atonement through execution and burial, but the reason we do not mourn for them is that mourning begins from the sealing of the golel, etc.” This is the answer of Rav Ashi, except that in the wording of the Beit Yosef there is a scribal error: he wrote “those executed by the government,” but it should read “those executed by the court.” If so, it is clear unlike Maharam, who obligates a minor who became adult, and also unlike the Rosh. According to the Rosh, the exemption is because it is compensation, and anyone exempt at the time of the obligation has no compensation, implying that in a place where compensation is not involved there would be obligation, and he would agree with Maharam. But here in Perek Nigmar HaDin there is no compensation, for it was not fit for mourning initially, and nevertheless we say he is exempt afterward. Rather, it is clear that the exemption is not because compensation is inapplicable; rather, the Sages imposed mourning upon him from the first hour, when the bitterness is strongest, namely from the sealing of the golel. From that hour he mourns and continues according to his law. But anyone who is exempt at the essential hour of bitterness, namely after the sealing of the golel, has no obligation afterward. Therefore those executed by the court, and likewise a minor who became adult, are completely exempt, since they were exempt at the essential hour of bitterness. Rashi’s explanation that I copied accords beautifully with this: it is not comparable to one who buries his dead on a festival, who is obligated in seven and thirty after the festival, because there, at the essential hour of bitterness, he was not exempt, for he has some mourning even on the festival. There is no comparison to havdalah, where he is obligated to perform it the next day although on motzaei Shabbat he was exempt, as the Shulchan Aruch wrote in siman 341. There, the essential time of havdalah was initially designed to extend all week, or until Tuesday night. We do not say that if he did not make havdalah on motzaei Shabbat, the later havdalah is compensation for motzaei Shabbat; rather, it is still its actual time. This is comparable to the evening prayer and Shema, about which the Sages said in tractate Berakhot that their time is before eating, and anyone who transgresses is liable to death, and nevertheless its time is all night, as they explicitly said that its time is all night. So too exactly with havdalah; this is included in what they said, that one who did not make havdalah on motzaei Shabbat continues to make havdalah all week. Nor is there difficulty from what we find in Sukkah, Perek HaYashen 27, that a minor who became adult during Sukkot is obligated to make a sukkah. There too, its time is all seven days, and there is no compensation for what he did not sit in a sukkah during his minority, as the Mishnah there says that there is no compensation for one who did not eat in the sukkah. This is unlike mourning, where the essence depends on the beginning of mourning at the essential hour of bitterness, and anyone who did not begin does not complete it. Therefore it appears for halachah and practice like the Rosh’s ruling regarding mourning, that he is exempt here, though not for his reason, but for the reason we stated; and regarding havdalah, like the view of Maharam and like the Shulchan Aruch’s ruling in both places. They are correct and clear. Our teachers, the fathers of the world, left us a place to merit through it with the help of Heaven.
