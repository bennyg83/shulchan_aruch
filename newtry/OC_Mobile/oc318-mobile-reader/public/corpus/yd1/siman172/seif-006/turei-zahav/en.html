When the tax is money. For this tax is not imposed on his money but on the body of the land of the borrower; but if the tax is in produce, that the master takes a share in the produce of the land, it is forbidden, because the share belonging to the master in the produce, the owner of the land cannot sell it nor mortgage it; and when the borrower pays it to the master, it is ribbit that he is giving to the lender; so wrote Beit Yosef in the name of the author of Sefer HaTerumos in the name of the Ramban. And the explanation of his words appears to be that if the giving of the tax is in money, automatically the tax belongs from the body of the land, and the body of the land is the borrower's, for he did not sell it to him with a deduction except the produce that grow in it for some time; and therefore the borrower is obligated to give that tax by law.
One who mortgaged a house, etc. This is from a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of the siman. The principal novelty is that one might otherwise say: Since this is a loan, the rent that the borrower pays the lender, which the lender was compelled to pay to others, should be regarded as interest. Even Rav, who holds that one who sells property that is not his is entitled to repayment of the purchase money and the appreciation, nevertheless concedes that with a loan it would be interest. The ruling therefore teaches that it is permitted here, because the deduction is regarded as a sale with respect to the produce. The Rashba concludes that the matter requires further consideration and deliberation, etc. That is, perhaps it is not regarded as an outright sale to the extent that the borrower must reimburse the lender for the rent he paid to the owner of the house. The Rama’s citation requires further study, for in the Rashba’s responsum it is stated that the borrower expressly accepted responsibility, and on this basis the Rashba ruled that the borrower must pay what he had stipulated under the law of responsibility. Why, then, did the Rama not mention here that the borrower accepted responsibility? It appears that he assumes that here too we say that responsibility omitted from the document is treated as a scribal error, as stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 225. This is no proof, however. The reason that responsibility is treated as a scribal error, stated in the first chapter of Bava Metzia 14a, is that a person does not cast away his money for nothing. That reasoning does not apply here. Even if the owner of the house takes it from the lender, the lender will suffer no loss, for the money he lent will be returned to him, and he will also make no deduction for the residence, since it belonged to others. This should not be compared to Choshen Mishpat, siman 373, concerning one who sells a field that is not his. There the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Rosh that even regarding the appreciation we treat responsibility as a scribal error. That case is different, because it is evident that anyone who purchases a field intends to improve it, and for that purpose he exerts himself and spends considerable money; it is therefore insufficient that the seller merely return his purchase money. Here, however, with a deduction, the lender deducts only a small amount in exchange for produce worth much more. The essential permission rests upon his entering into a doubt that perhaps no produce will grow at all. Certainly, he does not expect the borrower to pay from his own pocket whatever amount the lender may need to pay others beyond the amount of the deduction, since the lender will not suffer any actual loss. He willingly enters into such a doubt, for perhaps he will spend little and consume much if the owner does not take the property from him. If the owner does take it from him, he will nevertheless suffer no loss, for he lived there in any event, and the borrower will not deduct anything for that residence. Thus, the arrangement is close to substantial profit and entirely remote from loss. Why should we say that responsibility is treated as a scribal error when the borrower did not expressly accept it? Furthermore, even there in Choshen Mishpat, siman 373, the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Maggid Mishneh that according to the Raavad, with respect to appreciation, responsibility is not treated as a scribal error. Therefore, it appears that here, with a deduction, where no sale is mentioned at all but only a loan accompanied by an arrangement permitting the lender to consume the produce because of the deduction, that arrangement is sufficient to permit the produce. We should not say that it also causes the borrower to become obligated to pay from his own pocket for the produce taken by the owner, unless he expressly accepted responsibility. This is certainly in keeping with the Torah’s ways, which are ways of pleasantness. It was not for nothing that the Rashba explicitly mentioned in his responsum that the borrower accepted responsibility. Moreover, even in that case the Rashba concluded that the matter required further deliberation, whereas the Rama presented it as a clear halacha. We should therefore not extend it beyond its stated case. It applies specifically where, at the time of the deduction, the borrower expressly accepted responsibility to pay the rent from his own pocket if the owner took the house from the lender. In that case, a monetary stipulation is binding. If he did not expressly accept responsibility, he must pay the lender only the money that was lent, without any deduction, even though the lender was compelled to pay rent. Responsibility is not treated here as a scribal error. This appears to me.
