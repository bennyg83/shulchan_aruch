One who pledged a house, etc. This is from the responsum of Rashba cited by Beit Yosef at the end of the siman; and the principal novelty is that you should not say: since it is a loan, whatever the borrower gives the lender — namely the rent that the lender was obligated to pay to others — constitutes interest; and even according to the view that holds "the seller of something not his own has a claim to the money and the appreciation," he concedes regarding a loan that it would constitute interest; therefore it teaches us that it is permitted here, for the deduction is like a sale with regard to the fruits — and he concludes that the matter requires further consideration, meaning perhaps it is not a complete sale with regard to this, that he should pay the lender what he needed to give to the homeowner. And there is a difficulty regarding Rema's transcription here: for in the responsum of Rashba it is mentioned that the borrower accepted liability, and based on this the Rashba ruled that the borrower is obligated to pay what he stipulated under the law of liability — but why did Rema not mention that the borrower accepted liability? It seems he believes that even here we say liability is a scribal error, as written in Choshen Mishpat siman 225. But that inference is unwarranted: for the principle that liability is a scribal error is derived in chapter 1 of Bava Metzia (14a) because a person does not throw money away — meaning a buyer would not buy something knowing someone can seize it; and here one cannot apply this reasoning, for even if the homeowner takes it back from him, the lender will suffer no loss — the money he lent will be returned to him, and nothing will be deducted from him for the dwelling on account of its belonging to another. And one cannot compare this to what is written in Choshen Mishpat siman 373 regarding the seller of a field not his own — where Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Rosh that even with regard to appreciation we say liability is a scribal error — for there it is different: we know that any buyer of a field intends to improve it, and labors and spends much money for this purpose, and it would not suffice for him if the seller merely returned his money; whereas here with the deduction, only a small amount is deducted in exchange for fruits worth much — and the principal purpose of the arrangement is that the lender takes upon himself the risk that perhaps no fruit will grow at all; but certainly he does not want the borrower to pay out of his own pocket what he will be forced to give to others, beyond what accrues to him from the deduction — since the lender will suffer no loss, and he willingly accepts such a risk: possibly he will spend a little and eat much if the homeowner does not take it back, and if the homeowner does take it back, he will at any rate suffer no loss, for he lived there and the borrower will not deduct anything — so it approaches great profit and is far from any loss; and why would we say there is a scribal liability here when he did not explicitly accept it? And furthermore, in that very Choshen Mishpat siman 373, Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Maggid Mishneh that Raavad holds that with regard to appreciation we do not say liability is a scribal error. Therefore it appears that here, with the deduction — where a sale is not mentioned at all but only a loan, and the parties merely arrange a permitted arrangement so that the lender can eat the fruits on account of the deduction — we should not say that this permitted arrangement causes the borrower to be obligated to pay the lender out of his own pocket for the fruits if the homeowner takes them back, unless the borrower explicitly accepted liability; and this is certainly the way of the Torah: "its ways are ways of pleasantness" — and not without reason did Rashba in his responsum mention that the borrower explicitly accepted liability, and even so the Rashba concluded the matter requires further consideration; Rema however treated it as settled law — and one should not add beyond it: specifically when the borrower explicitly accepted liability at the time of the deduction, he is obligated to pay rent out of his own pocket if the homeowner takes it back; but if he did not explicitly accept liability, he pays only the money lent him without deduction, even though the lender was compelled to pay rent — and this does not constitute scribal liability, in my view.
