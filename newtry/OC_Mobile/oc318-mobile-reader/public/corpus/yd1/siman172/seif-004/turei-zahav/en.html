For this sum. This means for less than its value, where he gives him a reduced price because of the loan. If, however, he says that he will sell it to him at its value, meaning that he even fixes the price at the actual value of that land, since he does not reduce the price for him it is permitted. So wrote the Beit Yosef, unlike the Maggid Mishneh, who forbids fixing a price even at its full value. See what I proved from this in siman 160, seif 23. The Levush wrote that although the law of a neighboring landowner does not apply to collateral, as stated by the Tur, and the lender takes precedence over the neighboring landowner, nevertheless, in this loan, where he said, ‘Do not sell it to anyone but me at its value,’ it appears to him that the lender does not displace the neighboring landowner. Otherwise, the benefit of displacing the neighboring landowner would constitute interest for him, since he has no acquisition in this land. End quote. This is entirely inaccurate. What interest applies here, when the borrower loses nothing? Why should it matter to him that the lender benefits by displacing the neighboring landowner? According to his reasoning, it would be forbidden to lend against movable collateral, since the lender benefits from the perutah of Rav Yosef, and for this reason he is considered a paid guardian in Choshen Mishpat, siman 72. Rather, it is obvious that the lender may receive any benefit in which the borrower has no involvement. For added clarity, I will bring proof. In the chapter Eizehu Neshech, Bava Metzia 65a, the Gemara asks: Why is it called a mortgage? Because it dwells with him. What practical difference follows? The law of a neighboring landowner. We see that since it is called a mortgage, this is its law. The Gemara there on 65a also calls the arrangement in which he says, ‘Sell it to me at its value,’ a mortgage.
