And he writes, etc. This implies that the basis for the permission is that he writes this formulation in the document. The Tur also implies this, and Tosafot likewise wrote so on page 67, at the beginning of the second side. Accordingly, even if the lender has the power to collect his debt whenever he wishes, it is permitted. This is also explicit in the Rosh. It is possible that the Maggid Mishneh agrees, and that when he wrote that the reason is that the lender lacks the power to collect his debt, he cited one of two reasons. A practical difference arises wherever the lender cannot compel the borrower, even though the borrower can remove him: even if the document does not state, “when he pays,” etc., it is permitted. The Maggid Mishneh also concludes there with this reason in the name of Ramban. Nevertheless, the plain meaning of his words there implies that we always require that the lender have no power to collect any part of his debt. This also appears to be the Mechaber’s view.
That after ten years. The same applies after a longer period. See Responsa Maharam Alashkar, siman 115, concerning the law of a mortgage.
Likewise, if he stipulated, etc. Meaning, even if the borrower stipulated that he could remove him, etc., it is permitted, and certainly where he made no stipulation.
From the field, etc. Meaning, not only a field, which sometimes produces no crops and therefore involves the possibility that it will yield nothing, but even a house or courtyard, whose produce, namely its occupancy, is regularly available, is permitted. A house too can sometimes suffer damage, as stated below in the gloss.
He can remove him, etc. See below, seif katan 26, regarding what is considered a case in which he can remove him.
Even a small amount. Even less than the valuation used for an ancestral field, namely one sela and one pundion per year for land capable of being sown with a kor of barley. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe.
Some say there is no distinction, etc. This wording is imprecise. According to these authorities, namely Rashba and those who follow him, there is certainly a distinction between a place where the borrower may remove the lender and a place where he may not, when there is no deduction. In a place where he may remove him, it is fixed ribbit; where he may not remove him, it is avak ribbit, as their words state explicitly. Since there is a prohibition in either case, the Rama did not trouble to distinguish between them. Their words also clearly imply that, according to both views, even if the lender can collect his debt, the arrangement is permitted when there is a deduction. This is also the prevailing custom, unlike the Levush, who inserted into the words of the “some say” that the lender may not compel the borrower, etc., and then wrote, “and this is the custom,” etc. See there, for his words are confused.
And some permit. See the responsum of Maharil cited there. It implies that there is room to permit even fixed ribbit, because he compares it to orphan funds. Yet according to his conclusion there, which forbids fixed ribbit even with orphan funds, and as the Rama wrote above in siman 160, seif 18, the same applies here. Maharshal likewise wrote so in chapter HaChovel, siman 72. See there, where he discusses this at length.
On books, etc. The Sema wrote in Choshen Mishpat, siman 72, seif katan 5, that this requires study, since Ravyah, cited by Mordechai at the end of chapter Elu Metziot, forbids using books as collateral even without deduction. This is no difficulty, because Rashba too permits books only when there is a deduction. None of this escaped the Rama, for in Darkei Moshe he wrote: “Mordechai, at the end of chapter Elu Metziot, states that if one lent money against books, he may not study from them, even if the borrower stipulated otherwise. With a deduction, however, it is permitted. In chapter Eizehu Neshech he wrote that according to Rashi, who forbids a house or courtyard even with a deduction, books are likewise forbidden even with a deduction. This implies that according to Rabbeinu Tam, who permits it, books are likewise permitted. Beit Yosef also wrote this, unlike Nimukei Yosef. Beit Yosef further cited, at the end of this siman, Rashba’s responsum that even books are permitted with a deduction. Maharil wrote in responsum 37, and the Agudah states, that the custom is to lend money against synagogue seats and sit in them because this is a mitzvah, etc. It appears that books are certainly included.” These are the words of Darkei Moshe. Thus the Rama was fully aware of the view of Ravyah and those who follow him, who forbid books without a deduction. Based on their words, he wrote that it is proper to be stringent and arrange a deduction. This is clear.
Except when the responsibility for the collateral, etc. This does not mean that if the collateral is lost the borrower is exempt from paying the debt, for the Maggid Mishneh’s words in the name of Ramban imply that it is permitted even when the lender may collect from the borrower’s other assets. Rather, it excludes a case in which the borrower writes that his other assets are responsible for the collateral, for then, as soon as the collateral is damaged, the lender immediately has other property serving as collateral. If the borrower did not write such responsibility over his assets, then even though the lender may collect from other assets if the collateral is damaged, it is permitted, since he does not immediately have replacement collateral. The Tur also implies this. The Bach wrote in seif 12 that it is permitted even though he may collect from other assets. According to what I have written, there is no need to force the Tur’s use of the word “immediately” into the explanation offered by the Bach. Rather, the meaning is that the lender need not seek other assets, because he immediately has replacement collateral. The Levush did not understand it this way. See there, for his words are confused.
But, etc. For if the house collapses or burns, the borrower must immediately repay him from his other assets. Tur. Although one might say that the lender may not need the house and may fail to find tenants for it, as Tosafot wrote, that reason alone does not suffice to permit the arrangement. It serves only as an additional consideration alongside other possibilities, such as fire or collapse. Darkei Moshe, unlike Beit Yosef.
The produce that he consumed, etc. Certainly, if he consumed more than the amount of the debt, we do not extract it from him.
Since, etc. Meaning, this is not comparable to the case above in siman 167, seif 3, because here the mortgaged property was initially in the lender’s possession as security for the loan, as stated there in seif katan 11.
Nevertheless, etc. Likewise, if the borrower said, “I will make the effort and bring the money,” the Gemara states that the lender may no longer consume the produce. Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Rashba that this refers to a place where the borrower may not remove the lender, where there was a deduction and the stipulated term ended, and the lender continues consuming the produce after the term with the same deduction as before. This is permitted even though the document did not state, “Consume with a deduction for as long as this property remains in your possession.” Beit HaTerumot likewise wrote this in the name of Raavad. See there, and see Beit Yosef, beginning with the words, “If the lender said,” etc.
If he said, etc. Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Rashba’s students that some explain this as applying specifically when he made the declaration before a beit din, while others explain that it applies even when he did not do so before a beit din.
My produce as ribbit, etc. This implies that we deduct the value for him after he protests only where the arrangement constitutes avak ribbit. The Levush likewise wrote this. But where it does not constitute even avak ribbit, such as a mortgage with a deduction in a place where the borrower may remove the lender, then since the arrangement is permitted, even if the borrower protests we do not deduct the value until he actually brings the money. Beit Yosef wrote this in the name of Rashba, and the Bach ruled accordingly.
Even, etc. This implies that even in a place where the borrower may not remove the lender until a specified time, we deduct everything the lender consumed before the removal date. This follows Ramban’s view, cited by Beit HaTerumot and Rivash. Rivash wrote that the borrower nevertheless cannot remove the lender entirely, since the property had been transferred to him; rather, the lender consumes the produce at an assessed value, and the value of all the produce, as appraised by a beit din, is deducted from the debt. This resolves the apparent contradiction with the Mechaber’s ruling in Choshen Mishpat, siman 74, seif 3, that in a mortgage whose produce the lender consumes in a place where the borrower may not remove him, the lender need not accept repayment before the stipulated time unless the borrower leaves him the produce to consume. The Rama also rules there without qualification. The meaning there is that the borrower must allow him to consume the produce at its assessed value. Raavad, however, as cited by Beit Yosef, maintains that before the removal date the borrower cannot protest and no deduction is made, since it is a place where the borrower may not remove the lender. Rashba’s view, cited by Nimukei Yosef regarding the statement, “I will make the effort and bring the money,” also proves this. It appears to me that this is an unresolved question of law, and the burden of proof rests upon the claimant.
We deduct for him, etc. Since the borrower protests that the lender must not consume the produce, the lender’s continued consumption is robbery. Nimukei Yosef.
