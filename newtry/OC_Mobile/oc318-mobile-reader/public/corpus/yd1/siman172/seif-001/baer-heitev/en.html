Of Sura. Beit Yosef explains in the name of Ramban that in a Sura mortgage the lender bears no responsibility for the land. If a river washes it away, he receives nothing beyond whatever produce it yielded, and he cannot compel repayment from this land or any other assets. In an ordinary mortgage, however, the borrower remains responsible; the lender may demand repayment, and if the land is lost he collects from other property. Shach implies that the permission follows from the wording written in the document, and according to that view it may remain permitted even if the lender can collect whenever he wishes. The Mechaber, however, appears to require that the lender have no power to collect the debt.
A small amount. Even less than the rate used for an ancestral field, namely one sela and one pundeyon annually for land requiring a kor of barley seed. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe.
To remove him. Shach writes that even if the lender can collect the debt, it is permitted because the arrangement includes deduction from the principal. This is the basis of common practice, unlike Levush.
Books. Taz questions why Rama relied upon this view, since in Choshen Mishpat 72 Beit Yosef cites Rashba and Mordechai that without deduction it is forbidden and constitutes fixed interest. Rama should at least have cited the stringent view. Shach writes that Rama was aware of these sources, as seen in Darkei Moshe, and therefore concluded that it is proper to be stringent and use deduction.
When responsibility. Meaning the borrower writes responsibility against his other assets, so that if the collateral is impaired the lender immediately has other collateral. If he did not write such responsibility, then even though he may ultimately collect from other assets, he does not immediately possess substitute collateral, and it is permitted. Shach.
Forbidden. Taz writes that it is nevertheless only avak ribbit.
They deduct. Once the borrower protests against the lender's continued eating of produce, further consumption is theft. Beit Yosef cites two views whether the protest must be made before beit din. This deduction applies where the arrangement is avak ribbit. Where there is no avak ribbit at all, such as a mortgage with deduction in a place where the borrower may remove the lender, the lender continues eating until the borrower brings the money. In a place where removal is not permitted, if the term ended and the lender continues under the same deduction, it remains permitted even without an express clause. Several authorities hold that before the removal date the borrower cannot protest and no deduction is made. Shach regards this as a monetary doubt, with the burden of proof upon the claimant.
