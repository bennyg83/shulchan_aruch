(Addendum) Some say that she should not, etc. See Tosafot there, s.v. 'she opened,' etc. Rabbeinu Tam explains differently, as cited by the Rif. The Raavad explains that the rejection applies only from the teaching concerning the mark of bloodletting and onward, namely to the three discussions of the bloodletting mark, eye discharge, and eye paint, while Rabbi Yochanan's own ruling remains authoritative. The Gemara's citation of Reish Lakish supports Rabbi Yochanan's ruling, whereas the earlier teachings were stated only regarding handling sacred food in purity. This is why those teachings use the terms, 'constitutes an interposition' and 'does not constitute an interposition,' while Rabbi Yochanan's teaching says, 'her immersion did not count,' clearly referring to a woman immersing for her husband. The listener heard Rabbi Yochanan's ruling and mistakenly applied it to the earlier teachings as well. According to this explanation, dry eye discharge, eye paint even upon the eye itself, and a bloodletting mark even after three days do not invalidate immersion. This resolves Rashi's questions there. The Raavad limits the rejection to those three teachings because they are not formulated in the feminine, unlike the other teachings. Certainly Rabbi Yochanan's ruling is supported by Reish Lakish, whose statement comes to be stringent, not to reject Rabbi Yochanan. The Rashba agrees.
Rambam, however, understands the rejection as applying from the case of a woman placing food before her nursing child and onward, including Rabbi Yochanan's ruling. In Rambam's text the word 'eye discharge' is missing and must be restored. He accordingly groups all these cases together, but does not include the earlier teachings there. Rambam's version places the teaching about a woman lifting her child before the discussion of an earthenware vessel and after the words, 'she immersed and her immersion counted,' which is where this statement belongs. He therefore begins the rejected teachings with the case of placing food before her child. The teaching concerning a harbor cannot be included, because that ruling certainly follows the law, as derived from the father of Shmuel, as Rashi objects in Tosafot there.
Tosafot understand that the rejection applies from the earthenware vessel onward, including Rabbi Yochanan's ruling. See Tosafot on Niddah 66b, s.v. 'if,' and 67a, s.v. 'she opened.' The Rosh rules likewise at the end of siman 38. The Semag, in the name of the Aruch, extends Tosafot's view to Reish Lakish's statement as well, explaining that it certainly concerns purity, since it is derived from the laws of impurity and purity of skin lesions. Nevertheless, the Rosh writes that one should be stringent in accordance with Rashi, and Tosafot there also incline this way. The Shulchan Aruch therefore rules stringently without qualification in several places in this siman. Here, however, he writes, 'some say,' because his primary view follows the Rif and the other halachic authorities. The Semag in the name of the Raem, the Mordechai, and Hagahot Maimoniyot nevertheless write that even according to the Rif and those who agree with him, the leniency applies only after the fact, but initially one must be stringent even for immersion to permit her to her husband.
Accordingly, concerning the bloodletting mark, eye discharge, and eye paint, the Shulchan Aruch uses the Gemara's language, 'constitutes an interposition,' each according to the relevant view, initially or even after the fact. Concerning an earthenware vessel and a harbor, however, even according to the Raavad the immersion counts after the fact, as stated above in seifim 31 and 34, because the concern is fear or embarrassment. If she knows that she immersed properly, the immersion is valid. The Torat HaBayit likewise rules this way. Here the Shulchan Aruch writes, 'some say,' because of the Raavad's view. The statement in seif 35, 'and if she deviated,' follows the Semag and Aruch, but all the other halachic authorities disagree, as explained above; this is the opinion introduced there as, 'there is one who says.' The ruling in seif 38, 'if she jumped, her immersion did not count,' is accepted by all, since it is an explicit Mishnah. The halachic authorities distinguish between that case and closing one's eyes.
The Shulchan Aruch's unqualified rulings concerning a woman lifting her child in seif 41 and placing food before her child at the end of siman 199 are therefore difficult. He followed Rambam and the Tur when copying them, without addressing the preceding discussion. The ruling in seif 33, 'some say that she should not,' follows both Rashi and the Raavad. The Raavad disagrees with Rashi's interpretation but agrees with the law, as he writes in his work cited by the Beit Yosef. He distinguishes between immersing a bed and the case of a harbor, because water can enter between a person's toes. The Ran likewise writes this. The Torat HaBayit adds that some apply the same rule to a person, and explains that the case here concerns mud on the riverbank. This may be what Rashi means by saying in Niddah that women walk across them. See above, seif 31. The Rosh also records in seif 33 that some distinguish between people and vessels, but he disagrees, writing that according to the rationale that the water reaches the object first, there is no basis for distinction. He therefore agrees with Tosafot's explanation, which is why the Shulchan Aruch writes there, 'some say,' etc. (End of addendum.)
