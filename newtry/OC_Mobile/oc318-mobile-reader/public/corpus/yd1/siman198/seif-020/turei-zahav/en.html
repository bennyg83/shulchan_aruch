But the fingernail itself, etc. This is because it is part of the woman's body and resembles her hair.
And since the custom has already become established to cut them, etc. The glosses to Shaarei Dura state: "If she forgot and did not cut her fingernails before immersion, they do not constitute a chatzitzah, provided that there is no clay beneath them. Nevertheless, it is proper to be stringent and immerse a second time, because it is impossible that there should be no clay beneath them." End quote. The Maharshal cites the responsum of Maharam that her immersion is valid whenever she checks beneath her fingernails before immersion and finds no clay or excrement there. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, writes that even if she is certain that there was no excrement there, she should immerse a second time, because some authorities hold that a fingernail standing to be cut constitutes a chatzitzah. This is unlike the hair of the head, which most women do not ordinarily cut, whereas everyone cuts fingernails, and therefore an uncut fingernail constitutes a chatzitzah. End quote. His words are astonishing, for all the poskim write here that cutting the fingernails is merely a custom and is not required by law. It appears to me that whenever we find that a woman who forgot to cut a fingernail must immerse again, this applies only so long as she has not had relations with her husband before remembering. But if she remembers only after relations, one should not be stringent and require a second immersion, since this is merely an added stringency. This applies even if she does not know that there was no clay or excrement there. Although the glosses to Shaarei Dura and the responsum of Maharam cited above imply that by law she requires a second immersion, this is only before she had relations, but not afterward. Even if excrement was present there, according to Torah law it does not constitute a chatzitzah, because a substance must cover most of the body and be something about which she is particular. A substance covering a minority about which she is particular is only a rabbinic decree, as stated at the beginning of the siman. Thus, this is a doubt in a rabbinic matter. All the more so according to what I wrote in seif 18 in the name of the Semag and Rabbeinu Tam, that specifically firmly adhering clay is required. Although the Shulchan Aruch did not rule accordingly, once she has already had relations and may have conceived, to require a second immersion would cast aspersions upon the child. We find similar language in the first chapter of Gittin: "If you come to be stringent with her, you cast aspersions upon her children." This appears entirely clear to me. I was subsequently informed that a great authority ruled in a responsum as I have written.
And since it was established above that a woman's cutting of her fingernails is only a custom through which women accepted stringency upon themselves, it appears to me that the Mordechai in Hilchos Niddah, cited by the glosses to Shaarei Dura, which states that if her immersion occurs on Chol HaMoed it is proper for a non-Jewish maidservant to cut them for her, because instructing a non-Jew is a shevus and the Sages did not decree it in the place of a mitzvah, and that if she has no non-Jewish maidservant she should clean thoroughly beneath the fingernails, applies specifically on Chol HaMoed. On Shabbos or Yom Tov, however, if she forgot to cut her fingernails, she should not instruct a non-Jewish woman to cut them. Since cutting the fingernails is not an absolute mitzvah, for cleaning beneath them alone suffices, why should we override the shevus of instructing a non-Jew? Moreover, this would be a stringency that leads to leniency. Although the non-Jewish woman cuts them, the Jewish woman assists by extending and positioning her hands for her, and it is as though she performs this melacha, which is forbidden by Torah law on Shabbos and Yom Tov. This resembles what we find in siman 181 concerning rounding the corners of the head, where the person whose hair is cut is also liable if he assists by positioning himself to be cut. It is impossible to avoid this when allowing the non-Jewish woman to cut the fingernails without the Jewish woman extending her hands toward her. One cannot say that in such a case she should simply not immerse on Shabbos or Yom Tov. Even on Chol HaMoed they permitted her to clean beneath the fingernails, so why should Shabbos and Yom Tov be worse than the case in which she has no non-Jewish maidservant on Chol HaMoed? It is therefore clear that on Shabbos and Yom Tov she should not have them cut by a non-Jewish woman, but should clean them thoroughly and immerse. The Mordechai required cutting by a non-Jewish woman only on Chol HaMoed, when the law is lenient enough to permit even her own cutting. So we rule in Orach Chayyim siman 532 concerning immersion for a mitzvah according to all opinions, as the Rama writes there. The Mordechai, however, was a student of Maharam, who was stringent that fingernails may not be cut on Chol HaMoed except by a non-Jewish woman, as the Beit Yosef writes there. Therefore, according to his view, he required them to be cut by a non-Jewish woman. According to our ruling in siman 532, where cutting on Chol HaMoed is permitted, she may cut them normally on Chol HaMoed. On Shabbos and Yom Tov she should not cut them at all, but should clean them thoroughly. This appears entirely clear to me. I saw someone err in this matter and instruct that they be cut on Yom Tov by a non-Jewish woman, but in my humble opinion he did not rule correctly.
