Two hairs, etc. The Tur records this law twice in this siman, once here and a second time where he writes that when most of the hair is knotted, one strand, etc. His intent is that this law is likewise found twice in the Gemara. One source is in the chapter Bameh Ishah, where all agree that hair does not constitute a chatzitzah. This means that if she wound the hair of her head with other hair, it does not constitute a chatzitzah because it cannot be tightened well. The second source is in the final chapter of Niddah, cited by the Beit Yosef, where it states: One strand constitutes a chatzitzah; regarding two, I do not know; three do not constitute a chatzitzah. That source concerns the woman's own hair. If most of her hairs were tied individually, only then does it constitute a chatzitzah. This is the case discussed later by the Tur. Here, however, he discusses external hair attached to her hair. A practical distinction between the two cases is that here, whenever the attachment constitutes a chatzitzah, it does so even over a minority, like the woolen threads of seif 2, as I wrote there. Therefore, the Tur did not write here that most of her hair must be affected, as he writes later. There, since he discusses her own hair that became knotted, a majority is specifically required, for otherwise she is not particular. The Beit Yosef writes concerning the Tur's words here that this case concerns one who is very particular, etc., but that is imprecise. This consideration does not apply here, but only later, for the reason stated. Nevertheless, regarding the number of hairs, the laws are equal. Just as with her own hair the matter depends upon how the hairs were tied, whether one at a time or two at a time, so too if she wound one external hair around her hair it constitutes a chatzitzah. This applies even if she wound many hairs in separate places, provided that each winding consists of one hair, because one hair can be tightened well. But if she used two or more hairs for each winding, it does not constitute a chatzitzah. This is what the Tur means here by, "When does this apply? When they consist of two hairs," meaning each individual winding, as I explained. These words accord with the Ran cited by the Beit Yosef, who writes: "They agree concerning threads of hair that they do not constitute a chatzitzah. This applies specifically to hair placed upon girls' heads, for hair cannot be tightened well. But tying one strand to itself or one strand to another can be tightened, for we say in the final chapter of Niddah that one tied strand constitutes a chatzitzah, etc. Even winding these hair threads around her own hairs is permitted, because it is impossible for these threads not to contain more than one strand, etc." End quote. Thus, he too writes that the first law concerns winding hair around her own hair, while tying one strand concerns her own hair. His words, "or one strand to another," are likewise called one strand, for what difference is there whether it is tied to itself or to another strand, as the Beit Yosef writes later. The Tur therefore properly ruled like R' Yochanan, whom the Beit Yosef cites, and the Bach needlessly wrote that he ruled like Rabbah. The Ran concludes that external hair wound around her hair does not constitute a chatzitzah because such a winding is not made from a single strand. The Tur writes that if it was made from one strand, it does constitute a chatzitzah. Their legal rulings do not differ, only their assumptions about the facts. Accordingly, the words of the Shulchan Aruch are clear. What he writes in seif 4, that threads of hair do not constitute a chatzitzah, refers to the winding discussed above. Even there, however, the law depends upon whether the winding was made from one hair or more. If it was made from one hair, it constitutes a chatzitzah even if it affects only a minority and she is not particular, like the woolen threads in seif 2, since water cannot enter until she loosens them. Here, in seif 5, the Shulchan Aruch discusses the second law, namely her own hair, and this depends upon whether she is particular, as the Shulchan Aruch concludes here.
And there is no distinction whether she tied two hairs, etc. This is the correct reading.
