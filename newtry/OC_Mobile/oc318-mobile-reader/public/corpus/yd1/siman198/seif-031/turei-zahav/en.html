And not upon boards, etc. The Gemara states that Rava said: A woman should not stand upon an earthenware utensil and immerse. Rav Kahana initially thought that the reason was the decree concerning bathhouses, for in their bathhouses people would sit upon earthen platforms resembling earthenware utensils, and one might come to say that immersion in them is valid. According to that reasoning, standing upon a thick wooden beam would be permitted. Rav Chanan of Nehardea told him that the reason there is that she is afraid she may fall and therefore may not immerse properly; upon a beam too she is afraid. The Beit Yosef cites the Ra'avad that even according to the conclusion, the concern for bathhouses remains, based upon the Mishnah stating that one may not immerse upon a bench. There, fear cannot be the reason, since the Mishnah discusses utensils. He therefore explains that the Mishnah likewise prohibited this because of the decree concerning bathhouses. Thus, one may not immerse upon anything bearing the status of a utensil, even a flat wooden utensil, since it is susceptible to impurity. An earthenware utensil itself does not contract impurity through contact, since it becomes impure only through its airspace, and a thick wooden beam likewise would otherwise have been permitted. Rava therefore taught that one should not immerse even upon these because of fear. The practical difference between the bathhouse decree and fear is that anything prohibited because of the bathhouse decree is invalid even after the fact, because an observer may mistakenly conclude that immersion in a bathhouse is permitted. When the reason is fear, everyone knows that she must immerse properly; the concern is merely that she may inadvertently become frightened and fail to immerse properly, and therefore it was not invalidated after the fact. This appears to be the reason for the distinction. Accordingly, the Ra'avad wrote, as quoted in the Shulchan Aruch, that one may not immerse upon anything susceptible to impurity. He mentions boards fit for midras impurity, because midras impurity applies only when one intended them for that purpose, and sometimes even an act is required, as stated by the Rambam in chapter 25 of Hilchos Keilim. There he writes that boards of a bathhouse that were trimmed do not contract impurity because they are not made for sitting. Certainly ordinary construction boards sold for building do not contract midras impurity. The Ra'avad nevertheless holds that since they could contract midras impurity if designated for making a bench or the like, we must be concerned that an observer might mistakenly say that the same law applies even after they were designated for sitting and became subject to midras impurity, and from this he may come to immerse in bathhouses. This is the intent of the Ra'avad's phrase, "boards fit for midras," and so too writes the Ritva, as cited by the Beit Yosef. According to this, the custom in our countries to place boards in a mikveh for the immersing woman to stand upon is very difficult. The Levush discusses this at length. Earlier authorities already addressed it, namely Maharam Padua in responsum 31, in response to Maharar David Vital. His principal argument is that our mikveh boards are not deemed designated for midras, since they are made to stand upon for immersion. On the contrary, this removes them from the status of utensils and makes them like the roof of a house, particularly once they are fixed with nails, since they never previously bore the status of a utensil.
Furthermore, since these boards are placed upon beams and water floats over them, they are like part of the structure. Thus, even if they had already been utensils according to Torah law, they are now nullified, etc. His words are exceedingly difficult. The Gemara did not say that the bathhouse decree is removed except regarding a thick wooden beam, not ordinary wood or even flat wooden utensils. This is the Ra'avad's principal inference, from which he learned the law for anything fit to contract impurity. Furthermore, what merit is there in the fact that it never previously bore the status of a utensil? An observer may nevertheless err, as explained above. This is why the Ra'avad wrote, "fit for midras." Most astonishing is his statement that even if it previously had the status of a utensil, it is nullified because the water floats over it and it is like part of the structure. It is obvious that even actual construction does not nullify a utensil, as shown by a pipe that was hollowed and afterward affixed, which invalidates a mikveh. Maharam himself wrote this immediately earlier in that responsum, so how could he forget it within the same discussion? He further justified the custom on the basis that we follow those who hold that flat wooden utensils do not contract midras impurity. This is a valid reason, as will be explained, but it does not accord with the Ra'avad, who explicitly includes even flat wooden utensils and derives it from the Gemara's specific reference to a thick beam. In truth, one cannot criticize Maharam for that responsum, because he did not see these words of the Ra'avad and Rashba, as he himself writes there. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, writes that all of Maharam Padua's words are correct. It is surprising that he says the primary permission is that the boards are fixed with nails, but if not fixed they are forbidden. Maharam explicitly permits them even when not fixed, writing that the water itself renders them fixed. Mahari Katz of Cracow likewise sought permission on the ground that they are utensils made to remain stationary, meaning not for use but merely to remain in place, and therefore they do not contract impurity. According to him too, there is permission even when they are not fixed with nails. His reasoning is even more difficult, for although a utensil made to remain stationary does not contract impurity through contact, it certainly can contract midras impurity, as I cited from the Rambam concerning bathhouse boards, which certainly contract midras impurity if designated for sitting. In sum, my mind is not settled by any permission for mikveh boards according to the Ra'avad and Rashba cited above, and the Levush properly wondered about the custom according to their view. It appears to me, however, that we have a clear permission from another direction. Other poskim do not agree with the Ra'avad. They maintain that when the Gemara attributes the prohibition to the bathhouse decree according to Rava, the prohibition is not due to susceptibility to impurity at all, but to other reasons that do not apply to these boards. This is proven from Rabbeinu Shimshon, cited by the Beit Yosef in this siman. He asks why a spring led over a trough, meaning an affixed stone utensil, is invalid. Why should it be worse than a utensil immersed inside another utensil in a mikveh, when the outer utensil has an opening the size of a wineskin spout? He answers that there is a distinction between detached utensils, in which immersion is valid when they are connected to the mikveh, and fixed utensils over which the spring flows, because this may lead to error. Sometimes they are fixed at the water's source without an opening the size of a wineskin spout, and all water passing over them becomes invalid. It is possible that this is likewise the reason for the Mishnah's prohibition against immersion upon a bench, and also the reason for the bathhouse decree discussed above, which concerns earthenware fixed there to prevent mud. They prohibited it for the reason explained. The Rosh responds that there is no difficulty. The comparison to immersing even within the utensil itself is imprecise, for there the inner utensil itself is immersed within the mikveh, since the outer opening is the size of a wineskin spout. But one may not immerse within a utensil connected to a spring, lest one come to immerse in a vessel containing 40 se'ah without any connection. We must understand the Rosh's distinction. It appears that when one utensil is inside another and both are immersed in a valid mikveh, there is no reason to decree lest one immerse in a utensil without a connection, since both utensils are now within valid mikveh water and no mistaken inference follows. By contrast, when a spring flows over a trough, the water continually passes over the trough, and sometimes the spring or mikveh connection is interrupted. One may then mistakenly immerse there even at that time. The Beit Yosef writes that the Rosh disagrees with Rabbeinu Shimshon, for according to the Rosh even detached utensils may not be used. Nevertheless, in a responsum concerning a channel connected to the floor of a mikveh, the Rosh cites Rabbeinu Shimshon's explanation that the decree is because it might sometimes be placed at the water's source, etc. He writes that this concern does not apply to a channel. Furthermore, an ordinary wooden channel made for water has no rim and is a flat wooden utensil, which does not contract impurity and does not invalidate through drawn water, as the Beit Yosef cites shortly. His words appear to mean, first, that the concern that it may be placed at the spring's source without an opening the size of a wineskin spout does not apply to a channel, since every channel has such an opening. Second, we decree that something might be placed at the spring's source only concerning a complete utensil whose validity depends upon an opening the size of a wineskin spout. A flat wooden utensil does not contract impurity, so there is no concern that it will invalidate the water if placed at the source. The boards in our mikvaos are certainly no worse than such a channel. Just as Rabbeinu Shimshon's concern does not apply to the channel, it does not apply to these boards. Likewise, according to the Rosh's reason for prohibiting even detached utensils, lest one immerse in them without a connection, this applies only to a trough in which one could immerse without connection to the spring, since water could remain within it. It does not apply to boards or a channel, for without connection to the spring there is no water there at all. This appears entirely clear to me. All the more so according to the Beit Yosef's conclusion regarding the Tur's words, "My father the Rosh made no distinction," that the Rosh is not concerned with susceptibility to impurity but only with fear, there is obviously no concern regarding boards. Everything I have written to permit boards applies likewise to wooden steps in a mikveh, which are found throughout our countries, for the same reasoning governs both. I found written in the name of Maharal of Prague: "Wooden steps are permitted, because the reason is the decree lest one place the bench at the spring's source, and steps are not made for water to pass through them." End quote. This is precisely as I have written. Therefore, according to the established custom, we certainly do not follow the Shulchan Aruch in this seif, which rules like the Ra'avad and makes the entire matter depend upon susceptibility to impurity, including flat wooden utensils and anything fit for midras. Rather, we follow Rabbeinu Shimshon and the Rosh, according to whom these boards are entirely permitted and are exactly like a channel attached to the mikveh floor. One who invalidates the boards must likewise invalidate the channel, since it too is a flat wooden utensil, yet in truth both are unquestionably valid. Even if the boards are laid without being fixed by nails, everything is permitted, as ruled by Maharam Padua and Mahari Katz, though not for their reasons, but for the reasons stated above.
Wooden steps, etc. According to what has been explained, we do not rule this way. They too are permitted even if the step was first constructed and only afterward fixed in the mikveh, for they have the same law as the boards discussed above. However, I have seen many people err by failing to make them four handbreadths wide. There is then concern that she may fear falling, and it is forbidden ab initio. Every halachic authority should therefore pay attention to this.
