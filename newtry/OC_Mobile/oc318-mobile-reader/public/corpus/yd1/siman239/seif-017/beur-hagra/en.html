If he made them conditional, etc. This is the Tur’s ruling, but it requires study. Nedarim says there, “For he is not careful regarding the condition,” etc. Why should adding that the loaf is also a condition make the matter seem less serious to him? This requires study.
If he ate, etc. This is the case in Shevuot where he made each dependent on the other. Here there is certainly no difference which one he ate first, because each is both a condition and a prohibited item. In the second clause, however, Rashi and Rabbeinu Yitzchak differ. In the first clause they agree. According to Rashi, if he ate each knowingly with respect to itself but unknowingly regarding the other, he is exempt from the first, since although the oath took effect through his knowing act, he incurs neither a sacrifice nor lashes for the first, as explained above. This compelled Rashi not to explain like Rabbeinu Tam; according to Rabbeinu Tam he should be liable for the first. Tosafot struggle to answer for Rabbeinu Tam and call the answer forced. Rashi also did not wish to explain like Rabbeinu Yitzchak, that “the first” always means the condition, because in the latter clause the Gemara says, “he ate its condition,” rather than again saying “the first and then the second”; why change the wording? Tosafot discuss this. He is also exempt from the second, because the first was eaten unknowingly with respect to it. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak too he is exempt from both, because with each item its condition was eaten unknowingly. If he ate each unknowingly with respect to itself but knowingly regarding the other, according to Rashi he is liable only for the second, not for the first, because the first was eaten unknowingly with respect to itself and the oath did not take effect upon it. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak he is liable for both, since even when the prohibited item is first eaten unknowingly and the condition later knowingly, liability applies retroactively. If both were eaten unknowingly he is exempt; if both knowingly he is liable for both, following Rabbi Yochanan. Thus all agree on exemption from both and liability for both in those final cases. The Tur again writes that the dispute has practical effect only regarding a sacrifice, but regarding prohibition one may not eat either item, and if he ate one and then remembered, the Shulchan Aruch follows this. These words are surprising, for that is correct only according to Rashi. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak, even if the first was eaten entirely unknowingly and the second knowingly, he is liable to a sacrifice for the first, yet the Tur says the second may be eaten initially. The Perishah labors greatly to explain this, but the truth is that the ruling follows Rashi. Perhaps the Tur rules anonymously like Rashi, as the Ran writes in his novellae that Rashi’s view is the clearest, since all other approaches face the difficulties noted above. The same applies to the statement in seif 16, “Likewise, if he made a loaf conditional,” etc., and then forgot and ate it, whether it was the condition or the prohibited item. Still, this requires study, for if so, why say there specifically, “If he first ate the condition”? It would be a greater novelty even if he first ate the prohibited item, as stated here in seif 17. The wording “he ate it” there also sounds like the condition. Thus seif 16 appears to follow Tosafot, while seif 17 follows Rashi. The Tur’s statement that the dispute has no practical effect regarding prohibition, and certainly the Shulchan Aruch’s statement here that it has no such effect, also remain difficult. The Rambam has a different reading; see Kesef Mishneh. Nevertheless, in my humble opinion Rabbeinu Yitzchak’s view is the clearest. Although the wording “he ate its condition” is imprecise, as Tosafot note, Rashi’s reading requires differentiating between the opening clause, “he ate the first unknowingly,” and the second clause, “the first knowingly,” etc., while Rabbeinu Yitzchak does not. In the final case where each is made dependent on the other, the second clause, “this one unknowingly regarding itself,” etc., would have to mean that liability applies only to the second, which should have been explained; moreover, it would not parallel the opening exemption. Finally, since the Gemara concludes, “both knowingly,” concerning the second one, it implies that in the preceding clause liability applies to both according to all opinions.
