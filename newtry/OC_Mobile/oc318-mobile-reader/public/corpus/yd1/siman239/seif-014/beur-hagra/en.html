He said, “An oath,” etc. As stated in seif 12 regarding “I am a nazirite.” See the Ran on the Mishnah there, s.v. “An oath that I will not,” etc. This differs from a case in which everything depends on whether the second obligation is capable of taking effect, and likewise concerning vows, as stated in seif 15.
This does not appear correct to me, etc. The Beit Yosef writes: “This is surprising. The difficulty applies only to naziriteship, because an unspecified term of naziriteship is thirty days; but not in such a case. It resembles one who says, ‘An oath that I will not eat these nine,’ and then, ‘An oath that I will not eat ten,’ where, if he eats those nine and one additional item, he violates both oaths.” His words are difficult. What he says, that the difficulty applies only to naziriteship, is correct according to all opinions except Shmuel, but according to Shmuel even one who says twice, “I am a nazirite,” must observe two terms. The Darkei Moshe already questioned this, as noted above in seif 12. His comparison to nine and ten in siman 238, seif 12, is also inexact. There, when he says, “An oath that I will not eat ten,” he becomes forbidden in every set of ten in the world, and the oath can take effect regarding those nine plus one additional item through an expanded scope. That is not so here. His words are therefore very difficult, while the Tur’s ruling is entirely correct.
Specifically, etc.; and even, etc.; but, etc. The Rama’s words are also difficult. He derived this from Mordechai, chapter 3 of Moed Katan, concerning one whose relative died during the seven days of mourning. Rabbi Meir of Rothenburg ruled that the days do not count for both periods, and Mordechai brought proof from Nazir 13b, where the Mishnah states, “I am a nazirite,” etc., “I am a nazirite,” etc. See Tosafot there. Mordechai writes that the Mishnah says specifically that the second term began; if the triggering event occurred before the first term began, the days count for both. This differs from the Nedarim case, “I am a nazirite, I am a nazirite,” where he counts two terms, because there, if one term alone discharged him, why did he accept two? Here, however, his intent was only that if the son were born at a time when the son’s naziriteship would extend beyond his own, he would observe the excess; if one can be absorbed into the other, it is absorbed and both are fulfilled. The same applies to mourning. The Rama transferred that rule here and wrote that this is also implied by Tosafot in Nazir. His words are difficult, because according to Mordechai one period counts for both only when it can be wholly absorbed. Where he adds time, it cannot be wholly absorbed, so this ruling would be entirely inapplicable to an additional period. Nor would it apply to two twenty-day periods. His concluding distinction, that once the first has begun the second cannot be absorbed, applies equally to the opening case. Furthermore, no proof can be brought from Nazir, because there the person intended only the excess if the son were born while his own naziriteship continued, which is not so here. We also rule that mourning always counts for both periods, as stated in siman 375, seif 10. The Rama did not examine Tosafot in Nazir carefully, for they imply the opposite. On the Mishnah there, Tosafot explain that when both terms begin together and one can be absorbed, the days count for both, as with a term extending until seventy days. But Tosafot limit this to where he accepted the son’s naziriteship first. Their proof is that the Mishnah discusses only that arrangement. The reason is that he is necessarily required to interrupt his own term; if the balance can be absorbed, it counts, but if even one day remains, since naziriteship cannot be less than thirty days, he must count a new term. Here, however, it resembles one who accepted his own naziriteship first, because no interruption is required. The Tur’s ruling therefore stands: he always counts forty-two days. This also appears from Tosafot to Nedarim, s.v. “There is a vow.” Their answer means that with vows, since he said “today” in both statements, that very day is necessarily intended. In naziriteship, however, when adding thirty days to thirty days, even if he says “today,” it cannot be literal; it merely prevents an interruption. Where he did not say “today,” as here, the case can arise even with vows, and likewise with oaths, as the Ran explains, since an oath cannot take effect upon an existing oath. Therefore this was not listed among the stringencies of vows over oaths.
