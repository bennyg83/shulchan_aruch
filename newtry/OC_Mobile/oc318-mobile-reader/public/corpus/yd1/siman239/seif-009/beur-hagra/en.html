One who links, etc., according to Rava; nevertheless, linking to a vow is like a vow, as stated at the beginning of siman 204; likewise, if he handed over, etc. This is the Rif’s view. Rashi, however, holds that according to Rava even linking to a vow is ineffective. See his comments there, s.v. “Provided that it was vowed,” and s.v. “Correct,” etc. The Rif explains that the initial assumption was that “issar” denotes linking. Since the scriptural issar is a form of linking, it would necessarily apply also to an oath, for its principal verse concerns an oath, “or bound herself by an issar upon her soul through an oath,” and vows are learned from there. The tanna mentioned a vow to teach the greater novelty. Thus the Gemara asks: this is understandable according to Abaye, etc., but difficult according to Rava, who said that issar is not linking, while here it is said to be linking; moreover, if so, it should apply to an oath as well. The answer is: correct the teaching. It is not discussing issar, but means: what expression renders something forbidden like a vow? “It is upon me,” etc. In other words, linking to a vowed object is itself a vow, but linking to an oath is not effective. The law of linking is not learned from issar, but from “When a man vows a vow,” requiring that he vow through something already vowed, as stated in Nedarim 14a, where something inherently forbidden is excluded. So too, one may exclude something merely sworn upon. The Ran states this in the Ramban’s name, and the Rosh agrees. See Tosafot there, s.v. “Rather,” etc. The Ran explains “correct the teaching” like Rashi, as referring to the basic law of a vow, which takes effect only when attached to something vowed. Linking is therefore also a vow, since the essence of a vow operates through linkage, unlike an oath. Nevertheless, linking to a vow is valid, contrary to Rashi, as Tosafot and the Rif prove from the beginning of chapter 4 of Nazir: one who said, etc. Although there the rule applies specifically within the time required for speech, while linkage can occur later, as in “like the day Gedaliah died,” the cases differ. There he did not say “and I am like you,” but merely “and I,” which has no such implication after that interval. Nor can one say that saying “and I” immediately is simply equivalent to saying “I am a nazirite,” rather than linkage, for the latter clause teaches: if the first was released, etc. We also learned: “I am like Samson, like Delilah’s husband,” etc., and many similar cases. This is the Ran’s explanation.
