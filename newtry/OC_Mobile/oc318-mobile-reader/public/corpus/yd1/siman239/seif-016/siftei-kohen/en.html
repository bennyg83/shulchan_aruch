If he swore, “I will not eat,” etc. Derishah asks how, according to this, a mistaken expressive oath can ever require a korban, since the Tur and Mechaber rule above at the beginning of siman 236 that an oath concerning either the past or the future can require a korban. He answers that if a person swears falsely about a fact, such as saying, “I swear that I ate today,” when he did not, his forgetfulness makes him inadvertent and liable. Here, however, he did not swear directly to a fact but conditionally, such as, “If so-and-so said this, I will not eat this loaf,” and it turned out that he had said it; because the oath rested upon a mistaken condition, it never took effect. This is surprising, for an explicit Gemara contradicts him. Shevuos 26 asks what constitutes inadvertence in an expressive oath concerning the past and answers: he knew that the false oath was forbidden but did not know that it required a korban. Rambam rules accordingly. Nevertheless, regarding the permissibility of a conditionally prohibited loaf, it appears that the case is considered a mistaken oath only when, at the moment of swearing, he was certain that the condition was false. For example, he swore not to eat the loaf if a certain person had said something, while being certain that the person had not said it. This accords with the Gemara’s expression, “Your heart compelled you.” If he was uncertain whether the statement had been made, or did not know at all, the oath is not a mistaken oath and the loaf is forbidden.
We are not concerned that he may forget, etc. A person is careful regarding the prohibited item itself, but is not equally careful regarding the condition, as stated above at the end of simanim 213 and 220. See there.
But the prohibited item is forbidden, etc. If he first ate the prohibited item, even inadvertently, he may not afterward eat the item serving as the condition. So writes the Levush. See seif katan 20.
