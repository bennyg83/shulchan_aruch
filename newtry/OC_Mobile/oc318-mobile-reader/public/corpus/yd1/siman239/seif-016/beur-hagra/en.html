Since, etc. Nedarim 25b discusses mistaken vows, etc., and teaches, etc. See Tosafot there, s.v. “Just as,” etc.
Likewise, if, etc. This is stated there, and upon it the Gemara says, “We too have learned,” etc. See what is written below.
He may, etc.; but, etc. See Nedarim 14b and the Ran there on 15b, s.v. “Regarding the law,” etc., and the end of siman 213.
If he ate, etc. See the discussion there. Rashi’s view is that everything depends upon the first eating, whether he first ate the condition or the prohibited item. If he ate it knowingly, the oath takes effect; if unknowingly, it does not, and he may initially eat the other item, as the Ran explains. This follows from the Gemara’s statement, “We too have learned: Konam,” etc., “he is permitted,” meaning initially permitted with his wife, and from the baraita cited there. As stated above, since it is a mistaken vow he is permitted in the loaf. Certainly, if he ate the prohibited item first, he may then eat the condition, because the oath never took effect. Rashi interprets “the first” as the condition. When the Gemara says, “The first unknowingly and the second knowingly, he is exempt,” the same applies if he first ate the prohibited item unknowingly and later the condition knowingly. The wording follows the latter clause. In both cases “exempt” means exempt and permitted, as above, and the Ran agrees. When it says, “The first knowingly and the second unknowingly, he is liable,” that applies specifically where the condition was eaten first. If he first ate the prohibited item knowingly and later the condition unknowingly, he incurs neither a sacrifice, because the inadvertence must concern the prohibited act, nor lashes, because the condition was later eaten unknowingly. Tosafot’s discussion supports this reading. Thus the opening clause also says specifically, “the first unknowingly,” etc. If both were eaten unknowingly, he is exempt, whether the condition was first, and it is obvious that even if the second was eaten knowingly he remains exempt. If both were eaten knowingly, he incurs lashes whether the condition or the prohibited item was eaten first, for we rule like Rabbi Yochanan. Rabbeinu Yitzchak’s view in Tosafot is that everything depends on eating the condition. If the condition was eaten knowingly, the oath takes effect whether it was eaten first or later; if unknowingly, it does not take effect in either order. “The first” means the condition, as Rashi explains, but the clauses are exact: “the first unknowingly,” etc., means that whenever the condition is eaten unknowingly and the prohibited item knowingly, he is exempt, regardless of order; “the first knowingly and the second unknowingly, he is liable,” means that when the condition is eaten knowingly and the prohibited item unknowingly, he is liable, regardless of order. Thus the dispute between Rashi and Rabbeinu Yitzchak concerns only a case where the prohibited item was eaten first unknowingly and the condition later knowingly: according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak he is liable, while according to Rashi he is exempt. The Tur writes that their disagreement has practical effect only regarding the sacrifice, not regarding the prohibition, and the Shulchan Aruch follows that formulation. This requires study, because it also affects the prohibition: according to Rashi, after eating the prohibited item unknowingly he may eat the condition initially, while according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak he would even incur a sacrifice. The statement here, “If he first ate the condition,” therefore follows all opinions, but if he ate the prohibited item first it is disputed. In seif 17 the Shulchan Aruch rules like Rashi, as explained there. The Raavad also follows Rabbeinu Yitzchak, and Rabbeinu Chananel does likewise, although he calls the prohibited item “the first” and the condition “the second,” and reverses the readings of liability and exemption. The Rambam has another approach: he also reads like Rabbeinu Chananel but, following Tosafot’s terminology, calls the condition “the first,” understands liability as lashes, and makes everything depend on the time the prohibited item is eaten, regardless of whether the condition was eaten knowingly or unknowingly. See Kesef Mishneh; his proof from “Konam if I ate or drank,” etc., requires study. Rabbeinu Tam follows Rashi but interprets “the first” as whichever item was actually eaten first, whether condition or prohibition, in both clauses. He disagrees with Rashi in holding that if the prohibited item was first eaten knowingly and the condition later unknowingly, one is liable to a sacrifice, because inadvertence is needed when the oath is violated, not specifically when the prohibited item is eaten. On his reading the discussion is straightforward, since in both clauses the order makes no difference; Rashi must distinguish between the clauses. See what is written below in seif 17.
