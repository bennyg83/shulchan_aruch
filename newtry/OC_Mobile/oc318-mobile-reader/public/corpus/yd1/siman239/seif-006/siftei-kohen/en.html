The same applies to something that is, etc. See above, siman 236, seif katan 3.
But if he swore to violate a rabbinic commandment, etc. This is difficult, for above, siman 238, seif 4, the Mechaber rules like Rambam that one who swears to eat less than a kezayis of neveilah is bound by the oath, although eating half the prohibited measure is forbidden by Torah law, since no Torah lashes are imposed for it. Here, however, he rules that an oath does not take effect even against a rabbinic prohibition. Perhaps his meaning is that the oath does not authorize the transgression, but must instead be released. Likewise, in siman 238, “he is liable for the oath” may mean that he requires release. Another resolution follows the responsum of Maharal ibn Chaviv, siman 103, explaining the Ran: a rabbinic prohibition may be more stringent in this respect than half the Torah measure. The latter is derived only from an inclusive phrase and has no distinct negative commandment explicitly directed to it, and an oath can take effect upon a matter whose prohibition is not explicit. A rabbinic prohibition, however, is supported by the commandment not to deviate from the sages, and the sages may also have reinforced their enactments like Torah law. Maharal ibn Chaviv discusses the practical ruling at length, as does the Mabit in part 2, siman 51. Maharshal and Bach rule that when one swears to commit a rabbinic transgression, although some say the oath takes effect with respect to a korban and lashes, we compel him to seek release and not transgress. The Levush writes similarly. This implies that even where the oath inclusively covers permitted and forbidden matters, he should not transgress but should obtain release, as is also evident from the Ran’s responsum concerning an oath that included principal and interest. Maharshal writes concerning the Tur in the name of the Rosh that other authorities disagree and compel him to seek release from his vow, and that this is the primary ruling; the Bach cites this in his conclusion. It therefore appears that even one who swore to nullify a rabbinic mitzvah is compelled to seek release. Although I do not know precisely which authorities Maharshal intended, and the plain meaning of the poskim and the ruling above in siman 215 do not appear so, one should nevertheless be stringent. Alternatively, the present discussion addresses only the strict law, while it is certainly proper to compel him to seek release.
