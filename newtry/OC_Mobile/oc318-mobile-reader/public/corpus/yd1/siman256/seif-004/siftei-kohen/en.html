For whatever purpose they wish. This applies even to a non-mitzvah purpose. Maharik, at the end of responsum 128, writes similarly in the name of many authorities and concurs. This is specifically where the collection is a fixed one, so that if funds are lacking they will collect again. But where an unforeseen need arose and they collected for particular poor people, such as for clothing, or many poor people arrived and money was collected for them by name, the funds may not be redirected to another purpose, not even to other poor people. So rule the Rosh and Tur, and this also appears to be the view of Mahari Weil, responsum 26. See also Responsa of Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrachi, responsum 9, and Responsa Maharam Alashkar, responsum 10. It appears that for redeeming captives they may redirect the funds even in such a case, as stated above at the beginning of siman 252. The Beit Yosef writes that the Rosh appears to understand the Yerushalmi in Shekalim as permitting a change only to another need of the poor, but not to a non-mitzvah purpose, whether by the trustees or by the community. The Darkhei Moshe questions this, because the Tur writes that even redirecting the funds to other poor people is forbidden. There is no difficulty. The Rosh explicitly cites the Yerushalmi, 'surplus funds collected for particular poor people may be used for other poor people,' and explains that the trustees are not restrained if they see a genuine need to redirect them. Yet the Rosh himself explains that the case concerns an unforeseen collection for particular poor people. The Yerushalmi therefore means that when the trustees discern a genuine need, they may redirect the funds for a mitzvah purpose, even though an unforeseen collection may ordinarily not be redirected, even to another mitzvah. The Beit Yosef accordingly explains that the Yerushalmi permits the trustees to redirect only for another need of the poor, but not for a non-mitzvah purpose. This is clear.
The same applies to an appointed charity collector, etc. The Rama's wording is imprecise, for it implies that a charity collector has the same authority as the community in every respect. This is not so. The community may redirect funds even to a non-mitzvah purpose, as many authorities agree, and the Semak records that this is the common practice. Maharik concurs in responsa 5 and 128. A charity collector, however, may redirect funds only for a mitzvah purpose, as stated by the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Batra and by Maharik in responsum 5 in the name of the authorities. So too the Gemara states in the first chapter of Arakhin that once funds have reached the charity collector, they may not be redirected. A city sage is different, for he may redirect them even to a non-mitzvah purpose, like the community itself. As is evident from the Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch, a city sage is a preeminent scholar on whose judgment everyone contributes and whom everyone relies upon to act as he sees fit. The Bach likewise writes that this applies specifically to a city sage, but an ordinary charity collector may not redirect funds as he wishes, since he was not appointed without limitation to exercise such discretion, unless prior custom established this authority under communal enactments. Perhaps the Rama refers to a charity collector comparable to a city sage, namely, where local custom empowers the collector to act as he sees fit. See Maharik and Beit Yosef for the various distinctions in these laws.
