This applies specifically to charity that they would not otherwise have given, etc. The Mordekhai writes in the name of the Or Zarua: This applies specifically where a fast was decreed because of drought, an outbreak of wild animals, or another calamity, and the residents undertook charity and likewise required visiting townspeople to contribute, in order to annul the decree through repentance, prayer, and charity. In such a case the visitors must give to the local charity collectors because of suspicion, and they must also give through the city sage where there is one. But charity customarily given at all times and fixed in every place, which they would not have refrained from giving had they remained in their own town, need not be given to the local collectors or city sage merely because they are presently elsewhere. They may take it home with them. Accordingly, villagers who come to larger communities for Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, recite memorial prayers, and pledge charity are not subject to suspicion, for they will certainly give it. Nor must they give it to the local city sage, because this charity does not arise from the local community but from established law and custom. They may therefore take the charity back to their villages and use it as they see fit. This is quoted by the Beit Yosef and Darkhei Moshe. It nevertheless appears that if they purchased honors in the synagogue, such as mitzvah honors or gelilah, they must pay the local community and may not retract and take the money home. They purchased those honors in this synagogue, and otherwise the local charity would suffer a loss because local residents would have purchased them. This is the accepted practice. I later found the same ruling in Responsa Mabit, part 1, responsum 60. The Kol Bo, siman 116, records an ancient enactment that villagers without a minyan who come to established communities and bring wax candles lit on Yom Kippur must leave the unused portion in the synagogue where they were lit. If they prepared two candles, one should remain where it was lit, and the other may be lit in their own town where they pray privately.
One who has money belonging to others, etc. Rabbi Toviah writes in the glosses to Mordekhai: You are not bound by the local oath concerning money from whose profit you receive no share. Even if the enactment of your place preceded that of the owner's place, I would instruct you to send the money to him, and he should bring the tithe into his local treasury, unless their enactment explicitly requires tithing all profit generated by their money, whether it comes into their own hands or into the hands of others. The same appears to me regarding money lent under a half-profit arrangement: the agent should pay half the tithe to the owner of the money, and the owner must bring it into the treasury, since half the profit belongs to him. The Beit Yosef cites this in siman 258. The Rama understood this passage differently, followed by the Levush. In truth, however, the words 'unless they explicitly stipulated in their enactment' refer even to the enactment of the lender's place. Maharam Tiktin explains this way in his gloss to Mordekhai, chapter HaGozel Batra, and the work Gedulat Mordekhai notes that another version states explicitly, 'even if they did not stipulate.' The resulting law is this: one who conducts business with another person's money, where all profit belongs to the owner and the agent has no share, must send everything to the owner, who gives the tithe in his own locality. This applies even if the enactment of the agent's locality preceded that of the owner's locality, and even if the owner's locality did not explicitly require tithing profits earned by others through local residents' money. Under a half-profit arrangement, the agent pays the owner his half of the profit, while the agent gives the tithe from his own half in his own city. Consider this carefully.
