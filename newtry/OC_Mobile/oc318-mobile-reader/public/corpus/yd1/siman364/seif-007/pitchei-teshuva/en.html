A grave for his father. See Shivat Tziyon responsa siman 58 regarding one who ordered a stone as a marker for his father's grave and had his father's name carved into it. For some reason it did not work out to place that stone as a marker on his father's grave, and the matter was delayed for several years. In the meantime the place of his father's grave was forgotten, and with time the son too died. May the son's children take this stone, place it as a marker on their father's grave, erase their grandfather's name, and carve their father's name into it? One decisor held to forbid, like one who hewed a grave for his father, since a monument is like a grave, as the Rama wrote in seif 1, that a monument is forbidden in benefit like the grave itself. Nevertheless, he held that the heirs may sell the stone for use as a monument for another dead person, as with a grave. The author agreed with him that it is forbidden to place it on the son's grave, but regarding what he wrote that the heirs may sell it for another dead person, he disagreed. What the Tur and Shulchan Aruch wrote, "but another may be buried in it," does not mean that even the son himself may give this grave to another. Rather, it speaks where he hewed a grave for his father in ownerless land or in a cemetery belonging to the public, where every person has permission to bury his dead in that place; therefore they wrote that another may be buried in it. But one who builds a grave for his father in his own field is plainly forbidden to sell or give it to another. If so, in our case they may not sell the monument. Although the son has now died too, grandchildren are also obligated in the honor of their grandfather, as the Rama wrote above in siman 240 seif 24. See what I wrote above in siman 161 seif 6 on this and examine carefully. Nevertheless, he concludes that one should not object to a decisor who permits the heirs to sell this monument for another dead person, for he has pillars of the world upon whom to rely leniently: the view of the Rosh, that a monument is not forbidden at all; the view of Tosafot, that anything not forbidden has no issue of honoring his father; and also the view of Maharik, that grandchildren are not obligated in the honor of their grandfather. Since it appears that even this prohibition is only rabbinic, one who is lenient loses nothing. He further wrote that when the Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch used the phrase "one who hewed a grave for his father," they used the wording of the first Tanna of Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel in the baraita in Sanhedrin 48, not Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who forbids even one who hewed stones for his father. This is not decisive, and depends on the views about whether the rule that wherever Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel taught in our Mishnah the halachah follows him applies specifically to Mishnah or also to a baraita; see there.
