A grave that is found. See Chavot Yair, in the responsum of Rabbi David Oppenheim, concerning a synagogue that collapsed and was to be rebuilt. When they dug deeply for the foundations, human bones were found in several places, including multiple graves at varying distances. An elderly non-Jew, speaking incidentally, said that non-Jews had been buried there during the Swedish war. The question raised several issues: whether the findings established a cemetery neighborhood, which would prohibit relocation; whether the spacing rules in Nazir 65 and Bava Batra 101, four to eight cubits, apply only to ancient burial caves or to modern graves as well; whether, if these were non-Jewish graves, the synagogue area required examination in order to protect kohanim from tumah; how far and how deeply any search must extend; and whether the expense falls only upon kohanim or upon the entire community. After a lengthy analysis of Rashi, Rambam, and Tosafot in the name of Ri, he concludes that this case does not establish a Jewish cemetery. Nazir 65 teaches that an irregularity in burial that strongly indicates non-Jewish burial prevents us from establishing a cemetery status out of doubt. Here there were several such indications: Jews are ordinarily buried in uniform order, whereas these burials were irregular; no pottery, boards, or recognizable grave structures were found, although Jewish custom is to place shards over the eyes; and if they were Jews buried under emergency conditions during war or plague, Semachot states that such circumstances do not create a cemetery presumption. The fact that one grave contained bones from several dead is also evidence against ordinary Jewish burial, since Jews do not normally bury multiple people in one grave, as explained in siman 362. Added to this is the incidental testimony of the elderly non-Jew. Since the prohibition of relocating graves is rabbinic, such testimony may be relied upon here. No surrounding examination is required for kohanim to walk there, as explained below in siman 372, and even the Rosh would agree in this case because it remains uncertain whether any grave or bones are present at all. Nor is it necessary to take the surrounding earth. As to benefit from the place, it is plainly permitted even if the graves had certainly been Jewish, because the Gemara concludes that the prohibition of benefit applies to a constructed grave, not to earth attached to the ground. This is all the more so where it is highly probable that the graves were non-Jewish. In addition, construction of a synagogue is a mitzvah and mitzvot were not given for personal benefit. Nevertheless, because it is improper for a holy place of prayer to stand over a possible area of tumah, he directs that the full synagogue area be dug and examined lengthwise and widthwise to approximately a cubit and a half, as one would ordinarily dig for graves. Any bones found should be removed. If none are found, the soil is pure and the area surrounding the synagogue may be traversed without inspection. Where bones had already been reburied in a particular location, however, kohanim should not walk there. Even around definite non-Jewish graves it is proper to be cautious, all the more so where there remains some possibility that the bones were Jewish. That spot should therefore be cleared again or clearly marked. The entire community, including Israelites and Levites, should share the expense of the excavation, as in Choshen Mishpat 163:3. This is especially so because Israelites too are cautioned regarding tumah on the pilgrimage festivals and are obligated to hear Birkat Kohanim. Thus all members of the community share responsibility for paying from communal funds. See the responsum at great length, and Responsa Even Shoham, simanim 10, 11, and 12.
