For the earth that they took. See Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 45, who writes that if other earth lies upon it and there is reason to assume that the prohibited earth was nullified in a majority of permitted earth, it is permitted to tread upon it even if the exact measure of nullification is not certain. He further writes that, were he not hesitant to say so, the Tur's citation in the name of Rabbi Yeshayah, that the earth with which one covers the deceased is prohibited, may refer only to the amount needed to cover the face of the deceased, since that is done for the deceased's honor. The earth above that level may be placed merely to level the ground and prevent people from falling. If so, when graves are now dug for relocation, the different layers are mixed and the permitted earth may constitute the majority. However, the words of the Taz in Orach Chaim 526:2 and Magen Avraham there, seif katan 10, do not appear to support this.
To sit on the stone. See Responsa Yad Eliyahu, siman 54, who gives two reasons for the prohibition: one is deriving benefit, and the other is degradation of the deceased. A practical difference is walking across a grave. There is no benefit in walking over it, since if no grave were there it would be even easier to walk on the ground; therefore the only concern is honor of the deceased, and for a mitzvah it may be permitted. Sitting on a grave, however, provides benefit, since without the grave there would be nothing suitable to sit upon, and is therefore prohibited as benefit. For that prohibition a mitzvah does not create a license; see there.
And some permit. See Responsa Radbaz II, siman 741, who writes that the practice is to be stringent and treat the gravestone itself as included in the prohibition of benefit. Birkei Yosef records a case of a widow who ordered a stone for her husband's grave. Later she saw that it was small and wanted to replace it with a finer stone, adding money to the stone seller for her husband's honor. His teacher was asked whether this was permitted, since she herself gained nothing from the stone and was spending additional money for her husband's honor, or whether the first stone, having become prohibited for benefit, could not be used as part of the consideration for the new stone. Moreover, the seller would later profit from prohibited benefit when reselling it. His teacher discussed the matter at length in a manuscript responsum and concluded that she may neither sell nor exchange it in any manner, because every such arrangement is considered benefit. Rabbi Yosef Navon in Kiryat Melech Rav II, siman 27, inclined to permit it as a matter of theory but not for practical ruling. See also Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 335, concerning an old cemetery wall that collapsed and was replaced with a better wall, whether the communal trustees could use the old stones to build an entrance house for a hospital according to the custom of being stringent with gravestones. He concludes that this is plainly permitted for several reasons, and that the trustees, who have the authority of the seven good men of the city, may redirect the stones to another communal building; see there.
