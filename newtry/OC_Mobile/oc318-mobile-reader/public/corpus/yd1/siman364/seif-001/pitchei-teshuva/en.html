And they permit. Radbaz, part 2, siman 741, writes that the custom is to be stringent and treat the gravestone itself as included in the prohibition of benefit. Birkei Yosef recounts a widow who had a stone placed on her husband's grave and later wished to replace it with a finer one. She proposed giving the original stone back to the stone seller and adding money for the new one. The question was whether this is permitted since she personally gains no worldly benefit and is spending more for her husband's honor, or forbidden because the seller takes the old stone as part of the price and later profits from selling an item prohibited in benefit. His teacher ruled that she may neither sell nor exchange the stone in any manner because this is legally benefit. R. Yosef Navon, Kiryat Melech Rav, part 2, siman 27, inclined to permit it in theory but not in practice. Chatam Sofer, siman 335, discusses stones from an old cemetery wall that collapsed and was rebuilt with a better wall. Could communal leaders use the old stones to build an entrance structure for a hospital, given the stringent custom regarding gravestones? He permits this plainly for several reasons and rules that the gabbaim, who function like the seven communal leaders, may redirect the old stones and bricks to another public need.
