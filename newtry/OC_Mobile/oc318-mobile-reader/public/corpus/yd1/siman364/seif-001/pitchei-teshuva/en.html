The ground that they took. See in the responsa of Rabbeinu Akiva Eiger of blessed memory, siman 45, who wrote that if there is other earth upon it, and one can attribute that the forbidden earth was nullified in the majority of permitted earth, even if it is not clearly the measure of nullification, it is permitted to tread upon it. And he wrote further: were I not afraid, I would say that what the Tur wrote in the name of R' Yeshayah, that the earth with which they cover the dead etc. — one can say it is specifically the measure of covering the face of the dead, for this is for the honor of the dead; but what is above this, one can say they do so that the ground be level so that one who falls should not fall. And if so, what they now dig for the relocation of the dead mixes this with that, and it is a majority. However, from the words of the Taz, Orach Chayyim siman 526 seif 2, and in the Magen Avraham there seif katan 10, it does not imply so; see there.
Sit on the stone. See in the responsum Yad Eliyahu siman 54, who brought that there are two reasons for this: one because of the prohibition of benefit, and one because it is a disgrace to the deceased. And the practical difference is that to walk upon the grave, there is no [prohibition] because of benefit, for if there were no grave here at all it would have been better to walk on the ground, and it is forbidden only because of the honor of the deceased; therefore, for the sake of a mitzvah it is permitted to walk. But to sit upon the grave, in which there is benefit, for if there were no grave it would not have been fit to sit upon it, is forbidden because of benefit, and in this no mitzvah is of any avail. See there.
And they permit. Radbaz, part 2, siman 741, writes that the custom is to be stringent and treat the gravestone itself as included in the prohibition of benefit. Birkei Yosef recounts a widow who had a stone placed on her husband's grave and later wished to replace it with a finer one. She proposed giving the original stone back to the stone seller and adding money for the new one. The question was whether this is permitted since she personally gains no worldly benefit and is spending more for her husband's honor, or forbidden because the seller takes the old stone as part of the price and later profits from selling an item prohibited in benefit. His teacher ruled that she may neither sell nor exchange the stone in any manner because this is legally benefit. R. Yosef Navon, Kiryat Melech Rav, part 2, siman 27, inclined to permit it in theory but not in practice. Chatam Sofer, siman 335, discusses stones from an old cemetery wall that collapsed and was rebuilt with a better wall. Could communal leaders use the old stones to build an entrance structure for a hospital, given the stringent custom regarding gravestones? He permits this plainly for several reasons and rules that the gabbaim, who function like the seven communal leaders, may redirect the old stones and bricks to another public need.
