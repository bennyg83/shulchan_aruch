During the festival, on chol hamoed. For on Yom Tov itself there is a distinction: on the first day of Yom Tov he does not practice aninut unless he wishes to bury him through gentiles, while on the second day he practices aninut in every case. But on chol hamoed he always practices aninut.
We do not say that Shemini Atzeret cancels. Meaning, I might have thought that Shemini Atzeret, being a festival in its own right, comes and cancels the mourning, as above at the beginning of this siman where he practiced one hour of mourning before the festival. So too here, during the festival itself before Shemini Atzeret he practiced a little mourning, namely pressing clothes and haircutting, as written at the end of seif 1. Therefore it teaches that we do not say so; Shemini Atzeret has significance only if he had already departed from the seven days of mourning before the festival, unlike here. The Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Hagahot Maimoniyyot that the same applies: Shemini Atzeret has no significance to be counted as seven days, as mentioned in seif 11, unless he was buried before the festival. But if he died during the festival, Shemini Atzeret is counted in the thirty only as one day. So wrote Tosafot in Perek Elu Megalchin, Moed Katan 24, and so wrote the Mordechai in the name of Rivash, unlike the words of Rabbi Yosef, who holds that Shemini Atzeret, and likewise Shavuot and Yom Kippur, are all counted as seven days if he died on that very day, regarding the thirty. Maharshal in Responsa siman 5 disagrees and says that even if he died during the festival, Shemini Atzeret is counted as seven days regarding the thirty. He has no clear proof, only that he brings proof from the Tur’s writing here that Shemini Atzeret does not cancel, implying that specifically it does not entirely cancel the seven days of mourning, but nevertheless it is counted as seven regarding the count of thirty. If so, he holds like Rabbi Yosef whom we mentioned. There is no proof from this, for the Tur used the language of cancellation to exclude what he wrote initially, that the festival cancels, as I wrote. So too it appears to me from the Rambam’s words, unlike Maharshal in this. The Rambam wrote: one who buries his dead during the festival, etc.; the Shulchan Aruch copied his language in Orach Chaim siman 548 and concluded there, “and he counts thirty from the day of burial.” This implies that even if he died at the end of the festival days, he counts thirty days from the day of burial, and we do not say that those festival days are counted as seven days. For why should this differ from one who died on Shavuot or Yom Kippur according to Rabbi Yosef’s view that we mentioned? Rather, certainly the festival has no elevation at all to be counted as seven unless he was buried before the festival for one hour. So too it appears to me from the sugya of the Gemara, where Abbaye asked Rabbah: if he buried him on the festival, does it count for him toward thirty or not? Toward the seven he did not ask, etc. Rabbah said to him: it does not count. Afterward they challenged him and the conclusion was that he was refuted; rather, we say it counts. Since Abbaye asked whether it counts toward thirty, we learn that he was uncertain whether the festival days are counted in the number of days of thirty, namely that the festival days replace ordinary days of the thirty. It did not enter his mind that they should have an additional advantage and be considered as seven days. Rabbah answered that they do not count, meaning that they do not count even in place of other days, and all the more so they are not counted as seven. In the conclusion this was refuted, so they do count toward thirty; but regarding their being counted as seven, we did not explicitly conclude that they have this advantage. Whence can we say this, to exclude Rabbah’s position? There is another proof from the words of Rabbi Zerachiah HaLevi, cited in his name in Teshuvot Maimoniyyot, Sefer Shoftim siman 17, regarding one who did not know that his relative died before the festival until after the festival. We do not say that the festival days and Shemini Atzeret should each be counted as seven, automatically making it a distant report; for this purpose the festival days have no special elevation, but are counted like ordinary days. He wrote there as follows: “All the more so this person, who did not know and did not hear of the death before the festival; how should the festival count for him to make one day before the festival, the festival, and Shemini Atzeret into twenty-one days? If they said this regarding one whose mourning was interrupted by the festival, would they say it regarding one who did not know and did not hear, etc.?” End quote. Thus he gave the festival an advantage to be counted as seven only where it interrupted mourning. It does not suffice that he heard but it did not interrupt, such as where he heard during the festival; otherwise he should have said, “If they said this regarding one who heard, would they say it regarding one who did not hear?” Rather, it is as I explained. He stated it as an all the more so: if they said this regarding one whose seven days of mourning were interrupted by the festival, it is obvious they would not say it regarding the distant matter where he did not know and did not hear. The same applies to one who heard during the festival: it does not help him to have it counted as seven; only his case there dealt with one who did not know and did not hear. Maharshal in the responsum cited above wrote further: since Rabbi Zerachiah HaLevi wrote that all those festivals are always counted as seven days even regarding a distant report, even though he never practiced mourning, and some sages were uncertain about this, etc.; although we do not rule like Rabbi Zerachiah HaLevi in this, that is because it is not logical that one who did not hear of the death should have it count as seven, and therefore it is logical to be stringent. But regarding one who knew, it is obvious that it counts as seven; end quote. This is astonishing, for he wrote in the name of Rabbi Zerachiah HaLevi to be lenient, but did not examine the words of Rabbi Zerachiah HaLevi himself, who rules stringently, as I copied. Even more than this, I was astonished at the responsum of Maharar Binyamin, who wrote that one should rule like Maharshal in this law because he has proof from Rabbi Zerachiah HaLevi, yet he did not examine the body of Rabbi Zerachiah HaLevi’s words. This should not be done. We have already mentioned the truth of Rabbi Zerachiah HaLevi’s words, from which the opposite can be proven, as I wrote. My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, also wrote unlike Maharshal in this law.
