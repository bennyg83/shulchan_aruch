And he buried him that day, he observes for him, etc. In the Rambam here, before this, there is another clause: namely, one who buries his dead on a festival, etc. In places that observe two days of Yom Tov, he counts the seven from the final second day of Yom Tov. Although he does not practice mourning on it, since it is rabbinic it counts toward the seven, and he counts only six afterward. The Shulchan Aruch did not mention it here because he mentioned it in Orach Chaim siman 548.
And it is written in Maharil as follows: “Regarding the second day of Rosh Hashanah, I do not rule that it counts toward the seven, and he said that so it is in Maimonides; but regarding other festivals he agrees that it counts, although he did not find explicitly where he buried him during the moed, where the last day of Yom Tov was not the day of burial, that it counts toward the seven”; end quote. Maharshal wrote regarding this: “This astonishes me; see there that Hagahot Maimoniyyot disagrees with this distinction”; end quote. That is, it is explained there in Hagahot Maimoniyyot in the name of Rav Yehudah, regarding a practical ruling with his mother who died on the first Yom Tov of Sukkot and was buried on the second Yom Tov, that after Sukkot he observed only six days of mourning. The Raah also ruled this way where he buried him on the last day of Yom Tov, but not where he buried him during the festival before then. This is his language: “One who buries his dead on the last Yom Tov or on the second day of Shavuot counts six after the festival, for the day of death counts for him in the number, although he does not practice mourning on it because it is Torah-level, etc.” End quote. Nevertheless, we have only the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch in Orach Chaim, that the second day of Yom Tov in the diaspora counts, even if he died and was buried on the first day by gentiles. The reason Maharil was stringent regarding Rosh Hashanah is that it is like one long day together with the first day, and therefore there is no logic to count from the second day whenever he died and was buried on the first day. Just as the first does not count toward the seven, so too the second, for they are one day. But if he was buried on the second day of Rosh Hashanah, it appears obvious that he counts from it, for in any event it is rabbinic, as the Tur wrote according to the view of the Ramban and the Geonim. There is no impediment in saying that it belongs to the first day. This is certain: wherever a count of days is needed, we certainly count Rosh Hashanah as two days. Only if the burial was on the first day of Rosh Hashanah, and we do not count that first day, then the second day is drawn after it because we consider it one long day. This is not so when he was buried on the second day. So it appears to me clearly.
I saw fit to mention here what I have seen in some places: when they bury a dead person on the first day of Yom Tov through gentiles, Jews come and return the earth into the grave over the dead until the final filling. In my eyes this is a complete prohibition, for it is full labor and involves building, and it is obvious that it is forbidden through a Jew. Therefore one must be careful to do this through gentiles, may no trouble come.
