And not merely by virtue of the festival, etc. This is the Ramban’s reasoning, and he proved it from the fact that we asked whether a festival counts toward the thirty or not, but with respect to the seven the Gemara did not ask, for it certainly does not count, since the mitzvot of the seven are not practiced during the festival, as there is no prohibition of wearing shoes or overturning the bed. When it asked, it asked with respect to the thirty, for the mitzvot of the thirty are practiced during the festival. Rashi explained: for on the festival too pressing clothes and haircutting are forbidden. The Ramban asked: some of the law of the seven is also practiced during the festival, for he does not launder his garment even with water alone, and many people involve themselves in comforting him, like the law of a mourner. Rather, certainly for the counting of the thirty, all the law of the thirty, even what is not forbidden because of the festival, must be practiced in him; unlike the law of the seven, of which only a little is practiced. Therefore he wrote here that even the law of pressing clothes and haircutting is practiced upon the mourner independent of the law of the festival, as written here. The Tur wrote that from the words of the Rosh it appears that their prohibition is not because of mourning beyond the prohibition of the festival. That is, he holds that although there is no prohibition because of mourning beyond the festival, only what is forbidden during the festival, nevertheless the festival counts toward the thirty, since in any event he practices a prohibition. The Beit Yosef ruled like the Ramban, for his reasoning is logical, and so he ruled in the Shulchan Aruch. In Responsa Maharar Binyamin he disagrees with the Shulchan Aruch, saying that it is not logical to rule like the Ramban, for his words are rejected: his question from the counting of the seven is no question, because the essence of the law of the seven is peh-chet-zayin, nun-tav-resh, namely uncovering the head, removing shoes, etc. Although the law of laundering with water is practiced, this is not the essence of the law of mourning. This is unlike the law of the thirty, whose essence is pressing clothes and haircutting, and that is practiced also on the festival; therefore it counts. Furthermore, who says that one who buries his dead during the festival does not launder his garment with water alone? Perhaps indeed it is permitted. These are his words, but they do not appear correct at all. What he wrote, that the essence of mourning is peh-chet-zayin, nun-tav-resh, who told him that this is the essence? These were mentioned in the Gemara only regarding Shabbat, where peh-chet-zayin is obligatory and nun-tav-resh is optional; but laundering with water is not relevant to Shabbat at all, and therefore it was not counted among peh-chet-zayin, nun-tav-resh. In truth, there is no distinction between these and laundering at all, and laundering is mentioned in the Yerushalmi. If you would think that part of the law of the thirty suffices, so too we should say regarding the seven that part suffices. And what he wrote, perhaps laundering with water during the festival is not forbidden because of mourning, he was not precise at all, for it is certainly a complete prohibition according to all, since private matters are forbidden to him, and this is certainly private, for who will notice that he does not launder his garment with water? The Rosh disagrees only regarding the addition of pressing clothes and haircutting, which are public matters; therefore there is no additional prohibition beyond the festival. The Ramban considered even this private, meaning that he does not wear a pressed garment on those days, for who will say that he has a pressed garment and is not wearing it? Also what the Ramban further counted as an additional prohibition, that he does not shave even if he came from overseas, is not so public, and likewise the other matters he counted here. In truth, these are correct words, and the Beit Yosef judged well like the Ramban.
And I saw further in the above responsum that there was an incident with a groom who married a woman on erev ha-regel, and during the festival a relative died. He ruled there that although an ordinary groom to whom mourning occurs within the seven days of celebration counts the thirty only after the days of celebration, because the days of celebration are stronger than festival days, since he is permitted in pressing clothes and haircutting, as written in siman 342, this groom’s days of celebration were during the festival, and automatically he was forbidden in pressing clothes, etc. Therefore, according to the Ramban above, who holds that the festival counts toward the thirty because of an added prohibition, this groom, who is permitted in that added prohibition, does not have his days of celebration count for him. But according to the Rosh, for whom they do count even if the days of mourning have no greater prohibition than the festival, so here too, since he observes the prohibition of pressing clothes because of the festival, it also counts for him toward the thirty. So he ruled there in practice. In my eyes these words are very wondrous. Certainly even according to the Rosh, it counts only where there is a prohibition upon the mourner because of his mourning, except that that prohibition is also because of the festival; regarding this he said that it counts properly, since in any case he practices that prohibition and can say: I do this because of mourning. What difference does it make if there is an additional prohibition? This is unlike this groom, who has no obligation of mourning at all; the prohibition he practices is only because of the festival. How can we find our hands and feet to say that this should be counted as days of the thirty? We see that he practiced the prohibition specifically because of the festival, not because of mourning. To what is this comparable? To a groom within his seven days of celebration in a place where it is impossible for him to press clothes, or where an oath rests upon him not to press clothes. Would we say that this counts toward the thirty? Certainly not. Therefore it appears that the law he wrote, that the groom’s days of celebration count toward the thirty, is a novelty, and we do not learn from it. They do not count according to anyone, just as with any other groom. This is not comparable to what is written in siman 341, that the groom can defer the seven days of celebration and advance the seven days of mourning for his own benefit. There, with the days of mourning, the matter depends on an affirmative act, for he must perform an action to show the mourning, such as removing shoes, overturning the bed, and the like; therefore we properly say that through that act he cancels his seven days of celebration. This is not so with the law of thirty, which does not depend on an act, but on what we forbid him, pressing clothes and haircutting. In this, certainly we say that he cannot cancel it. If he says he wants to cancel, because in any case it will be forbidden to him because of the festival, we see explicitly that he does not want to perform any mourning, only what he must do because of the festival; this certainly does not count for him. So it appears to me in this matter.
