And not merely because it is a festival, and so forth. This is Ramban's position. He proves it from the Gemara's question whether a festival counts toward the thirty days. Regarding the seven days the Gemara does not ask, because the festival plainly cannot count as seven when the core practices of shivah, such as removing shoes and overturning the bed, are not observed. Regarding thirty, however, the question arises because thirty-day restrictions are practiced during the festival. Rashi explains that pressing garments and haircutting are prohibited on the festival as well. Ramban asks: some shivah restrictions also remain during the festival, such as refraining from laundering even with water and receiving consolers. Why then should the seven days not count? He concludes that for the thirty-day count, the full character of the thirty-day mourning must remain, including restrictions that would not otherwise arise merely because of the festival. This is why he rules here that the mourner's restrictions on pressed garments and haircutting remain in force as mourning restrictions themselves, not only as festival prohibitions. Tur understands Rosh differently: even if the mourner is not subject to any additional restriction beyond what the festival itself prohibits, the festival can still count toward thirty because those same prohibited acts are actually being observed. Beit Yosef rules like Ramban because his reasoning is persuasive. Responsa R. Binyamin disputes Shulchan Aruch and argues that Ramban's proof is weak. He says the essential markers of shivah are the classic practices of head covering, removal of shoes, and similar matters. Laundering in plain water is not one of the principal markers. By contrast, the essential thirty-day restrictions are precisely pressing and haircutting, which remain prohibited during the festival. He further asks who says a mourner buried during a festival may not launder clothing in plain water. In my opinion these objections are unconvincing. The Gemara's list concerning Shabbos does not establish that only those items are principal mourning practices. Laundering simply was not relevant in that particular discussion. The Yerushalmi expressly treats laundering as a mourning restriction. If partial observance were enough for thirty, partial observance should likewise be enough for seven. As for laundering during the festival, it is certainly prohibited as private mourning, and all agree private mourning restrictions remain. Rosh disagrees only about the added public restrictions of haircutting and pressed clothing. Ramban classifies even those as sufficiently private because refraining from wearing a pressed garment does not necessarily publicize mourning, and likewise some haircut situations are not obvious. His reasoning is sound and Beit Yosef ruled correctly.
I also saw in that responsum a case of a groom who married on Erev Yom Tov and whose relative died during the festival. Ordinarily, if a groom suffers a bereavement during his seven wedding days, he begins the thirty-day count only after the wedding week, because the wedding celebration is stronger than an ordinary festival and he is allowed pressing and haircutting. In this case, however, the wedding week fell within the festival, so he was in any event prohibited from those acts because of Yom Tov. The responsum therefore rules that according to Ramban the days should not count because the groom is personally exempt from the extra mourning prohibition, while according to Rosh they should count because he is in fact refraining from pressing and haircutting. I find this very surprising. Even according to Rosh, the festival counts only where there is a genuine mourning obligation and the same acts happen also to be forbidden because of the festival. Then the mourner can say he is observing them for mourning as well. A groom during his wedding week has no mourning obligation at all. His abstention comes solely from the festival. How can those days count toward mourning? This would be like a groom whose wedding week happens to coincide with a circumstance making laundering impossible or with a personal oath not to cut his hair. Surely that cannot count toward thirty. Therefore, the rule that a festival counts toward thirty is a specific novelty and should not be extended to wedding days. The groom's wedding week does not count toward the thirty days, according to everyone.
