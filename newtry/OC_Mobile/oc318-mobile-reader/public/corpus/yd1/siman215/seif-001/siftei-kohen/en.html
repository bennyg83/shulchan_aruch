Vows, etc. This applies specifically to vows, but not to an oath, as stated below in siman 239, seif 4; see there regarding the law of excommunication.
A sukkah in which I sit, etc. However, if he said, “The benefit of a sukkah is forbidden to me,” he may sit in the sukkah, because mitzvos were not given for personal benefit. The Ran and Bach wrote this, and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch likewise ruled in Orach Chaim, siman 586, seif 5, regarding a shofar. See also Responsa Menachem Azariah, siman 31.
In which I sit. This is not considered a matter lacking substance, since he mentions the object that becomes forbidden. The Ran wrote this in the name of Tosafos, and the Bach cites it. See above, siman 213, and siman 239, seif 4.
And they release his vow, etc. The Derishah writes that this implies that even regarding a sukkah, or where he explicitly vowed to fast on Shabbos or Yom Tov, they release his vow by saying to him, “Had you known that it is forbidden to cause yourself distress on Shabbos and Yom Tov,” and the like. This also appears from Orach Chaim, siman 570, and this is the primary ruling. It likewise appears from the words of the Bach.
And some say that there is no, etc. Nevertheless, it appears that they would administer lashes of discipline, as the Rosh writes in Nedarim at the end of the chapter Rabbi Eliezer, based on the Yerushalmi. In this way, the statements of the Raavyah can be reconciled and do not contradict one another. The Mordechai at the beginning of Shevuos writes in the name of the Raavyah that there are no lashes for a vow. It then states: “Elsewhere I found in the name of the Raavyah that he ordered Rabbi Yisrael to be lashed after he vowed not to review the halachah with his brother the following day. They found an opening for him based on regret, and he ordered that he be lashed after his vow was released.” End quote. The lashes ordered for Rabbi Yisrael were rabbinic lashes of discipline, since formal lashes are not administered nowadays. So it appears to me.
