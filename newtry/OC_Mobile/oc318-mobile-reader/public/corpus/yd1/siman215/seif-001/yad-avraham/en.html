(Siman 215, seif 1) Vows take effect even with respect to a mitzvah. How so? If one said, "Konam, the sukkah in which I sit," and so forth, he is forbidden to sit in it. The Shach wrote that if he said, "The benefit of a sukkah is forbidden to me," he may sit in a sukkah for the mitzvah, because mitzvot were not given for personal benefit. It appears that this is not limited to where he explicitly said, "the benefit of a sukkah." The same applies if he said simply, "A sukkah is forbidden to me as a konam," for his words imply only benefit. This is explicit in the Ran to Nedarim at the end of folio 16, and likewise appears from the Yerushalmi cited there on folio 34 regarding one who vowed against a loaf: may he warm his hands with it, meaning whether that counts as benefit? This shows that if such use were not considered benefit, an ordinary vow would certainly not prohibit it. The same may be inferred from Rashi and Tosafot to Eruvin, folio 30, regarding one who said, "This loaf is forbidden to me." According to the view that an eruv may be made only for a mitzvah, and mitzvot were not given for personal benefit, he may use it for an eruv. The same appears in Rosh Hashanah, folio 28. This was also written in Eliyah Zuta on Orach Chaim. See Korban Netanel to Eruvin.
Regarding one who vowed not to eat at an optional feast, Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 70, writes that there is room to question the status of a feast celebrating the dedication of a house. Although Scripture compares building a house to marrying a woman and redeeming a vineyard, which are mitzvot, as stated in Targum Yonatan, from where do we know that the feast itself is a mitzvah? He did not mention the passage in the Yerushalmi to Sotah.
