We will now briefly explain the words of the Tur concerning his citation of the Sefer HaMitzvos and the continuation of his discussion, based upon what the Beit Yosef brought in Orach Chayyim, siman 418, from the four poskim. From this it will become clear that the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch in seif 4 requires study. The Tur wrote in the name of the Sefer HaMitzvos to distinguish between one who accepted upon himself to fast on Yom Tov itself and one for whom Yom Tov occurred during the period of his fast, etc. The poskim found two Talmudic passages contradictory. In the first chapter of Taanis it is stated that if an individual accepted upon himself to fast on Mondays and Thursdays throughout the year, and festivals listed in Megillas Taanis occurred on those days, our decree nullifies his vow. This is difficult, for we say that vows take effect upon matters involving a mitzvah. Furthermore, we say that one may begin the process of release by invoking the honor of Shabbos and Yom Tov, which implies that the vow takes effect. The Ramban answered that the teaching that vows take effect upon matters involving a mitzvah refers to one who uses an explicit expression of a vow, saying, “Eating on such-and-such a day is incumbent upon me.” The passage in the first chapter of Taanis, however, concerns a mere acceptance of a fast, such as one who says, “I will be fasting tomorrow.” In the Semak, siman 81, which is the Sefer HaMitzvos cited by the Tur, and not the Semag as the Beit Yosef understood, thereby leading him to raise his difficulty, this distinction is stated. The teaching that vows take effect upon matters involving a mitzvah refers to one who explicitly vows to fast on Yom Tov, while the passage in Taanis refers to a case where Yom Tov occurred during the period for which he vowed to fast. In that case, we say that one who vowed to fast did not intend to nullify the decrees of the Sages, and certainly not a Torah law forbidding fasting on that day. Nevertheless, it is obvious that according to the Sefer HaMitzvos, release is effective in every case, unlike what the author of the Prishah thought, as I mentioned in seif 1. The Beit Yosef in Orach Chayyim, siman 418, challenged the Sefer HaMitzvos from the Mishnah that states: We do not decree a communal fast on Rosh Chodesh, Chanukah, or Purim, but if they began, they do not interrupt. Thus, even though Rosh Chodesh and Chanukah occurred during the days that they accepted to fast, they nevertheless fast. Yet one may not begin fasting on those days, and it implies that even if they decreed that the fast should begin on them, their decree is not valid. So too it appears from the words of the Maggid Mishneh and the Raavad that there is greater reason to override a fast when one vowed to fast on Yom Tov itself than when Yom Tov occurred during the period for which he vowed, which is the reverse of the words of the Sefer HaMitzvos cited by the Tur here. I do not detect even the slightest difficulty in this. Certainly, regarding the prohibition against initially accepting a fast, it is more severe when one vows concerning Yom Tov itself than when Yom Tov merely arrives during a previously accepted period, for the latter acceptance was made in general terms, and no beit din decrees a fast on Yom Tov. However, regarding a person who already transgressed and vowed to fast on Yom Tov itself, the vow takes effect more strongly than when Yom Tov merely occurred during the period, for in the latter case it is possible that he never considered Yom Tov. This is obvious. Concerning the Ramban’s resolution of the two Talmudic passages mentioned above, the Tur cited the words of the Sefer HaMitzvos, which distinguishes in a different manner from the Ramban. The Tur wrote, “And it is written in the Sefer HaMitzvos,” rather than, “But the Sefer HaMitzvos wrote,” as he usually writes when presenting a dispute, because the Sefer HaMitzvos does not disagree with the Ramban’s distinction between acceptance of a fast and the language of a vow. That is a correct distinction, and it appears to me that it is compelled. If one used the language of accepting a fast, he forbids himself from eating, which resembles an oath that does not take effect upon a matter involving a mitzvah. Only a vow takes effect, as stated at the beginning of this siman. The Semak, however, did not consider it proper to resolve the Talmudic passages this way, since both passages use the expression of a vow. He therefore resolved them through a different distinction. The Rambam makes a third distinction, explaining that the passage in the first chapter of Taanis concerns a rabbinic Yom Tov, and that the Sages strengthened their enactments so that a vow does not take effect on such days even when one explicitly vows to fast on them. The Tur concludes that the Rosh’s final ruling follows the Ramban. Accordingly, since the Shulchan Aruch ruled here according to the Ramban, why did it rule in the following seif that the vow does not take effect on a rabbinic Yom Tov, which is the opinion of the Rambam? These opinions disagree. In Orach Chayyim, siman 418, seif 1, the Shulchan Aruch indeed presented them as a dispute, whereas here it wrote according to both. The Rama therefore correctly wrote that in Orach Chayyim it did not rule this way. The Shulchan Aruch there, in siman 418, rules that a vow takes effect on a rabbinic Yom Tov, but not on a Torah Yom Tov. The Rama in Orach Chayyim, siman 570, wrote that the custom does not follow the Rambam’s opinion cited here. Thus, as practical halacha, we have no distinction other than between the language of a vow and the language of accepting a fast. When one uses the language of a vow, it takes effect in every case and requires release. When one uses the language of accepting a fast, it does not take effect at all and requires no release. This is the opinion of the Ramban.
And even if he accepted upon himself to fast only on Shabbos and Yom Tov. This means that it is not only in the first case, where he did not intend Shabbos and Yom Tov at all, but even if he explicitly accepted upon himself to fast on Shabbos and Yom Tov, the vow nevertheless does not take effect, since he did not express it in the language, “It is incumbent upon me.” My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, needlessly wrote concerning the wording of the Shulchan Aruch here that it was imprecise.
An incident occurred with a man who vowed to give a tithe from the profit generated by his money. He conducted himself this way for several days, his financial situation deteriorated, and he regretted it. It would appear that he may retract, because the profit had not yet come into existence, and one cannot consecrate something that has not yet come into existence, as explained in the chapter “An ox that gored four or five.” However, if he had said, “From all the profit that will accrue from this money, I will give a tithe,” the consecration would take effect because the money being consecrated already exists. The Derishah copied this in the name of the Maharshal, citing Me’il Tzedek. We do not rule this way. Rather, the halacha is as written in Choshen Mishpat, siman 212, seif 9, that when one says, “When I acquire it, I will give it to hekdesh,” it has the status of a vow upon him, and he must fulfill his vow. So it appears to me. The Derishah also brought a gloss in the name of the Maharshal, citing R’ Eliezer of Metz: If one vowed to fast for a month, they say to him, “Within this month that you vowed are Yom Tov and Shabbos.” If he says, “I did not intend those days,” he does not require even release, for thoughts of the heart that are so clearly demonstrated are considered matters of substance, end quote. It appears to me that this follows the opinion of the Sefer HaMitzvos mentioned above. According to our ruling like the Ramban, however, release is required here whenever he used the language of a vow, as explained above. Nevertheless, if he says that he explicitly intended to fast only on weekdays, he is certainly believed, for this does not contradict the wording of his vow that he would fast for one month. In this manner, the words of the gloss may be upheld. The ruling of the Shulchan Aruch in seif 2, that release is required, does not concern such a case. Rather, it concerns one who did not think of Yom Tov and Shabbos at all when he made the vow. The Derishah also brought another gloss in the Maharshal’s name concerning a woman who vowed after Pesach to fast Monday, Thursday, and Monday until Rosh Chodesh. After Pesach, she asked when she should begin. It appears that she should begin after the days of Nissan, if the custom in all places is not to recite Tachanun throughout Nissan, end quote. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that this applies only when she used the language of a mere acceptance of a fast. If she used the language of a vow, she requires release, for she explicitly vowed to fast after Pesach, and this is no better than a vow to fast on Yom Tov.
