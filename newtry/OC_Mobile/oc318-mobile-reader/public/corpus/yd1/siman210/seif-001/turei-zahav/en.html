And he uttered with his lips merely 'bread.' The Rosh writes: we learned that one may make vows to tax collectors and murderers, as below in siman 232, seif 14, where what is in his heart is effective regarding an extortionist. This implies that without an extortionist we do not follow unexpressed thoughts. He answers in the name of the Ramban that the cases differ. There, there is no mistake in his spoken words. He intended in his heart to say with his mouth, 'The produce is forbidden forever,' but inwardly he meant it only for that day. Therefore his spoken words prevail, because unexpressed thoughts are not legally effective. Here, however, his speech was itself mistaken. In his heart he intended to say 'wheat bread,' and therefore what came from his mouth has no independent force. Strictly speaking even his intended thought should be ineffective because he did not verbalize it, but Scripture includes from the verse 'whatever he expresses' that we follow his intent whenever his mouth does not contradict it, because wheat bread is also called bread. End. The Tur's words should likewise be understood this way. When he writes that he uttered 'bread' without qualification, he means that he did not intentionally choose to say only 'bread.' Since he had resolved in his heart to say 'wheat bread,' why would he deliberately say only 'bread'? Rather, he simply forgot to complete the words 'wheat bread.' Tosafos raise this same difficulty in chapter 3 of Shevuos, daf 27, and in one answer distinguish between an am ha'aretz in the case of vows to murderers and a talmid chacham here. This is what the Tur means when he writes, 'And some say this applies specifically to a talmid chacham, but my father the Rosh did not write so.' The Rosh instead follows the Ramban's distinction just cited. The Mordechai likewise writes this in the name of Ravyah, and the Beit Yosef cites it, although he writes that the straightforward reading of the Gemara does not appear so. This is astonishing, because otherwise how would one answer the question of Tosafos and the Rosh? Maharshal likewise writes that the rule applies specifically when he mistakenly said merely 'bread.' But if he deliberately says 'bread' without qualification, we follow his spoken words and even barley bread is forbidden, because unexpressed thoughts are not legally effective.
