Lest he die, and it will result, etc. The Tur writes that he likewise may not write a promissory note against him, because if the document does not state that it is an iska, the entire amount is treated as a loan and is close to profit, etc., even if they orally stipulated otherwise, since we follow the written document and the whole amount appears to be a loan. This is difficult, for it would then seem that even where there is profit he may not take it. Why, then, did the Gemara say the prohibition is because perhaps there will be no profit? It is also difficult that the Gemara gives the reason that perhaps the giver will die and the document will fall into the hands of his heirs, for even while the giver is alive it should be forbidden to collect such ribbis. The Tur's words appear to mean as follows. He first states that one who gives an iska to his fellow should not combine the principal and profit in one sum. That is, he should not write in the document, 'Reuven received from Shimon such-and-such a sum,' where that sum includes principal and anticipated profit. Although we could say he received it as an iska, there is nevertheless a prohibition because he converted the anticipated profit into principal, and perhaps that amount of profit will not be realized. The Tur then writes that he should not write even the principal alone as a promissory note, meaning in the language of a loan. The reason is that such a document enables him to demand the entire principal even if the principal suffered a loss. Although witnesses know that it was arranged as an iska, the written obligation is clear while the limiting condition exists only orally. Since the document leaves open the possibility that he can collect the entire principal despite a loss, it is close to profit and remote from loss. Therefore, even if no loss actually occurred, the document contains a forbidden feature and may not be used for collection. This resolves the difficulty from the Tur's citation in Choshen Mishpat, siman 176, seif 18, of a responsum of the Rosh, which implies that one may take profit when the principal was written in a document. There, the document did not use language of debt, but was written in the first manner described here. This is clear to one who studies the Rosh's responsa, klal 89, which is the source of the Tur's ruling that one should likewise not write a promissory note, etc.
