(Siman 177, Taz seif katan 3) He was already preceded. The Derishah, the Bach in his Kuntres Acharon, Maharash Eidels, Maharam of Lublin in their novellae, and other Acharonim already anticipated most of his discussion. Nevertheless, some of his words and some of the words of the Acharonim do not appear correct in my humble opinion, as I shall explain. The Bach elaborated in his Kuntres Acharon and wrote, “May their Master forgive the sages of the generation for what they wrote,” and so forth. His words are not clear to me, while the words of the sages of the generation appear correct. What difference does it make that the labor is great? He is paid in accordance with the burden of the work. Even were you to maintain that this is the view of the Tur and Maharshal, although in my humble opinion it is not correct and the truth accords with the Beit Yosef's understanding, we may still answer the objections raised by all the sages of the generation against Maharshal. They argued that Maharshal, in criticizing the Beit Yosef, did not penetrate the depth of the matter. According to his approach, Tosafot should likewise have challenged in the chapter Eizehu Neshech, “But he is not idle,” whereas the Rosh already explains that the Sages were lenient because the matter involves only avak ribbis. They further asked why Tosafot did not give the same explanation regarding returning a lost object, and how their question, “If so, how may he receive payment for returning a lost object?” depends upon their explanation. They also challenged Maharshal from Tosafot in the chapter Ad Kamah of Bekhorot, where the matter is explained explicitly in accordance with the Beit Yosef. All of these objections are discussed at length in Torat Chaim, the novellae of Maharsha and Maharam of Lublin, Maadanei Melech on the chapter Eizehu Neshech, the Derishah, and the Bach. According to them, Maharshal erred in several respects. I shall state my own view, explain Maharshal's intent, and defend him against all these objections. Maharshal certainly knew that the Sages were lenient here because the prohibition was only avak ribbis. Rather, he understood that the enactment allowing the custodian to receive the wage of an idle laborer was made so that his unpaid labor in caring for the deposited portion should not appear as ribbis. He therefore asked: How could the Sages enact that he not be paid for the work in which he is presently engaged? It would still appear as ribbis. If the answer is merely that the Sages were lenient regarding avak ribbis, they should instead have enacted that he receive no compensation for the work from which he desisted. Maharshal therefore understood Tosafot's answer, “like one who sits entirely idle,” as coming only to exclude Rashi's interpretation that he is paid for both forms of labor. In truth, he is paid for one form of labor, while with respect to the other he is treated as one who sits entirely idle. Presumably, the Sages fashioned their enactment according to the circumstances. Regarding ribbis, where the concern is that the arrangement should not appear as ribbis, it is reasonable that he be paid for the second labor, so that his performing that labor without compensation should not appear as ribbis. Regarding a lost object, however, payment for the second labor would appear to be payment for returning the lost object. He therefore receives no payment for the second labor, but is compensated for the work from which he desisted. Accordingly, Maharshal's objection is sound. Even according to the Beit Yosef, one may still ask regarding ribbis: “But he is not idle from the labor he is now performing,” and the arrangement still appears as ribbis. The leniency regarding avak ribbis can explain only why he is not paid for the work from which he desisted. It is nevertheless obvious that the arrangement still appears as ribbis if he is not paid for the labor he presently performs. Tosafot could not explain the case of returning a lost object in the same manner as the case of ribbis. When the Gemara asks there, “But he is not idle,” it implies that he should receive payment for the labor he performs with the lost object, since he is not idle. This would imply that one may receive payment for returning a lost object, for otherwise what is the Gemara's question? If such payment is permitted, why should any amount be deducted? He is then no different from one performing ordinary labor rather than a mitzvah. It is therefore impossible to say that Abaye answered that he is paid as one who is entirely idle, with a deduction for one form of labor. If payment is permitted, why should anything be deducted? Moreover, if no deduction is made for the second labor, certainly no deduction should be made for the first, since in the case of a lost object there is greater reason not to pay for the second labor, as explained above. Abaye must therefore have answered in accordance with Rashi's explanation. Tosafot's question is now precise: “If so, he receives payment for returning a lost object.” In the absence of Rashi's explanation, we would have explained that a deduction is made for one form of labor, and presumably, in the case of a lost object, the Sages enacted that the deduction relate to the second labor. According to Rashi, however, their question remains. It is also now clear why Tosafot in Bekhorot wrote that the deduction is made for the second labor, since ordinarily it is likewise forbidden to receive payment for judging, which is the second labor. Thus none of the objections raised by the sages of the generation against Maharshal is difficult. Examine this carefully. The Derishah. At the end of his discussion, the Derishah expressed surprise at the Beit Yosef, arguing that the Tur follows the interpretation of Rabbenu Chananel, Ramban, and Rashba. In my humble opinion, this is incorrect. The Tur's interpretation has no connection to that of Rabbenu Chananel, Ramban, and Rashba. According to them, he is not paid at all for the second labor. The distinction explained by the Gemara is therefore necessary, because he is compensated for the first labor according to its reduced value. This is not the Tur's view. In my humble opinion, this is also the intent of Rabbenu Chananel as cited by Tosafot in the chapter Ad Kamah. Tosafot merely copied what Rabbenu Chananel wrote in the chapter Elu Metziot and what he wrote in the chapter Eizehu Neshech, but his intent in both places is one and the same. See Ramban's Milchamot in the chapter Eizehu Neshech, and you will see that this is correct. The Beit Yosef wrote that Rambam, in his Commentary to the Mishnah in Bekhorot, explained the matter like Rashi, and that the Maggid Mishneh wrote the same in Rambam's name at the end of Hilkhot Gezeilah. I did not find this in the Maggid Mishneh. I also saw that in chapter 12 of Hilkhot Gezeilah the Maggid Mishneh promised to explain the matter at the end of those laws, but no such explanation appears there. I examined Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah in Bekhorot and found that his words are closer to Tosafot's view, for he makes no mention at all of the second labor. See there and examine carefully. The Beit Yosef further wrote that the Semag and Hagahot Maimoniyot explain the Rif in accordance with Tosafot. I found, however, that the Nimukei Yosef and Ramban explain the Rif in accordance with Rabbenu Chananel. In Milchamot, Ramban cites a responsum of the Rif himself that explains the matter in this way. The Maggid Mishneh likewise writes in chapter 12 of Hilkhot Gezeilah, in the name of the Rif, as stated there. The Bach wrote in the body of his work that the Rosh explains the matter like the Tur. This is incorrect. The Rosh's intent is as the Beit Yosef wrote. Although I defended Maharshal above, the truth nevertheless accords with the Beit Yosef. When the Rosh writes that “his wage is equal,” he means that the assessment is always made according to the wage of an idle laborer. The Bach's objection to the Beit Yosef at the end of his discussion is likewise unfounded. The Beit Yosef also understood that the Rosh's words, “whether much or little,” refer to the first labor. His intent is that whether the labor was extensive or slight, the assessment is always made as though the person were sitting entirely idle. The Bach also left unresolved, according to the Tur's understanding, why the Tanna did not simply teach, “He gives him his wage,” rather than “like a laborer.” This is not difficult. Had the Mishnah merely said, “He gives him his wage,” we might have thought that he receives full compensation for the first labor from which he desisted. It therefore teaches that he receives only the wage appropriate to a laborer performing the second labor. Examine carefully.
