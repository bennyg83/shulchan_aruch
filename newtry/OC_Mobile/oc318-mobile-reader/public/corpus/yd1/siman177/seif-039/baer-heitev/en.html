Themselves. Perhaps what he bought for himself is not as choice as what belongs to the partnership, so his fellow is thereby harmed. Where there is a doubt in law or a dispute among the poskim whether it is fixed ribit or avak ribit, it appears to be a monetary doubt and we are lenient, and the burden of proof is on the claimant, unless most poskim agree that it is fixed ribit, for then it is a doubtful Torah prohibition and we follow the majority. It appears to me that beit din should tell him he is committing a prohibition and should warn him, and it is proper for him to be stringent for himself. But if he pays no attention to all this, beit din has no power to extract from him, since it is a doubtful law. At first glance there is proof from what we say regarding priestly gifts, explained above siman 61 seif 23, and below regarding a firstborn in siman 305 seif 13, and in several places, that he is exempt because the burden of proof is on the claimant, even though it is a positive Torah commandment. See above siman 259. Thus the Shach. The prohibition of ribit is at the beginning of the loan even if the borrower does not give the ribit. Torat Emet siman 162. It is forbidden to lend with ribit to a Jew on condition that it be given to the poor or to other mitzvot. A rich borrower and poor lender may not borrow from him for the poor person's benefit. One who borrowed from his fellow on a pledge and, because of the prohibition of ribit, made it like a complete sale, although it was worth more, and the lender promised that if the borrower later did his will he would sell it back to him, as is customary among those who regularly give profits, and now the lender does not wish to return it at all, claiming he bought it completely, while the borrower claims it was done because of the prohibition: the Tzemach Tzedek, siman 20, ruled that since the borrower gives him the customary addition to his money, this is called doing his will, and he must return his pledge. See Maharam MiTrani siman 123. He further wrote in siman 21 about one who borrowed on a pledge in the manner mentioned; when much time passed and the borrower did not redeem it, the lender came to make an accounting so the borrower would pay the principal with the profits and take his property. According to the accounting he owed more than the pledge was worth. The lender said, "I will not return the pledge unless you pay me in full, for to me it is worth as much as you owe me," while the borrower said, "I will give you only the value of the pledge and take it." He concluded that the law is with the borrower, since at the outset the lender lent him less than the pledge's value, revealing that he wanted security for the principal and for the additional amount that would arrive through a heter, not through prohibition; if he takes more, it is certainly forbidden. See there, and see the Kuntres Acharon of Lechem HaPanim, who copied from the Sema the enactments from the Pinkas HaAratzot regarding ribit.
