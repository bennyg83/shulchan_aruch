A young person, etc. Beit Yosef writes in the name of Shibbolei HaLeket that this means one who has not yet reached the age of mitzvos. Darkei Moshe and Perishah cite this.
Only that he is great, etc. Tosafot in the first chapter of Kiddushin, folio 32b, and Semak, siman 32, write that he must be outstanding in wisdom, and Beit Yosef cites them. The Rosh writes likewise. This does not mean the degree of distinction discussed below in seif 10, where he is more outstanding than the other sages of his generation and therefore has the status of one's primary teacher. Rather, here it means that he is more learned than ordinary people. This follows from Tosafot and the Rosh, who state this only to explain why Scripture mentions both an elder and one of advanced age, since a young but wise person means one outstanding in wisdom. Thus, he is distinguished from the general population. This also resolves the Tur's citation in the name of Rabbeinu Peretz, unlike Bach, who maintains that Rabbeinu Peretz disputes Semak. The straightforward wording does not imply a dispute. What the Tur attributes to Rambam should read Ramah, as Perishah notes from an old parchment copy of the Tur, and as Rabbeinu Yerucham writes in the name of Ramah. This is unlike Beit Yosef and Bach, who emend it to Ramban. I later found this emendation also in Bedek HaBayis. Ramah's ruling, that one who is great in wisdom and fit to learn from is entitled to honor, likewise concerns one who is more learned than ordinary people. Otherwise, an unlearned person or one of little Torah knowledge would have to stand before everyone, which is untenable. The Torah obligated standing only before a sage. This is why the Tur did not repeatedly specify that the sage must be distinguished, because it is self-evident that he must be distinguished from ordinary people. According to this, Rama's words are understood. Ran also writes like Ramah, and concludes that perhaps even two equals are exempt, because one is obligated to stand only before a sage greater than himself. This plainly concerns someone more learned than ordinary people, for otherwise it would be obvious that equals are exempt. Ramah and Rabbeinu Peretz differ only regarding one who is not among the outstanding sages of the generation and is not his teacher at all. Rabbeinu Peretz appears to hold that even if he is greater in wisdom, one is exempt, whereas Ramah requires standing because one who is fit to teach him is considered his teacher, though not his primary teacher. Rabbeinu Yerucham states this explicitly.
