(Siman 25, margin a) One who slaughters must check the simanim, etc. And if he did not see that the majority were slaughtered — forbidden. Here: if it is permitted to milk this animal and drink its milk — much investigation is needed. For in the sugya it is clear there is a dispute: R' Eliezer says treifah and forbidden in eating; in the baraita it teaches neveilah and it imparts impurity by carrying; and they interpret their dispute: one holds it stands in presumption of prohibition and now it died; and one says we do not say presumption of prohibition — we say presumption of impurity — clear from this that according to the baraita tanna, since for eating prohibition we judge from presumption it was not slaughtered — automatically it imparts impurity as neveilah; for to forbid it in eating and not to impart impurity would be two contradictory matters; or as Rashba holds in his chiddushim that for impurity too there is somewhat presumption of prohibition since it died before you — and any live thing that died, you are in doubt to remove it from its impurity — do not remove it — see there. And for R' Eliezer, for eating prohibition it stood forbidden from before, we establish it in its presumption; but for impurity, where there is no presumption, we do not impart impurity. Even according to Minchat Yaakov: whether permitted in eating or neveilah and imparts — nevertheless they distinguish between eating prohibition; if so, one may say for R' Eliezer the same: we distinguish between the animal's flesh and its milk — for in milk there is no presumption of prohibition — permitted. But for the aforementioned baraita tanna one may say forbidden, for even according to Rashba's view one may say so in milk: any live thing that died — its milk is forbidden, and you are in doubt to remove and permit its milk — do not remove it from doubt. And if so, since the halakhah was not decided in the Talmud, and the essence of eating prohibition of the animal itself is also rabbinic, as Tosafot and Rashba wrote there — from the Torah we follow the majority, and the majority of times one slaughters properly — automatically in rabbinic matters we hold like the lenient; and like R' Eliezer who distinguishes eating and impurity; or possibly even for the baraita tanna it is permitted: specifically for impurity we are stringent, since there is no presumption of permissibility — what was not impure was because live things do not receive impurity, and behold it died before you — therefore presumption of prohibition of the animal avails that it impart impurity as neveilah. But for milk, which has presumption of permissibility — in its lifetime there was no eating prohibition — we establish it in its presumption that it is still permitted. And see Turei Zahav, who raised Rambam's view that it does not impart by carrying. And it appears to me the reason: even though Rambam holds it is d'oraisa neveilah, as Turei Zahav agreed — that is, he holds this reasoning itself: it stands in presumption of prohibition and now died; this reasoning is rabbinic, that they were stringent it should impart impurity lest there be two contradictory matters; and in rabbinic matters we follow the lenient, and we hold like R' Eliezer that it does not impart. And if so, the same for permitting milk — we do not forbid it because of presumption of the animal. And all the more according to what Perishah wrote (siman 87): milk of neveilah is forbidden only rabbinically, because it was not completed in prohibition — automatically one may rely on Tosafot and Rashba that most slaughterers slaughter the majority of simanim, and from the Torah the animal too is permitted and forbidden only rabbinically — automatically we hold like the lenient, like R' Eliezer who distinguishes eating and impurity; the same: distinguish between prohibition of the animal and milk. And it appears further that even for the baraita tanna he admits milk is permitted, and there are not two contradictory matters here; and this is what Shach wrote (siman 86, s.k. 11): if he slaughtered a chicken and there was doubt in shechitah and he found complete eggs in it — the eggs are permitted, for egg of neveilah is rabbinic and its doubt is lenient — see there. Behold Shach holds: even though the chicken is forbidden, nevertheless the egg is permitted. And see Minchat Yaakov (klal 6, siman 71, s.k. 3), who challenged Shach: if we say doubt in rabbinic matters where presumption became prohibition — forbidden; and since every doubt in shechitah has presumption it is not slaughtered, this egg has presumption of prohibition and is forbidden from doubt — see there. And in truth it appears to me Minchat Yaakov's words are correct. For we hold (first chapter of Ketubot) like one who says: according to the view that permits the mother, he permits the daughter — presumption of the mother avails for the daughter. And even according to the view that disqualifies the daughter — that is because of severity of lineage, as Tosafot wrote (Ketubot daf 26). And since to be lenient presumption of the mother avails for the daughter — the same: presumption of the chicken avails for the egg, and the egg itself has presumption of prohibition. In any case, Shach's view is clear: one may distinguish and forbid the chicken and permit the egg. And Minchat Yaakov too admits to his words — he only disputes regarding presumption of prohibition. And if so, here in our case it is fine that Minchat Yaakov too admits milk is permitted; and this is since in truth it is majority of majorities of slaughterers who slaughter the majority of simanim, and rabbinan were stringent. And this is that they were stringent: rely on minority near presumption and it becomes half and half. In any case, again the animal is not called "presumption became prohibition," for majority is preferable to presumption — rather rabbanan said rely on minority near presumption and it becomes half and half. And since the animal is forbidden only from doubt — automatically milk is permitted, for it is doubt in rabbinic matters according to the aforementioned Perishah that milk of neveilah is forbidden only rabbinically; and since so, one may say the reverse in our case: for all opinions milk is permitted. And even for the baraita tanna — and this is specifically for impurity: without presumption too it is fit to impart from doubt, and we cannot purify the animal except because of majority that most slaughter properly — we would say it is certainly pure; with this there would be two contradictory matters, since it is forbidden in eating — accordingly it is certainly not permitted, and how can you come to purify it and say it was certainly slaughtered properly? But for milk, truly we say there is doubt both for the flesh and for the milk; and what has d'oraisa concern is forbidden from doubt; and milk, which is rabbinic, is permitted from doubt — with this there is no contradiction. In this manner, according to the aforementioned Perishah, it is clear in my humble opinion milk is permitted, and it is exactly like the egg that Shach permits. And even for Minchat Yaakov it is permitted in our case, for it is only half and half, as explained. And the reason: either we rule like R' Eliezer that it does not impart; or even for the baraita tanna it is permitted, for there are not two contradictory matters, as explained. However, according to Bach in siman 101, milk of neveilah is d'oraisa, for it is like a limb from its limbs — if so, one cannot compare to that law of Shach mentioned. And in this it requires study regarding the law of milk; nevertheless it is possible to permit because of the first reasoning I wrote: since for most poskim the animal itself is forbidden only rabbinically, we hold like R' Eliezer who distinguishes eating and impurity — the same: distinguish between flesh and milk. And even for Rambam, who holds d'oraisa neveilah — nevertheless, since he ruled it does not impart, as Turei Zahav raised in his view — the same: milk is permitted. And what I have to question on this: possibly according to Bach's view, milk is worse than impurity, and even for R' Eliezer, who holds it does not impart, one may say milk is forbidden — and this is Bach's view, that milk is like a limb from its limbs and the milk became neveilah; automatically one may say: also in its lifetime, as long as it is in the animal, it is like its limbs and forbidden because of eiver min hachai — only when it separated from the animal by itself and the Torah permitted it; but as long as it is inside, it is like the animal itself — if so, one may say also for milk it is called presumption of prohibition: as long as it did not separate from the animal, prohibition of eiver min hachai applies to it. And it requires study according to Bach. Nevertheless for practical halakhah, since according to Perishah the matter is clear that milk is permitted, as I wrote, and also according to Bach the matter stands in doubt — automatically one may rely on most poskim that the animal body is forbidden only rabbinically and milk is permitted. And one may be aided by the view of Tiferet Lemoshe, who wrote (siman 87) that milk of neveilah, even rabbinically, is permitted in eating — as appears to me for practical halakhah to permit milk. May God save us from mistakes.
