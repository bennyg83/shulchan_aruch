To open the grave. At the end of Chavot Yair responsa, in the responsum of the Gaon Rabbi David Oppenheim on p. 247, he wrote in the name of the Gaon Rabbi Gershon of Metz regarding two dead persons who were buried and in each case they forgot to dress the deceased in one customary garment of the shrouds. One was over twenty years old and one under twenty. He instructed that the minor's grave be opened to dress him in the missing garment, because with a minor there is no trembling of judgment; but the adult's grave should not be opened because of the trembling and fear of judgment, as Shmuel said, "Why have you disturbed me?" He z"l challenged this from Bava Batra 155; see there. See Chacham Tzvi responsa siman 47, and see Knesset Yechezkel responsa siman 44 on this, who concludes that the Gaon Rabbi Gershon z"l ruled well without hesitation; see there. See Shivat Tziyon responsa simanim 64, 65, and 66, regarding a murdered person who was found with no facial form recognizable, but from the clothing and writings it appeared that he was the husband of a woman who had left his house half a year earlier. They buried him, and afterward the woman came and said her husband had definitive identifying marks on his body. Is it permitted to open his grave to check for the signs, in order to release her from being an agunah? Rabbi Elazar Fleckeles held to permit, for even purchasers can say, "We gave money; let him be disgraced," were it not for the reason that signs may change, as explained in Bava Batra 154. If so, this woman, so that she not remain an agunah all her life, can say: what do I care about his disgrace? These signs never change, and especially since it is also for his honor, so his children will mourn and say kaddish. The author disagreed and concluded to forbid, and Rabbi Elazar Fleckeles then acknowledged his words, because there in Bava Batra they know with certainty who the dead person is and were only uncertain whether he was a minor; but here perhaps it is someone else, and the disgrace would be for nothing, so certainly one should not permit. See there. I later saw in Knesset Yechezkel, Even HaEzer siman 46, s.v. amnam misham, that he wrote simply that it is permitted to open the grave and see him in order to permit his wife. One should not forbid because of "why have you disturbed me," for if it is permitted to dig up his grave in order to bury him in his own, all the more so to permit his wife. See there. However, his proof is not compelling.
