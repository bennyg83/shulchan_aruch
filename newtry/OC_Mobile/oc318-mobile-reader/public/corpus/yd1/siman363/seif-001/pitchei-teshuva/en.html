One does not remove. Baer Heitev of Maharit cites Chacham Tzvi that one buried in gentile-owned ground may be exhumed and reburied in a Jewish cemetery. Tiferet Tzvi, Yoreh Deah simanim 59 and 60, agrees and adds that people cannot be forced to remain buried in an old cemetery merely because choosing a new cemetery may imply slight disrespect to those already buried there. A person need not accept even a small degradation in order to avoid such an implication. Noda BiYehudah, Yoreh Deah siman 89, cites R. Moshe Fischels regarding a cemetery leased for only one hundred fifty years, after which the land was to revert to the owners for cultivation or building. The descendants wished to purchase a permanent cemetery and transfer the graves there. He ruled against immediate exhumation for several reasons and distinguished the Chacham Tzvi case, where transfer to a Jewish cemetery itself constituted greater honor. Noda BiYehudah agreed, adding that perhaps in the distant future circumstances would change, the dead would arise, or the land could be purchased permanently. However, this applies only where the community is assured that when the lease expires it will still be permitted to move the remains before the land reverts. If there is a genuine concern that permission will later be denied, they may move the remains now. In any case, from now on they should not bury additional dead in a temporary cemetery when permanent land is available. Chatam Sofer, siman 334, discusses a cemetery assigned during an epidemic, not given permanently, unfenced, unguarded, and subject under local law to removal after six years. A permanent Jewish cemetery had become available. He rules that transfer is plainly permitted and indeed a mitzvah. This is especially so for those whose relatives are buried in the established cemetery, but applies to the other epidemic dead as well because the temporary place is unsecured and liable to desecration. Once the townspeople's own relatives are removed, the public cannot be compelled to undertake large expense to preserve a temporary cemetery for transient strangers. Moreover, burial in land not belonging to the Jewish community resembles burial in a grave not one's own, whereas the established cemetery belongs to the communal burial society and every buried person has a share in it. He therefore urges prompt transfer.

Or it is a grave that was found. Birkei Yosef recounts a case where the seven communal leaders sold a woman a clearly marked grave plot in writing. Later someone unknowingly buried a stillborn infant there. He ruled that the infant may be moved because a grave unexpectedly found in another's established plot may be cleared. Mahari Basan, responsum 21, had ruled similarly in an almost identical case.
