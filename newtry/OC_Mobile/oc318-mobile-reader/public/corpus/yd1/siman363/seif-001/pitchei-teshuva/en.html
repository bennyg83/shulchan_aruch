One does not move. See the Be'er Heitev of Rabbi Maharit z"l, and what he wrote in the name of Chacham Tzvi siman 50, that one who was buried in non-Jewish land may be removed from there to be buried in a Jewish burial place. See Tiferet Tzvi responsa, Yoreh De'ah simanim 59 and 60, who also ruled this way. He further wrote that it is obvious that one should not prevent those who want to be buried in a new cemetery because this will cause disgrace to the dead buried in the old cemetery. What has that to do with them? In any case, they do not want to be disgraced and buried in this place, and one is not obligated to suffer even slight disgrace for his fellow; see there. See Noda BiYehudah, Yoreh De'ah siman 89, in the name of the Gaon Rabbi Meir Fischeles z"l, regarding a community that bought land for graves not permanently but only for one hundred and fifty years, after which the land would return to the owners for plowing or building. Now their children arose and want to buy other land as a permanent acquisition. Is it permitted to move those buried there and bury them in this new land so they will have eternal rest? He ruled to forbid for three reasons. He wrote that this is not comparable to the responsum of Chacham Tzvi, for there it is greater honor not to have a non-Jew buried near a Jew; here it is different. The author agreed with him and added another reason to forbid moving them now: perhaps with the passage of much time the dead will be resurrected, or they will be able to buy the land permanently. He wrote, however, that this applies only if they are certain that even after the time expires they will have permission to remove the bones from there to another place before the land returns to its owners. But if there is concern that afterward they will not grant permission to move them, it is permitted to move them now. In any case, from now on, since they are able to buy land permanently, they should no longer bury in this place. See the responsa at the end of Sidduro Shel Shabbat, responsum 2, and see Shivat Tziyon responsa siman 43. See Chatam Sofer responsa siman 334 regarding a cemetery given to Jews to bury those who died in a plague, may the Merciful One protect us, but not given as a permanent holding, not enclosed by a wall, and not guarded. Under non-Jewish law, after six years they remove the bones and sow there. Now they found a place where they can easily arrange to clear this cemetery, remove the dead, and bury them in the old cemetery; most of those who died in the plague were townspeople whose relatives lie in the old graves. Is it permitted to move all those graves? He wrote that it is plainly permitted and there is a great mitzvah to pursue this. This is true not only for those whose fathers and relatives lie in the old cemetery, but even for the other dead, since the place is not enclosed and guarded and they are destined to be desecrated. Although perhaps with great effort and expense they could manage to build a wall around it, close it, guard it, and make it a permanent holding, nevertheless, once all the people connected to the townspeople who have relatives there are removed, and only those who were temporary residents remain, who can impose upon the public great expense and effort? They would remain unguarded. Moreover, since the graves are not Jewish-owned, it is like a righteous person buried in a grave that is not his, see Bava Batra 102a, unlike the old cemetery which belongs to the society as customary, where all those buried there have a share like a synagogue of a city. Therefore they should be zealous in this mitzvah; see there. See what I wrote below in siman 403, s.v. ha-melaket.
