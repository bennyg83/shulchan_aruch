Every red appearance. This is Tur's language: By Torah law, only five shades of blood are impure in a woman, and no others. Nowadays, however, because expertise has diminished, they reverted to treating as impure anything that has a reddish appearance, and so forth. It appears to me that for this reason Tur and the Mechaber did not reproduce the rules governing how a sage should examine niddah blood, such as whether blood may be examined at night and whether he should cast a shadow over the blood with his hand, as explained at the end of the chapter Kol HaYad and codified by Rambam in chapter 5 of Hilchot Issurei Bi'ah. For according to our practice, only a white or green appearance is pure, and these require no careful scrutiny, since they can readily be distinguished. Therefore, a sage may examine the stain in whatever manner he chooses, whether to declare it pure or impure. Raavad likewise writes this in his glosses to chapter 5 of Hilchot Issurei Bi'ah; see there.
Rather, a white appearance. Even when it is not completely white, but resembles a white garment upon which dust has fallen and whose whiteness has thereby been dimmed, it is pure. Beit Yosef, with the agreement of the later authorities.
And likewise a green appearance. Bach writes that when a green or white appearance is brought before the halachic authority while it is still moist, he should not rule on it until it dries, because after drying a little red may sometimes be found at its edges. Taz and Shach dispute this at length and write that this is not the accepted practice. It appears to me that there is proof from the beginning of the first chapter of Berachot, where David said, 'Am I not pious?' and described his hands as soiled with blood, a fetus, and a placenta in order to permit a woman to her husband. This explicitly indicates that even while the matter is still moist it may be examined in order to declare it pure, since the expression 'my hands are soiled' would not apply once it had dried. This is clear.
