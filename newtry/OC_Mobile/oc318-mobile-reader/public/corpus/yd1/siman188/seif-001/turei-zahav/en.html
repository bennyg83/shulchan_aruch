And certainly a green appearance, etc. And certainly a white appearance that does not incline at all toward redness. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that he found in writings in the name of Mahariv Margolis that a woman from whom a thick, white substance emerged approximately two or three hours after she bathed in a bathhouse is tamei, because blood commonly becomes white as a result of bathing, etc. These words are astonishing, for no allusion to this is found in the Talmud or the poskim, nor is Mar bar Rav Ashi signed upon them to establish that these words indeed came from Mahariv Margolis. In the Gemara we say regarding a black appearance that it is red, except that it deteriorated. Were this reasoning correct, it should have stated an even greater novelty, that even a white appearance is sometimes found to have deteriorated. Likewise, it is explained below in this seif that a white, thick substance is permitted, and no distinction is made whether she had previously bathed in hot water. Rather, this is an excessive stringency. One who wishes to be stringent should be stringent for himself, but he should not issue such a ruling to others at all. So it appears in my humble opinion. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, also introduced another stringency, that when a green or white appearance is brought before the halachic authority while it is still moist, he should not rule concerning it until it dries, because sometimes, after it dries, redness will be found at its edges. End quote. His words are astonishing. If so, what is the meaning of what they said in siman 186 regarding a woman who has no fixed veset, that she should examine herself before relations? What benefit is there in the examination, since according to his words she must wait until it dries, and while it remains moist she knows nothing? Furthermore, this is difficult in light of an explicit Talmudic passage at the end of the chapter Kol HaYad, which cites a Baraisa that states expressly: A sage should not say, 'Had it been moist, it would certainly have been tamei.' Rather, we say that a judge has only what his eyes see. If so, the same applies in the reverse direction: he has only what his eyes see while it is moist. From where do we derive a distinction based merely on reasoning? Furthermore, it is stated explicitly there that Rebbi saw blood at night and ruled it tamei; by day he ruled it tahor. He waited one hour, then again ruled it tamei, and said, 'Woe is me, perhaps I erred.' The Gemara asks: 'Perhaps I erred?' He certainly erred. Rashi explains that the Gemara initially understood that by day Rebbi ruled it tahor, and afterward he did not see it again, but rather reconsidered on his own and ruled it tamei. He reasoned that perhaps it had been tamei the previous night, but now that it had dried its appearance had changed, and had it remained moist as it initially was, it would have appeared tamei. He therefore retracted his ruling of tahor. Concerning this he afterward said, 'Perhaps I erred,' meaning in having retracted and ruled it tamei, since its present appearance was tahor. The Gemara therefore asks that he certainly erred, for it was taught that a sage should not say, etc. We thus see that, according to this explanation, Rebbi viewed the blood at night while it was moist, and he was not concerned that although it then appeared tahor, it might appear tamei the following day after drying. This reasoning remains valid even according to the conclusion there. Rather, it is obvious that we are not concerned for this, because here too a judge has only what his eyes see. One cannot say that Rebbi viewed it while moist only for purposes of stringency, so that if it appeared tamei he would rule her tamei, but not conversely for purposes of leniency. This is untenable, for the statement, 'Rebbi saw blood at night,' implies that he viewed it in order to rule upon it according to law, whether to declare it tahor or tamei. Furthermore, Rashi explains there that Rebbi acted according to his own reasoning, as it is stated that Rebbi examined by candlelight. Were it conceivable that he viewed it only in order to be stringent, why did Rashi connect this with the fact that he examined by candlelight, which is an excellent examination even for purposes of leniency, whereas here it would have been only for stringency? Rather, it is clear that he viewed it even for purposes of leniency, if it appeared lenient, and he was not concerned that it was still moist. There is also proof from siman 52 above, where the Tur wrote regarding an animal that fell into a fire that if the appearance of its organs changed after they were boiled, the animal is a tereifah. Nevertheless, it is permitted to eat them roasted, and we are not concerned that perhaps they would have changed had they been boiled. Rashi explains the reason in the Gemara: we do not presume a defect where no defect is visibly evident. The same applies here, and all the more so, for while it is moist there is no defect at all. It therefore appears that no one is concerned for this stringency of refusing to inspect it while it is moist.
