To leave the deceased overnight. Radbaz, new responsa siman 311, writes that one violates the negative commandment only by leaving one's deceased unburied the entire night until morning. A partial night does not violate the prohibition, though prompt burial is praiseworthy, as stated in seif 2. Chavot Yair, siman 139, discusses a Jewish thief who was captured and hanged, and the local ruler demanded an enormous sum before permitting burial in a Jewish cemetery. Were the Jews obligated to spend a very large sum to avoid the prohibition of leaving a corpse overnight? He concludes that the prohibition falls specifically on the person responsible for burial, not upon everyone else. Even that responsible person need not spend all his money to appease the ruler, because where outside coercion prevents burial he is not actively violating a negative commandment and the obligation resembles a positive commandment. This differs from an actively violated negative prohibition, for which one must spend all his property, as Rama writes at the beginning of siman 157. According to this, however, it would appear that one should spend up to one fifth, as Shach indicates regarding a positive commandment. Chavot Yair further suggests that perhaps there is no prohibition even upon the responsible relative where an outside party prevents burial, since burial may sometimes be delayed even for monetary reasons. This reasoning is unclear to me, and his wording requires correction. Choshen Mishpat siman 107, seif 2 in the gloss says only that the obstructing creditor does not violate the prohibition. The heir who fails to make reasonable efforts to satisfy the creditors may still violate it. Besamim Rosh, siman 64, writes that even a person wicked throughout his life may not be left overnight. Magen Avraham, siman 526, proves that the prohibition applies even to nonviable infants. Arba'ah Turei Even, siman 23, initially brings proof from Tosafot Yom Tov in Shabbos, Perek HaMatznia, and then rejects it. See R. Yonatan Eybeschutz's chiddushim to Rambam Hilchot Yom Tov chapter 1, halachah 23, and Tiferet Tzvi, Orach Chayyim siman 27, addressed to R. Yeshayah Berlin. Chatam Sofer, siman 338, discusses letters printed in Bikkurei HaItim from 1772 in which Moses Mendelssohn argued that rabbinic opposition to delaying burial should be reconsidered because contemporary physicians claimed there was no certain boundary between life and death until bodily decomposition. He cited the Mishnah at the end of Niddah concerning various impure persons remaining impure until the flesh decays, and Evel Rabbati chapter 8, which speaks of checking graves for up to three days and recounts one person who later lived another twenty-five years and had children. He argued by kal vachomer that if burial could be delayed for the deceased's honor in order to obtain a coffin and shrouds, certainly it could be delayed out of possible danger to life. Yaavetz forcefully rejected the proposal. Chatam Sofer also responds at length. His essential point is that when the Torah commanded prompt burial and prohibited leaving a corpse overnight, it necessarily transmitted to us a practical criterion of death. Perhaps that criterion was known to early natural philosophers though forgotten by later physicians, perhaps Moshe received it at Sinai, or perhaps Chazal derived it from "all in whose nostrils was the breath of life," making cessation of breathing the decisive measure, as stated in Yoma 85a. One cannot distinguish a collapsed building, because the verse does not address only such a case. Indeed, sudden collapse is precisely where apparent death due to shock or paralysis might be more suspect, yet once breathing ceases Shabbos is no longer desecrated. This demonstrates a general transmitted standard for all deaths. The Mishnah in Niddah does not prove otherwise. The incident in Evel Rabbati is an exceedingly rare occurrence, not even a meaningful minority, comparable to Choni HaMe'agel sleeping seventy years, and does not create a pikuach nefesh concern. That incident was recorded only because visiting or tapping a grave might otherwise resemble an idolatrous or superstitious practice; since such an extraordinary event once occurred, the visitor can be understood as acting from concern for a remote possibility rather than following superstition. Chatam Sofer concludes that we have only the Torah, the received tradition of our ancestors, and the halachic criterion handed down to us. The prohibition against delaying burial remains fully in force, except in places where governmental law itself requires delay.
They left him overnight for his honor. See in the responsa of the Radbaz volume 1 siman 107, who was asked about one who died on Thursday, and his father wanted to wait from burying him until tomorrow at dusk close to the entrance of Shabbos, in order to save him from chibut hakever: whether it is permitted to do so, if this is called for his honor; and also whether it helped to save him from chibut hakever by leaving him until close to sunset. And he answered that even if we say that he is thereby saved from the law of chibut hakever, nevertheless it is forbidden to do so, and this is not called for his honor, except such as to bring a coffin and shrouds; but a matter of punishment is not called honor. And all the more so that the matter is clear that it did not help at all to save him from the law of chibut hakever, for several reasons; see there.
