To leave overnight. See the new Radbaz responsa siman 311, who wrote that one violates the negative commandment only if he leaves his dead overnight the entire night until morning. For part of the night he does not violate, and there is not even a prohibition; only as a praiseworthy matter one should hurry to remove the dead, as below in seif 2; see there. See Chavot Yair responsa siman 139 regarding a Jewish thief who was caught and hanged, and the official demanded an enormous sum to allow him to be buried in a Jewish grave. Were they obligated to spend much money so as not to violate the prohibition of "you shall not leave overnight"? He concludes that the prohibition applies only to the person upon whom burial is incumbent, not to others; and even the one upon whom it is incumbent is not obligated to spend all his money to appease the official so as not to violate this prohibition. Since he does not actively violate the negative commandment, it is only like a positive commandment, and not like an active negative commandment for which one must spend all his money, as the Rama wrote above at the beginning of siman 157; see there. According to this it implies that he must spend up to one fifth, as follows from the Shach there that for a positive commandment one must spend up to one fifth. What he further wrote there, that there is no prohibition of leaving overnight even for the one obligated to bury, since another is delaying and preventing it and therefore burial may be delayed even for money, is not clear to me. His language there is also confused and needs correction. In truth, in Choshen Mishpat siman 107 seif 2 in the gloss, it says only that the one delaying has no transgression; but the heir, if he does not try to settle with the creditors, may be said to transgress. See Besamim Rosh responsa siman 64, that even if he was entirely wicked all his life, it is forbidden to leave him overnight; see there for the reason. See Magen Avraham siman 526, who proved that even with stillborns one violates "do not leave overnight." See Arba'ah Turei Even responsa siman 23, who brings proof from Tosafot Yom Tov in Shabbat, chapter HaMotzi, and then rejects it; see there. See the novellae of the Gaon Rabbi Yonatan z"l on the Rambam's laws of Yom Tov, chapter 1 law 23, and see Tiferet Tzvi responsa, Orach Chayim siman 27, what he wrote to the Gaon Rabbi Y. Berlin z"l. See Chatam Sofer responsa siman 338 concerning the letters found in Bikkurei HaItim from the year 5532, where the sage R. M. D. argued to permit what the sages of Israel forbade, saying that contemporary physicians said there is no known boundary between life and death and that one cannot know except by decomposition of the flesh. He brought proof from the Mishnah at the end of Niddah, that the zav, zavah, and niddah, etc., convey tumah until the flesh decomposes, and from Semachot chapter 8, that they inspect the dead for up to three days, and once one lived afterward for twenty-five years and had children. If so, he argued by kal vachomer: if for the honor of the dead, to bring him a coffin and shrouds, one may leave him overnight, all the more so because of possible saving of life. In former times they buried in niches and it was possible to knock on the grave, but nowadays, when they bury in the earth itself, there is therefore an obligation to leave him overnight. These were his words. But the Gaon Rabbi Yaavetz struck him with a hundred measures and showed him no favor. Chatam Sofer too discusses this at length to reject that sage's words. The root of his argument is that without doubt, when the Torah said, "you shall not leave overnight," etc., and "you shall surely bury him," and one who violates this with any dead person transgresses a positive and negative commandment, a measure of death was thereby handed to us. Perhaps there was then a tradition from the early naturalists, even if it has been forgotten by contemporary physicians, and Chazal relied upon them in many Torah matters, as explained in chapter Rabbi Akiva, Shabbat 85a; or Moshe Rabbeinu received the measure at Sinai; or they relied on the verse "all in whose nostrils was the breath of life," meaning that everything depends on nasal breathing, as explained in Yoma 85a. To say that a collapsed building is different is incorrect; does the verse about the breath of life speak only of a collapsed building? Moreover, it is known to the contrary: in sudden death there is more concern that one merely appears dead because of shock or a kind of paralysis, and nevertheless once breathing has stopped we no longer desecrate Shabbat. It must therefore be a rule for all dead persons, that this is the measure received in our hands. All the winds in the world, even if they fill their hands with wind, will not move us from the place of our holy Torah. There is no proof at all from the Mishnah in Niddah. The incident in Semachot is an isolated occurrence, one of the rarest events, one in a thousand years, and it is not even a minority of a minority, like Choni HaMe'agel sleeping seventy years; it does not enter the category of concern for a minority in matters of saving life. The reason that incident was brought there is that knocking upon the dead is an Amorite practice and should have been forbidden, so it says that this is not the way of the Amorites, since there was once a case where one found his relative alive. Thus one who inspects his dead may be understood as wishing to consider the possibility that his relative may live, even though it is far and removed from reason, like a nail from the crucified, and the like. He concludes: we have only the words of the Torah and the tradition of our fathers; whoever questions this questions the Shechinah, and the prohibition of leaving the dead overnight remains in force, except in countries where the government is strict about this. See there at length.
