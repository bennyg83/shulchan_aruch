Forbidden to redeem him. And it implies the same — forbidden to feed and sustain him; and in Tur and Semak it implies he is not obligated but there is no prohibition; and Bach wrote they distinguish between sustenance and redemption — not clear; and moreover they wrote thus in name of Ra'em; and Ra'em's words are in Sefer Yere'im siman 47 and it implies there explicitly there is no distinction in this — see there. And it seemed to me at first glance that what is written here deals with one who is not an apostate to anger — meaning habitual in this — and meaning they only wrote one who transgressed a sin to anger; and below end of siman 252 Tur wrote one who apostatized even for one mitzvah such as one who eats nevelot to anger — forbidden to redeem him; the language "hamir" implies he is habitual in this; and so in Perishah — only it is strained to distinguish regarding apostate to anger between habitual or not — for what of it, end of siman 2 immediately does to anger; and so above siman 2 s.k. 16 that apostate to anger even once his law is like a gentile; and so in Rambam's words and Mechaber above siman 158 seif 2 that even if he did a transgression once to anger — behold he is an apikoros and we lower him and do not raise him. And it seemed to me to say Tur here deals with one who is not apostate to anger nor for appetite but does not care about the matter whether eating the forbidden or the permitted — in such a case there is no prohibition in the matter (and in such a case Ran distinguishes in chapter 1 of Chullin and Beit Yosef brings it above siman 2 between one who does not care and to anger — see there; and even though Rosh there wrote his law is like to anger — this is because he does not care to slaughter properly and is considered to anger regarding slaughter as stated in Beit Yosef there — unlike here — examine); and it implies thus per Ra'em's words in Sefer Yere'im there — for he wrote that Rav Ami who forbids redeeming an apostate deals with to anger who is a min; and why not establish it simply as in Talmud that an Israelite sees him abandon the permitted and eat the forbidden — rather certainly because in such a case there is no prohibition in the matter for perhaps he is not doing to anger; and what Tur wrote at the end "and also apostate for appetite" teaches a novelty that even for appetite one is not obligated to redeem him; or alternatively in the beginning it deals with one who is not habitual and does neither for appetite nor to anger, and at the end it deals with apostate for appetite who is habitual — and this resolves it; this seemed to me correct to resolve Tur's words — and meaning he did not mention to anger in the beginning; but Semak wrote to anger in the beginning — possibly it is a scribal error.
