It is forbidden to redeem him. It appears that it is likewise forbidden to feed and support him. The Tur and Semak, however, imply only that one is not obligated, but that there is no prohibition. The Bach writes that a distinction is made between support and redemption, but this is not convincing. Furthermore, they wrote this in the name of the Ra'em, whose words appear in Sefer Yere'im, siman 47, and there it is explicit that no such distinction exists. See there. At first glance, I thought that the ruling here concerns one who is not a habitual spiteful apostate, meaning that he does not regularly act this way, since the wording here speaks only of one who committed a transgression spitefully. Below, at the end of siman 252, the Tur writes that one who apostasized even regarding a single mitzvah, such as one who regularly eats neveilot spitefully, may not be redeemed. The expression 'apostasized' implies habitual conduct, as the Perishah also writes. Yet it is difficult to distinguish regarding a spiteful apostate between habitual and isolated conduct, for ultimately he acts spitefully. I likewise wrote above in siman 2, seif katan 16, that even one spiteful act gives him the status of a gentile. This also appears from the Rambam and Mechaber above in siman 158, seif 2, that even one spiteful transgression renders him an apikores, whom one lowers into danger and does not rescue. I therefore thought that the Tur here concerns one who acts neither spitefully nor from appetite, but is simply indifferent whether he eats the forbidden or the permitted. In such a case there is no prohibition. The Ran distinguishes similarly in the first chapter of Chullin, cited by the Beit Yosef above in siman 2, between one who is indifferent and one who acts spitefully. Although the Rosh there writes that his law is like one who acts spitefully, that is because he is indifferent to proper slaughter, which is deemed spiteful regarding shechitah, as stated in the Beit Yosef there, unlike the present case. Examine this. This also appears from the Ra'em in Sefer Yere'im. He explains that Rabbi Ami's ruling forbidding redemption of an apostate concerns one who acts spitefully and is therefore a heretic. Why did he not explain it in accordance with the Gemara's plain case, where a Jew sees the captive abandon permitted food and eat forbidden food? Evidently, in such a case there is no prohibition, since perhaps he does not act spitefully. The Tur's concluding phrase, 'and likewise an apostate because of appetite,' teaches the additional point that even one who acts from appetite need not be redeemed. Alternatively, the opening case concerns one who does not act habitually and acts neither from appetite nor spitefully, whereas the concluding case concerns a habitual apostate because of appetite. This explanation appeared correct to me in resolving the Tur's words, and explains why he did not mention spiteful conduct in the opening clause. The Semak, however, does mention spiteful conduct there, and perhaps this is a scribal error.
