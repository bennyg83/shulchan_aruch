Who baked his bread and slaughtered, etc. The Bach writes that this applies specifically where he prepared all the needs of his chuppah, but if he did not prepare everything, for example he baked his bread and slaughtered his animal but did not mix his wine, or the reverse, so that he necessarily still must exert himself to prepare wine, the chuppah should be postponed and he should prepare again another time also the bread and meat. His words are not compelling, for in any event, if he does not make the chuppah immediately, he will lose what he already prepared, and they will not again prepare for him what was already lost. Granted that he must exert himself to prepare wine, but how will he exert himself to prepare bread and meat? There is proof from the remazim at the beginning of Ketubot, which wrote only of one whose animal was slaughtered and wine mixed and the father of the groom died, etc., but did not mention his bread being baked. Likewise, what the Bach wrote, that the text must read that they prepared all the adornments, etc., is not necessary for the reason stated, since with adornments too there can be loss, as the Ran and the Mechaber wrote, such as women's cosmetics that will spoil. They bring the dead into a room, etc. For if they bury him, mourning will take effect upon him with the closing of the grave, and he will no longer be able to enter the chuppah until mourning has passed. Although an onen is also forbidden in marital relations, as above siman 341, nevertheless here they were lenient and permitted relations to an onen but not to a mourner. Because of the lighter status before the closing of the grave, he will not come to treat mourning lightly, for the primary seven days, namely the days of mourning that always begin from the closing of the grave onward, are stricter than the time when his dead lies before him; people do not learn from the lenient case to the stringent one. So writes the Ramban in Torat HaAdam at the beginning of page 24. And he separates. It is forbidden to have relations a second time even before the dead is buried, because an onen is forbidden in marital relations. Ramban there. And he observes the seven days of feasting, etc. Even according to the one who holds that the first day of mourning is Torah law, the reason he observes the days of feasting first is that beit din can uproot a Torah matter passively, through shev ve'al ta'aseh. According to this, it is not proper for him to occupy himself with matters of joy during that entire day; rather, he is not obligated to perform an act of mourning, which is shev ve'al ta'aseh on the first day. Thus the Ateret Zekenim, from the words of the Ran brought by the Beit Yosef. It appears that for us, since we hold below in siman 399 at the end of seif 13 that the first day is only rabbinic, he may occupy himself with matters of joy. For this reason the Mechaber and the Rav omitted these words of the Ran. The Ateret Zekenim also seems to have written them only according to the view that the first day is Torah law. Know this, for we hold that even laundering and haircutting are permitted all seven days, as the Ateret Zekenim himself also wrote, and this implies even on the first day. There is no greater active deed than that. Rather, it is certainly as I explained. And he is forbidden in marital relations. The Tur writes in the name of the Raavad that specifically relations and seclusion are forbidden, but other forms of separation, such as mixing the cup, making the bed, and washing his face, hands, and feet, are permitted; not only that, but even embracing and kissing may be permitted. So too it appears from the words of the Rosh in Perek Elu Megalchin, cited by the Tur, that he agrees with the Raavad on this, and so too appears to be the view of the Mechaber, who wrote only to forbid marital relations. However, this is astonishing, for at the beginning of Ketubot the Rosh wrote, and likewise in the remazim there, that since this mourning is light to him compared with niddah, she may not mix his cup, make his bed, or wash his face, hands, and feet. It is forced to say that in Perek Elu Megalchin he speaks specifically by day; moreover, from the language of the remazim in Perek VeElu Megalchin, siman 83, "but other separations, such as making the bed and the like, are permitted," it implies that they are always permitted. As to practical law, Rabbenu Yerucham, Netiv 22 part 2, also holds it forbidden, writing: the Raavad wrote that even though he sleeps among the men and she among the women, she is permitted in mixing the cup and making the bed; they were astonished at him, and my teacher R. Avraham ben Ismael wrote that since it is forbidden for him to sleep with her in seclusion, all the more so it is forbidden for her to mix his cup, make his bed, and wash his face, hands, and feet. So ends his language. This is also proven from the Gemara at the beginning of Ketubot 4a, for with niddah seclusion is permitted once he has had relations with her, and nevertheless mixing the cup is forbidden; all the more so with this mourning, where seclusion is forbidden, mixing should be forbidden. One cannot say that regarding niddah it is more reasonable to forbid mixing the cup, etc., while regarding mourning it is more reasonable to forbid seclusion, for if so what does the Gemara ask, "shall we say mourning is lighter to him than niddah," from the statement of R. Yitzchak bar Chanina that all labors a woman performs for her husband, etc., and regarding mourning it was taught, etc.? Perhaps the Raavad's view follows the conclusion that in this mourning, since the Rabbis were lenient with it, he may come to treat it lightly; by strict law even seclusion would have been permitted in this mourning, but the Sages forbade seclusion so that he not treat it lightly. If so, it is enough that they made a distinguishing mark between this mourning and ordinary mourning by forbidding seclusion, so that he not treat it lightly; why should we further decree and forbid the other forms of separation? I later found that Maharsha at the beginning of Ketubot left the Raavad's words requiring study. And he sleeps among the men, etc. The Ateret Zekenim writes that although another mourner may seclude himself, here it is different because she is a new wife to him, etc. He was not precise, for according to this, even if mourning of seven days occurred during the seven days of feasting, seclusion should be forbidden, and this is not so, as explained below in siman 383. The truth is that he did not examine the Gemara at the beginning of the first chapter of Ketubot, 4b, where the whole sugya proves that one should not distinguish between an old wife and a new wife. Rather, here the reason is as Rav Ashi says there and as stated in the poskim: this mourning is light to him, for his relative died before the seven days of feasting took effect, and the Sages permitted him to bring the dead into a room and the groom and bride to the chuppah in order to defer the mourning from him; there is concern lest he come to treat it lightly. He sleeps among the men, etc. The Bach writes that this speaks of a woman who was already married, but with a virgin seclusion is permitted, for although mourning is light, since he had relations she is forbidden to him because of niddah blood, which is stringent. This was explained above in siman 192 s.k. 13. And the custom is to take, etc. Although mourning for a groom who prepared the needs of his chuppah is uncommon, and custom does not apply to something uncommon, as above siman 120 s.k. 3, nevertheless, since this is the custom where his bride became niddah, as above siman 192 seif 4, the same applies here, for it has one law in this respect. A minor, etc. The Bach writes that they must know the nature of intercourse and that she does not submit herself to intercourse, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 22. He counts only from the seven days of mourning, etc. Although regarding a festival we hold below in siman 399 that it counts toward the number, there it is different, for he observed the laws of thirty days during it, unlike here, where even laundering and haircutting are permitted. Ramban and other poskim. I wrote below in siman 399 about the law of a groom to whom mourning occurred on a festival, whether the seven days of feasting count for him; see there. And all seven days of feasting he is permitted in laundering, etc. The Bach writes that it appears one should forbid laundering and haircutting, because it is not publicized that he is doing so because of mourning, for perhaps he has no laundered clothes or no barber is available to cut his hair, and these are private matters, etc. This is astonishing: how could he go so far as to disagree with the Ramban and the Tur based on mere reasoning? The Rosh and Rabbenu Yerucham, Netiv 22 part 2, agree with them, and so too is the view of the Mechaber, and so writes responsa Masat Binyamin siman 19. Rather, the reason is that a groom is like a king, "your eyes shall behold the king in his beauty"; therefore, whenever he does not cut his hair or launder, it is publicized that he does so because of mourning, for he should have exerted himself to obtain laundered garments and a barber to cut his hair, since the obligation lies upon him to launder and cut his hair.
