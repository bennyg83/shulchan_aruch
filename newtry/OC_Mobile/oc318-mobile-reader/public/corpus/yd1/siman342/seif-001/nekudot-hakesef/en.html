(Siman 342, Taz seif katan 1) Taz elaborates at length with proofs, but all his proofs are questionable and can be rejected. In particular, his difficulty from Ran at the beginning of Ketubot is no difficulty because we rule like the Sages, as stated below in siman 399, seif 13 in the gloss. More importantly, his lengthy discussion was unnecessary, because these laws are explicitly stated by Rabbenu Yerucham, Netiv 22, part 2. He writes: if the groom already entered marriage but had not yet had marital relations, and on that same day after the wedding blessings one of his relatives other than his father died, some authorities hold that he observes the seven days of wedding celebration first and afterward the seven days of mourning, comparable to one whose relative dies during a festival. Since the wedding blessings were already recited, the wedding days have taken effect. Although when his father dies the Gemara says he first has the mitzvah intercourse, implying that the wedding celebration has not taken effect until intercourse, that case is different because mourning had already taken effect before the marriage, and everything had merely been prepared. The existing wedding preparations are not strong enough to displace mourning and establish the seven wedding days before intercourse, so he first has the mitzvah intercourse and only then the wedding days begin. Here, however, where he already married before the relative died, the wedding celebration has already taken effect even without intercourse. Therefore, he does not have relations until after completing the seven wedding days and the seven mourning days. Ritz Giat and R. David Kohen rule this way. Rosh, however, wrote and ruled in practice that mourning comes first, because the celebration has not taken effect until intercourse when the deceased is not the groom's father but another relative. This is Rabbenu Yerucham's wording. Where there is no financial loss, or another person can undertake the wedding preparations, it is obvious that father and mother have the same law as other relatives, as implied by the Gemara and poskim. Rabbenu Yerucham further writes that if the deceased is the father or mother but they were not the ones able to undertake the preparations, mourning comes first even where financial loss will result. In my view, nowadays even when the groom's father or mother dies, their case has the law of other relatives, because it is common for other relatives to take responsibility for preparing the meal and the bride's ornaments. Therefore, in all cases he should observe mourning first and should not have marital relations until after the seven days of mourning. He then observes the seven days of wedding celebration. This appears correct.
