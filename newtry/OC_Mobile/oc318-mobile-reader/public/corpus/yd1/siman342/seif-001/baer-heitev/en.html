Prepared. Bach writes: but if he did not prepare everything — such as he baked his bread and slaughtered his meat but did not mix his wine, or vice versa — in that case he must now exert himself to prepare wine, and the wedding should be postponed; and he will prepare the bread and meat a second time. Shach writes that this is not conclusive, since in any case he will lose what he already prepared, etc.; see there.
The bed. Tur writes in the name of Ra'avad that only intercourse and seclusion are forbidden, but types of separation such as mixing the cup, making the bed, and washing his face, hands, and feet are permitted — and even embracing and kissing may be permitted. Shach disagrees and brings several proofs from the Talmud and poskim to refute Ra'avad's words; see there. He concludes that he found in Maharsha at the beginning of Ketubot that Ra'avad's words were left as a difficulty.
minor. Bach writes that this minor boy and minor girl must know the meaning of intercourse, and that she does not surrender herself to intercourse — as written in Even HaEzer siman 22. Taz writes that even if there is a niddah prohibition on him, no watchfulness is needed if he already had relations — for he is certainly not suspected regarding niddah. But for us who separate after the obligatory marital union as with other niddah — why did they require watchfulness? One can say: since in Talmudic times watchfulness was obligatory, we should not be lenient. (Netivot HaMishpat writes that Rosh's words were overlooked — Rosh wrote explicitly that according to the latter ruling, since they said the blood of virgins is like niddah blood, she is permitted to sleep with him without watchfulness; but here we deal with a non-virgin.) Taz further writes of an incident involving a virgin who married on Friday night and had not yet had relations; on Shabbat the bride's mother died, and he was asked what the law is regarding her mourning. Some scholars wanted to compare this case to the present ruling; he said they erred — the permission here to have relations is only because of the loss of the prepared feast; but where the wedding canopy has already taken place, there is no such loss — therefore relations are forbidden, since marital relations are forbidden even before burial. But regarding the mourning of this bride — it seems she should begin immediately from the first day and count seven days, then have relations; see there at length. Derishah writes that covering the bride's head in the morning is considered the wedding canopy, and one may rely on this — that if mourning befell the groom or bride after her head was covered, they need not interrupt the seven days of feasting; they bury the deceased immediately, and the seven days of mourning are deferred until after the wedding week. Taz disagrees and concludes that if it is mourning not for a father or mother, one should defer the wedding canopy until after the seven days of mourning, even if they already covered the bride's face with the veil. (Netivot HaMishpat writes that Rosh ruled practically that he should first observe mourning, since it is not for a father or mother but other relatives — and so R. Yeruham wrote in his name. He wrote further that it seems nowadays even for a deceased father or mother, they have the status of other relatives — since it is very common for other relatives to trouble themselves and prepare the wedding feast and jewelry for the bride; therefore in every case mourning is observed first and he does not have relations until the seven days of mourning pass, after which the seven wedding days are observed.)
and onwards. Even though regarding a festival we hold it counts toward the thirty — that is different since he observed the obligations of thirty during it; but here he is even permitted laundering and haircut. Ramban and other poskim. And the law of a groom whom mourning befell during a festival — whether the seven wedding days count for him — I wrote in siman 399; see there — end quote Shach.
and a haircut. Bach wrote it seems to forbid laundering and haircut since it is not publicized that he does so because of mourning, making it a private matter, etc. Shach objected and wrote that he exceeded his bounds in disagreeing with Ramban and Tur on mere reasoning; see there.
