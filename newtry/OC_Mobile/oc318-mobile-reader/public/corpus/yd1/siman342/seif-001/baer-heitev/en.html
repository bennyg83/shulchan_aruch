That he prepared. Bach writes that if he did not prepare everything, for example he baked bread and slaughtered meat but did not prepare wine, or the reverse, then since he must in any event make further preparations, the wedding is postponed and he can prepare the bread and meat again later. Shach writes that this is not compelling, because he will still lose whatever he already prepared.
The bed. Tur cites Ra'avad that only marital relations and seclusion are forbidden, while other forms of separation such as pouring a cup, making the bed, and washing his face, hands, and feet are permitted. Ra'avad even allows embracing and kissing. Shach disagrees and brings several proofs from the Gemara and poskim against Ra'avad. He concludes that he later found Maharsha at the beginning of Ketubot also leaving Ra'avad's position requiring study.
A minor. Bach writes that the male and female minors used as guards must be old enough to understand the nature of sexual relations but young enough not to submit themselves to such relations, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 22. Taz asks that where a niddah prohibition is present, no guard is ordinarily needed once the husband has already had relations with his wife, because he is not suspected of violating niddah. Since nowadays after the first marital relations the bride separates as a niddah, why is guarding required? He answers that because in the time of the Gemara guarding was required in this mourning case, we should not be lenient now. Nekudot HaKesef responds that Taz overlooked Rosh, who says explicitly that according to the later ruling treating hymenal blood like niddah blood, she may sleep with him without guarding. The present case therefore concerns a woman with whom he had previously had relations, and Bach writes likewise. Taz further records a case of a virgin bride married on Friday night who had not yet had relations, and on Shabbos her mother died. Some scholars wanted to compare it to the present law. Taz ruled that they erred. Here the Sages allowed relations before mourning only because otherwise the prepared wedding feast would be lost. Once the chuppah has already occurred there is no such loss, because the feast will not be prepared again. Therefore marital relations are forbidden, since they are forbidden even before burial. He rules that the bride should begin mourning immediately on Sunday, observe seven days, and only afterward have relations. Perishah writes that covering the bride's head in the morning is considered chuppah, and therefore if mourning unfortunately occurs after her head was covered, the seven days of celebration should not be interrupted; the deceased is buried and the seven days of mourning are postponed until after the wedding week. Taz disagrees and concludes that where the mourning is for a relative other than a father or mother, the chuppah should be postponed until after the seven days of mourning even if her face was already covered with the veil. Nekudot HaKesef writes that Rosh explicitly ruled in practice that mourning is observed first when the deceased is not the father or mother, and R. Yerucham reports the same in his name. He further writes that nowadays even the death of a father or mother should be treated like other relatives, because it is common that other relatives expend effort and prepare the feast and jewelry for the bride. Therefore in all cases mourning should be observed first, the marriage should not be consummated until after seven days of mourning, and afterward the seven days of celebration are observed.
And onward. Although a festival counts toward the thirty-day period, that case differs because some laws of the thirty days are observed during the festival. Here even laundering and haircutting are permitted during the wedding week. Ramban and the other poskim. Shach writes that the law of a groom who experiences mourning during a festival, and whether the seven days of celebration count for him, is discussed in siman 399.
And haircutting. Bach writes that laundering and haircutting should be forbidden because it is not publicly evident that he abstains because of mourning, and they are therefore private mourning practices. Shach rejects this and writes that Bach went too far in disputing Ramban and Tur merely on logical grounds. ..
