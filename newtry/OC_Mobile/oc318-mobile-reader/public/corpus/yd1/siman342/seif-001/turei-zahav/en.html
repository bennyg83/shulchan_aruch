They bring the corpse in. An incident occurred with a virgin who married on Friday night and had not yet had relations. On Shabbat day the bride’s mother died, and I was asked what the proper law is regarding her mourning, because some students wanted to compare this law to the case here. I said that they erred, for here they permitted intercourse for the mitzvah only because of the loss of the meal already prepared, since they would need a new expense if they postponed the chuppah until after the mourning. As the Rosh wrote in the first chapter of Ketubot, they permitted chuppah and intercourse to an onen only because of loss. Therefore it says, “they bring in, etc.,” so that the mourning not take effect upon him until the golel is sealed. But here, where the chuppah was already performed and only intercourse had not yet occurred, certainly if they delay intercourse there is no loss of a meal, for what was, was, since they will not make a second meal. Therefore intercourse is forbidden, for marital relations are forbidden even before burial, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch wrote in the preceding siman. Another proof is from the words of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch at the end of siman 383, where they wrote that if a relative died for a groom after the chuppah, he is forbidden even in seclusion if he has not yet had relations. However, it appears to me regarding this bride’s mourning that she should begin immediately on Sunday and count seven days, and afterward have relations. One should not say that since the chuppah took effect upon her, it is like a festival, and automatically she should not begin mourning until the seven days of chuppah are completed, as with one whose relative died during a festival. This is not so. The seven days of celebration were enacted by the Sages to gladden the groom and bride, and they do not want this enactment, for if she first observes the seven days of celebration while she is forbidden to her husband, she will then need to wait the seven days of mourning and be forbidden for fourteen days. Even in monetary matters we find in several places that a person can say, “I do not want this enactment,” as at the beginning of Bava Kamma and in Perek HaKotev regarding, “I will not be supported and I will not work.” All the more so in a matter of fulfilling a mitzvah, where one should advance it as much as possible. Another proof is from Perek Shiluach HaKen 141, where we require the extra word “send” to include the birds of a metzora, so that you should not say that the positive commandment of the metzora’s purification comes and overrides the positive commandment of sending away, because the positive commandment of the metzora’s purification comes to permit marital relations. The extra “send” teaches that it does not override. We learn from this that because of permitting marital relations I would have said that it overrides the opposing positive commandment, were it not for the extra verse. All the more so for mitzvah-intercourse, that one should set aside for it a rabbinic matter, namely the seven days of celebration. Therefore I ruled that she should begin counting the seven days of mourning immediately after Shabbat, and afterward have the mitzvah-intercourse. So it appears to me correct and clear.
After the above matters I saw in Sefer Derishah in this siman, regarding what the Tur wrote, “since the chuppah took effect upon him, it is like a festival for him.” He wrote as follows: according to what I wrote below in siman 361, and brought proof from the Gemara and poskim that covering the bride’s head in the morning is called chuppah, it also appears to me that one may rely on this: if, Heaven forbid, mourning occurred to the groom or bride after the bride’s head was covered, they need not interrupt the seven days of celebration, but they bury the dead immediately and the seven days of mourning are deferred until after the seven days of celebration, as in the law here where the groom’s father died. Perhaps one can distinguish, for specifically in their days, when they performed kiddushin earlier at the time of the betrothal, therefore from the beginning of the chuppah in the morning seclusion was relevant after she had already been betrothed; unlike in our time. However, it appears that even in the days of the Mordechai they did not perform kiddushin except as in our time, and even so he calls it chuppah from the time of covering the bride; end quote. The master’s words greatly astonish me. First, in considering the covering with the veil as chuppah: the Rama wrote in Even HaEzer siman 54 that our custom is to call chuppah specifically the sheets over poles, etc., which is the chuppah practiced now, etc. Many times we see that the groom’s dowry arrangement is removed after the covering with the veil; if it is not arranged properly, the groom goes on his way, and there is still no obligation upon him to marry her because of the veil-covering. How then can we say leniently that his festival has already taken effect from the time of covering, in a place where there is no loss, namely where he is mourning not for his father? For if it is for his father, the author of the Derishah did not need to seek permission because the chuppah had already taken effect, since in that case even before chuppah they permitted performing the chuppah and even having mitzvah-intercourse as long as the dead had not been buried. Apart from this, there is a great difficulty from what the Rosh wrote in the first chapter of Ketubot. He first copied the Rif’s words, who wrote: we learn from this that where there is no loss, he does not observe the seven days of celebration first. The Rosh wrote that from this language it implies that even where there is no loss, they bring the corpse into the room and the groom and bride to the chuppah, he has mitzvah-intercourse and separates, buries his dead, observes the seven days of mourning, and afterward the seven days of celebration. According to this, there is no difference between where there is loss and where there is no loss except regarding observing the seven days of mourning first; but in both cases he first has mitzvah-intercourse. The Rosh says this is not logical at all: what benefit is there in hastening the chuppah, degrading the dead, and interrupting mourning between the chuppah and the seven days of celebration? They permitted chuppah and intercourse to an onen only when it was the groom’s father or the bride’s mother and there was loss; then they permitted chuppah to an onen and advancing the days of celebration before mourning. But where mourning precedes celebration even after chuppah, etc.; end quote. It is explicit that according to the Rif, where he permissibly brings her into complete chuppah and has full relations, nevertheless his festival does not take effect upon him to make days of celebration, but he must wait and begin mourning first. Even the Rosh, who disagrees with him, does so because one should not interrupt mourning between chuppah and the days of celebration. It never entered his mind that he should immediately observe the seven days of celebration because his festival had taken effect upon him by entering chuppah. This is because of the reason explicit in the Gemara: they permitted performing the days of celebration only where there is loss, namely where the groom’s father or bride’s mother died; but without loss, it is forbidden to perform the days of celebration at all. This is simple: they permitted performing the days of celebration here only in a place of loss, namely where the groom’s father died. Then they permitted two things: mitzvah-intercourse while he is an onen, and deferring mourning after burial until after the days of celebration; but not for another mourning. Still more difficult than this is what the Ran wrote in the first chapter of Ketubot: although the chuppah took effect upon him and it is like a festival, nevertheless they were lenient only in passive non-observance of mourning, but he is forbidden to involve himself in matters of joy throughout that day of death and burial, according to the view that the first day is Torah law. If so, how can one perform the chuppah on the day he became a mourner because they covered the bride on the day he became a mourner? There is no greater act of joy than this. One cannot say that we may rely on the view that there is no Torah-level mourning at all, as stated in siman 398, for he did not decide the matter, and here it is not relevant to say that the halachah follows the lenient view in mourning. The Beit Yosef in siman 399 ruled like the view that it is Torah law. Furthermore, the Ran already wrote in a responsum, brought by the Beit Yosef in siman 228, p. 268, that in every matter where we are uncertain whether it is Torah law or rabbinic, it is called a Torah doubt and we are stringent. Furthermore, an onen on the first day is Torah law even according to the Rosh, as the Tur wrote in siman 391, and in Perek Tevul Yom, Zevachim 100, we say that even after burial he remains an onen on the day of death until evening regarding eating kodashim. For this reason the Rosh wrote in Perek Elu Megalchin that Rabban Gamliel did not want to wash on the day of death, although he was delicate, since the day of death is Torah law. Therefore it appears to my humble opinion that in every mourning that is not for the groom’s father or the bride’s mother, one should defer performing the chuppah until after the seven days of mourning, even if the bride has already been covered with the veil. They will thereby gain advancing mitzvah-intercourse by seven days, as I wrote above. So it appears to me correct.
The corpse into the room. For as long as it has not been buried he is an onen, and they permitted the prohibition of relations for an onen in this case because of his loss, since he has no father to prepare another meal. But after the dead is buried, mourning begins, and they did not permit him here the prohibition of mourning; rather, they deferred mourning until after the days of celebration, all because of loss.
And he has mitzvah-intercourse and separates. Although with every mitzvah-intercourse one separates nowadays, nevertheless here it speaks in the time of the Talmud, when they did not separate after mitzvah-intercourse. Even so, here, because of mourning, he must separate. Also what the Tur wrote according to the Raavad’s view, that embracing and kissing are permitted, likewise speaks of the time of the Talmud. With this it is also resolved what one might ask about what he wrote, that the groom needs guarding after he has had mitzvah-intercourse. Did they not say in the Gemara that specifically regarding the prohibition of mourning they were stringent here that he needs guarding even after intercourse, lest he err and be lenient further, since the Sages were lenient for him regarding the permission of relations while an onen? But regarding a niddah prohibition, he does not need guarding if he already had relations, for he certainly is not suspected regarding niddah. If so, for us, who separate after mitzvah-intercourse as with other women, why did he require guarding in our time? One may say that since in the time of the Talmud there was an obligation of guarding, we have no power to be lenient in this, only to be stringent. So it appears to me.
By day as by night. In the Tur it is written in the name of the Rosh that through sight the yetzer grows stronger. Many ask from what we say regarding the night of Yom Kippur, that some have the custom to light a candle so that he not have relations with his wife. This is an error, for there we are concerned only lest he forget and have relations; therefore a candle serves as a reminder. This is unlike here, where we are concerned lest he intentionally transgress, and the sight of his eyes will cause this.
And all seven days of the chuppah he is permitted in pressing clothes, etc. This gives the reason for what he said earlier, that he counts the thirty only after the festival. For with another festival, one counts the thirty from the festival days, since he is forbidden even on the festival in pressing clothes and haircutting because of mourning. Here, however, he is permitted in pressing clothes and haircutting, as it is written, “Your eyes shall behold the king in his beauty.”
