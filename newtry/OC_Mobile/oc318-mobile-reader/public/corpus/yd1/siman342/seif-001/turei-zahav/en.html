They bring the deceased inside. A case occurred where a virgin bride was married on Friday night but had not yet had relations. On Shabbos day her mother died. Some students wished to compare her law to the case here. I ruled they were mistaken. The permission in this siman to perform the initial marital act before mourning takes effect exists only because of substantial financial loss from the wedding feast already prepared, as Rosh explains in the first chapter of Ketubot. The wedding and marital act are accelerated so that mourning does not begin until after burial. Here, however, the chuppah had already taken place. Delaying the marital act creates no new loss from the meal, because what was already spent is already spent. Therefore, marital relations are prohibited, since they are forbidden even during aninut before burial. Tur and Shulchan Aruch at the end of siman 383 also state that if a relative dies after the chuppah and the marriage has not yet been consummated, even yichud is prohibited. As for the bride's mourning, she should begin immediately on Sunday, count seven days, and only afterward have the initial marital relations. One should not say that because chuppah already took effect it is like a festival for her and therefore mourning should wait until after the seven wedding days. The seven days of celebration were instituted for the joy of bride and groom. They can say they do not want that rabbinic benefit if it would cause them to remain apart for fourteen days, first seven days of celebration and then seven days of mourning. Even in monetary law a person can sometimes decline a rabbinic enactment that was made for his benefit. All the more so here, where an actual mitzvah is advanced by beginning mourning immediately. There is also support from Chullin regarding purification of a metzora, where one might have thought an affirmative mitzvah could override another because it permits marital relations. If that possibility required a special verse to reject it, then certainly a rabbinic seven-day celebration need not be allowed to delay the mitzvah aspect of marital life. I therefore ruled that she should begin mourning immediately after Shabbos and, after seven days, consummate the marriage.
Afterward I saw Derishah in this siman. He writes that because covering the bride's head in the morning is itself considered chuppah, if mourning occurs afterward, the seven days of celebration need not stop; the deceased is buried and mourning waits until after those seven days. I find this very difficult. Rama in Even HaEzer siman 54 identifies the accepted chuppah as the canopy on poles, not the earlier covering of the bride. Indeed, we often see the groom withdraw from the wedding even after the bride's head has been covered if the dowry arrangements are not completed, which shows the legal chuppah has not yet taken effect. Furthermore, Rosh in Ketubot explicitly discusses a case where actual chuppah and even marital relations may occur before burial, yet where there is no financial loss the seven wedding days do not take precedence over mourning. The permission to celebrate first exists only in the special case of the groom's father or bride's mother, where there is a financial loss. In other cases, even if chuppah already occurred, mourning takes precedence. Ran likewise writes that even where chuppah has the force of a festival, it displaces only passive mourning practices. Active conduct of joy on the day of death and burial is still prohibited according to the view that the first day has Torah force. For all these reasons, where the deceased is not the groom's father or bride's mother, the chuppah should be postponed until after shivah, even if the bride's head covering already took place. This appears correct.

By day as by night. Rosh explains that the sight of one another causes the yetzer hara to intensify. Some ask from the custom on Yom Kippur night of lighting a lamp so a husband will not have relations with his wife. There is no difficulty. On Yom Kippur the concern is only forgetfulness, so the lamp serves as a reminder. Here the concern is deliberate transgression caused by seeing one another, so visible presence itself heightens the danger.
