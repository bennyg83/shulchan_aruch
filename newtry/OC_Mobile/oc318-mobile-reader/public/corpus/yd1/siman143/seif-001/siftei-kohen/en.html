One whose house was adjacent, etc. The words of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch imply that even if the house itself is not worshipped, it is forbidden to build a wall for the house because he thereby benefits the idolatry. This is likewise the plain meaning of the Mishnah and is also proven from Rashi at the beginning of Avodah Zarah, daf 16a. On the Mishnah there, which states that one may build with them bathhouses, Rashi explains: 'Bathhouses are buildings not themselves for idolatry, while the sacrifices offered there are only an appurtenance of an appurtenance of idolatry.' The Tur cites this below. This implies that an actual appurtenance of idolatry is forbidden. Thus a house in which idolatry is placed is an actual appurtenance of idolatry and is forbidden. This is why a dome in which idolatry is set up is forbidden, because it is an appurtenance of idolatry, as stated in the Gemara. The Bach writes that the Mishnah in the chapter Kol HaTzelamim, 'one whose house was adjacent to idolatry,' appears to mean that if the house itself were not worshipped but merely contained idolatry, building its wall would not be considered benefiting idolatry. He further argues that according to Rashi one may build a palace in which the non-Jew will place a dome containing idolatry. End of his words. I do not understand his reasoning. On the contrary, that is a proof against him, because Rashi permits specifically a palace, which is only an appurtenance of an appurtenance. Otherwise it would be forbidden. Why then should it be permitted to build a wall for a house in which idolatry is placed? As for Rashi's statement that the house itself is worshipped, his intent is not as the Bach understood. Rather, it addresses the latter clause of the Mishnah: 'its stones, wood, and dust convey impurity like a sheretz; R' Akiva says like a niddah.' If the house itself were not worshipped but merely served as an appurtenance of idolatry, R' Akiva would not say that it is like a niddah. So the Gemara explicitly states at the beginning of the chapter R' Akiva: according to R' Akiva, for what law is it compared to a sheretz? For its appurtenances, which do not convey impurity by carrying. See there. This makes my explanation clear. The Prisha explains Rashi differently, but Rashi's wording does not support his explanation. The words of Tosafos, 'and so Rashi explained,' refer to the fact that by saying the house itself is worshipped, Rashi thereby resolves their question. What I have written is clear.
He enters within his own. Four cubits — so it is in the Mishnah, Tur, and poskim.
His portion is permitted for benefit. And his portion is counted for him — if the wall’s thickness is two cubits, he counts one cubit as his and enters a further three cubits within his own — Rashi and the Ran.
