And it is forbidden to benefit from them with favor. Rashi explains that this means when one pays them a fee. According to this, it is forbidden to grind or bake in a mill or oven belonging to an idol. Rabbeinu Tam explains that “with favor” means that it is forbidden when one expresses gratitude to them, but it is permitted when one does not express gratitude, even though one pays them a fee. So wrote the Tur. He then wrote that this is not comparable to a fair of non-Jews, where it is forbidden to pay a tax to idol worship. There, the tax is used for the idol itself and its adornments, whereas when it is merely for the lawfully imposed support of the priests, it is permitted. This refers to Rabbeinu Tam's explanation, which permits payment here because the money does not go to the idol itself. At first glance, one may ask why the Tur found the tax case difficult. There the idol gains substantial benefit from the Jew, who gives it money without receiving anything in return, whereas here, when the Jew purchases something from him, the non-Jew could have sold it to another non-Jew as well. One can answer that even without this, the distinction is proven from the ruling in seif 5 that it is forbidden to rent shops belonging to an idol. This is difficult according to Rabbeinu Tam. Necessarily, one must distinguish that there the rent is paid to the idol itself. The Tur therefore gives the same answer regarding the tax, since both laws are stated in the Yerushalmi. The Maharshal wrote that “without favor” means that everyone benefits from it without favor. If they waive payment specifically for one individual, it is forbidden, for on the contrary, he benefits from the non-Jew who grants him that benefit, as stated below in siman 149 regarding a tax. End quote. The Rambam wrote that payment is even worse. It appears that this is the ruling we follow. Whenever benefit with favor is forbidden, it is forbidden both through payment and through expressing gratitude. The Derishah asked how this differs from wax candles in siman 139, where if they were extinguished and the priests sell them, they are nullified, even though one thereby benefits the priests. He answered that the candle was originally permitted and was later made into an adornment for an idol, so it becomes permitted when the adornment is nullified. A garden or bathhouse, however, was set aside for this purpose from the outset. I do not understand his words, for the candle too was set aside for the idol from the outset. In truth, there is no question at all. The prohibition applies only where they established a fixed entitlement for their priests that it should always belong to them. A candle is different, for on the contrary, their law requires that it belong entirely to the idol, and the priest merely sells it incidentally. This is like selling other movable items that have no association with the idol at all, and it is permitted.
But only to its worshippers. This means the common people who worship that idol.
