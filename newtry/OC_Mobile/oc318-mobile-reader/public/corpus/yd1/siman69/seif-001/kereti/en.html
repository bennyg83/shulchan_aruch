To rinse, etc.: whether blood that was salted or cooked is forbidden d'oraisa or d'rabbanan — the early ones disputed, and the later ones agreed it is d'rabbanan; and see Peleti what he wrote at length resolving Rashi's words in everything they challenged against him — and there is no need for Peri Chadash's forced reading.
First, etc.: two reasons are stated among the authors — either because the flesh must be through water softened and the power of salt rules over it to expel the blood, and if so it suffices when he rinses every rib of flesh and every bird together and afterward cuts it into many pieces, for behold it is softened and standing; and the second reason is because through the pieces of the knife, through pressure of cutting, the blood is squeezed and floats on the piece and is like visible blood, and the salt absorbs the blood instead of expelling it.
The butcher rinsed — meaning proper rinsing to rub every limb and limb; but not like the simple-minded notion to wash in a little water without intention to remove blood — and blood smeared on its surface nevertheless; and the butcher must also rinse it after he cuts, for if he cut afterward the first rinse does not help — and it is simple.
Cut — Shach's view is specifically cutting with a knife, for knife pressure expels — unlike if he cut by hand into two, which is not needed; and Peri Chadash is stringent — and see Peleti; one should not be stringent.
Or if he removed, etc. — but if he did not remove at all, everything is considered one thick piece and permitted. Shach.
A little and not much, so the flesh be moist and the salt melt and spread well into the flesh to act. Orchot Chaim.
And also the vessel is forbidden — meaning to use it hot.
