To rinse. Shach wrote in the name of Ran: the reason is so the meat softens and its blood exits through melicha; and Mordechai wrote the reason that the first rinsing is to remove the filth on the piece, for otherwise the salt becomes full from it and the salt no longer expels the blood in the piece — and for these two reasons it helps if he returns and rinses and salts it again even after time; but Hagahot Maimoniyot wrote in the name of Semak: the reason is because salt absorbs and does not expel visible blood, and it is not applicable to say m'sharek sharek nor kevul'o kach polto; therefore we also do not say iydei d'tarid lefalot lo bala; and Ra'ah wrote: because of visible blood that is on the meat, for salt does not expel except absorbed blood that is moist, but not what dried on its surface — and if he does not rinse it first there is concern that perhaps after he leaves it to expel blood and gravy it will melt and absorb it. And the two views in seif 2, if he salted and did not rinse first — they disagree in these reasons; see Taz (and see likewise the ramifications in these reasons); and see above end of seif 1:
The butcher. Shach wrote: it does not help even b'dieved what butchers rinse whole ribs in the slaughterhouse as they are accustomed to pour on them in mere passing to beautify them, for this is not called 'the butcher rinsed it' except when he soaked it somewhat in the manner of rinsing meat when one wishes to salt it; however, if we know the butcher rinsed it we say stam he rinsed it well:
To return. Shach wrote: even b'dieved one must forbid if he did not rinse, for through pressure of the knife it expels (and Peri Chadash wrote: even if he only cut by hand he must return and rinse); and it deals specifically when he removed the hooves of the legs, but if he did not remove them everything is considered as one piece of meat and rinsing them as they are suffices, since there is no visible blood:
Well. Shach wrote: specifically if it was not yet salted, but if it was salted even slight rinsing suffices for him b'dieved:
To use. Shach wrote: and b'dieved if he used it, even hot, it is permitted if he rinsed it before:
Forbidden. For pickled is like cooked; but if only rinse-water remained in it there is no prohibition since blood is mixed in the water; however, if there are not sixty against the blood one may not permit except through hag'alah (by fire):
And to soak. And the reason is in Shach in the name of Orchot Chaim: for one does not soak a second piece of meat overnight in it and it does not absorb — and it implies it is forbidden to soak in it overnight meat that was already salted and rinsed properly, because it becomes pickled in a forbidden vessel — and this is simple; however b'dieved it is permitted, for it only becomes pickled after overnight and then it is nosen taam lifgam (and Peri Chadash disagrees); see above siman 105 and beginning of siman 91:
Frozen. Maharshal wrote: meat that froze — it is forbidden to salt it until he softens it as it was initially; and if it was salted and afterward froze before it remained the time of melicha, if it thawed afterward it is good to salt it a second time and leave it the time of melicha; and if he did not salt it a second time and left the time of melicha after thawing and it was cooked — permitted b'dieved (and if not — forbidden, for the time of freezing does not combine. Maharshal. And Peri Chadash wrote: but if it waited a while from softening before the beginning of freezing, even though it did not remain after thawing the time of melicha, since the time of melicha combined in the first and second thawing even though it froze in between they combine. See there. Mateh Yehonatan wrote in his book siman 14: meat that he soaked in water and the meat froze immediately with the water and became ice and remained with the ice several days without melicha — he raised that if it remained three days within the ice one must forbid the meat for cooking in any case: if we consider the ice as water, then it is like meat that remained in water overnight that absorbs blood; and if we do not consider ice as water, then it is like meat that remained three days without rinsing — but for roasting it is permitted in every case. See there that he proved ice is not considered as water. Bach wrote: what they are accustomed to soak in a vessel called 'fendil' or 'kesli' — they do not act well unless they afterward wish to kasher it. And Peri Chadash s.k. 8 wrote that one should not nullify the custom, and likewise in chiddushei Rashba: any vessel whose way is for a person to rinse it before use — we do not refrain from using it for cold forbidden food and afterward rinsing and using it for permitted food, and we are not concerned for negligence.
