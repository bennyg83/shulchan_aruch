Nine letters. Three times the word asher, as the Rambam wrote. The Beit Yosef writes that perhaps it is not invalid when he left less than asher asher asher, provided there is still the measure of nine small letters; but when there is not even nine small letters, perhaps it is also invalid, for they received that any leftover space less than nine letters is not considered a leftover space. Nevertheless, some poskim hold that a space of three letters suffices; see what the Derishah wrote on this. He should not depart from the Rambam's words in this, etc. This is the Rambam's language at the beginning of chapter 8 of Hilchot Sefer Torah: an open parashah has two forms. If he finished in the middle of the line, he leaves the rest of the line empty and begins the parashah that is open at the beginning of the second line. When does this apply? When there remains on the line a space the size of nine letters. But if only a little remains, or if he finished at the end of the line, he leaves the second line without writing and begins the open parashah at the beginning of the third line. A closed parashah has three forms. If he finished in the middle of the line, he leaves a space of the proper measure and begins writing at the end of the line one word from the beginning of the parashah that is closed, so that the space appears in the middle. If there is not enough left on the line to leave the required space and write at the end of the line one word, he leaves the whole remainder empty, leaves a little space at the start of the next line, and begins writing the closed parashah from the middle of the second line. If he finished at the end of the line, he leaves at the beginning of the second line the measure of the space and begins writing the closed parashah from the middle of the line. Thus you find that an open parashah always begins at the beginning of the line, and a closed parashah always begins from the middle of the line. Thus his language. The Ateret Zekenim writes that regarding the second form the Rambam wrote for an open parashah, "when there remains on the line a space," etc., it appears to him that the same applies if this occurred on the bottom line at the end of a column, where only a little remains or he finished at the end of the line: he leaves the top line at the beginning of the next column blank and begins writing on the second line, so that one empty line is left between them. According to the Rosh, this is closed. See further in Tur and Beit Yosef regarding the view of the Rosh and other poskim as to what is open or closed according to them, and see the Rambam, chapter 8 of Hilchot Sefer Torah, who elaborated in explaining the closed and open parashiyot. The Tur and other poskim also discuss another matter, the ordered parashah, but I did not see fit to elaborate on it, because the practice is already known among scribes, and they know and learn properly what to do. See responsa Maharshal siman 37.
Nevertheless, etc., because there are great authorities, etc. Whether a change is found in the forms of the open or closed parashiyot, or whether a change is found in what the Rambam counted as needing to be open or closed and it is not a parashah at all, in every case one should not invalidate bedieved. Since in some places great authorities have other views on this, even in a place where no one is found to disagree with the Rambam one may say that that Torah scroll was written according to some great authority who disagrees with the Rambam. So too it is implied in the Ateret Zekenim, who wrote: nevertheless, if a Torah scroll is found not written according to the Rambam's words, one should not invalidate it, for several great poskim have other views in explaining open and closed parashiyot, and we presume that scribe wrote according to one of the great poskim. So too it is implied in responsa Mahari Mintz siman 8, who wrote there that since we find a dispute regarding one of them, one may say the same regarding the others. Therefore it appears to me that no Torah scroll should be invalidated if the Rambam's order was changed. Nevertheless, it implies that this is specifically when a Torah scroll is found not written according to the Rambam's words, for one can attribute it and say it was written according to some other great authority. But if it is known that it was a scribal error, that he erred and changed from the Rambam's order in a place where no posek disagrees with him, it appears that it is invalid even bedieved. This is also precise in the language of the Rav and the Ateret Zekenim, who wrote, "nevertheless, if a Torah scroll is found not written," and did not write simply that bedieved it is not invalid if he changed from the Rambam's order. Rather, it is certainly as I explained.
A responsum that I sent to our teacher R. Eliezer, may his Rock preserve him.
Concerning the Torah scroll in which, in Parashat Tzav, the verse "Vayedaber Hashem" of "kol chelev" ought to be open, as it is in all Torah scrolls, but it is not open, and you wished to invalidate it: the matter is clear that an exacting scribe wrote it according to the Rambam. The Rambam counted all the open and closed parashiyot of Vayikra and wrote there: "Vayedaber" of "zeh korban," "Vayedaber" of "daber el Aharon," "vezot torat ha-asham," "vezot torat zevach," "Vayedaber" of "daber el Bnei Yisrael hamakriv," and "Vayedaber" of "kach et Aharon" are all open, and they are six. Thus his language. He did not count this "Vayedaber" of "kol chelev," which shows that it is not open. Although the Hagahot Maimoniyot wrote that they checked all good books and found both open, and he thinks there is a scribal error here, even though the count fits from every side, perhaps it too was written according to the error, nevertheless it is certain that in truth there is no scribal error in the Rambam's words. For he wrote "and they are six"; if so, he should have said "and they are seven." Further, he wrote at the end that the number of all open parashiyot is fifty-two, and according to him it should have said fifty-three. Likewise, when he wrote that the closed ones are forty-six, total ninety-eight, it should have said total ninety-nine. It is a great strain to emend all these places. The Kesef Mishneh, who was a great later authority in his generation, also challenged the Hagahot Maimoniyot on this and wrote that it is an error and there is no parashah there at all. In Sefer Maaseh Rokeach he also copied the Rambam's language as I wrote. I also checked all the old and new Chumashim, and in all of them there is no open parashah there. So too in the Amsterdam printed tikkunim of closed and open parashiyot according to the Rambam, there is no open parashah there, and likewise in Chumashim with the Masorah and in many books. In truth I checked many Torah scrolls, and in all of them an open parashah was found there, but without doubt they followed the Hagahot Maimoniyot, while this scribe followed the truth in the Rambam's words. If my strength were sufficient and I had the power to cancel the scribes' practice, I would correct all the Torah scrolls that have the open parashah there. But since the scribes have already practiced this way and adopted the words of the Hagahot Maimoniyot, who also testified that they had checked earlier in many Torah scrolls and found them open, one should not invalidate them. Certainly, however, this Torah scroll of yours is valid, upright, and clear: the Torah of Hashem is pure.
