It is permitted, etc.; and likewise, etc. The Tosefta in the first chapter of Kilayim states: One may sow ordinary seed together with the seeds of trees. One who sows grape seeds together with wheat incurs forty lashes. The Yerushalmi in the first chapter of Peah likewise teaches: One may sow ordinary seeds and tree seeds together, but one who sows grape seeds with wheat incurs forty lashes. Rabbi Zeira said: Scripture states, “You shall not sow your vineyard with kilayim,” meaning that the principal species of your vineyard may not be sown together with kilayim. Rabbi Yudan of Cappadocia asked Rabbi Yosi: Elsewhere we say that tree seeds are called “seeds,” yet here you say that tree seeds are not called “seeds.” He answered that there Scripture limits the term because people do not ordinarily call them seeds, whereas here Scripture includes them through the phrase, “every seed that is sown,” meaning with regard to susceptibility to tumah. The Gemara in Bechoros 54a states similarly: Just as wine and oil, etc.; wine and grain, etc. As explained above, wheat and grape seeds are liable as kilayei hakerem, in accordance with Rabbi Yonasan and not Rabbi Yoshiyah. This is also the meaning of the Yerushalmi cited above, “the principal species of your vineyard,” which follows Rabbi Yonasan. The Yerushalmi in the eighth chapter of Kilayim states according to Rabbi Yonasan: Scripture says, “You shall not sow your field with kilayim.” For what purpose, then, does it say, “You shall not sow your vineyard with kilayim,” etc.? It is as Rabbi Zeira said: “You shall not sow your vineyard with kilayim,” meaning that the principal species of the vineyard may not be sown together with kilayim. Rabbi Yudan of Cappadocia asked Rabbi Yosi, etc., as above. The expression “the principal species of your vineyard” does not follow Rabbi Yoshiyah, who explains the verse as referring to two kinds of grain sown with the vineyard. Rather, it means the principal species of the vineyard itself, since all other trees may be planted together with seeds, as explained above. This was the basis of the Yerushalmi's question. See the Behag. The Beis Yosef also wrote this, but retracted in Bedek HaBayis. That retraction is mistaken, for the Yerushalmi states the opposite, namely, that such a combination is kilayim. See there. The Rash cites the Yerushalmi in his commentary to the second Mishnah of the first chapter, and again at the end of the seventh Mishnah, where the Yerushalmi asks whether Rabbi Yosi's position is inconsistent. There is no difficulty, however, because any two distinct species may be termed kilayim even when their combination is permitted. Thus, “its essence is kilayim” means merely that it consists of two species. The question at the seventh Mishnah means as follows: Rabbi Yosi permits grafting a vegetable onto a tree because they do not unite with one another, yet there he rules that one removes it, etc., because they do unite there, etc. The Tur writes: It is permitted to mix tree seeds with field seeds and sow them together, for the Torah prohibited in Chutz LaAretz only grafting, etc. The Beis Yosef understood from his words that this would be forbidden in Eretz Yisrael. Heaven forbid that such an error be attributed to the Tur. The Gemara in Bechoros cited above must refer to Eretz Yisrael, since it discusses wine and oil as kilayim, which applies specifically in Eretz Yisrael, as stated in Kiddushin there and as explained in siman 296. Rather, the Tur's text contains a scribal error and should read, “and the Torah did not prohibit,” etc. This begins a separate statement. This is also the view of the Rosh.
