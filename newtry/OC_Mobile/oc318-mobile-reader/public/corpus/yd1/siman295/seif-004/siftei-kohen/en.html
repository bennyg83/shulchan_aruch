And it is permitted to sow beside it. Grafting applies only when one sows directly upon the vine, because the roots of the seeds descend beneath them and enter the vine. When sown at the sides, the roots do not spread laterally. One might ask that even if the vegetable roots do not spread laterally, the vine's roots do, and the vegetable roots may enter them. This is not a difficulty, because the vine's roots likewise do not spread sideways until they have descended three handbreadths, and at that depth the roots of the seeds can no longer enter them. This is the Levush's explanation, based on the Ran as cited by the Beit Yosef. It is surprising, however, because the Ran holds that grafting is also prohibited upon exposed tree roots, whereas the Mechaber rules in seif 5 that grafting does not apply there because the name ‘tree’ no longer applies to the root. According to the Mechaber, even if the vegetable roots enter the tree roots it should be permitted. The Levush himself cites the Mechaber in seif 5, so his statements are inconsistent. The Ma'adanei Yom Tov likewise indicates that the Ran and the Rosh disagree. As a practical matter, it appears proper initially to be stringent like the Ran. Tosafot at the end of the first chapter of Kiddushin, cited by the Beit Yosef, likewise wrote that one should avoid sowing seeds within three handbreadths of the base of a tree because it appears as though the seeds are being grafted into the tree. This implies that where one actually sows upon the roots and it is known that the roots enter them, it is prohibited by law.
