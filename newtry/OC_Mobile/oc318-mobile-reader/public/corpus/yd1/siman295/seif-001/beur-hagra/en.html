And likewise, one who grafts, etc. This is Rambam's language. He omitted grafting one vegetable onto another because the Gemara in Chullin 60a raises the question of one who grafted two species of grasses, etc. Rambam understood that the question applies even in Eretz Yisrael. As for the Mishnah in the first chapter, which states, “One may not graft,” etc., he understood this as a rabbinic prohibition alone. [From here he derived that one who grafts a tree onto a vegetable, or a vegetable onto a tree, incurs lashes.] See Tosafos there, s.v. “One who grafted,” etc. The Rash explains all this in his commentary to the Mishnah there. The Rosh, in Hilchos Kilayim, follows the first answer, that the question concerns Chutz LaAretz. It appears, however, that the final answer is preferable, namely, that the question concerns the descendants of Noach. This is also stated in the Yerushalmi, in the first chapter of Kilayim: It was taught in the name of Rabbi Elazar that a gentile may wear and sow kilayim, but may not crossbreed his animals or graft his trees with unlike species. Why? Because the expression “according to their kinds” is written regarding them. Yet “according to their kinds” is also written regarding grasses. There, however, it is not stated as a command, but only in the account of their emergence. This is also the view of the Tur, who writes without qualification that the prohibition applies even to grafting one vegetable onto another. See there.
In every place. With regard to grafting, there is no distinction between Eretz Yisrael and Chutz LaAretz, as stated in Kiddushin there: “That expression excludes seeds in Chutz LaAretz.” Thus, only seeds were excluded in Chutz LaAretz, but not grafting.
