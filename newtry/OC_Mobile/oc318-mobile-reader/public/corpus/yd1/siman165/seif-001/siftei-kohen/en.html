And if they did not depreciate because of the addition. Even if they depreciated because of the market rate, such as where most of the produce became available and prices fell, he does not deduct anything. Gemara.

Regarding non-Jewish artisans. One asks two of them separately, each speaking incidentally, just as we do regarding Persian documents. Sefer HaTerumah. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 68, seif 2.
And if the king decreed, etc. The Rama writes at the end of Choshen Mishpat, siman 74: "If the king enacted how debtors are to make payment, dina d'malchusa dina, and payment is made in accordance with his enactment. Some disagree. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 369, regarding dina d'malchusa dina." In the Sema there, the opinion of "some disagree" is cited from Responsa Rosh, kelal 103, siman 1 or 2, and Rivash, siman 196, and so too in Darkei Moshe there. This requires study, because no such ruling is found in that responsum of the Rosh, while Rivash there explicitly writes the opposite. He states that although, had the king not expressly commanded how debts were to be paid, the law would have been to pay according to the ordinary halachic rules already explained, once the king expressly commanded concerning repayment of debts that the new currency be accepted at the former value, and he did not enact this merely for one individual but as a rule throughout his kingdom, this is dina d'malchusa. It is possible that for this reason the Rama did not bring the dissenting view here, or because in Choshen Mishpat, siman 369, seif 8, he rules primarily like those who hold that dina d'malchusa dina applies in every matter. Accordingly, regarding currency we rule that dina d'malchusa dina, and this is the principal halacha. Moreover, it appears clear to me that even those who hold that dina d'malchusa applies only to taxes, customs, and matters connected to land agree that matters of currency are comparable to taxes and customs. This is explicit in Sefer HaTerumah, Gate 46, part 1. After explaining the laws discussed by the Tur and the Mechaber in this siman, he writes that if the king decreed throughout his kingdom that every creditor must accept repayment in the second currency newly issued, even though its silver content was reduced, or conversely must accept the second currency even though its value was increased substantially, there is certainly an opinion that dina d'malchusa dina applies. He then records a tradition from his teachers, received from the French authorities, that dina d'malchusa was said only regarding matters that are the king's own affairs, such as roads, customs duties, and land taxes. He asked Ramban about this. Ramban answered that although he was aware of that tradition from some earlier authorities, he did not accept its application to currency, because there is no royal matter greater than currency. Kings control the coinage, issue it in their land, and their gain and fiscal rights are bound up with it. The law of currency is therefore fully comparable to land subject to royal tax, regarding which the king's decree is binding. Ramban further writes that after examining the underlying principle, that restrictive view itself does not withstand the Talmudic evidence. Sefer HaTerumah concludes that the law of the kingdom is valid in this matter and the king's expropriation is effective. I later found that Responsa R' Aharon Sasson, siman 153, cites these words of Sefer HaTerumah and likewise clearly rules that dina d'malchusa is applied to currency matters. He discusses this at length, though this is not the place to elaborate. I have extended the discussion only because I saw that Maharshal, at the end of siman 13 in the chapter HaGozel Etzim, writes that Mordechai cites Rabbeinu Tam and Rashbam concerning the application of dina d'malchusa to a currency that was invalidated, but since this ruling was not brought by Tosafos or other major authorities, it appears those words were rejected and were only a fixed custom in their time, whereas nowadays one follows the ordinary law. Bach, at the end of Choshen Mishpat, siman 74, cites Maharshal and writes that the Rama also brought an opposing view, based on Responsa Rosh, kelal 103, siman 2, and Rivash, siman 196. Bach therefore concludes that because the authorities disagree, the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. I am astonished by this. The law stated by Mordechai is accepted by Ramban, Sefer HaTerumah, and Rivash. Beit Yosef likewise cites Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 4 of Hilchos Malveh, to this effect, as well as Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 112; Rabbeinu Yerucham, Meisharim, netiv 6, part 1, writes that the later authorities likewise ruled this way. The same appears in Responsa Maimoniyos to Hilchos Malveh, chapter 14. Nimukei Yosef in chapter HaCholetz writes in the name of Ritva, regarding a convert who returned to his former ways and has the status of an apostate Jew, that if the law of the kingdom provides that he acquires and transfers property like a non-Jew, dina d'malchusa dina. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite this below at the end of siman 268. Beit Yosef at the end of Choshen Mishpat, siman 369, likewise cites authorities who hold that dina d'malchusa applies in all matters. The reason the other poskim did not repeat the particular currency law brought by Mordechai in the name of Rabbeinu Tam and Rashbam is simply that they already ruled elsewhere, in several places, that dina d'malchusa dina applies generally, so the same rule obviously applies here. In addition, they hold that currency is comparable to land, as Sefer HaTerumah explains. Once they write generally that dina d'malchusa applies to land, this case follows as well. What Bach attributed to the Rama has already been explained: that is not the Rama's final halachic view.
