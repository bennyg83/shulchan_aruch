Up to one-fifth. The Beit Yosef cites the Rosh's reason: there is no gain with respect to bullion, because if he melts the new coin back into bullion, one-fifth will be lost through the melting process and the silversmith's fee. End. The Derisha writes that one should not ask that if the lender had melted the silver of his original loan he too would have suffered a loss, because the lender can answer: I would not have melted my silver, but would have spent it in its existing form; your silver, as it stands, is no better for spending. End. In my humble opinion his explanation is difficult. Why should one treat the old coin as though it remains intact and the new coin as though it is melted? In any event, the lender gains now if he makes a vessel from the new coin. The Levush answers that we do not take this into account, because the Sages knew that among the old coins there were also some heavy ones exceeding the others by approximately one-fifth, and the lender could select those. This is imprecise. If so, why did the Rosh need the reason that one-fifth is lost in melting? In fact, the Beit Yosef cites this reason concerning heavy coins in the name of the Rashba in a responsum, and the Rashba does not adopt the Rosh's reason. The Levush began with the Rosh's reason and concluded with the Rashba's, which is not correct. Rather, the matter should be explained as follows. When the Gemara asks, 'But he gains with respect to bullion,' it does not mean that whereas from the old coin he could have melted and made a small vessel, from the new coin he can now make a larger vessel. Whenever one claims an advantage in the new coin only by changing it from its present form and making something else, I can likewise say that if you gave me the produce I bought with the old coin, I could now use that produce to obtain new coins, since the produce did not fall in value because of the currency change. We do not consider an advantage that arises only if the coin is no longer kept in its present form. Rather, the Gemara's question is that the borrower can say to the lender: I can take the new coin, melt it down, remake from it a coin of the same size as the old coin you gave me, and still have a small piece of silver left over. This is the meaning of the question, 'But he gains with respect to bullion,' meaning the small piece of bullion that remains. The Gemara therefore answers correctly that if one melts the coin in order to thicken and remake it, he must pay a silversmith and also loses silver through the melting process, so that in the end he gains nothing. This appears to me clear and correct.
He deducts the entire addition. And it does not suffice to deduct only up to the fifth, for since he is obligated to deduct, he deducts all of it. Analogy for this is in "the seller of fruits" and in CM siman 229: if he comes to deduct, he deducts all of it.
And likewise the lender, etc. The Tur concludes: and likewise is the law if they deal in commerce, etc. — meaning not specifically by way of loan but likewise by way of commerce. And he includes in this two matters: the one, if they added or diminished in weight, i.e., the denomination was added or reduced; and the second, that a change was made in the matter of mixing with copper. In all cases, if no change was made regarding fruits or other things given for it, he deducts only up to its fifth.
