And he gave him more. This is the Rambam's language. He holds that even Torah scholars were not permitted to stipulate the extra amount initially. Although the Gemara states that Shmuel said to Avahu bar Ihi, “Lend me 100 peppers for 120 peppers, and it is proper,” and Rashi explains that it is proper to do so, which implies that he stipulated initially, it appears that the Rambam explains the word “proper” in this manner: “If it will be good and proper to do so in my judgment.” He therefore reserved the choice for himself from the outset. From here I am greatly astonished at what the Tur writes at the beginning of siman 160, that if the borrower voluntarily gives more, the Rambam permits it. Here the Rambam permits it only for Torah scholars, in a small amount, and with food. Even according to what I mentioned above, that there the borrower gives it without specification and we presume that he did not intend it because of the loan, there is still a difficulty, for here too he gives it not because of the loan, as the Gemara states: Torah scholars know that ribbis is forbidden and give it as a gift. Nevertheless, it is forbidden if it is not food. It appears that this can be resolved as follows. In truth, the Rambam's permission cited by the Tur above is what the Rambam writes in chapter 8 of Hilchos Malveh regarding a sale. A sale is more lenient, because it involves only ribbis by rabbinic law. With respect to a loan, the Rambam indeed agrees that it is forbidden, as we have demonstrated here according to his view. This also resolves the Tur's wording above: “Although Rashi permits it, and the Rambam also wrote a permissive ruling, this did not appear correct to my father,” etc. This wording is seemingly excessive; he should have written, “Although Rashi and the Rambam permit it,” or at least, “and the Rambam also permits it.” Rather, his intent is: Although Rashi permits it in every circumstance, even with a loan, and the Rambam wrote a permissive ruling, meaning that a permission is found in his words regarding a sale, the Rosh disputes both. See what I wrote above in this siman, seif 6.
Even if they stipulated, etc. The Beit Yosef cites the Hagahos Ashiri: “If he is not a Torah scholar, even if they stipulated initially that it would be a gift, it is forbidden.” The Beit Yosef explains that stipulating that it be given as a gift is preferable to making no stipulation at all. He afterward brings proof from an Ashkenazic responsum, which states that the Or Zarua distinguished a case where he stipulated initially that it would be a gift, etc. According to this, the word “even” used here by the Rama in permitting it is not satisfactory. In truth, however, I am astonished at the Beit Yosef, for that Ashkenazic responsum is the responsum of the Maharil, siman 37. It concerns a man who held the money of his orphaned stepson on terms of fixed ribbis, fed the orphan, provided all his needs, and also taught him. The Maharil ruled that there was certainly a prohibition of fixed ribbis, but he sought merit for the orphan. He reasoned that it was at least as though they had stipulated that the guardian would provide from the profits the amount needed for the orphan's minimal support. If he spent more than the minimum, the excess could be viewed as a gift made in honor of his wife. Concerning this he wrote: “The excess he gives him as a gift. Although some authorities forbid rabbinic ribbis even when designated as a gift, nevertheless the Or Zarua distinguished that where he stipulated from the outset that it be a gift, it is permitted for a Torah scholar. The same applies to a minor orphan who is not subject to punishment. All the more so, this is not actual ribbis but only appears like ribbis, and all the more so here, where he stipulated nothing. Rather, by rabbinic enactment it is as though they stipulated that he should provide the orphan's support up to the amount of the profit, while the remainder is not an obligation but charity and affection for his wife, as is the practice of householders.” End quote. His intent is clear: A stipulation is worse. The Maharil was troubled by his own statement that the excess is a gift, because some authorities forbid ribbis even when given as a gift, apparently referring to the Rambam cited by the Tur in this siman. It appears that there is a scribal error in the responsum, and it should read: “Although some authorities forbid ribbis when designated as a gift,” namely the Rambam, who disputes the Geonim who permit even Torah-level ribbis in such a form. To this the Maharil answered that here it is not actual ribbis but only appears like ribbis, because it is normal for a man to support his stepson, as he writes there. He afterward adds, “All the more so, here he stipulated nothing,” meaning that he did not stipulate to support him, and in this respect the case is even better. When he writes that the Or Zarua distinguished, his meaning is as follows: He first said that even if there had been a stipulation with the orphan to provide all his needs as a gift, which would be forbidden with another person because merely mentioning the word “gift” would not help, nevertheless for a Torah scholar the use of the word “gift” is effective, and it is as though he made no stipulation for fixed ribbis at all. The same therefore applies to an orphan: Mentioning that it is a gift is effective, and the stipulation to provide all his needs does not harm him. He then concludes, all the more so here, where there was no stipulation at all and no correction is needed. The Hagahos Ashiri is likewise readily understood. Its meaning is that for a Torah scholar, if he stipulated and explicitly mentioned that it was a gift, this is effective. If he is not a Torah scholar, however, even though he stipulated and mentioned that it was a gift, this correction is ineffective, and because he stipulated it constitutes fixed ribbis. The Rama therefore properly writes here: “There are those who say that even if he stipulated initially in this manner,” for the stipulation is indeed a disadvantage; nevertheless, since he mentioned that it was a gift, it is effective, as we have explained. Everything is thus satisfactory.
