If the borrower took as his share an equal amount of profit, etc. With another person this would not be permitted. Whenever the loan was arranged in a forbidden manner, it is forbidden to take the return even if the borrower earned a great deal. Here, however, what was done unlawfully is treated as though it was never done, so as not to harm the orphans. It is as though no condition was made at the time of the loan, and for the benefit of the orphans we say that although they did not stipulate a permissible arrangement, it is as though they had done so. Any amount that could permissibly be taken is therefore effective. It appears to me that the orphan takes half of everything the recipient earned, even if this is much more than the stipulated amount, provided that the recipient's compensation for his labor is deducted. Since it is as though there was no condition at all, the orphan is entitled to the arrangement most favorable to him, as though they had stipulated that he receive half the profit, even if that exceeds what the guardian unlawfully fixed. The forbidden stipulation is considered nothing, as though it had never been made. The Levush writes that he may in any event take no more than the stipulated amount. This is exceedingly astonishing. Why should the stipulation matter, when, because it was made unlawfully, it is treated as nonexistent? This is not comparable to a sale made in a forbidden manner, which remains valid, for there the sale itself is proper and the prohibition arises from another factor. That is not so here. This is also explicit in the Beit Yosef's language, where he writes that it is as though they stipulated terms close to profit, meaning that he takes half the profit. This implies as I have written.
