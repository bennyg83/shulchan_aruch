Because of danger to life. The Beit Yosef writes this in the name of Tosafos in the second chapter of Avodah Zarah, 26b, and proves it from the fact that Ovadiah borrowed with ribbis from Yehoram son of Achav in order to support the prophets who were hidden in a cave. One must explain that here too the case is where no upright person is willing to lend without charge, and only one wicked Jew is willing to lend with ribbis. It is then permitted for the borrower to take a loan with ribbis because of danger to life. The lender, however, obviously commits a prohibition. What danger to life requires him to take ribbis? On the contrary, he is obligated to lend without charge, as it is stated, “You shall not stand idly by the blood of your fellow.” Just as all other Jews are obligated to save the endangered person, so is he. The danger arises only because he is unwilling to lend without charge. One cannot derive permission for the lender from Yehoram, for he acted unlawfully and was an apostate Jew, as Tosafos writes there. Accordingly, the author of the Shulchan Aruch should not have recorded this as a ruling. From the lender's perspective there is in truth a prohibition, while from the borrower's perspective it is obvious, since nothing stands before danger to life. Tosafos did not write this to teach a new law, but only as an answer to their question why Ovadiah paid ribbis to an apostate Jew, since this is forbidden, as written at the beginning of siman 159. The Rama's nearby permission concerning communal affairs in a case of great need that is like danger to life must likewise be understood in this manner: There is only a wicked person who insists upon lending with ribbis. It must not be understood as those who err and say that the lender too has permission. Heaven forbid that one should say so.
