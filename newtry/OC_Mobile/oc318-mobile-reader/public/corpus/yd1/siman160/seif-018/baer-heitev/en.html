Orphans. Beit Yosef concludes that a widow does not have the law of orphans in this regard, because she manages her own money like other women.
The practice. Shach writes that in all the places he visited he never saw this practice, nor heard of people lending orphans' money through rabbinic interest except by means of a formal heter like other funds.
To manage. The same applies to an adult whose father died and who does not know how to manage his affairs. One whose father simply refuses to support or manage him does not have orphan status, and his money may not be invested on terms close to profit and remote from loss. If the recipient claims he accepted ordinary equal profit and loss, he is believed through a migo. If the orphan holds security, the guardian is believed to claim the favorable arrangement, and if absent we advance that claim for the orphan up to the collateral's value. Shach.
Believed. Shach writes that this principle applies not only to orphans but to anyone, because we do not presume people wicked; we assume the arrangement was made permissibly. Taz rejects the entire rule, even for orphans, and writes that one who seeks to extract money without an oath based upon Rama must bring proof. Nekudot HaKesef supports Shach.
