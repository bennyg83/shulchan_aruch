He wrote. Responsa Noda BiYehuda, first edition, siman 40, permits investing orphans' money on terms that give them a greater share of profit than of loss. He writes, “This is how I acted, and in the heter iska document that I made with the recipient I stated that the funds belonged to orphans and that he accepted terms close to profit,” etc. See there. Responsa Masas Moshe, Yoreh De'ah, siman 17, discusses widows who lend money with rabbinic ribbis in a place where the custom has become widespread and no one protests. There is room to argue that it is implicitly stipulated that the borrower will exert himself in the investment on her behalf. If he now refuses to pay, he has no authority to do so. This is all the more true if he swore to uphold the compromise he promised and not deviate from the written agreement, in which case he is obligated to fulfill it. Regarding Torah-level ribbis, however, they must be stopped and punished. See there. He further discusses a minor whose money was lent with rabbinic ribbis. The minor subsequently reached adulthood, and when payment became due the borrower refused to pay the ribbis. He concludes that the minor may collect the ribbis accrued during minority. There is even room to permit the amount accruing after the minor reached adulthood, because the principal reason for the leniency is that a minor is not accustomed to engage in business. If, at the time of the loan, the lender capitalized the ribbis together with the principal, and the ribbis was of the rabbinic type permitted for the minor, the borrower is obligated to pay it. He also discusses adult and minor brothers who are supported jointly from their father's estate, which is administered by guardians. The guardian lent estate funds with rabbinic ribbis for their support. He concludes that the borrower must pay the guardian, who may allocate this portion to the minors. The adult brothers may then take a corresponding amount from the other estate assets. See there. He further discusses a promissory note stating an obligation of one hundred groschen, equivalent to forty rospidi gold coins. At the time the note was written, a Venetian coin was worth two and one-half lion coins, whereas at the time of payment it was worth three. He concludes that the debtor must pay forty rospidi. He cites responsa Maharashach, part 2, which rules that this does not involve ribbis, and that the principle of following the lower expression in a document does not apply. Regarding consecrated funds, which Eliyah Rabbah permits even initially, it is certainly permitted, especially according to what I have written, that there is not even a rabbinic prohibition.
