It is forbidden to give ribbit in advance or afterward. How so? If he intended to borrow from him and began sending him a gift. The Rama writes that this means either that he explains that the gift is being given so that the recipient will lend to him, or that it is a substantial gift. A small gift, however, is permitted without explanation after repayment. It appears to me that the same applies to anything in which the borrower parts with no actual property, but merely assigns a benefit that he would otherwise have given to someone else. Even if it is a substantial gift and provides the lender with considerable benefit, since the borrower loses nothing, it is treated as a small gift. This is explicit in the Gemara, Rashi, Tosafot, and the other poskim in chapter HaZahav, page 49a. See there carefully. See also the Rambam, chapter 7 of Hilchot Mechirah and chapter 7 of Hilchot Maaser. Accordingly, later ribbit is permitted without explanation where one benefits and the other sustains no loss. The Beit Yosef cites the Hagahot Ashri at the end of Eizehu Neshech as ruling that a benefit not previously customary is forbidden even where one benefits and the other sustains no loss. The Beit Yosef understood this to refer to later ribbit and to prohibit it even without explanation. See there. It appears to me, however, that the Hagahot Ashri of Mahari'ach refers explicitly to Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai's teaching: from where do we know that one who owes another one hundred zuz, and was not accustomed to greet him first, may not begin greeting him? Scripture states “neshech,” including every matter, even speech. This is the ruling cited by the Tur and Mechaber in seif 11. The Hagahot Ashri means that the same applies to every benefit that was not previously customary, even if one benefits and the other sustains no loss, like greeting him. The case is while the debt remains outstanding, for the teaching speaks of one who owes another one hundred zuz. This is also implied by Tosafot in Eizehu Neshech, page 73b, who write: “This is not later ribbit, since they did not mention that they were giving it because his money had remained idle with them, as Rashi explained. It is not comparable to one who lent him money and then lived in his courtyard, where he must pay rent, as stated above on page 64, even though he did not say that he would live there because of the money's being idle, for there the debt still remained outstanding.” Similar language appears in the Hagahot Ashri. It follows that after repayment he need not pay rent in the case of one who lent him money and then lived in his courtyard. Rashi explains that case as involving a courtyard that was not normally rented and a person who generally rents lodging, so one benefits and the other sustains no loss. Tosafot interpret it this way according to Rashi, and they likewise explain it so in their first answer on page 64b. This is also explicit in the rulings of Tosafot. It is further evident from the Rambam, chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh, copied by the Shulchan Aruch in this siman, seif 10, which states that one may not teach the lender Scripture “so long as his money remains in his possession.” This implies that after repayment it is permitted, although the lender benefits, since the borrower sustains no loss. The Rambam derived this from “neshech,” including every matter, even speech, as in Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai's teaching. The same implication appears in the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in this siman, seif 9, which likewise state “so long as his money remains in his possession.” See there.
