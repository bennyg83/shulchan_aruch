Even to his sons, etc. This implies that they borrow money from him and pay him ribbis. What is written afterward, “and he certainly gives it to them as a gift,” would then mean that he returns and gives it back to them, or that he does not take it from them at all. This is forced. The Rambam, however, writes explicitly that he is not particular with them and gives it to them as a gift, implying that he pays them ribbis, but there is no prohibition in this payment because it is merely a gift. This is likewise the intent of the Tur and the Shulchan Aruch. The phrase “it is forbidden to lend” means to enter into a loan from them, and the Levush likewise records: It is forbidden to borrow from his sons, etc. Yet there is a difficulty from what the Gemara states: A person is permitted to lend to his sons with ribbis in order to let them taste the taste of ribbis, but this is not proper, lest they become accustomed to it. Rashi explains that this means to let them taste the pain of ribbis, so that they will know how distressed the payer is, etc. According to the Rambam, we cannot explain it this way. It appears that the Rambam explains the Gemara as follows: If a person wishes to train his sons so that they may earn a livelihood by lending to non-Jews with ribbis, he accustoms them by paying them ribbis, so that they will know that it is a good means of livelihood. The Gemara concludes that this is forbidden, lest they become accustomed to it and do so even with a Jew. This explanation is preferable to Rashi's for two reasons. First, according to Rashi, why did it say “it is permitted”? It should have used language indicating that there is a mitzvah to do so, in order to separate them from a prohibition. Second, the Gemara says “the taste of ribbis”; it should have said “the pain of ribbis.” According to the Rambam, this is satisfactory.
