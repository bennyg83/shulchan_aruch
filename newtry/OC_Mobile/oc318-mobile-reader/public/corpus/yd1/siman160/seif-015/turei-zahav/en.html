Tell a certain person to give you four dinars. The Beit Yosef writes this in the name of Rabbeinu Yerucham and gives a weak reason for it, namely, that since there is a prohibition where one says, “Give a maneh to a certain person and I will lend to you,” the same should be forbidden here. There is no need to answer this, for it refutes itself. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that this ruling is a scribal error, since the borrower suffers no loss, and that it should read: “that he should give me four dinars,” etc., which is obviously forbidden. It appears to me that there is no scribal error, and Rabbeinu Yerucham's words are correct. I will set out before you the words of the Gemara in the first chapter of Kiddushin, 6a. A woman who said to a man, “Here is a maneh, and I shall become betrothed to you,” is betrothed when he is an important person, for through the benefit that he accepts from her she resolves to transfer herself to him. Rashi explains that the benefit is that she would be willing to give a perutah to another person to persuade this man to accept a gift from her. End quote. So too is the ruling in Even HaEzer, siman 27. It appears to me that there the requirement that he be an important person applies only where he himself accepts the gift. If he were not important, although she derives pleasure from his accepting it, since she desires this, the benefit nevertheless has no monetary value through which we could say that she transfers herself to him, for a woman is betrothed specifically through money, not through a benefit that is not worth money. Rashi therefore had to explain that, with an important person, she benefits by not having to give a perutah to someone else to persuade him to accept the gift. Accordingly, if she said, “Tell a certain person to give you a gift, and I shall become betrothed to you,” she is betrothed even if he is not an important person. Since he spoke at her direction and she desires this, had he not done so she would have paid another person a perutah to say it. She therefore derives monetary benefit from his agency, for a person does not trouble himself to speak without compensation, and she is particular that the statement be made. It therefore appears to me that she is betrothed in this case even if he is not important. As a matter of practical halacha, although we would not rely upon this view leniently to say that she is certainly betrothed, such that if another man betrothed her she would not require a get from him, nevertheless stringently we certainly say so, and she may not marry another without a get from the first. The same applies to the prohibition of ribbis. If a lender says to a borrower, “I will lend you money on condition that you accept a gift from me,” then if the borrower is an important person who does not ordinarily accept gifts without persuasion, there is a prohibition of ribbis; if he is not important, there is no prohibition. But if he says, “Tell a certain person to give you a gift, and I will lend you,” the borrower desires that statement, for otherwise he would pay someone else a perutah to make it. This is therefore absolute ribbis. It appears clear to me that Rabbeinu Yerucham's words are correct and well founded.
