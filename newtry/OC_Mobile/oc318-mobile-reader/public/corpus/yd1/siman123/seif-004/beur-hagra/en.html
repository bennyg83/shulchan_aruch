Wine into which one mixes, and so forth. The Yerushalmi teaches in the name of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi that sweet, bitter, or sharp wine is not subject to the concern of uncovered liquids. Rabbi Simon adds that it is also not subject to the laws of non-Jewish wine, and explains that 'sharp' means conditum. Pesikta, Yitro, compares words of Torah to conditum, which contains wine, honey, and pepper. This is the drink called anomalin in the Gemara. The Ramban limits the rule to a mixture of one-third honey and one-third pepper, as indicated by the Yerushalmi's account in which the Sages prohibited a weaker mixture. The Rashba, however, holds that no exact ratio is required, provided the taste has changed, and writes that one who is stringent is praiseworthy.
(Supplement) If its taste changed, and so forth. This rejects the Rambam, who permits even a little honey because the wine is no longer fit for libation. That reasoning is incorrect, for it remains fit for their worship, and even diluted wine is subject to non-Jewish wine although it is unfit for the altar. See Bava Batra 97b. The same applies, and so forth. The Gemara's phrase 'sharp wine and peppers' shows that peppers alone suffice. The Yerushalmi mentions honey and peppers only because that is the customary recipe. Torat HaBayit and our Gemara, which gives no fixed measure, support the Rashba. Rashi also explains 'sharp' accordingly. Beit Yosef writes that honey alone has the same law, as does the Rashba in a responsum.
