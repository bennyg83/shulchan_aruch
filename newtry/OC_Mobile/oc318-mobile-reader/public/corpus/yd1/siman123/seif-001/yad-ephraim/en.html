If it has honey. Be'er Heitev and responsum Avkat Rokhel siman 16: barrels of oil coming from Candia on a ship entirely of non-Jews, and on the mouths of the barrels are wax seals; but in every barrel there is a small hole to release air so it will not burst — see there: permitted because all Candia wines are known and famous that they put honey among the winepresses; therefore they are not careful about the touch of a non-Jew in bringing wine from winepresses to the city. And in Egypt the common practice is to drink Candia wine — because they rely on the rulings of the Geonim of the West, and also for exchange through the hole we are not concerned, for there are many doubts that perhaps he had no wine of his own to exchange — see there. And he wrote in Shevut Yaakov part 1 siman 62 regarding wine standing in a vessel with a little oil on the mouth of the vessel so the wine will not spoil; it stood several days and a non-Jew touched the oil — responsum Halakhot Ketanot part 1 siman 10 ruled to be lenient; and Shevut Yaakov wrote this is his explanation, and it is obvious to forbid unless where it became mixed in a mixture of spices — then there is no status of libation, as explained in siman 123; and if the non-Jew did not touch the oil itself, only through a stream, there is reason to permit in a place of loss — see there. And he wrote that yayin shel Yisrael from Jewish wine that non-Jews touched afterward is not forbidden, for cooked wine has no status of yayin nesech, and the taste also changed like aluntit — and not like responsum Halakhot Ketanot on this, which is stringent. And in Shevut Yaakov siman 63 regarding wine called vermouth: Tzemach Tzedek siman 74 and Chavot Yair siman 11 forbid, and their words are not conclusive since the taste changed, and everyone who tastes it knows that anise was mixed into it; nevertheless one should not act against these two great ones. Nevertheless he ruled leniency even for drinking in a case where a large barrel of vermouth was sent by a non-Jew; at the place of the stopper it is sealed with wax, but at the spigot it is not well sealed, only tightened — according to what I raised in Minchat Yaakov kelal 32, that for stam yeinam we rely on one seal, for we are not concerned that they actually poured a libation, only because of exchange or taking from there; and since in Eliyah Rabba siman 58 he permits when the spigot is well tightened so that there is effort to open it — which is like one seal, even though his words are not conclusive, and especially at a spigot that when one presses on it it comes out without effort; and also his intent is to be stringent — nevertheless in this case, where the barrels were full without any deficiency and the stopper sealed properly, and it is impossible to take and return and mix unless one stands the barrels on other bases and removes the spigot to fill and afterward replaces it as at first; and since the barrel is large and the effort very great, all the later authorities agree that one would not trouble himself so much to falsify, per the words of the above Eliyah Rabba — therefore it appears the wine is permitted for drinking without hesitation — see there. And in responsum Tashbetz part 1 siman 85: wine into which a little honey was mixed — if it has the status of yayin nesech, it should be forbidden — see there; and see part 2 siman 168 also on this matter — see there. And in siman 66: wine into which fig beer was mixed — if the majority is beer, it is permitted — see there.
It constitutes drawing off. See Baer Heitev and Panim Meirot, part 2, siman 111. Household owners purchased a trodden vat from a non-Jew in order to produce kosher wine. The servants of the local authority first took the portion due to their master by placing a basket into the vat and drawing clear wine from it in the required quantity. It is explained in siman 123 that placing a basket in the vat constitutes drawing off, because clear wine enters the basket and becomes separated from the grape skins. He concludes that initially one may certainly not rely upon this procedure to produce kosher wine from a vat into which such a basket was inserted for the collection of the tax. After the fact, however, if a Jew saw that when the basket was placed into the vat the non-Jew did not touch the wine with his hand, the wine may be permitted in a case of substantial loss. In assessing the loss, all the individuals who produced the wine may be combined, even though no single individual would otherwise sustain a substantial loss. Moreover, each individual may be regarded as sustaining such a loss because he would have to purchase kosher wine at a high price. One may then rely upon the Rama in siman 124, who rules that their contact is considered unintentional. If no Jew was present when the basket was placed into the vat, however, and there is reason to suspect, indeed it is likely, that the non-Jew touched the wine by hand, one may not be lenient even after the fact.
