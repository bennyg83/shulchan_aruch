They tread. Shach writes that only treading is forbidden in a sealed and full press, while mere touch does not forbid it; with shackles it forbids only drinking. He questions why the non-Jew's foot-contact itself does not forbid benefit. One must say either that he tread upon boards, or that because he was occupied in treading the wine is permitted for benefit. Taz distinguishes that here a Jew treads together with him, and assistance by a Jew creates leniency. Shach concludes that wherever the law forbids drinking, nowadays it is permitted even for drinking in a case of loss, since contemporary non-Jews are not considered idolaters. Taz rules that in any vessel with a spigot, even a large one, one should prevent non-Jewish treading because clear wine may flow when the spigot is moved. In a vessel without any spigot there is no prohibition, and he acted accordingly in Moravia where grapes were not ordinarily pushed aside to expose clear wine.
Outside. Shach writes that wine remaining in the press or tub is permitted even if it had been drawn, because the non-Jew's force forbids only wine that actually exited. This applies where he did not touch the grapes, such as when boards were placed over them and he stood on the boards. If he touched them after drawing began, the wine is forbidden through touch. Before drawing, even direct touch is permitted after the fact. Even after drawing it is forbidden only for drinking, because a non-Jew's force forbids only drinking.
In a trough. Taz writes that one should not rely upon this, because the grapes may previously have been trodden in a place where drawing occurred and then poured into this trough because the non-Jew knew a Jew would not buy from the original vessel. He heard many scholars and pious people decree not to buy trodden grapes from a non-Jew in any vessel, and this is proper practice where possible. Nekudot HaKesef disagrees: we do not presume a prohibition without evidence.
