Although it is unfit for libation, they prohibited it entirely. I do not understand from where the Rosh knew this. Perhaps non-Jews customarily pour diluted wine as a libation, similar to the Rosh's question against the Rambam regarding wine into which a small amount of leaven was placed. Even according to Rabbeinu Yonah, who holds that regarding a non-Jew's contact with grape-water the ruling depends on whether three measures were poured in and four emerged, that is because when it is not considered wine for the blessing of Borei Pri HaGefen, it was never included in the Sages' decree, for they decreed only concerning wine. This is especially so because the principal decree was due to intermarriage, and they decreed only regarding wine. Nevertheless, one may still say that non-Jews customarily pour diluted wine as a libation. As to the Rosh's principal question, I have not merited to understand from where he knew that the prohibition of a non-Jew's contact was enacted because of intermarriage. Plainly, intermarriage is irrelevant to mere contact. Rather, because they prohibited ordinary non-Jewish wine from benefit, treating it as though it had been poured as a libation, they were likewise stringent regarding a non-Jew's contact out of concern for libation. Indeed, the Rosh follows his own view that the contact of a resident alien permits the wine even for drinking, while wine produced by him is forbidden for drinking. It is therefore clear that the prohibition of contact is not due to intermarriage. This also follows from the Rosh's words concerning the Rashbam's position regarding the non-Jews of our times: "These reasons suffice regarding our wine that a non-Jew touched, but their ordinary wine, which they prohibited because of intermarriage," and so forth. Thus, intermarriage is not relevant to contact. This is especially so according to the Rosh's later suggestion that originally ordinary non-Jewish wine was prohibited only for drinking, and only afterward, when non-Jews commonly poured libations, was it prohibited from benefit. It may therefore be said that the original decree did not include a prohibition of their contact at all, and only later, when libation became common, did they prohibit their contact. Accordingly, regarding our cooked wine, where the issue is only a non-Jew's contact, there appears to be little basis for the Rosh's question. This requires study.
