This constitutes drawing forth. See Baer Heitev and responsa Panim Me'iros, part 2, siman 111. Household owners purchased a pressed vat from a non-Jew in order to make kosher wine, but the ruler's servants first removed the ruler's tithe by placing a basket into the vat and drawing clear wine from it in the amount due. Siman 123 explains that placing a basket in this manner constitutes drawing forth, because clear wine enters the basket and separates from the grape seeds and skins. He concludes that one must never rely upon this initially to make kosher wine from a vat into which such a basket was placed for collecting the tithe. After the fact, however, if a Jew saw that when the basket was placed in the press the non-Jew did not touch the wine by hand, one may permit the wine in a case of substantial loss. The losses of all those who made the wine may be combined, although each individual's loss alone may not be substantial. One may also regard it as a substantial loss for each individual because he would otherwise have to purchase kosher wine at a high price. In such a case one may rely upon the Rama in siman 124, who rules that all contact by contemporary non-Jews is considered unintentional. If no Jew was present when the basket was inserted, and there is strong reason to suspect that the non-Jew touched the wine by hand, one may not be lenient even after the fact. He cites Tzemach Tzedek, siman 12, who ruled that where it is customary to place a basket in the vat to draw clear wine, one may not permit the wine after the fact, except in a place where they have no winepresses among the vineyards. A mere concern that priests may come and ask to be given wine, using small vessels to draw clear wine, does not forbid the wine, since their arrival itself is doubtful. He was also asked about someone who purchased wine from a vat fitted with a spigot. He permitted it in a case of substantial loss or pressing need where no other wine was available. From then onward, however, it was proper to place under ban those who purchased wine from such a vat unless they sealed the spigot, spread a cloth over the vat, and guarded and sealed it so that the non-Jew could not draw wine with small vessels.
