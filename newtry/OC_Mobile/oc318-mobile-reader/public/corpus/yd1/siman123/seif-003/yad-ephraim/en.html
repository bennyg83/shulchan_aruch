If it contains honey. See Baer Heitev and responsa Avkas Rochel, siman 16, concerning barrels of wine brought from Candia on a ship crewed entirely by non-Jews. The mouths of the barrels were sealed with two seals, but each barrel had a small hole through which air could escape so that it would not burst. He permitted the wine because it was well known that all Candian wines had honey added to them at the winepresses, and they therefore were not careful about contact by non-Jews when transporting the wine from the presses to the city. In Egypt, the accepted practice was to drink Candian wine for this reason, relying upon the rulings of the western Geonim. There was also no concern that the wine had been exchanged through the hole, because several doubts applied, including whether the non-Jew had any wine of his own with which to exchange it. See there. Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 62, discusses wine kept in a vessel with a little oil covering the opening so that the wine would not spoil. After it stood for several days, a non-Jew touched the oil. Responsa Halachos Ketanos, part 1, siman 10, ruled leniently. Shevut Yaakov rejected that ruling and wrote that the wine is plainly forbidden unless spices had been mixed into it, in which case it is not subject to the law of libation wine, as explained in siman 123. If the non-Jew did not touch the oil itself, but affected it only through a poured stream, one may permit it in a case of substantial loss. See there. He further wrote that Jewish brandy distilled from Jewish wine that was later touched by non-Jews is not forbidden, because cooked wine is not subject to the prohibition of libation wine and its taste has also changed, like aluntis. This is contrary to the cited responsum of Halachos Ketanos, which is stringent. Shevut Yaakov, siman 63, also discusses wine known as vermouth that was touched by a non-Jew. Tzemach Tzedek, siman 74, and Chavos Yair, siman 11, forbid it. Although their reasoning is not conclusive, since its taste has changed and anyone tasting it knows that wormwood was mixed into it, one should not act contrary to these two great authorities. Nevertheless, Shevut Yaakov permitted even drinking in a case where a large barrel of vermouth was sent to someone through a non-Jew. The stopper was secured with two seals, but the spigot was not properly sealed and was merely tightly fitted. Based on Minchas Yaakov, rule 32, one seal may be relied upon for ordinary non-Jewish wine, since the concern is not that the non-Jew actually offered it as a libation, but that he exchanged it or took some of it. Avodas HaGershuni, siman 58, permits a spigot that is fitted so tightly that opening it requires effort, considering this equivalent to one seal. Although his reasoning is not conclusive, especially because striking a spigot may dislodge it without effort, and although he himself intended to rule stringently, this case was different. The barrel was completely full, the stopper was properly sealed, and it would have been impossible to remove wine, replace it, and mix it back in without turning the large barrel onto another base, removing the spigot, refilling it, and then returning it to its former position on the supports. Since the barrel was large and this would require great effort, all later authorities agree that a non-Jew would not trouble himself so extensively to falsify the seal. The wine was therefore permitted for drinking without hesitation. See there. Responsa Tashbetz, part 1, siman 85, rules that wine into which a small amount of honey was mixed remains forbidden if it retains the law of libation wine. See also part 2, siman 168, on this subject. In siman 66 he rules that wine mixed with fig beer is permitted if the beer constitutes the majority. See there.
