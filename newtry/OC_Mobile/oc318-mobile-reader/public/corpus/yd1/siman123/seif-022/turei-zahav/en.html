It is forbidden for drinking. This is because only the force of the non-Jew is involved, for he did not touch the wine even with his foot. These are the words of the Rambam. I am astonished by what the Beit Yosef wrote in siman 124 in the name of the Rashba, that according to the Rambam, contact with the foot does not forbid the wine for benefit, although it does forbid it for drinking. Why, then, did the Rambam write here that he did not touch it? Even had he touched it with his foot, it would have been forbidden only for drinking. One can answer that the Rambam teaches a greater novelty: even when he did not touch it at all, it is nevertheless forbidden for drinking. Indeed, the same law would apply had he touched it with his foot. Here the discussion concerns fully developed wine, part of which has already been drawn away. If none of it was drawn away, there is no prohibition except due to Rabbeinu Tam’s stringency discussed above. The words of the Tur are to the same effect, for he writes: “Even if he did not touch it, but merely trod the grapes, the wine in the pit is forbidden.” End quote. This means that if he did not touch the wine at all, even with his foot, all agree that it is permitted for benefit but forbidden for drinking. If he touched it with his foot while treading, he is considered preoccupied with his work, as explained in siman 124, and the law is likewise the same. If he touched it with his foot other than while treading, there is a dispute in siman 124: the Rashba permits benefit, while the Rosh forbids even benefit. The Drishah raises a difficulty in siman 124 from that ruling against what the Tur writes here, but there is no difficulty. What is difficult, however, is this: If the Tur means here that even without contact, the wine in the pit is forbidden because of the non-Jew’s force, since the non-Jew is preoccupied with his work this should be considered force exerted without intent, which is permitted even for drinking, as written at the end of siman 125. See siman 124, seif 19.
