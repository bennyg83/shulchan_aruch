It is stated only in Responsa Beit Zvi that such is the custom, and it appears that the case concerns a heset oath. He continues that the laws of gilgul of an oath are learned from sotah, and regarding sotah the Rambam rules, in chapter 2 of Hilchot Sotah, law 7, that a pregnant woman is given the water and we do not wait until she gives birth. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 77. One student wished to reject the challenge of Tumim from sotah by arguing that there the husband imposes the oath and the fetus is his; if he wishes to cause its loss, who can object? Here, however, the claimant has no right to cause loss to the fetus of another husband. Chatam Sofer replied that this is not correct, because the difficulty remains where the claim concerns the husband's property, the wife conducts business in the household, the husband cannot make a definite claim and swear, and the wife therefore must swear. The claimant can then say to the husband: what concern is it of mine that this is your fetus? Either pay, or establish the matter through your own oath or your wife's. Indeed, most oaths imposed on women in our times concern the husband's property. Nevertheless, the challenge of Tumim is not difficult for another reason. It appears that the reason a pregnant woman is not made to swear is not danger to the fetus, but danger to the woman herself, lest she be endangered during childbirth, Heaven forbid. Even a true oath can create danger, as explained in the chapter HaShole'ach, Gittin 35a. This resembles what is written in Orach Chayyim, end of siman 602, that a cherem is not imposed and oaths are not administered until after Yom Kippur; see Peri Megadim there. Thus there is no proof from sotah, where there is no concern for bodily loss either way. If she was defiled and swears falsely, that is precisely what the sotah procedure is intended to expose. If she was not defiled and swears truthfully, there is no concern that childbirth will endanger her, for the Torah promises that if she was innocent she will conceive seed, and if she previously gave birth with difficulty she will give birth more easily. Chatam Sofer concludes that Tumim went too far, and one who seeks to depart from the Rama and established custom bears the burden of proof. His words imply that this custom applies even to a Torah oath, and a pregnant woman should not be made to swear until after she gives birth.
