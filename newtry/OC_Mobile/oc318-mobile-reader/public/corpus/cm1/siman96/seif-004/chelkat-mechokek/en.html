The plain language implies that even if the woman was in possession before her death and her heirs are presently in possession, there is an opinion that neither form of possession helps and that even merely to retain the money we say one cannot transmit an oath to one's children. In truth, one who carefully examines the authorities will not find an explicit dispute on this point. Mordechai, in chapter Kol HaNishba'in, proves from the law of a partnership document produced against orphans that, for purposes of retaining property, an oath can be transmitted to heirs. Mahari Weil rules this way plainly, citing Mordechai, Hagahot Ashri, and Tosafot in chapter HaCholetz. Tosafot in chapter Mi SheMet likewise write that if the property was in the woman's heirs' possession she could transmit such an oath to her children. I have already noted that the Tur in siman 108 says this plainly, as does Ran in the name of the French rabbis, and Sefer HaTerumot even holds that seizure by the heirs alone helps. It is logical, since one who ruled like Rabbi Elazar has acted validly. Terumat HaDeshen expressed hesitation only where the heirs themselves were not in possession and only the woman had been. In the usual practice, if an widow dies without having sworn, her heirs receive nothing, whether she had previously held the assets or not, when after her death the property is in beit din. But if the heirs themselves are in possession, who can remove it from them? As stated, however, Maharam requires a genuine possession, not one where the assets were shown and witnesses establish that they belonged to the husband. See Beit Yosef in Choshen Mishpat 252 on the Rashbatz and Beit Yosef's objection.
