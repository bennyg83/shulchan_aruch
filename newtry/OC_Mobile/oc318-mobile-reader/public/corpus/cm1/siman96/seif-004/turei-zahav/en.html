(See there, but not her husband) Beit Yosef cites Sefer HaTerumot: if there are witnesses that the actual loan funds, or property derived from them, still exists, the lender is certainly repaid from those assets. I saw my teacher and father-in-law infer from this that where one borrower owes many creditors and purchased merchandise on credit from one of them, if that merchandise or its proceeds still exists, the other creditors have no share in it. He wrote that the Tur likewise rules in siman 108 and siman 280, seif 1, in the name of Ramah, and that Rif says similarly in the chapter Af Al Pi concerning a rebellious wife. This does not appear correct to me. In an ordinary loan, as soon as the money enters the borrower’s possession, even if the same coins remain intact they are no longer legally identified as “the money of this particular lender,” because a loan is given for expenditure. The lender merely has a lien upon those assets, and is therefore no stronger than the other lenders, who likewise have liens against the borrower. The case of Sefer HaTerumot is different: the husband has no lien at all upon money that came from the lender, even while that money remains intact. There is therefore no basis to defeat the lender’s right in it. This distinction likewise rejects the other cited proofs, which bear no resemblance to multiple lenders who all possess liens. This appears correct to me.
