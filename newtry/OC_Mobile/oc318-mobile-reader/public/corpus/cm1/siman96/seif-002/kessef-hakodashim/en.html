In the gloss. (One who conducts business. See Shach, seif katan 9.) I wrote elsewhere about a wife who sold property and stated that she acted with her husband's authorization. It requires study whether we presume that she acted with permission, just as we rule that where a husband sells or pledges his wife's clothing we do not ordinarily presume him to be a thief. I wrote that where she actually transferred the item from her possession, perhaps the same presumption applies to her. Where she did not transfer it, however, the case differs from a pledge in which the husband actually delivered the property. A wife who regularly conducts business with movables in the house is not necessarily presumed to have authority over her husband's movables outside the house, even if the values are similar. Likewise, one who conducts business in small amounts is not thereby presumed authorized to transact in much larger amounts, as implied by the Shach and Urim VeTumim in siman 97. Logic also supports such distinctions. If the husband is entitled to void a sale made by her, and the purchaser already paid her the money, can she nevertheless compel the purchaser to keep the transaction if the market price later fell? One finds that where a partner deviates from instructions, profit can still belong to the partnership while loss falls on the deviating partner. Here, however, perhaps a transaction cannot be effective only on one side. This requires study. If the husband heard of the sale and remained silent, then later objected and refused to deliver the property, does his silence constitute admission? One can argue that so long as the movables had not yet been delivered to the purchaser he had no need to respond, because the purchaser bears the burden of proving that she had authority for a transaction of that amount. With land, perhaps "I had no need to respond" is less persuasive because no physical delivery is required and the property may automatically stand in the purchaser's legal possession. Even if the husband protests only after the property increases in value, his earlier silence may still not amount to admission regarding movables, and perhaps not regarding land either. Perhaps a wife's presumed business authority applies only to movables and not to land, even where the land is worth less than the movables she ordinarily handles, because people do not readily sell real property. The verse describing the capable woman who "considers a field and acquires it" concerns purchase, and it is common for a husband to authorize his wife to buy land with movable funds. It does not prove authority to sell land. Likewise, the verse comparing her to merchant ships concerns commerce in movables. Perhaps the same limitation applies to movables uniquely associated with the husband, analogous to the Sages' statement that people do not ordinarily lend a purse or wallet, and the sacred works write similarly regarding personally designated clothing. She may not be presumed authorized over such items merely because she otherwise conducts business. If she had previously conducted business for him but shortly before this sale the husband was heard to revoke her authority, perhaps the market regulation should nevertheless preserve the sale because some people knew of her prior authority but did not hear of the revocation. On the other hand, absent an express enactment of this kind, money should not be extracted on a doubt. Nowadays ordinary wives are generally considered to conduct business to the extent appropriate to their husband's means and commercial activity. If she undertakes an extraordinarily large transaction of a kind plainly inconsistent with his ordinary business, however, she may not be regarded as his agent. If a debtor paid money to the wife, she brought it home short, returned to demand the deficiency from the payer, and he claimed that he had paid the full amount, then after the wife's death the husband's claim based on her statement presents difficulties. Her statement may have been interested, and she cannot now receive formal authorization. If the payer says, "I do not know," the husband himself has no direct certain claim, and he likewise does not know whether full payment was made. Perhaps, however, because he trusted her and knows with certainty that she did not retain any of the money herself, together with the concept that a wife is in some respects like her husband's own person, his claim may be treated as sufficiently definite. Where a wife regularly conducts household business, especially nowadays when that is commonly presumed, the husband may be liable for obligations to others from which he derived benefit. If she customarily takes merchandise for brokerage, or on terms that if she sells it she keeps the excess above a fixed amount and if she does not sell it she returns the goods, and the merchandise is then lost or stolen while in her possession, perhaps the husband must pay. Since her regular business benefits the household beyond the value of any particular lost item, if he takes the profit but refuses to bear the loss, merchants may cease entrusting her with goods. This resembles a loan from which the husband derived benefit and for which he must pay. Although in an ordinary loan the principal money itself remains in his hands for his use, whereas here the entrusted merchandise may never remain with him, the regular commercial arrangement benefits the household because entrusting goods to her enables additional sales. Whether the husband is liable even for unavoidable loss while she transports merchandise to sell is less clear. If she received money permitted for trade and could use it in her business, that is still lighter than an ordinary loan. In a loan, the husband benefits immediately and the money is definitively given for expenditure. Here the money remains the giver's property, unavoidable losses are uncommon, and the liability arises only if such a loss occurs. Even if she herself would bear liability for unavoidable loss, merchants may not refrain from entrusting her merely because the husband would not pay in an exceptional case. Certainly this is true for an extraordinarily rare accident, and perhaps every unavoidable loss is sufficiently uncommon for this reasoning. Where the husband would otherwise be liable for her, if he himself was present in a manner that would ordinarily create an exemption under custodial law, perhaps that exemption does not fit this derivative liability. If she is treated as his commercial agent, then a transaction she makes does not subject her personally to mi shepara. Regarding even slight onaah committed through an agent, the same should logically apply to a wife who conducts business for him. The principle "his wife is like his own person" does not by itself validate the sale, because if she lacks business authority her sale is void. Her power comes only from agency, and agency is granted for proper execution of the transaction. If two pieces of flax were sold together, they are presumably of unequal value. If the purchaser took possession of only one piece, he can void the entire sale because a transaction partially void is void in its entirety. The same should apply where a wife conducts business. Even if there had been a kinyan sudar, if her authority was inferred only from the fact that she later transferred possession, then where only one item was transferred the entire transaction may be void. Suppose a wife who managed the household business stated during her lifetime that she had delivered a payment to a particular person, and after her death the husband says that he heard this from her. If she managed business without being accountable to him for every item, she was not interested in the dispute. According to what I wrote at the end of siman 75, this may create a sufficiently definite claim for shevuat heset, and perhaps even for a Torah oath where the defendant admits part. This requires careful study. Even if testimony through a relative ordinarily does not suffice, and the Shach raises doubts regarding it, one might still invoke the principle that a wife is like her husband's own person, analogous to the discussion in Sanhedrin concerning dividing a person's statement. The principal point, however, is that this may fall within the category where the husband was not expected to know, in which case one who says, "I do not know whether I paid you," can be exempt. He says that his knowledge came from his wife, who specifically told him that she had paid, and no greater knowledge was realistically available to him. This differs from the shopkeeper and workers, where the employer was negligent because he could have arranged for witnessed payment, and the Gemara itself asks why he should bear the loss. The worker also had the choice to take goods from the shopkeeper without making them a direct payment mechanism. A wife managing household business is different. The same may apply to two partners where one says, "My partner told me during his lifetime that he paid." Even though partners account to one another and the deceased partner may have been interested in the matter, the surviving partner may still fall into the category of one who was not expected to know more, because the partner gave him a definite report of payment. The same is true where husband and wife regularly accounted together regarding individual transactions. Still, a bare claim, "Perhaps she paid," or "Perhaps my partner paid," is not enough where the deceased never reported such payment. Even if the person died suddenly, perhaps the survivor was expected to maintain a practice whereby payments were reported immediately or written down at once. To speculate that payment was made immediately before a sudden death is an unusual possibility. Where the partners maintained accounts, perhaps the survivor is less expected to know independently because each party is interested in the entries. Yet prompt verification ordinarily presents little risk of denial while both parties are alive, for how can one living person simply deny another living person's direct statement? Even in a case of "fifty I know I owe," the law may be more lenient where the party was not expected to know. Perhaps there was room for a special enactment analogous to the shopkeeper and workers when all parties are alive, while a wife may in any event be treated even more strongly as the husband's own person. All this requires careful study.
