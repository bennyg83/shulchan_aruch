See Be'er HaGolah, letter 90, who cites the Bach, etc., and concludes that according to this, the same applies to a borrower who lacks sufficient assets to pay all his creditors: if the merchandise he bought on credit is still intact, etc. These words of the Bach are also cited by Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer siman 90, seif katan 38, and he appears to agree. There he adds that if it is known that the borrower bought merchandise of this type, we say that the merchandise presently in his possession is from those goods even though it is not known with certainty that it is the merchandise of that particular seller. See there. Ketzot HaChoshen here likewise cites the Bach as law. Below, siman 114, seif katan 14, he raises a difficulty against the Bach and resolves it, indicating that he agrees with him in practice. The Taz here, however, disagrees with the Bach. The words of Responsa Mabit, part 2, simanim 200 and 213, cited by the Shach above, siman 39, seif katan 49, likewise support the Taz. Mabit ruled there that seizure of the wool was effective for the creditor. Even those who disagree there, whose view the Shach adopts, do so because the wool was still in the seller's possession and because the seller could retract the sale itself. See there. Beit Meir, Even HaEzer siman 90, seif 9, also discusses these words of the Bach at length and writes that both opinions in Responsa Mabit prove that they do not accept the Bach's rule. Otherwise there would be no room for their dispute, because even if the wool were in the buyer's possession, the buyer's creditor would have no right in it and only the seller would. What then would the creditor's seizure accomplish? He further writes that Responsa Rosh, rule 79, cited by the Sema, siman 47, seif katan 4, is contrary to the Bach. See there at length. Get Pashut, siman 108, seif katan 8, likewise writes that the Bach's ruling is open to serious challenge. He cites Responsa Maharshadam, siman 210, Maharshach, part 1, siman 35, Mahari A, siman 345, Mahar A Sasson, siman 100, and Mabit, part 1, siman 149, all of which contradict the Bach. He further writes that even according to the Bach, a distinction must be made: the Bach says that the goods are returned to their original owner only while they remain in the buyer's possession. If the buyer sold them or gave them away, his act is valid, since he had already acquired them. See there. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 117, also disagrees with the Bach as a matter of law, though he testifies that such is the local enactment. He discusses the matter at length and concludes: "Since it has been established that according to strict law this creditor has no right to take his own intact merchandise before the other creditors, but rather all creditors share equally because the price was converted into a loan, and only by virtue of an enactment of the sages of the generation does he have a right to take those goods, we should apply the principle: grant only what the enactment expressly grants and add nothing. The enactment applies only where those very goods remain intact, not to substitutes or proceeds derived from them. Although where a person has a right under strict law to the intact object, that right ordinarily follows what derives from it, this is not so with an enactment. One may not extend an enactment beyond its terms. It applies only to the goods themselves and not to what derives from them." See there. See further my Divrei Mishpat on this siman, what I wrote above, siman 91, seif katan 3, and Shaar Mishpat at the end of siman 104 on this matter. See also what I wrote below, siman 104, seif 3, seif katan 3, in the name of Responsa Noda B'Yehudah. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 55, likewise agrees with the above Tzemach Tzedek. He discusses a case where a person died owing several creditors, and among his estate was merchandise known to have been purchased on credit from a certain shopkeeper. He ruled that the shopkeeper who supplied the merchandise has no priority over the other creditors in that merchandise, even by virtue of the enactment. In his view, the great authorities who enacted the local rule enacted it only for a living borrower who absconds or becomes insolvent, where it is highly likely that the merchandise he purchased on credit and still possesses was bought with intent to flee and to become a wicked borrower. In such a case they enacted a departure from strict law. Where the borrower died and left an estate, however, there is no deceit or wrongdoing and they made no such enactment. This is explicit in the text of the enactment printed at the end of Misgeret HaShulchan, siman 27, which applies only to one who flees. Furthermore, siman 28 there states that the entire enactment concerning a fugitive was made only for the benefit of creditors residing in the lands where the enactment spread; with respect to people living elsewhere, the law remains Torah law. Chatam Sofer adds that he had not heard that the enactment spread in his lands, Urim VeTumim does not mention it at all even though its author was one of the great authorities of those lands, and it is not part of the law of the kingdom either. Therefore, according to strict law he saw no reason to grant priority unless the other creditors voluntarily agreed to a compromise. See there. See further Responsa Rishmei She'elah, simanim 49 and 50, where this subject is discussed at length. It likewise states that everything written at the end of Ateret Tzvi regarding intact merchandise is only an enactment against a fugitive, not against heirs or an widow's ketubah. One should not extend the enactment. The distinction is simple: a fugitive was treated as though he were a notorious thief. The author further questions what the law would be if the merchandise bought on credit changed form, as in the case there where most of the merchandise was fur from which, the seller claimed, hats had already been made. Perhaps in such a case it is no longer called intact merchandise. See there.
