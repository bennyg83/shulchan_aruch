On the basis of his father's claim. See Sema, seif katan 1. His words indicate that if the son is already an adult and sues on his father's claim, the defendant is obligated in a Torah oath. Sema therefore appears to rule like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov in Ketubot 19, who does not accept the reasoning that a person is more willing to deny the claim of a son. Tumim questions a case where the son first sued while a minor and the defendant admitted part, and afterward the son became an adult and sued again with another partial admission. Is a Torah oath now required? In my view the defendant is certainly exempt. When the first suit was brought during the son's minority, the defendant was legally like one returning lost property, and that status itself provided evidence that he was speaking truthfully. The special credibility of one returning lost property is stronger than an ordinary migo and should continue to protect him. There is a second reason. Ran in Perek Shevuat HaDayanim writes concerning a claim involving measure and weight that an admission made under circumstances of exemption remains exempt because the admission originally emerged without oath liability. Tumim's own additional rationale is also correct, that the later case resembles an admission preceding the claim.
He swears that he was not negligent. Tumim asks that with ordinary custodians the Gemara in Perek Shevuat HaPikadon asks why the custodian should not be believed through a migo that he could deny the deposit entirely or claim that he returned it. The Gemara answers that the deposit was documented in writing. Rosh and the poskim, however, rule that even where the deposit was not documented, the custodian takes a Torah oath because the alternative denial would be an especially brazen claim. If a minor is the claimant, such denial is not considered brazen. It would follow that a custodian sued by a minor should be obligated in the Torah custodial oath only where the deposit was documented. In my view there is no difficulty. The custodian does not truly have a migo to say, "There was never a deposit," or, "I returned it," because he fears that the minor may sue him again after reaching adulthood. At that time he will no longer be able to brazenly maintain the denial, will have to admit the truth, and will be liable. This differs from the partial admission case above, where exemption results from the admission having preceded a legally effective claim, and that exemption survives the later adulthood suit. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 122, s.v. Aval achlu, however, imply that even if one tells an adult, "I returned it to you while you were a minor," the claim is still not regarded as brazen. According to them, where the claimant was a minor after his father's death, even a later adult suit on the father's claim could be defeated through a migo that the defendant returned the property while the claimant was still a minor. This would also resolve Tumim's doubt in the partial admission case cited above.
He admitted that he was a partner. Tumim questions whether, if a partner admits that some partnership money still remains, he should be treated as one returning lost property and thereby be exempt from the partners' oath. In my view he is certainly obligated in the partners' oath. Compare the custodial oath according to the Mechaber, which applies even where the custodian voluntarily admits part. The exemption of one returning lost property applies specifically where the admission itself creates the oath obligation, as in an ordinary partial admission. There, the Sages exempted him as a social policy so that people will not refrain from returning lost property or admitting what they owe. A custodial oath or partners' oath is different. Even if he had made no admission at all, the law would still impose the oath once the statutory conditions were met. Since his admission did not create the oath, the status of one returning lost property does not remove it. This also explains siman 88, seif 35. Sema, seif katan 97, labors to explain why, when the claimant demands selaim and dinarim and the borrower admits three, a hesset oath remains due, whereas if the borrower says, "You reminded me," he is exempt even from hesset. On the explanation given here, the distinction is clear. When the creditor claims five and the borrower admits three, even without the admission concerning the third unit the remaining denial would itself generate hesset, so the voluntary admission does not remove the oath. In the "you reminded me" case, however, without the claimant's reminder there would have been no oath at all. The liability to hesset is generated solely by the admission induced through the reminder, and the policy favoring restoration of lost property exempts him so that people will be willing to remember and return what is due.
Beit din shall appoint a guardian. A guardian is required because, as explained in siman 93, one does not become obligated in the partners' oath unless the claimant suspects him regarding at least two silver maah, meaning that there must be some evidentiary basis for suspicion, as Taz explains there. The minor therefore needs a guardian who can formulate and support the suspicion. A custodial oath does not require such a guardian. Tumim likewise writes this.
