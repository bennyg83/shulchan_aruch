As one who returns a lost article. This is the Rambam's language. There is an apparent difficulty: what difference does this make, since the Rambam holds that we do not accept a migo to exempt a person from an oath? And if the reason is that a minor's claim is not legally a claim at all, then the rationale should not be that the defendant resembles one who returns a lost article, but simply that there is no valid claim. See what I wrote in the rules of migo, rule 112. In any event, the Rambam's words prove that a minor's assertion is called a claim. Otherwise, there would be no need for the rationale of returning a lost article; he could simply have said that there is no claim. The same follows from Tosafot, who challenge Rashi's view that when a minor made the claim, if the original delivery occurred when he was already an adult it is considered a valid claim. Tosafot object that both elements are required, but they do not object that the minor's assertion is no claim at all. It must therefore be that it is considered a claim, and the exemption is only a Scriptural decree. The Ran writes this explicitly: a minor's claim is a definite claim, but Scripture decreed that it cannot impose an oath. Accordingly, if the father delivered the property while the son was a minor, the son witnessed the delivery, the father died, and the son then sued after reaching adulthood, an oath applies. Both the delivery and the claim relevant to the oath are associated with adulthood. The fact that his knowledge derives from something he saw while he was a minor is immaterial, because his assertion is itself a proper definite claim. The reason the Gemara does not establish the Mishnah's phrase "one swears to a minor" in such a case, where the observation occurred during minority, may be that the wording implies that the claimant is a minor at the time of the oath. We should not extend Rashi's position further than necessary by saying that an actual minor can impose the oath. There remains a question. If the minor sued while still a minor and the defendant admitted part, that proceeding has no effect. Later, after the claimant reached adulthood, he sued again. Do we say that now both the relevant delivery and the operative claim are associated with adulthood, so the oath applies? Or do we say that although the minor's original claim had no legal effect, the defendant's admission was still an admission, and therefore the admission preceded the later valid claim made in adulthood? This requires study. In any event, the Rambam's requirement that the claim precede the admission is itself disputed by other authorities. See the discussion in Gittin concerning the case of the two purses.
Only that the delivery occurred while he was a minor. If the father entrusted the item to a guardian and then died, leaving a minor heir, the Sema, following some of the authorities cited by the Maggid Mishneh, holds that the minor cannot impose the oath of a guardian. The Shach follows the Raavad and Ramah, who hold that the guardian does swear. It seems that according to the Rashba, who holds that even one who negligently mishandles property entrusted to him by a minor is exempt, as inferred from the wording "one does not swear," and Tosafot likewise hold this in many places, the law here is clear. If the father entrusted the property and then died, the guardian is certainly liable for negligence. His status as guardian cannot have disappeared merely because the heir became a minor. If the minor waited to sue until adulthood, the guardian would plainly be liable under all the laws of guardians. It must therefore be that the guardianship was never extinguished during the heir's minority, because if it had been extinguished it could not later revive when the heir became an adult. If liability for negligence remains, then the guardian's oath also remains, because according to these authorities the two rules depend upon one another, as is well known. Those authorities who disagree must therefore follow the Rambam, who separates liability for negligence from the oath. But according to our ruling above in siman 66, following Tosafot and those who agree with them, that liability for negligence and the oath are linked, the law is clearly as the Shach writes. One should not object that according to this reasoning the Rosh's question against the opinion that a minor can impose a guardian's oath remains unresolved. The Rosh asks why the Mishnah's phrase "one swears to a minor" is not established as a case of a guardian's oath. Why not instead establish it as a case where the father entrusted the item and the minor later sues on the basis of his father's entrustment, so there is no need to say that an actual minor can independently impose the oath, and the Mishnah could be understood according to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov? The answer is that we already have to explain why the Gemara does not establish the Mishnah as a case where the minor sues on the basis of one witness, according to the Rosh's view that such testimony can impose an oath. Evidently, an oath generated by the witness is not described as an oath "to the minor," because the oath is directed to the witness's evidence and is comparable to an oath imposed in favor of an adult. The same is true of a guardian where the father made the original entrustment. Since the minor's present demand has no independent legal force and the oath arises from the delivery made during the father's adulthood, it is not properly called an oath "to a minor." By contrast, if the minor himself delivered the property for safekeeping and could thereby impose a guardian's oath, the phrase would fit perfectly because the guardianship arose directly from the minor. Thus the Rosh's question is well founded. There is nevertheless room for doubt according to the Rosh and those who agree with him that we do accept a migo to exempt from an oath, and that a guardian lacks a migo only because denying receipt would be brazen. In a case involving a minor, must the property have been delivered in the presence of witnesses with an instruction not to return it except before witnesses, so that the guardian has neither a migo of "it never happened" nor a migo of "I returned it"? Otherwise he does possess such a migo. One cannot say that denying receipt would be brazen against the minor, because what brazenness is there in denying the assertion of a minor? Even concerning an adult there is a dispute whether one is brazen toward the claimant's son. With respect to a minor, the rationale is still weaker. This therefore requires study.
Beit din appoints a guardian, etc. With respect to guardians' oaths, which the Rambam also holds may be imposed on behalf of a minor, he does not write that beit din appoints a guardian. The distinction must be that the partner's oath requires that the claimant suspect the partner of at least two silver ma'ah, while a minor's suspicion has no legal weight. Beit din therefore appoints a guardian who investigates the partnership. If he finds grounds for suspicion amounting to two silver ma'ah, he can impose the oath. This also resolves the Rosh's difficulty. The Rosh asks why the Gemara does not explain the Mishnah's statement that "one swears to a minor" as referring to a guardian's oath. Yet he does not ask that the Mishnah could refer to the oath of partners, even though the Rosh agrees that such an oath may be imposed for a minor. It would be strained to answer that the Gemara understood "one swears to a minor" as referring specifically to a Torah oath because the Mishnah has not yet taught the oath of partners at that stage of the chapter. According to what was explained, the answer is straightforward. The minor himself does not impose the oath of partners. Beit din appoints an adult guardian to impose it, so the defendant is in fact swearing to the adult guardian rather than to the minor. The wording "one swears to a minor" therefore does not fit. However, the Rambam writes that where the defendant admits that he had been a partner, he must swear. If he further admits that some partnership assets are still in his possession, it requires study whether he should actually swear. Although we do not accept a migo to exempt from an oath, he resembles one who returns a lost article by voluntarily admitting an obligation to a minor. The Sages did not impose their enacted oath upon one who returns a lost article, as explained above at the end of siman 88. The practical ruling therefore requires study.
