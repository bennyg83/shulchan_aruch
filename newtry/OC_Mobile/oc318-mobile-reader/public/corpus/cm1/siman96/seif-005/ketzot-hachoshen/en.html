A mute person who is mentally competent in every respect but has lost speech presents the question how to take an oath. One view says he may write, "I swear with an oath that such and such is true," because writing is equivalent to speech. Another responsum rejects treating writing as speech. Tumim therefore suggests using the law of an oath imposed by others, as in the oath concerning a deposit: another person imposes the oath and the mute writes his denial, which should count as the relevant denial. Rashba's responsum concerning a mute widow is central. If she understands but cannot speak, she is legally competent. Head movement can sometimes function as speech, yet Rashba says that where an oath is required, mere nodding is insufficient, because perhaps she intended the opposite. Even with an imposed oath, in the laws of sotah and testimony an "Amen" or equivalent acceptance is needed. Rashba nevertheless inclines to allow a widow collecting from orphans, because that oath is only rabbinic, to be sworn by others where she cannot answer Amen. This does not prove that a Torah oath can never be imposed upon a mute person. Rashba's concern is that an oath imposed by others requires acceptance. One cannot force witnesses to accept an oath they reject. If the mute person writes clearly that he denies the claim, that writing can demonstrate acceptance of the oath imposed upon him even if writing does not itself constitute the oath formula. Tumim's specific formulation, however, requires correction. In an oath imposed by others concerning testimony, the claimant says, "I adjure you to come and testify," and concerning a deposit, "I adjure you to return my deposit." The other person's subsequent refusal then creates liability. One cannot simply say, "I adjure you that you do not owe me," and thereby force the person to swear about a past fact. That kind of factual oath ordinarily requires the swearer's own utterance or acceptance. Rashi's explanation of the Mishnah in Shevuat HaEdut resolves Tosafot's question: when the witnesses first said they knew nothing and the claimant then said, "I adjure you that what you said is true," their answering Amen is necessary because this is no longer merely an order to testify but a factual oath adopting their denial. In any event, even if an imposed oath concerning a deposit can create sacrificial liability, it does not follow that it discharges a judicial oath. A judicial oath requires the specific oath form administered by beit din. A person does not satisfy it through a ban, a vow, or another form of prohibition, and likewise not merely through the technical law of an oath imposed by others. In addition, several authorities require the explicit Name or substitute Name for the sacrificial oath of deposit, a practice avoided in our times. Tosafot also requires a designated object in certain loan related applications of the deposit oath. Finally, if an imposed oath could replace the judicial oath, the entire rule of "fifty I know and fifty I do not know" would collapse, since beit din could simply impose, "I adjure you to return what you owe," and accept the response of uncertainty. The same is true of the difference between Torah and rabbinic oaths regarding descent to assets. The conclusion is therefore that an oath concerning a deposit can in principle take effect when imposed by another, but the formal judicial oath ordinarily requires the litigant's own oath or explicit acceptance. Rashba's words support using the more lenient imposed form only for a rabbinic collection oath when the person cannot speak.
