A deaf-mute and an incompetent person, etc. Regarding a competent person who is mute, whose speech has been taken from him but who is mentally competent in every respect, and who conducted a transaction and became obligated to swear, the author of Shevut Yaakov writes in part 1, siman 156, that he should write, "I swear with an oath and imprecation that such and such is the case," and that writing is equivalent to speech. The substance of his position and his proof are taken from the responsum Chavot Yair, siman 194, which holds that if a person takes an oath in writing to do a certain act, the writing creates an oath or vow just as spoken words do. His proof is that Scripture had to exclude written testimony by saying that witnesses testify "from their mouths" and not from their writing. This implies that in other contexts writing is equivalent to speech. The author of Shevut Yaakov, however, in his responsa, Yoreh Deah part, siman 49, ruled to the contrary and held that generally writing is not equivalent to speech. He brought proof from the Gemara in Megillah, which states that one who writes a Megillah does not thereby fulfill the obligation of reading it. This proof has no substance. Quite apart from the fact that accepting something upon oneself through writing has nothing to do with a Torah requirement of verbal reading for the publicizing of the miracle, if writing counted as reading then a scribe who writes the paragraph of Shema would fulfill the mitzvah of reciting Shema according to the authority who does not require that one hear one's own voice. The two matters are unrelated. Moreover, the Megillah itself says "remembered," and the Gemara on Megillah 18a derives a verbal analogy from "remember" and teaches that remembering is with the mouth while forgetting is in the heart. Since remembrance there specifically requires the mouth, writing plainly cannot suffice. But where a person accepts an obligation upon himself, it may well be that writing and speaking are equivalent. What can he answer according to the Rambam and those who agree with him, who hold that a person capable of speaking may testify in writing? This itself shows that writing can be treated like speech, as appears from the Gemara in Gittin. Shevut Yaakov's rejection of the proof from "from their mouths," namely that the verse was necessary because testimony is merely a matter of revealing a fact and might otherwise have been accepted in writing, requires study. It is difficult to use such a speculative distinction to permit a prohibition. A stronger explanation would be that since we see that a signed document is valid, that a document of kiddushin is valid with witnesses, and that a get is valid with witnesses according to Rabbi Meir, or according to Rabbi Eliezer as understood by those authorities who hold that even witnesses to the delivery are decisive, one might have thought that the Torah validates testimony through writing generally. The distinction between a legal document and ordinary written testimony is indeed subtle, and the commentators labored greatly to define it. See what I wrote above in siman 46. This may explain Rashi's wording when he writes, regarding "from their mouths and not from their writing," that they must testify in beit din, and that this is unlike a document signed on the day of the transaction, whose normal manner is through writing. Rashi's added phrase requires explanation. If his only purpose were to answer the obvious question from legal documents, he should have explained it already in Yevamot, which appears before Gittin, rather than waiting until Gittin. According to what was stated, it is understandable. Rashi was troubled that Rav could have brought proof from the very fact that Scripture needed the phrase "from their mouths," which suggests that elsewhere writing counts as verbal declaration, and this is especially difficult for one who does not rule like Rav. Rashi therefore explains that testimony is unlike a formal document, which the Torah separately validated in its normal written form. The verse was needed lest we equate ordinary written testimony with such a document. Nevertheless, the Rashba's formulation in Gittin, that the exclusion of testimony through writing is a Scriptural decree, sounds as though writing is effective in other contexts. If the verse merely came to prevent us from mistakenly extending the special law of documents, it would not properly be called a Scriptural decree. Likewise, Tosafot and the Rashba ask why the phrase "from their mouths" is necessary, since Scripture already says "if he does not tell," and a mute person is incapable of verbal telling, just as the Gemara derives from "and she shall say" that a mute woman is excluded. This suggests that testimony is treated like other forms of speech. Otherwise their question would have no force, because testimony might have been uniquely lenient. Even so, Maharam of Rothenburg, the Mabit, Mishpetei Shmuel, and Chavot Yair himself in siman 16 write that with respect to an oath Scripture says "whatever a person shall express with his lips," "whatever proceeds from his mouth he shall do," and "he shall not profane his word." This leaves no genuine doubt. It is stronger than the phrase "from their mouths" written regarding testimony. I am therefore surprised that Shevut Yaakov remained uncertain about it. How could "whatever he expresses with his lips" be weaker than "from their mouths"? And they. The author of Shevut Yaakov brought proof from Sotah 27b, where the Gemara derives from "and she shall say" that a mute woman does not drink the sotah water. He argues that otherwise the Gemara should have excluded her simply because she cannot take the oath, and from the fact that a separate verse is needed he infers that an oath can be accepted through writing. But he brought evidence against himself. He saw one proof in the Gemara but did not look at Tosafot there, in the passage beginning "for it is written," who state explicitly that even if the verse "and she shall say" were not required literally, one would still agree that a mute woman does not drink because she cannot accept the oath. Thus Tosafot expressly state that a mute person cannot accept an oath at all. The verse is cited only for completeness. Their ruling should decide the matter. This is clear with respect to an oath of utterance, where Scripture says "whatever a person shall express." Whether the same principle is learned for other kinds of oaths might appear doubtful, although logic strongly favors learning one oath from another unless there is a basis to distinguish them. Shevut Yaakov nevertheless raises a serious question. Rashi explains "if he does not tell" as excluding a mute witness, who cannot testify, from the liability of an oath concerning testimony. Why is a verse needed to exclude him from that offering? If a mute person cannot speak at all, he cannot take an oath in the first place. At first sight this is a strong question, but in truth there is no difficulty. With an ordinary oath of utterance, if a person is placed under oath by someone else, the one being bound must answer amen, and only then does the oath take effect. With an oath concerning testimony, however, if one says in beit din, "I impose an oath upon you that if you know testimony you shall come and testify for me," and the witness remains silent, without answering amen, and withholds the testimony, he is liable for the offering for an oath concerning testimony. Therefore, if a mute witness could validly testify in writing, another person could impose the oath upon him by saying, "If you know testimony, testify for me." If he then failed to provide valid written testimony, and certainly if he wrote that he knew no testimony, he would have made a complete denial. If written testimony were valid, once he had testified he could not retract, so a written denial would be a full withholding of testimony. He should therefore be liable for the offering. There would be no distinction between a witness who can speak but remains silent and one who cannot speak and remains silent. In an oath concerning testimony, his silence can itself generate liability because no utterance by the witness is required. The person imposing the oath voices the oath, the witness hears it, and then denies or withholds his testimony. The Torah treats this as though the witness himself were sworn. The same reasoning applies to a mute witness. The verbal expression of the oath came from the mouth of the person imposing it, and the mute witness remained silent. Scripture therefore needed a specific exclusion for a mute person because he is not legally fit to testify, so there can be no oath concerning testimony at all. By contrast, if the mute witness acts on his own and writes, "I swear that I know no testimony for you," no verse is needed to exclude him, because an oath requires expression with the lips. This is clear and correct. The same principle applies to an oath concerning a deposit. We rule there too that one placed under oath by another need not answer amen. If he denies the claim and does not admit the deposit, he is liable for the offering. The person imposing the oath is the one who expresses the oath with his lips, and the Torah imposes liability upon the person who hears it and denies the obligation. Therefore, if a mute person writes on his own, "I swear that I do not have your deposit or loan in my possession," there is no oath, because an oath requires verbal expression. But if another person says to him, "I impose an oath upon you that you do not possess money, a deposit, or the like belonging to so-and-so," and the mute person writes that he has nothing belonging to him, this is a denial. He can certainly make an admission in writing, because a written admission by a litigant is effective according to everyone. All the more so when he writes expressly that he has nothing, which is a complete denial. For monetary admission and denial, his handwriting is plainly equivalent to speech. In such a case there is therefore a valid oath, and he is liable for an oath concerning a deposit just as a person capable of speech would be. His own verbal response is unnecessary. The oath was expressed by the lips of another person, and the denial was made by the defendant himself in a legally effective written form. Consequently, Shevut Yaakov was incorrect in saying that the mute defendant should write, "I hereby swear." That writing accomplishes nothing. Rather, another person must impose the oath upon him orally, such as, "I impose an oath upon you that you have money belonging to so-and-so and must admit it." If the mute person then writes that he has nothing, the oath takes effect through the other person's verbal expression, while the defendant's written response supplies the denial. Written admission and denial are certainly effective in monetary law. This is the correct practical rule. This also resolves the Yerushalmi in the chapter concerning the Kohen Gadol. It asks why a Kohen Gadol is not judged and answers by suggesting that he appoint a representative. The Yerushalmi then asks: suppose the Kohen Gadol becomes obligated to swear, can the representative swear for him? If a written oath were valid, this would be difficult. The Kohen Gadol could simply write in recognizable handwriting, "I swear that such and such is the case," send it to beit din, and it would be equivalent to swearing before beit din without any public degradation. According to what was explained, there is no difficulty. A self-imposed oath cannot be taken in writing. It would require another person to impose the oath upon him, which necessarily makes the Kohen Gadol participate in the judicial proceeding and creates the concern for his dignity. There remains some room for doubt concerning a person who is not mute and who writes an oath. According to Rabbenu Tam, such writing may perhaps be effective. Regarding testimony, Scripture expressly says "from their mouths," yet Rabbenu Tam holds that one who is capable of oral testimony may testify from writing, based on the principle that where a person is fit for the required act, actual performance of that form is not indispensable. Written testimony of a person capable of speaking is therefore treated as oral testimony. The same might apply to an oath. Although Scripture says "expression of the lips," one who is capable of speaking might have his writing treated as a verbal oath. Why should "from their mouths" regarding testimony differ from "expression of the lips" regarding an oath? This may also explain why later authorities write that one who writes words of Torah does not recite the blessing on Torah study, because the Torah says "you shall speak of them." Rabbenu Tam's rule itself is disputed, and in a case of doubtful blessings we are lenient. But with a doubtful oath, where the concern is a Torah prohibition, we are stringent and might have to consider that the law follows Rabbenu Tam. I did think to prove that writing is not treated like verbal utterance from the Gemara in Temurah. The Gemara asks why one who curses another with Hashem's name receives lashes, since this is a prohibition without an action. It answers from the verse "Hashem will make your plagues extraordinary," which teaches that the Torah nevertheless imposed lashes. This is difficult. Even after that verse, how do we know the Torah departed from the general rule that one does not receive lashes for a prohibition without an action? Perhaps the verse refers to a case with an action, where one writes, "So-and-so shall be cursed by this Name." Writing is certainly an action and was a skilled labor in the Mishkan. It must be that where the Torah specifically requires the mouth, writing is not included, so no case of cursing with an action exists. This would support the distinction. Even so, the matter requires study, because one should not rely upon a novel proof whose root is not established in the poskim. It is also possible that, as I wrote above in siman 28, Rabbenu Tam agrees that written testimony is ineffective in capital cases and permits it only in monetary cases. If so, his reasoning there may be that witnesses in monetary law were created primarily to clarify disputed facts. Outside that special setting, writing would not equal speech. Otherwise it should also work in capital cases. There is still another question. If the sole reason that a written oath of utterance is ineffective is the verse requiring "expression with the lips," consider the rule of Maharik, shoresh 112, cited by the Rama above in siman 73, seif 8. Maharik writes that the rule that unexpressed thoughts do not create an oath applies where a person independently prohibits something upon himself. But where a person makes a condition with another and swears to fulfill that condition, we follow even the intention in his heart, because whatever the condition encompasses is encompassed by the oath, and no separate expression of the lips is required. According to that rule, if a person makes a written agreement with another to perform or pay something and writes an oath to fulfill it, perhaps the oath is valid. In this setting Maharik says that "expression with the lips" is unnecessary. If even matters in the heart are effective, writing, which is certainly stronger than unexpressed thought, should be effective all the more so. It is true that the Shach disputes Maharik above in that siman. See what I wrote there in siman 73 defending Maharik from the responsum of the Rosh. If my defense is correct, one should not automatically compare ordinary writing to the special rule there. Nevertheless, according to Maharik himself, where an oath accompanies a condition and no separate verbal expression is required, the matter remains doubtful. The issue tends toward a possible Torah prohibition. Shevut Yaakov did not address this point. Since its proper place is in the laws of oaths, I have been brief here, and with Hashem's help it will be treated there.
