With respect to documents, one does not swear. The Rashba explains in a responsum that the purpose of every such oath is to appease the claimant's mind, and the Sages do not enact one enactment upon another enactment. The Sema understands the case as follows: the minor claims, "I delivered documents to you," and the defendant admits some of them. Although in the preceding siman we rule that an adult claimant can impose a hesset oath in such a case, a minor cannot. The Shach objects that we do not find that the oath in such a case was enacted merely to appease the claimant. He therefore understands the Rashba to refer to a creditor who impairs his document by acknowledging partial payment, or to a case where one witness testifies that the document was paid. The Gemara in Ketubot, chapter HaKotev, states that these oaths are imposed to appease the debtor's mind. Thus, if the creditor says that he was paid during the father's lifetime while the debt was still within its due date, or if he impaired the document, or one witness testifies to payment, a minor heir could collect without an oath. The Shach's statement that the oath of one who impairs his document also rests on appeasing the debtor is incorrect. Tosafot in Shevuot 49a, in the passage beginning "For everyone," indicate explicitly otherwise. Maharam likewise writes expressly in his glosses to Hagahot Maimoniyot concerning the laws of oaths that Rashi did not explain the oath of one who impairs his document as an oath taken while holding a sacred object because there the reason is not merely to appease the debtor. See there. Even with respect to a single witness, however, the Shach's explanation is difficult. Since an objective weakness exists before us because a witness testifies that the debt was paid, why should the creditor collect without an oath? Tosafot in the passage beginning "For everyone" explain that the hired worker's oath, which is also imposed to appease the employer, is lighter than the oath imposed where one witness testifies to payment. Therefore gilgul shevuah does not apply to the hired worker's oath, while it does apply to the oath against one witness. Nevertheless, above in siman 89, seif 2, we rule that if the employer is a minor, the hired worker still cannot collect without an oath. Why should the case of one witness be different? Plainly, whenever the law is that one swears and collects, we do not allow collection without the required oath. The same is indicated by the oath of partners and the Rambam's concluding phrase, "and similarly in every analogous case." The commentators explain that an oath imposed by the Mishnah was not excluded merely because the claimant is a minor. I initially thought that the Rashba might refer to a case without impairment of the document and without one witness testifying to payment, where the minor simply claims that the debt was paid and says, "Swear to me." In such a case, if the debtor did not demand an oath, beit din would not impose one on its own. This indicates that the oath exists principally to appease the debtor's mind. Indeed, the Rif records authorities who hold that this is merely a hesset level oath. A minor's assertion has no legal force for that purpose, because such an oath depends entirely upon his demand and exists only to appease him. Even so, I was not satisfied. What second enactment upon a first enactment is involved? The matter is merely an inversion of procedure. If the creditor were first paid and the debtor then claimed, "I paid you twice," the creditor would be required to swear hesset. All the more so according to the Shach's repeated rule that hesset is never found before payment. If the present discussion is before payment, no hesset would apply in any event. If it is after payment, what prevents the debtor from suing and requiring the creditor to swear that he was not paid twice? It therefore appears more correct to understand the Rashba as the Sema did, namely as a dispute concerning possession of the documents themselves, where no hesset is imposed for a minor. The Shach's objection, that we have not found that such an oath was enacted to appease the claimant, is not compelling. Tosafot and the Rosh infer that hesset applies to land and documents from the rule that where a creditor impairs his document or one witness testifies that it was paid, the Sages imposed an oath even regarding land and documents. This demonstrates that the same is true of hesset. But if appeasing the claimant had no relevance to hesset concerning land or documents, how could those cases serve as proof? In the case of one witness, absent the policy of appeasing the debtor, the Sages would not have enacted an oath where the subject matter is land or documents. How then could that enactment prove that hesset was imposed there? Rather, one must say that hesset too was instituted to appease the claimant, who cries out that the property is his and the like. The central reason for the enactment is therefore appeasement. With a minor, the Sages did not impose an additional enactment upon that enactment. This is straightforward. The Rashba happened to mention documents because the Beit Yosef quoted his responsum in answer to a question involving documents. The same rule applies to land, and there is no difficulty.
