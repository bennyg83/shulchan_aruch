Maharik writes that a minor's admission has no ordinary force, since the Talmud repeatedly treats a minor's legal acts as lacking substance, and testimony cannot ordinarily be accepted in his presence as though he were an adult litigant. Only where the minor receives a concrete benefit can his admission sometimes bind. Rambam similarly rules in Hilchot To'en. A difficulty arises from Perek Zeh Borer, where a minor litigated before Rav Nachman. Rav Nachman asked whether he had witnesses or proof, and after the minor answered no, ruled against him. When others later said they knew evidence concerning his father, Rav Nachman reopened the case because a minor does not know his father's affairs. This implies that if he had been expected to know, his statement that he had no proof could bind him. Rosh understands the original episode as involving an actual minor, and even Rambam's broader rule concerning one who later reached majority does not remove that difficulty. The present Rambam is not decisive, however. In the case he discusses, even if witnesses established the child's tort he would be exempt because minors are not responsible for damages. Rambam mentions admission only to teach that where the child received a benefit, liability can arise. He does not establish a general rule about a minor's admission in a case where witnesses would otherwise create liability. The matter therefore still requires study.
