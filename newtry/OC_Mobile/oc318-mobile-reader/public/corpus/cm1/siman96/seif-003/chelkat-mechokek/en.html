But if her heirs seized, they inherit her ketubah. There are three forms of seizure. First, if the woman herself seized during her lifetime, such as where particular movables were designated for her ketubah, she does not require an oath, so the rule that a person cannot transmit an oath to his children does not apply. Second, if the widow seized property of her husband, but the heirs are not presently in possession, as in Terumat HaDeshen, siman 330. Third, if her heirs seized after her death. In Choshen Mishpat 108 two kinds of seizure are explained. One is where the debt document was secured by a pledge and their father was holding it; then he could collect without an oath, and his heirs likewise collect. Rambam writes similarly that if a lender on a pledge dies after the borrower, the lender's heirs collect from the pledge in their possession. A second rule is stated there in the Tur: if the lender's heirs seized first, we do not remove it from them. Ran says so in the name of the French rabbis, and Sefer HaTerumot agrees. This is also logical, because a judge who ruled like Rabbi Elazar has acted validly; if the heirs execute such a ruling for themselves, who will remove the money from them? A less clear case is where the lender himself was in possession during his lifetime, the borrower died first, then the lender, but after the lender's death his heirs are no longer in possession and must come to extract from the borrower's heirs. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 330, discusses a case where the widow seized during life but her heirs did not, and the money lay with beit din. He suggests that where neither side is in possession and the money is held for both, the woman's heirs should prevail because they are not extracting; at minimum they should divide. He stated this only where the woman herself had possessed it, not the heirs. If the heirs themselves are in possession, then if they seized, they certainly retain it. Maharam, however, implies there that the heirs' possession must be complete, meaning that the property was not shown to witnesses, or the husband's heirs cannot prove it belonged to him. If witnesses identify the property as the husband's, their seizure is ineffective because wherever the property is, it remains legally in the husband's domain. Nevertheless, the Tur's simple statement that if the heirs seized first their seizure is effective implies that even seizure witnessed by others can suffice.
