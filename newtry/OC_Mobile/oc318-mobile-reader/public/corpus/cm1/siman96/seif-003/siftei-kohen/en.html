Regarding documents, one does not administer an oath. The Sema, seif katan 12, explains this as a claim for several documents with a partial admission. In my view, the reference is to oaths connected to collection upon a document, such as where the creditor impairs his document by admitting partial payment, or one witness testifies that it was paid. This explains Rashba's reason that such an oath is a rabbinic enactment intended to satisfy the claimant's concern. The Gemara expressly says at the end of the chapter HaKotev, Ketubot 87b, that the oath connected with a document was enacted to satisfy the opposing party. That language does not fit the Sema's case of a claim for documents with a partial admission. We do not find the phrase “only to satisfy the claimant” used for that ordinary type of oath. This is also why Rashba and the Mechaber specifically mention documents and not slaves or land. Rashba writes that imposing a rabbinic oath of denial in response to a minor's claim is itself an enactment, and the Sages did not create a second enactment upon an enactment. The rabbinic oath in favor of a minor was instituted so that people would not take a minor's money with impunity. It applies where the oath enables the defendant to be exempt. In the case of a document, however, the creditor may not collect until he swears and must swear before payment. The Sages did not add that document-related oath on top of the special enactment for a minor. Accordingly, if a person held a valid document against a minor's father in circumstances where the minor is obligated to pay, such as where the father admitted the debt and died during his illness, as discussed below at the beginning of siman 108, seif 3, and the minor later claims that he personally paid the creditor and demands an oath, or produces one witness that payment was made, the creditor does not swear before collection. The minor must pay, and afterward he may impose a rabbinic oath of denial upon the creditor. The Sema's interpretation, that the minor claims documents and the defendant admits some of them, is incorrect in my view, both textually and legally. For purposes of a rabbinic oath of denial there is no distinction between documents and other property. Since such an oath is imposed in response to a minor's claim to prevent others from taking his money, the same rule applies when the disputed property consists of documents. There is no basis to distinguish documents from other items. Nor can one say that this would be an enactment upon an enactment, because the law of a rabbinic oath of denial itself makes no distinction between documents and other property. This is clear. Consider it carefully.
