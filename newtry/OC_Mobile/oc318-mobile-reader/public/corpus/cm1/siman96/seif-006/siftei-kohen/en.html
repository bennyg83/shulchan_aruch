We administer an oath to her. This implies that a married woman is fully equivalent to a man and may be required to swear immediately in every case. The Bach writes that this applies only to a rabbinic oath, but not to a Torah oath. His distinction is incorrect. In particular, he writes that the responsum of the Rosh cited by the Tur in seif 21 concerns a rabbinic oath, since the Rosh states that the woman must also swear that the item is not in her possession, and such an oath is a rabbinic enactment. This is astonishing. It is only when a custodian wishes to pay that the oath that the object is not in his possession is rabbinic, because we suspect that he may have set his eyes upon it, as stated above in siman 72, seif 15. When he does not pay, however, the oath that the object is not in his possession is a Torah oath, as the Rosh himself writes in the chapter HaMafkid and as the Tur rules below at the beginning of siman 294. In my view, the difficulty raised by Beit Yosef should be answered as follows. The Tur's citation of Rav Hai concerns a case in which even if the woman admitted the claim she would not have to pay immediately, such as where she borrowed money and it is known that she already spent it. Even if she admits, she is not liable to pay until she is divorced. Likewise, the Semag and the other authorities who state that even regarding a deposit she is not required to swear refer to a case where it is known that the deposit was lost and the only proposed oath is that she was not negligent. Since even an admission of negligence would not produce immediate payment, there is no need to administer the oath, as is evident from the responsum of the Rosh cited by Rabbenu Yerucham. By contrast, the responsum of the Rosh cited by the Tur in seif 21 expressly concerns borrowed utensils. The Rosh therefore ruled that she must swear that the utensils are not in her possession, because if she admits that they are in her possession she must return them immediately. He did not rule that she should swear that she was not negligent, because even if she admitted negligence she would not pay immediately. The responsum of the Rosh cited by the Tur in seif 25 concerns a case in which there is reason to suspect that she possesses money over which her husband has no authority. The responsum to the Gaon cited in seif 23 likewise concerns cases where she is required to swear in the hope that she will admit that the property is still with her, for then she must pay immediately. Baal HaTerumot, gate 36, part 1, similarly writes that a married woman is required to swear when there is reason to suspect that the deposited or borrowed money remains under her control. This also appears from Ir Shushan, which distinguishes as I have written. If the claimant suspects that she has money over which her husband has no authority, she swears like other borrowers who are required to swear in response to an uncertain claim. See there. This is contrary to the Bach, who writes categorically that she is never required to swear unless it is known that she possesses money over which her husband has no authority. According to what I have written, all the objections of Beit Yosef are properly resolved. See Hagahot Maimoniyot at the end of chapter 8 of Hilchot To'en regarding the oath of a married woman, and Responsa Mabit, part 1, second collection, siman 42, folio 118, column 4, and part 2, siman 182. Even though she conducts business within the household, contrary to those who disagree. This follows the Darchei Moshe, who cites the Mordechai in the chapter HaChovel. Raavan ruled that since women in our times commonly conduct business, they are regarded as though their husbands appointed them as agents. If witnesses establish the transaction, the husbands must pay, by virtue of the commercial enactment that enables others to transact with such women. If there are no witnesses, the woman must swear. Hagahot Ashri in the chapter HaChovel states the same in the name of Raavyah. Maharik, root 192, writes that Maharam in the Mordechai and the other authorities disagree with Raavan, and that even Raavan spoke only of a small amount, not a large one. Since the other authorities disagree, money cannot be extracted on the basis of doubt. In my view, however, Maharik's analysis in that responsum is incorrect. It is clear that Maharam also agrees with Raavan. Maharam discusses an ordinary woman, whereas Raavan discusses a woman who actually manages the household's business. Maharik also argues that the fact that the other authorities did not mention this rule indicates that they reject it. This is no proof, for they speak of ordinary women, as I wrote above in siman 62, seif katan 8. It is wholly unreasonable that a woman who manages the household should receive money from others with her husband's knowledge, that both spouses should consume and benefit from that money, and that they should nevertheless owe nothing. Likewise, in matters that women customarily purchase, such as modest household necessities, the law governing an ordinary woman is plainly the same as that governing a woman who manages the household. Maharshal writes similarly in the chapter HaChovel, siman 29. He adds, however, that he found a responsum of the Rosh, general principle 13, siman 11, stating that Maharam did not rule like the great authorities who imposed liability because of the commercial enactment. I cited that responsum at the end of chapter 1, siman 59. In my view, the responsum of the Rosh is no proof against this distinction. The question there concerned a woman who managed the household, vowed to give charity, or hired a teacher, while her husband objected. The Rosh answered that the authorities disagreed. Some great authorities ruled that her sale and purchase were valid, and that her business transactions were binding because of the commercial enactment, but Maharam did not rule that way. Maharam declined to rule that way because the husband objected. Moreover, even where he did not initially object, Maharam may hold that he can object afterward. Even if she is regarded as his agent, he can cancel a transaction from which he receives no benefit, just as a principal may tell an agent, “I sent you to improve matters, not to impair them,” as ruled below in siman 182. But when she regularly conducts business and the husband remains silent and does not object, it is clear that we presume he consented to her acts and benefited from the money. He must therefore pay, since she manages the household. See Responsa Rabbi Moshe Alshich, siman 19. Maharik further writes that since Maharam disagrees with Raavan, Reuven cannot retain his father's property, because where the authorities dispute the matter the property remains in its presumptive possession. He cites an even stronger ruling of Maharam in the responsum beginning “Serach daughter of Asher,” where a litigant was not believed to retain books. This comparison is incorrect. Here, where the authorities dispute the law, the possessor may plead that he follows the authorities who support him, as Maharik himself writes in roots 22 and 161, and as I explained in Tekfo Kohen, siman 82. Perhaps Maharik referred there to real property, but the plain meaning of his words does not indicate this. The primary ruling is therefore as Maharshal states in the chapter HaChovel, siman 29. A husband must pay obligations incurred by his wife to the extent that she customarily conducts the household's business. In our time, husbands must pay even for ordinary women's small household transactions when the woman is legally liable, because of the commercial enactment. Everything depends upon the beit din's assessment. Some capable women support their husbands and control all of the husband's property. In such a case, even a large transaction must plainly be paid in full, according to the circumstances, local custom, and the judges' sound discretion. Rabbi Moshe Alshich similarly writes in responsum 19 that if women customarily transact in large amounts, their acts are valid even for large amounts. He presents this as Raavan's view, but I have already explained that Maharam does not disagree. All this applies to a loan or deposit from which the household derived benefit. If, however, the woman was negligent with a deposit, cast it into a fire, caused bodily injury, or committed a similar tort, the husband is exempt, as stated in Hagahot Ashri in the chapter HaChovel in the name of Raavyah.
