A married woman, etc. See Beit Yosef, who questions the Geonim who hold that one does not administer an oath to a married woman because Scripture states, "the oath of Hashem shall be between the two of them." Beit Yosef asks what proof there is from this verse. If the point is merely that the verse uses masculine language, the entire Torah ordinarily speaks in the masculine. It appears that the Geonim understood the phrase "between the two of them" to teach that no third person is needed at the oath. The oath is to be administered privately between the two litigants, for at the time of an oath the others are told to withdraw, since taking Hashem's name publicly is not an honor to Heaven. The Torah therefore says that the oath is to be between the two alone and in a private setting. A married woman cannot be placed in such a setting, because seclusion is forbidden. If she is a woman of improper reputation, even greater safeguards may be required. Thus the condition "between the two of them" cannot be fulfilled in the ordinary manner, and the Geonim therefore excluded a married woman from this oath.
Who became obligated, etc. See Urim, where I wrote that the Shach and the other later authorities are correct that where there is no realistic possibility that she can presently pay, there is no reason to administer an oath at all. The case of a wife who became her husband's guardian and whom some authorities permit the husband to make swear at any time, even while she is still married to him, is different. The husband owes her ketubah, and whatever she admits can be deducted from that ketubah. She therefore has a present source from which payment can be made. The Shach cites Responsa Rosh concerning a woman who borrowed utensils from another woman and they were stolen. The Rosh required her to swear only that they were no longer in her possession, but did not require an oath that she had not been negligent or used them improperly. The Shach explains that if she admitted negligence she still could not presently pay, so there would be no point in such an oath. I am not satisfied with this. Once we are already administering an oath, why should the additional issue not be included through gilgul shevuah, especially where the added matter is itself only an uncertain claim? If later she acquires money, how could one then administer an oath concerning negligence when the underlying claim would be only uncertain and the principal oath had been completed long before? Gilgul shevuah is Torah law. More fundamentally, however, the Shach's reading of the Rosh is difficult. What oath of nonnegligence could this woman take? She was a borrower, and the utensils were stolen. A borrower is liable even for unavoidable accidents. There is therefore no uncertainty whether she owes payment. The Rosh expressly says that beit din should write a judgment establishing her liability to pay. The only oath relevant to her is that the objects are not presently in her possession, because if they are, immediate return is possible. The other usual custodian oaths have no place. The Rosh's requirement that the husband swear that he was not negligent and did not use the objects improperly must mean that the husband himself was not a borrower. Yet since he knew the utensils were brought into his house, he accepted at least the responsibility of an unpaid custodian, and therefore those oaths apply to him. With respect to the woman herself there is no additional oath. Thus there is no basis here for the broader rule derived by the Shach.
When she is widowed, etc. See Urim, where I wrote that a ketubah cannot ordinarily be sold effectively because, as against her husband, a wife is presumed ready to waive it. But if she owes another person and has sworn to pay him, then according to the ruling below at the end of siman 97 that beit din compels a person to take every reasonable step necessary to fulfill an oath to pay, there is room to say that beit din can compel her to sell the contingent value of the ketubah and swear to the purchaser that she will not waive it to her husband. Tosafos asks how one can appraise what a person would pay for a ketubah, since a purchaser has no secure acquisition if she can later waive it. Tosafos answers that she can obligate herself by oath not to waive it. In the case of an ordinary woman who injured another, beit din cannot compel her to take such an oath. Here, however, she has already sworn to pay, and beit din compels her to fulfill that oath through every available means. It is therefore plausible that beit din can compel the sale together with the supporting oath. Indeed, one might ask even more strongly why she should not be compelled to do this simply in order to pay her debt, with physical coercion until she agrees to swear at the time of sale. The rule that one who injures another may have no available property to collect from does not resolve this. A woman who intentionally injured another is ordinarily disqualified from taking an oath, because one who raises a hand against another is suspected, and certainly one who actually inflicted injury. Such a woman cannot be trusted to take the oath to the purchaser, nor should beit din facilitate that oath. One cannot answer that she repented, for if she has the means to sell her ketubah and swear not to waive it, genuine repentance would require her to use that means to pay the damages. Perhaps the case includes inadvertent injury. A person is always liable for damage, whether accidental or intentional, but an accidental tortfeasor does not thereby become suspected regarding oaths. Still, this is somewhat strained because the sources could have specified the distinction. The matter therefore requires further study.
Unlike the dissenting authorities. See Urim, seif katan 17, where I wrote that common practice follows the dissenting authorities. Nevertheless, the Rama in Even HaEzer, siman 85, rules that where a wife conducts the household business and says that movable property belongs to another, she is believed if she has a migo that she could have given it to that person. He does not consider this a migo against the chazakah that property in the possession of a wife conducting household business belongs to her husband. If so, why is that particular migo needed? Even if witnesses saw the property, so that she could no longer give away the very objects secretly, she should have a migo that she could issue a handwritten obligation acknowledging a debt of one thousand dinarim to that other person, who could then collect from the husband's estate. Responsa Rosh, rule 68, expressly indicates that once witnesses saw the property and she has no migo that she gave the objects themselves, she is no longer believed, even though she conducts the household business. This is difficult. One might therefore say that the Rama's difficulty here led him to rule like the authorities who hold that the husband is not liable for such obligations of the wife merely because she conducts the household business. Yet according to our practical ruling against the Rama on that point, the question returns. It is strained to distinguish that a migo is effective against the chazakah only when it concerns the very object, whereas a migo arising from another source does not overcome the chazakah. Similar distinctions are made regarding special credibility clauses, but applying them here is not fully satisfactory. More importantly, my heart is uneasy with a rule that would destroy the position of orphans. A wife who conducted the household business could conspire after her husband's death by issuing handwritten acknowledgments for thousands of dinarim to others, who would then collect the entire estate and leave the orphans empty handed. Since this whole liability is itself rooted in market protection, it would be strange for that enactment to become a curse for orphans. It therefore appears that because many authorities disagree with the basic rule, and the Rama accepted it only in a limited form, we should require clear witnesses or other proof that the loan to the wife actually occurred during the husband's lifetime. A loan made during his life normally has publicity, the husband can learn of it and demand an accounting, and it is fair to say that he accepted the commercial consequences. But if after the husband's death someone produces only the wife's handwritten acknowledgment, and there is no independent proof that the loan occurred while the husband lived, we must suspect collusion. Perhaps she wrote it only now and antedated it into her husband's lifetime. Even if the instrument contains all customary clauses of credibility and is still within its ostensible term, it should not collect from the husband's estate. In a place of collusion we do not extract from orphans. The lender caused his own difficulty by failing to lend before witnesses. We should not extend market protection beyond its clear boundaries. This also answers the Rosh case. The wife could not have created an effective migo by issuing a handwritten acknowledgment after the fact, because without independent proof that the debt arose during the husband's lifetime it would not collect from his estate. A retroactive migo that she could have issued such a writing while he was alive does not help. This appears correct as practical law, though the judge must evaluate the circumstances carefully for the sake of Heaven. The Shach follows Hagahot HaRosh in Perek HaChovel, which says that one who lends or entrusts property to a wife who now conducts the household business may collect from the husband if she consumed the property, but if she threw it into the fire or was negligent the husband is exempt. I do not understand this distinction. If the entire reason for the husband's liability is that she acts as his agent in commercial matters, then if a person says, "Give one hundred to my agent," and the agent throws it into the fire, why should the principal be exempt? All the more so where the doctrine is based on market protection. Who would lend to a wife if the husband could always say that perhaps she later destroyed the money? A lender cannot stand beside her constantly to see what she does. It therefore appears that Hagahot HaRosh speaks where the lender knew at the time of advancing the funds that she was taking them for a purpose unrelated to the husband's household, such as wasting them on personal vanities or even giving charity that did not serve the husband's interests. Then the lender knows that she is not acting as the husband's agent, and the husband is exempt. But where no such purpose was known at the time, the husband is liable for an ordinary loan or commercial advance. With a pure deposit, other than an iska type arrangement containing a loan component, the husband is always exempt. His appointment of his wife as agent to conduct business does not make her his agent to accept safekeeping deposits. There is no market protection reason to extend liability to such deposits. Maharik likewise writes that if the commercial credit was extended secretly from the husband and the lender knew it was concealed from him, everyone agrees that the husband is exempt.
