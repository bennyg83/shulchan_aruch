The Bach limits the rule allowing the court to administer the oath to the woman to a rabbinic oath. The Shach objects that if she claims unavoidable accident and refuses to pay, the oath that the item is not in her possession is Torah law. There is no contradiction. If she admits negligence and says she wishes to pay but presently lacks money, there is no Torah custodial oath. The oath that the item is not still in her possession is then only the rabbinic oath instituted even for one who wishes to pay. The responsum of Rosh concerns precisely that situation. Poverty does not change the rule. Once she admits negligence and accepts liability, the Torah oath no longer applies even if she cannot presently pay.
If witnesses establish that the actual money of the loan, or property directly obtained from it, still exists in the wife's hands, the creditor may be paid from that property. Bach derives that where a debtor owes many creditors and acquired merchandise on credit from one of them, if that seller's merchandise itself, or its direct substitute, remains intact, the seller has a superior claim to it. See what I wrote in siman 104, seif katan 14.
