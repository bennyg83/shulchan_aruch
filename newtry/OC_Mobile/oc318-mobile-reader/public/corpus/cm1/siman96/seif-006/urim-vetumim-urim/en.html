We require her to swear. Beit Yosef writes this because he understood Rosh to disagree with the Gaon, who holds that she is not required to swear. Shach, Derishah, and other authorities explain that if there is reason to think that through the oath payment of all or part of the debt can presently be obtained, she is required to swear. But if it is clear that she has nothing and nothing can now be collected from her, so that the oath would concern only a future possibility, such as money she may later receive upon collecting her ketubah, she is not required to swear. This is the clear practical ruling, for most later authorities agree. If the claimant demands a specific article and there is some doubt whether it remains in her possession, so that an oath could lead to its return, she must swear that it is not in her possession. If he then seeks to impose through gilgul other uncertain claims which, even if she admitted them, she is known to be presently unable to pay, Shach holds that gilgul is not imposed. In Tumim I wrote that his proof is not decisive and that gilgul does appear to apply.

Unlike those who disagree. Shach argues at length in seif katan 9 that the primary ruling follows these dissenters, namely Raavan, and common practice is likewise that where a wife conducts business within the household, the husband must pay what she borrowed. Even according to the view above in siman 77 that where one partner borrows without witnesses, the other partner is not liable merely because he admits that the money reached the partnership, the present case is stronger. There it is possible that the borrowing partner borrowed for himself, and at the time of the loan it was not evident that the money was for the partnership. Here it is generally known that whatever she borrows is for her husband's needs and as his agent. Everything depends, however, upon the scope of her household business. If she handles only small transactions and not large ones, the husband is not liable for large obligations she undertakes. If she manages all his business affairs, he must pay everything she borrows for that purpose, as Maharik writes. This applies where she purchased on credit or entered into a business transaction. If she accepted a deposit and was negligent with it, the husband is not liable, because he did not appoint her as his agent to accept deposits. But if she purchased on credit or borrowed for business and afterward lost the money for a purpose unrelated to her husband, the husband is nevertheless liable, as I wrote in Tumim. Otherwise commercial reliability would be destroyed, because no one would extend credit to a married woman, since the lender could never know what she might later do with the funds. If, however, the lender knows that she is spending the borrowed funds on matters that do not benefit her husband, even charity of which the husband is unaware, and certainly if he knows that she borrowed secretly from her husband, the husband is plainly exempt and there is no market enactment in such a case. See Tumim. If the husband dies and others seek to collect from the heirs based on written acknowledgments made by his wife, it requires study whether we suspect collusion and require proof that the documents reached the creditors during the husband's lifetime. For one does not require a pregnant woman to swear. This rule has no clear source. It appears only in Responsa Binyamin Ze'ev, who writes that such is the custom. It seems to refer to hesset, which is only a rabbinic enactment and for which property is not seized, rather refusal leads to excommunication. A pregnant woman should not be excommunicated. With a Torah oath, however, for which property may be seized and no delay is ordinarily granted, the matter requires study. All oath law is derived from sotah, and we rule that a pregnant sotah is given the waters without waiting for her to give birth. Certainly, where a pregnant woman must swear in order to collect, nothing may be collected until she swears. If she wishes to delay the oath, collection is delayed as well. This is obvious.
