See Be'er HaGolah, letter 90, who cites the Bach and concludes that according to him the same applies to a borrower who lacks enough assets to pay all his creditors: if the merchandise he bought on credit is still intact, the original seller has a special claim to it. Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer siman 90, seif katan 38, also cites the Bach and appears to agree. He adds that if it is known that the borrower purchased merchandise of this type, we assume that the merchandise presently in his possession is from those goods even though it is not known with certainty that these very goods came from that seller. Ketzot HaChoshen likewise cites the Bach as practical law, and below in siman 114, seif katan 14, raises and resolves a difficulty in his words, also indicating agreement.
The Taz here disagrees with the Bach. Responsa Mabit, part 2, simanim 200 and 213, cited by the Shach above, siman 39, seif katan 49, also support the Taz. Mabit ruled that seizure of the wool was effective for the creditor. Even those who disagreed, whose view the Shach adopts, did so because the wool was still in the seller's possession and the seller could retract the sale itself. Beit Meir, Even HaEzer siman 90, seif 9, also discusses the Bach at length and writes that the two views in Responsa Mabit demonstrate that they do not accept the Bach's rule. Otherwise there would have been no room for their dispute, because even if the wool were in the buyer's possession the buyer's creditor would have had no right in it and only the seller would have. He further writes that Responsa Rosh, rule 79, cited by the Sema, siman 47, seif katan 4, is also contrary to the Bach.
Get Pashut, siman 108, seif katan 8, likewise writes that the Bach's ruling is open to serious challenge. He cites Responsa Maharshadam, siman 210, Maharshach, part 1, siman 35, Mahari A, siman 345, Mahar A Sasson, siman 100, and Mabit, part 1, siman 149, all of which contradict the Bach. He further writes that even according to the Bach one must distinguish: the seller receives back the goods only while they remain in the buyer's possession. If the buyer sold or gave them away, his act is valid, since he had already acquired them. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 117, also disagrees with the Bach as a matter of strict law, though he testifies that such is the local enactment. He concludes that since strict law gives this creditor no right to take his own intact goods before other creditors, but all creditors share equally because the price was converted into a loan, and only a communal enactment gives him the right to those goods, one may not extend the enactment beyond its stated scope. It applies only to those very goods while intact, not to substitutes or proceeds derived from them. Although a right that exists under strict law in an object can ordinarily extend to what comes from it, that does not apply to a special enactment. See further my Divrei Mishpat here and above, siman 91, seif katan 3, Shaar Mishpat at the end of siman 104, and what I wrote below, siman 104, seif 3, seif katan 3, in the name of Noda B'Yehudah. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 55, also agrees with the Tzemach Tzedek. He discusses one who died owing creditors and whose estate included merchandise known to have been purchased on credit from a particular shopkeeper. He ruled that the shopkeeper has no priority over other creditors in those goods, even by virtue of the enactment. In his view, the enactment applied only to a living borrower who absconds or becomes insolvent, where there is strong reason to suspect that he bought on credit intending to flee and act as a wicked borrower. Where the borrower died and left an estate, with no deceit or wrongdoing, no such enactment was made. This is explicit in the text of the enactment printed at the end of Misgeret HaShulchan, simanim 27 and 28, which limits it to fugitives and to creditors in the lands where the enactment spread. Chatam Sofer adds that he had not heard that the enactment spread in his lands, Urim VeTumim does not mention it, and it is not part of the law of the kingdom. Therefore, as strict law, he saw no basis for priority except by voluntary compromise with the other creditors. See further Responsa Rishmei She'elah, simanim 49 and 50, where the matter is discussed at length. It likewise states that the rule concerning intact merchandise in Ateret Tzvi is an enactment against a fugitive, not against heirs or an widow's ketubah, and one may not expand it. The author also questions whether merchandise bought on credit but later changed in form, as in the case where fur had allegedly been made into hats, is still considered the original merchandise. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 77, who answers that the reason for the custom is not danger to the fetus but danger to the woman herself during childbirth. Even a true oath may create danger, as explained in Gittin 35a. Sotah is different, because if she is guilty the danger is the intended result, and if innocent the Torah promises blessing in childbirth. He concludes that one who seeks to depart from the Rama and established custom bears the burden of proof. His words imply that even a Torah oath should be postponed until after she gives birth.
