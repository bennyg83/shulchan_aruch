And if there is, etc. Suppose the two witnesses sign on the same line, one at the beginning and one at the end, with a blank area between them large enough to write the formula, "We witnesses are signed on erased parchment and the document is on ordinary parchment." Even so, the document can be valid. We cannot say that the original document was on ordinary parchment, the witnesses on erased parchment, and that the holder later inserted the explanatory formula between them. Witnesses are not permitted to create such a dangerous configuration. Why? The holder could cut away the first witness and everything above him, erase the ordinary-parchment text, and write a new document on the erased area. He would then have one fabricated document with one witness on erased parchment. He could do the same with the second witness, cutting away the other side and producing a second fabricated one-witness document. Below, siman 51, two one-witness documents can combine. Thus the original witnesses would have handed over a paper capable of producing two fraudulent claims. Since valid witnesses are presumed not to do that, a document now appearing in this form can be assumed to have had the witnesses and text arranged lawfully from the start. One might object from Tosafot in Bava Batra 164b. Tosafot consider the possibility that the document was on ordinary parchment, the witnesses on erased parchment, and the explanatory words were written between them. Tosafot do not say such a form is prohibited because of the fraud scenario just described. They merely ask why the witnesses would act foolishly and sign on erased parchment when they could sign on the ordinary parchment. This does not necessarily refute the analysis. Tosafot may have written their answer more broadly, in a way that works even according to authorities who hold two one-witness documents do not combine. According to our accepted law that they can combine, the stronger anti-forgery reason applies. Still, that answer is somewhat strained. A better explanation may be that Tosafot require more than a mere one-witness writing. They may hold that a one-witness document becomes effective only when the second witness appears separately, or when one signature is independently authenticated in a manner that gives it proper documentary status. If the supposed forged document has room for only one written witness and no proper second attestation, the danger is less obvious. The physical dimensions also matter. Suppose the surviving strip can hold only a two-line mini-document. After the original witnesses sign, the holder cuts the parchment close to their names. He cannot then create a full new two-witness instrument because there is not enough space. If he tries to make one witness serve as the borrower or obligor through handwriting, other safeguards discussed below can expose the manipulation. This can explain why Tosafot focus on the impracticality rather than stating a categorical prohibition. There is an additional point concerning Ramban. Ramban raises a doubt where the borrower's own handwriting and the operative text are both on erased parchment. People may not scrutinize whether the area under the debtor's handwriting was erased once while the document area was erased twice. If a forger erases and rewrites, the surfaces can become similar. If that concern were accepted, even a one-witness document on erased parchment would be highly problematic because perhaps an old handwritten obligation of the debtor was erased and rewritten as testimony against another person. Tosafot in Bava Batra 164b, however, speak as though a single witness on erased parchment can have real evidentiary significance and worry that cutting the paper could create two such writings. This implies Tosafot assume people do scrutinize the erased surfaces sufficiently to detect one erasure from two. The same should apply to the borrower's handwriting. Accordingly, Ramban's doubt should not override the more definite implication of Tosafot. The practical result is that, where the witnesses' placement and the erased surfaces are regular, we rely on the presumption that the witnesses did not create a paper inviting the obvious two-document fraud. The current document can therefore be validated. There is another way to formulate Tosafot's reasoning more precisely. If the first witness is on erased parchment, the second on erased parchment, and the intervening space is ordinary parchment, the holder cannot be permitted to receive a document that allows him later to divide the page into two independent claims. The first possible forged claim would use the first witness. The holder cuts away the second witness, erases the original text, and writes a new obligation ending near the first signature. The second forged claim does the reverse. Because the two writings emerge from the same original page, the normal suspicion that two independent one-witness documents are unrelated is weakened. They can readily be understood as two pieces of testimony concerning the same transaction and combine under our law. This is especially serious where the forged texts are written identically and dated the same day. The very uniformity of the two fragments can make them appear intentionally parallel. Accordingly, valid witnesses would not knowingly hand over such a configuration. Tosafot's statement that they would simply sign on the ordinary parchment can be understood as the most immediate practical reason, while the underlying prohibition remains the same anti-forgery concern. This also explains why the exact width of the intervening space matters. If the space is too small to hold a legally usable one-line obligation together with the necessary identification, the fraud scenario disappears. If it can hold the equivalent of two lines, the danger is concrete. Thus the measure is not arbitrary. It reflects whether the blank can be transformed into an independent written claim. The same principle helps with the dispute concerning a debtor's own handwriting on erased parchment. Where his handwriting appears together with the document on erased parchment, a forger might erase the debtor's words and rewrite them as though they were a witness signature or vice versa. Ramban therefore worries that the physical layers of erasure may not always be examined carefully enough. Tosafot's structure assumes greater confidence in such examination. Since the practical halachah elsewhere relies upon the visible difference between once-erased and twice-erased parchment, Tosafot's certainty carries substantial force. Therefore, absent some concrete irregularity, we preserve the document rather than invent Ramban's remote double-erasure scenario.
