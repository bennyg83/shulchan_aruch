Other people are not permitted to do so. The Beit Yosef understands Rambam to mean that even if two people read the deed before the witnesses, this does not suffice. Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise states this in Rambam’s name. See below, seif 5, seif katan 10, and Responsa Maharash Kohen, volume 3, siman 40.
The readers should translate it for them. The Beit Yosef here extends the rule of Hagahot Ashri and Rambam to translation, following the Tur in Even HaEzer 130. Yet in Even HaEzer the Beit Yosef himself writes that Rambam requires the witnesses to understand the language of the document itself, that an interpreter does not suffice, and that this follows from Rashba and the Yerushalmi. The Darkei Moshe there agrees. How, then, can the Beit Yosef and Rama rule here without qualification that translation is effective? In law, the Tur’s ruling appears correct. There is no substantive distinction between hearing the deed read in a language one understands and hearing it translated into such a language. The Yerushalmi concerns other documents, where Rav requires the witnesses themselves to be able to read, in accordance with Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. It does not establish such a requirement for a get. The Mishnah’s word get may also denote any legal instrument, as elsewhere in the Talmud. The proof adduced from the Persian deed in Gittin 19b is likewise inconclusive. Amemar follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who does not allow others to read ordinary documents for the witnesses. Moreover, one cannot assume that gentiles reading innocently would suffice when Jews are initially signing a deed, even though such readers may be used later to interpret an instrument already executed in a gentile court. It is also plausible that Rambam requires only that the witnesses understand what is read to them, and therefore allows an interpreter. Hagahot Ashri’s wording supports this. Accordingly, although ordinary documents should not be signed this way initially, a get is valid after the fact where others read and accurately translated it into a language the witnesses understood, particularly where writing another get would involve serious difficulty.
This also appears to me to be the principal view. In my opinion, the first view is primary. The Rosh’s interpretation of Rif is unpersuasive. Rif, like Baal HaIttur and the Beit Yosef, holds that even two readers do not suffice. The attribution to Ri that two readers are effective is not established by Tosafot. Tosafot state that result only according to the Sages. If, as most authorities rule, the law follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, their words imply the reverse: even two readers do not help. Rashba and Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise indicate that Rif and Rambam require the witnesses themselves to know how to read. Nor do readers speaking innocently suffice when a Jewish deed can be prepared through witnesses who understand it. Nevertheless, this is an initial requirement. If witnesses signed after two reliable persons read the instrument to them, the deed is valid after the fact. Rambam and the Mechaber say only that ordinary people are not permitted to act this way; they do not declare the resulting deed invalid. This is unlike signing through a cutout pattern, which the Gemara treats as invalid after the fact. The authorities who validate that latter case certainly validate this one.
