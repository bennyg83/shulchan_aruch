The readers shall translate for them. Shach asks from Even HaEzer 130:16. There Mechaber and Rama rule like Rambam that the witnesses themselves must understand the language of the get and may not rely on an interpreter. Why, then, can witnesses here rely on readers who translate the document for them? There is already a necessary distinction between witnesses who cannot read at all and witnesses who can read but do not understand this particular language. In the first case, the Gemara allows others to read a get before the witnesses. Why should that be permitted while translation is not? The answer is the special concern for agunah. Gittin 19b shows that certain leniencies were accepted to avoid leaving a woman unable to remarry. If the only available witnesses are unable to read, requiring literate alternatives could leave her stranded. Chazal therefore allowed them to rely on others reading the text aloud. But if the witnesses can read and write, only not in this particular language, there is no comparable agunah concern. A get can be written in any language. They can simply write it in a language and script they understand. Therefore there was no need to relax the rule through translation. This distinction explains the law in gittin. But our present case concerns ordinary monetary documents. Where Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel validates reliance on readers even in other documents, that leniency cannot be based on an agunah enactment. It must reflect the substantive law of testimony. Once two trustworthy people read the document to the witnesses and the witnesses understand the meaning through them, the legal problem is not fundamentally different from direct reading. If so, translation should likewise be acceptable. Shach's question is therefore correct in the monetary setting: what principled distinction remains between having others read and having them translate? Rama here follows Rosh and rules that where two people read an ordinary document to the signing witnesses, the document is valid. That implies even Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's stringency regarding other documents does not extend to a case supported by two readers. Once the law recognizes two-reader assistance as sufficient in monetary documents, translation should also suffice. The leniency there is not a special enactment but the ordinary law. Why, then, did Rama not state in Even HaEzer that even two translators are acceptable? Because in gittin the consequences are severe. Rama was concerned for the authorities who disagree with Rosh and hold that Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's restriction applies even with two assistants. If so, the leniency in the get case remains only the special agunah-based allowance for reading, not a general rule of translation. Translation lacks the same necessity because the get can simply be written in a language the witnesses understand. Therefore Rama leaves the stricter Rambam rule in place in Even HaEzer. But in monetary documents, where he expressly accepts Rosh's two-reader ruling as law, translation by those readers is likewise valid. Thus the two sections are consistent. The difference is not that reading is inherently stronger than translation. It is that the get law contains a special stringency and a limited agunah leniency, while ordinary monetary documents follow the broader evidentiary rule when two trustworthy readers or translators are used. I have discussed the underlying Yerushalmi and Rambam at greater length in my Even HaEzer novellae.
And this appears to me to be the primary view. Shach, seif katan 4, disagrees with this conclusion, and what he says is well stated. Still, Rama's ruling is difficult. Rosh proves his position from the case of Rav Pappa concerning a document issued in a gentile court that was collected through the testimony of witnesses speaking innocently. Rosh reasons that if two people can establish the contents of such a document by reading it, then two Jewish readers should likewise suffice for witnesses who themselves cannot read the document. From this Rosh concludes that two readers are enough. That proof works only because Rosh understands Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's invalidation of other documents as applying even after the fact. Rav Pappa's case would then be difficult unless one distinguishes and permits two readers. But Rama below in seif 5 follows Mechaber and rules that if the witnesses say, "We had it read to one who knew how to read," the document can be valid. Shach explains there that Mechaber understands Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's stringency in ordinary documents as only initial procedure, while after the fact the document can remain valid. If Rama agrees with that approach, then Rosh's proof from Rav Pappa disappears entirely. There is hardly a more after-the-fact case than a document already executed in a gentile court, where no Jewish reader is available now. Certainly one would rely on the available testimony rather than leave the claimant unable to collect. If so, what basis remains for Rama to rule like Rosh that two readers are initially sufficient? We must therefore say that Rama does not accept Shach's distinction between initial procedure and after-the-fact validity. Rather, Rama understood Mechaber's wording in seif 5 as speaking of two people who read the document before the witnesses. Rama had already accepted Rosh's rule that two readers may do so. Although Beit Yosef's wording does not naturally sound that way, this must have been Rama's understanding. Accordingly, Rama's rulings are consistent: two competent readers may read for the witnesses, and the law does not depend on a special after-the-fact exception.
