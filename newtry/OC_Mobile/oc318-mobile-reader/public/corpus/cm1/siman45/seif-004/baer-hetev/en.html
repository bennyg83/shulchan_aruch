The traced writing. The Sema explains that they take a sheet of paper and cut the witnesses' names through it from side to side, place it upon the parchment, and fill the cutouts with ink. The ink passes through the openings and leaves the names marked on the get. They permitted doing this for a get because of the danger of leaving a woman unable to remarry, but for other documents it is forbidden. However, to sketch the form of the witnesses' signatures in ruled lines on the paper and have the witnesses fill those lines with ink is forbidden even for a get, because this is writing over writing. The Shach writes that the Mechaber's words should not have been explained this way, since his phrase “and they sign upon the tracing” indicates that he explains it like Rashi: the paper is marked with ruled outlines and the witnesses sign upon those outlines, and in a get this is valid. The Rashba likewise writes in his novellae to the first chapter of Gittin that tracing is not writing and is not engraving. He further writes that he found in the Jerusalem Talmud the formulation concerning one who cuts upon leather, which shows that marking is not considered writing. The Baal HaIttur explains it likewise, and the Raavyah in Mordechai, chapter 2 of Gittin, agrees. These are the Shach's words. In my work Menorat Zechariah, seif katan 55, I wrote that the Shach erred, because the Rambam explicitly disagrees with the Jerusalem Talmud and rules in chapter 4 of Hilchot Gerushin that tracing is considered writing. In Even HaEzer, siman 125, the Beit Yosef cites both opinions, that of the Rambam and that of the Jerusalem Talmud; see there. Editor's note: Although we have not merited to see the Menorah itself, and we do not know the author's proofs, nevertheless, with due respect to that great authority, it appears to this insignificant writer that he mistakenly described the Shach as having erred. The words “and so I found in the Jerusalem Talmud” are the Rashba's words, quoted by the Shach, and there the Rashba himself expresses wonder at the Rambam. He writes that it is surprising that the Rambam combined the two rules and held that both cutting into leather and tracing upon leather are valid forms of writing. The Ran and the Maggid Mishneh on chapter 4 of Hilchot Gerushin likewise question the Rambam on this point; see there. See also Get Pashut by Rabbi Moshe ibn Habib on the laws of gittin, Even HaEzer siman 125, seif 5, where he discusses this rule at length.
Them. Meaning the witnesses. Some editions read “him,” and on that basis the Sema wished to explain that it refers to the judge who directs the signing. This is entirely unconvincing, for neither the Tur nor the Shulchan Aruch mentions a judge here at all. It is therefore obvious that the correct reading is “them.” Shach.
