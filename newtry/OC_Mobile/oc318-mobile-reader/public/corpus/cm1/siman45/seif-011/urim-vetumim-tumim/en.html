Perhaps something detrimental to him had been written there and he cut it off, etc. The Mechaber derived this ruling from the Rosh. The Rosh follows his own view that an erasure anywhere in a document invalidates it because we fear that a condition was written there and erased; the same concern should apply where part of the parchment was cut away. But the Mechaber ruled above, siman 44, seif 5, like Rashba, that an erasure in the standard portion of a document is valid and we do not suspect that a condition was erased. Why then should we suspect a condition when the document was cut? It must be that the Mechaber distinguishes between an erasure, which may have occurred accidentally, and cutting, which is an intentional act and therefore creates a stronger concern that some condition was deliberately removed. The earlier proof for Rashba from a case where the date was cut away is not difficult. There too, there is no suspicious defect, because the holder could simply have erased the date and the document would have remained valid. Why would he choose cutting unless there were some other reason? Here, by contrast, if he had covered the area with ink or erased it, the document might have been invalidated because people would say that the witnesses signed on the inked area. He therefore may have cut it off specifically to avoid invalidating the document. Even according to Rashba, whose underlying leniency is itself difficult, we should not extend it to this more suspicious case.
If "valid and established" is written above at the end of a line, it is valid. The Shach asks that perhaps detrimental language was written after the concluding formula and cut away, since according to some opinions even detrimental material written after the formula may be relied upon. He says that the Rosh may follow Rashbam, who holds that even detrimental matter is not learned from the final line, while the Rosh's concern that detrimental matter may have been cut away applies only according to our practice of using "valid and established." But according to the Shulchan Aruch, which does not follow Rashbam and does follow Nimukei Yosef that detrimental material may be learned, the ruling should not stand. His words are difficult. At the beginning he says that perhaps detrimental material was cut away. If we had seen the concluding formula already written, then on his own account such material would not be learned. If the document comes before us without any concluding formula, under our custom the document is already invalid for that reason, and there is no need to invoke a possibility that detrimental material had been written. It is also strained to say that the Rosh disagrees with Tosafos, who explicitly permit learning detrimental material. The correct principle is what I wrote above in siman 44, seif katan 11, with support from Tosafos: once the formal phrase "valid and established" has been written, the document is complete, and even detrimental material written afterward is not part of the document. It is comparable to a document lacking the concluding phrase, from whose final line nothing is learned. There is further clear support from Tosafos in Bava Batra 164. They write that if one witness signs at the beginning of a line and the second at the end of the line, both on an erased area, while blank parchment remains between them large enough to write the words, "we are witnesses," this situation is not ordinarily found, because the witnesses could simply sign on the parchment. One must understand their point to mean that even the first witness could sign on the parchment. Yet how could one leave the preceding erasure and then sign on clean parchment, if we feared that detrimental language had originally been written in the erased area? Necessarily the Gemara's case is one where the formal conclusion had already appeared before this line, so nothing afterward, even detrimental matter, is treated as part of the document. Tosafos and the Rosh therefore agree. This also explains Tosafos in Bava Batra 163, who write that where a document was cut before the witnesses, there is a concern that there had been a one line document there which was cut off. They make the validity depend upon whether witnesses ordinarily leave so large a margin, and do not simply say that perhaps detrimental language was written in the removed portion. This again shows that after the formal conclusion we do not suspect omitted detrimental content. It is possible that even the Rama, who above cites Nimukei Yosef that detrimental content may sometimes be learned from the final line, agrees that after the concluding formula nothing is learned. Even if the Rama follows Nimukei Yosef absolutely, he did not object here to the Mechaber's ruling because Rashba independently validates an erasure in the standard text without suspecting that detrimental language was removed. Combined with the view of Tosafos and the Rosh, this is sufficient to uphold the document.
Provided that the witnesses are signed one beneath the other. One might ask according to the Mechaber's earlier ruling, following Raah, that a validation of erasures placed between one witness and another does not help because perhaps one witness forged it and we do not rely upon a single witness to extract money. Why, then, does it help here that the witnesses are signed one beneath the other? Perhaps there was originally a document and one witness on a single line, which is valid, as appears from Tosafos, the Rosh, the Shach, and the Sema. The holder then cut away that one line document and wrote whatever he wished across the margin above the first witness, after which the second witness signed without knowing what had been done. Would this not also extract money through the act of a single witness? This is almost a clear proof for Rashba's position above, that we ordinarily rely on the presumption that one witness did not forge. Perhaps Raah distinguishes. Ordinarily we rely upon the integrity of a single witness. In the case of a validation written between the witnesses there is an additional irregularity: why was the validation placed there instead of allowing the second witness to sign as well? Where the document itself has been weakened by an irregular form, we become concerned. Without such a defect, the presumption remains.
But if they are signed one after the other, it is invalid. The Shach writes that witnesses may nevertheless sign a one line document together with its text on that same line and leave a margin above. We do not fear that someone will cut off the text and write a new document in the upper margin above their signatures. When witnesses sign a one line document, they are careful to sign immediately adjacent to the text. If the text is later cut away, the witnesses' signatures will then have no blank space before them at all, while an ordinary document has some margin at the side, and the forgery will be evident. Accordingly, when the Shulchan Aruch says that if the witnesses are signed one after the other the document is invalid because perhaps there was originally a one line document, the cut must reach immediately up to the witnesses' signatures. If there remains some blank parchment before the signatures, the document is valid, because we can rely on the presumption that witnesses who sign a one line document act properly and leave no blank between the text and their signatures. Moreover, why would a forger use the obvious and detectable method of cutting off the old text and writing in the upper margin, when he could instead cut away the entire original text and write a new text carefully aligned with the witnesses, leaving a natural margin before them? This also resolves the Tosafos discussed above. They say that where one witness is signed on an erased area and there is clean parchment between him and the second witness, the first witness could have signed on the clean parchment. How could he do so if an erasure would remain before his signature, creating a concern that a one line document had been erased and a new document written above? Necessarily Tosafos means that he should leave a small amount of clean parchment after the erasure and then sign. If there is even a little clean space before the signature, we cannot say that there had been a one line document, because such a document would have been signed immediately adjacent to its text. This is a clear proof for the Shach and against the Sema and Bach.
