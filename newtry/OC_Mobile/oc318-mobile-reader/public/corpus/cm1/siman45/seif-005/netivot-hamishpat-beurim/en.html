If the townspeople accepted them. See Shach, who writes that where a person directly obligates himself, the arrangement can be effective by strict law. Ketzot HaChoshen argues that this is true only of an evidentiary document, not a constitutive obligation. His proof from the view that signed witnesses effectuate the transfer is not comparable. One cannot appoint an agent to become a witness in another's place; testimony is a personal function and an agent's report would be hearsay. That remains true even where the witnesses' testimony itself completes an acquisition. By contrast, when a person wishes to create his own obligation and his signature or mark is itself the means of acquisition, ordinary agency can be effective to perform that acquisition on his behalf. Still, the Shach's broader wording requires qualification. Whether for evidence or for obligation, if there is no recognized local convention giving a particular count, mark, or form the status of a document, there is no reason to assume it is authentic and it may lack the legal character of a deed altogether, as Urim VeTumim writes at the end of siman 68.
