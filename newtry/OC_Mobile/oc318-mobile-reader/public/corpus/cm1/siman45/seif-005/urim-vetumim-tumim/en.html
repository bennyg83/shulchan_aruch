We examine their oral testimony carefully, etc. Shach holds that if the witnesses remember the transaction orally, then even if they did not actually read what was written in the document before signing, the document is valid after the fact. Perishah strongly objects. Why should their oral memory help when they acted improperly and did not know what they were signing? What situation is more open to forgery than that? His objection is compelling. There is also proof from the first chapter of Gittin. Rava says that a Persian document delivered before Jewish witnesses can be used for collection. The Gemara asks: but the witnesses do not know how to read it. Likewise in the second chapter, regarding a Persian document signed by Jewish witnesses, the Gemara asks the same question: they do not know how to read it. What is the difficulty? Presumably they know the transaction orally. If oral knowledge were enough even though they never read the document, the Gemara's question would fall away. Tosafot and Rosh in the first chapter also imply that delivery witnesses must know what is written in the document. They discuss an instrument capable of alteration and distinguish according to whether the delivery witnesses know its contents. This proves that mere oral knowledge of the transaction does not transform an unread paper into valid documentary testimony. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 10, part 2, law 3, cites a Gaon explicitly: If one of the two witnesses knows how to read and the other does not, and the one who cannot read remembers his testimony orally, he should come and testify orally. One witness in writing and one witness orally can combine. This treats the witness who remembers the event but cannot read the document as an oral witness, not as a full document witness. That is directly contrary to Shach. However, Sema's explanation also appears strained. Sema says that we always need to assume the witnesses had the document read by someone who could read it. If the witnesses remember the transaction, we ask whether they gave it to another person to read; if they say yes, the document is valid. If they do not remember the transaction, we do not ask and instead presume the paper invalid, unless they independently say it was read to them. This reading is difficult both in Tur's language and in logic. There is also a problem from Rambam's view below in siman 46. According to Rambam, when witnesses do not remember what was written in the document, the document is fundamentally defective unless their signatures are independently authenticated. If they have a migo, they are believed to undermine the instrument. This is why, when a document was burned and the same witnesses testify to the burning, Rambam requires them to remember the underlying testimony, as explained above in siman 41 and elsewhere. If so, here too, where the witnesses could have said, "It was never read to us," they possess a migo. If they then say, "We do not remember what was in it," why should the document remain valid merely because their signatures are independently authenticated and someone supposedly read it to them? Rama's broad wording would then conflict with Rambam. The correct explanation is as follows. If it is known that no one read the document to the witnesses, for example because no one in the city knew how to read that script or language, the document is certainly invalid. That is explicit in the Gemara and in Baal HaTerumot. But ordinarily, where the witnesses' signatures are independently authenticated, we preserve the document's presumption of validity. If the witnesses themselves cannot read yet they know what was written in the document, that positive circumstance supports the assumption that someone capable read it aloud to them. We therefore validate the document. If, after independent authentication of their signatures, the witnesses say, "No one read it to us," they are not believed to destroy the document. But if they do not know what was written in it and the defect is before us that they themselves cannot read, then the presumption of proper reading is much weaker. If they affirmatively say someone else read it to them, they can explain the irregularity and the document is valid. If not, it is invalid. Thus the law depends on the combination of independent signature authentication, their ability to state the document's contents, and whether they affirmatively establish that the text was read to them. This harmonizes the Gemara, Baal HaTerumot, Rambam, and Rama without accepting Shach's view that oral memory alone is enough.
