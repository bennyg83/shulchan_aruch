If they say, “We had it read to us.” See Baer Heitev and Korban Netanel, chapter 2 of Gittin, letter 38. Korban Netanel writes that the Shach’s difficulty against the Rambam and Mechaber is no difficulty, because they too can be understood as speaking of two Jews where the non-Jews relate the matter incidentally, relying on the rule that incidental statements by two non-Jews may be effective, and the same would apply regarding Jews. He adds that the Shach overlooked the Rambam’s ruling cited by the Mechaber in Choshen Mishpat, siman 68, seif 2. I do not understand his words. It appears that the Shach did not overlook this at all. Rather, Korban Netanel overlooked the Shach above, seif 2, seif katan 4, who writes that according to the Rif and Rambam even an incidental statement is not sufficient ab initio. The incidental statement accepted in siman 68, seif 2, concerns a document already executed in a non-Jewish court. Although Korban Netanel himself disagrees with that understanding, as he writes in the preceding note, the Shach’s reasoning is internally sound. See Shaar HaMishpat, who suggests that when the Tur and Mechaber write, “If the witnesses are before us,” they mean that when present there is a remedy, because we can ask them whether the document was read to them. If they are not before us, however, and we know they cannot read, the document is entirely invalid. We cannot presume that the witnesses certainly acted properly, because in any event they acted improperly by signing in the first place, and we must therefore fear that perhaps the document was never read to them at all. See there. All this follows the Shach’s position. According to Korban Netanel, however, this suggestion plainly does not apply.
