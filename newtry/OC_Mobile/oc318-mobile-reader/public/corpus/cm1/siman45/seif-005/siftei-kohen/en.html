The scribe signs at the direction of the witness. The same applies where a borrower obligates himself through his own handwriting or through a customary written undertaking stating that a named person signed by transferring the pen, or that the named person instructed another to sign. The Sema cites Rashba to this effect. In such a case the practice is effective even if the townspeople never formally accepted it. Communal acceptance is required for testimony only, because otherwise it is testimony from the mouth of another witness. Here, however, the obligor himself undertakes the liability.
If the witnesses are before us and we know that they cannot read. Ordinarily we presume that witnesses can read, and need not question them even when they are present. This is implied by the Tur and the authorities.
If they say, ‘It was read to us.’ One may ask how this accords with Rambam and the Mechaber above, who rule that even two readers do not suffice. The Rosh and Tur follow their own view, and Baal HaIttur can be read as speaking of two persons reading innocently. The Mechaber, however, makes no such distinction. The answer is that the restriction applies initially. If the witnesses nevertheless signed after the document was read to them, the deed is valid after the fact, as explained above. This is the correct ruling.
If their account corresponds to the deed. The Sema says they must also be asked whether the document was originally read to them by persons capable of reading. If not, their signatures were initially improper and cannot be repaired merely because they now recount the transaction accurately. I disagree. The Tur, Baal HaIttur, and the Rosh imply that when their account matches the deed no further inquiry is required, and even an express admission that it was not read to them does not invalidate it. They should not initially have relied upon the creditor, because the deed might have been forged. But where they now identify the transaction and confirm that the deed is accurate, no forgery actually occurred. Had they then given it to competent readers, they would have signed it. The deed is therefore valid.
