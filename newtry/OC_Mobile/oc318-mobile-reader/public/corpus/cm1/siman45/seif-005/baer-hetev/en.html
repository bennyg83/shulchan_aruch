The same applies to a borrower who obligates himself by his own handwriting, or by a memorandum, where he writes that so-and-so signed by handing over the pen, or that so-and-so instructed another to sign. This is effective. The Rashba likewise writes regarding a ketubah that if another person signs on behalf of the groom, it is effective, although in practice the matter is improper because it may produce harmful consequences. Thus writes the Sema. The Shach writes: It appears that only with regard to a witness do we require that the townspeople have accepted this practice, because otherwise it is testimony through another witness and is ineffective. With regard to a borrower who personally obligates himself in this manner, however, it is effective even if the townspeople never accepted such a custom.

We read it to them. The Shach writes that this requires analysis, for above in seif 2 it is explained that even if two people read the document, this does not suffice. One must therefore say that only initially do we rule that they may not sign in this manner, but if they did so the document is valid after the fact, and this is the principal ruling. The Sema writes in the name of the Beit Yosef that the case is where the witnesses volunteered this information on their own before the judges questioned them. The Taz writes likewise and concludes: There are three rules here. First, if they say that the document was read to them, that is sufficient. Second, if they did not say so, they must give mutually consistent oral testimony. Third, if it is clear to us that it was not read to them, the document is invalid in every case.
