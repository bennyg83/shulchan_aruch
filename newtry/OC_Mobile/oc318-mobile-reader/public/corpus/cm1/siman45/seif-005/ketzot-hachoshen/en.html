In a place where the custom is that the scribe signs. The Sema writes that the same applies where a borrower undertakes liability in his own handwriting, or in a promissory note, by writing that so and so signed by his command. The Shach adds that here an established municipal custom need not have been formally accepted, because the rule requiring acceptance by the townspeople concerns testimony, lest it be testimony from the mouth of another witness. Here, however, the obligor undertakes the liability himself. In my view the implication from Tashbetz that ordinary handwriting works even without local custom applies specifically to an evidentiary instrument, such as a ketubah signed for the groom, where the writing is only proof and the underlying obligation already exists by condition of beit din. There is then no problem of testimony from another witness because the signer is reporting the litigant's own statement. But where a person seeks to create a new obligation through his handwriting, the actual signature itself is required. Commanding someone else to sign cannot create that personal written undertaking, because agency does not substitute for the obligor's own handwriting in such a mechanism. Hagahot Ashri likewise discusses a get whose witnesses did not sign personally but had the scribe sign for them, and records a dispute whether agency can work. Even if testimony from another witness is not the problem, the essential act required from the signer remains missing. Therefore, where a new obligation is created through personal handwriting, another person's signature at his command is ineffective unless the accepted local custom itself gives that form legal force. The Sema's language refers to a new obligation, and in that setting local custom is indeed required.
