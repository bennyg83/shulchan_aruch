Specifically if he filled it on the day it was written, etc. Mechaber states this rule without qualification, as though no authority disputes it. In my view Rosh does not clearly agree. Rabbenu Yonah gives the rule and reasons as follows. If we were to say that even after the original date passes, a two-line gap can be cured by filling it with relatives, because the document was intrinsically valid and merely exposed to forgery, then the classic law that a two-line gap invalidates would become difficult to understand. Whenever such a document came before beit din, the court could simply instruct the holder to fill the gap with relatives and thereby restore it retroactively. Rabbenu Yonah therefore concludes that the filling must occur on the day of the document. If done later, retroactive validation would make the paper antedated. One should not object from Bava Batra 162b, where the Gemara asks why a two-line gap between the document and the court's certification cannot simply be smeared with ink. The cases differ. The Gemara's question is not necessarily whether later ink can retroactively cure a creditor lien. It asks why the Mishnah specifically teaches filling with relatives rather than simply saying the blank can be made unusable by ink. Particularly if relatives are used because sometimes valid people were initially expected to sign and later unavailable, one can understand why the baraita discusses that method. Rosh then cites another opinion: once the document has already been brought before beit din with the two-line gap, filling it afterward no longer helps. Rosh says this position has good reason and explains it favorably. If that is so, Rabbenu Yonah's proof for the same-day requirement is weakened. The classic case of a two-line gap can now be explained simply: the document is invalid when it reaches beit din before the gap is properly filled. There is no need to say that filling on a later calendar day is inherently ineffective. Indeed, in Rosh's abridged rulings the explicit same-day condition is omitted. Rosh says only that the gap can be filled without the debtor's authorization and that this works before the document is presented to beit din, but not afterward. This suggests that Rosh accepted the beit din cutoff and did not necessarily accept Rabbenu Yonah's additional calendar-day limitation. Tur can likewise be read this way. He first records Rabbenu Yonah, then cites Rosh's position requiring the filling before appearance in beit din. The plain implication is that Rosh's limitation replaces the need for Rabbenu Yonah's proof. It would be forced to establish Rosh's case only where the holder happened to appear in beit din on the same day as the document, or where he erased the old date and rewrote the date of appearance. Accordingly, Mechaber, who follows Rosh in requiring filling before the document is shown in beit din, should not have stated Rabbenu Yonah's same-day rule so categorically if its proof no longer remains decisive. There is, however, a conceptual basis for Mechaber's ruling. The two-line-gap defect arises principally when the witnesses deliver the document to the creditor. While the paper remains in the witnesses' own hands, there is little fear they will forge it. It resembles a get safeguarded by delivery witnesses, all the more so because the document itself remains under the witnesses' control. The defect begins when they hand it to the creditor in a form he can alter. According to the opinion that witness signatures acquire immediately for the creditor, the property lien can arise at the moment of signing, when the document is still safely in the witnesses' hands and not yet defective. Only afterward, upon delivery to the creditor, does the paper become exposed to forgery. In that model, later filling can be understood as restoring a document whose lien had already arisen validly at signing, much like a document that was later erased and then reconstructed by witnesses. It would not necessarily be antedated. This is the conceptual framework behind Rabbenu Yonah's discussion. But Mechaber does not follow Abaye's broad theory that the witness signatures alone immediately acquire for the creditor. According to Mechaber, the lien arises only when the document reaches the creditor. At that moment, if the paper still contains the two-line gap, it is a document capable of forgery and is legally defective. No creditor lien arises from a paper that is then mere parchment for collection purposes. If it is later filled and made valid, the lien can begin only from that later validation date. Using the earlier written date would indeed create an antedated lien. From this perspective, Mechaber's categorical same-day rule follows directly from his broader theory of when the document's lien begins. One may ask: then why does Mechaber also need Rosh's separate rule that filling after appearance in beit din does not help? Perhaps even on the original day, before the date becomes antedated, once the holder brings the defective paper to beit din the court has formally exposed its defect. The document is then judicially established as invalid in its present form. Allowing the holder to leave, fill the gap, and return would undermine the integrity of court procedure and invite manipulation. Thus there can be two separate cutoffs: calendar delay can create antedating because the lien did not arise earlier; presentation in beit din can independently freeze the document's defective status even within the same day. There is another proof for the same-day aspect from the laws of a bald folded get. A bald get, whose folds exceed its witnesses, is rabbinically invalid. According to Ri, the missing witness should be added the same day. Otherwise, if the paper becomes valid only on the next day, the earlier date can create the same retroactivity problem. The Gemara says, "Go and complete it with a slave from the marketplace." Tosafot understand the language "complete it" as implying supplementation afterward, perhaps even after the get had already been handed over in some sense. Rashi, however, explains that the get had not yet been delivered. In my view this disagreement parallels the present issue. Rashi is concerned that once the get has been delivered in its defective form, later completion cannot retroactively make the earlier date operative. Therefore he insists it had not yet been delivered. Tosafot read the case more flexibly and may hold the later completion can relate back under the particular get rules. Their phrase that "delivered" is not literal but merely means the signatures were not yet complete reflects this tension. It is difficult to say that the Gemara's phrase "go and complete it" necessarily means completion on the very same day. The wording is broad. But Rashi's explanation is exact if one accepts the concern that a rabbinically defective document cannot simply be repaired on a later day while preserving an earlier operative date. Accordingly, Mechaber's practical ruling can be defended even if Rabbenu Yonah's original proof is not decisive after Rosh's additional beit din rule. The better conceptual basis is Mechaber's rejection of Abaye's immediate signature-acquisition model. When the creditor first receives the paper, it must already be in a form capable of creating the lien. If the two-line gap remains, no lien begins. Later filling validates only prospectively. Therefore, to preserve the original written date without creating antedating, the correction must be completed that same day and before the defective paper is presented in beit din.
