Yet in siman 154, seif 18, the ruling is that one who derives his claim from a non-Jew is treated like the non-Jew only to his disadvantage. One must distinguish: here, even between two Jews the creditor could have collected on the document, because we see that it contains no actual forgery; it was invalidated only because its form permits forgery. With regard to a non-Jew's document, the Sages did not enact a disqualification that would deprive the person who derives his right from that non-Jew of his money. In siman 154, however, the situation is the reverse: one party had possessed his window rights for many years, while the person deriving his right from a non-Jew seeks to harm him by constructing a building that blocks his light. This distinction must likewise be applied within siman 154 itself so that seif 18 and seif 19 do not contradict one another. For this reason the Mechaber writes in siman 66, seif 25, that if a non-Jew sells a promissory note to a Jew, we adjudicate according to their law so that if the seller later forgives the debt, it is not forgiven. There too, once the Jewish purchaser acquired the note from him, the seller has no power to cause him direct loss by forgiving it. Likewise, the Rama writes in siman 67, seif 17, regarding one who derives his claim from a non-Jew, that one who purchased from a non-Jew a promissory note against another Jew is not subject to cancellation by the Sabbatical year. There too, since the debtor would in any event have been obligated to pay the non-Jew and suffers no loss merely because his fellow Jew purchased the debt, it appears that all agree that in such a case the purchaser has the legal power that the non-Jew possessed.
