This rule helps defend Rambam's ruling in Hilchot Gerushin that if two blank lines separate the text of a get from the signatures, the get can still be valid with delivery witnesses. At first glance this is difficult. A document capable of forgery is generally treated as defective even under R. Elazar. The distinction between a document that is merely capable of forgery and one that is internally forged is that the former defect is visibly apparent, so the court knows not to rely on the signing witnesses alone and instead requires delivery witnesses. By contrast, an internal forgery can escape detection and be accepted on the strength of the signing witnesses. But here, where there are two blank lines, there is still a serious forgery risk. Someone could insert text and later readers might rely only on the signatures. Why then should delivery witnesses help? The answer is that Rambam follows Rabbenu Yonah. When we say R. Elazar agrees that an internally forged document is invalid, that means the paper is legally worthless in itself. Delivery witnesses cannot rescue a writing that has no documentary force at all. But a document with a two-line gap is not worthless in that sense. As Rabbenu Yonah says, the debtor cannot claim, "This transaction never occurred." The paper still preserves evidentiary force concerning the underlying act. Therefore, in a get, the delivery witnesses can establish the act of divorce and the paper can still function as the instrument of severance. It is not considered internally forged for this purpose. This also explains why delivery witnesses do not cure an ordinary loan document with the same defect. There the problem is not only internal forgery. The paper is a document capable of alteration, and, as explained above in siman 42, delivery witnesses are not relied upon to create a continuing creditor instrument in that form. But in acquisition documents, which Rama above validates with delivery witnesses even though the writing is alterable, the same two-line gap should also be valid with proper delivery witnesses, just as Rambam permits in a get. A related issue concerns the rule that we do not derive law from the final line. Suppose delivery witnesses testify that the final line originally contained the disputed clause. In my view, that should be enough. The document itself is otherwise valid. The only concern is that the final line may later have been altered or forged. If delivery witnesses establish exactly what stood there, they remove that concern. This resembles a document containing an erasure that is later formally confirmed. Once the specific uncertainty is resolved, the line can be used. The point is not that delivery witnesses repair every defective document. They repair a localized evidentiary concern in an otherwise valid instrument. This helps explain Ran in Gittin. Ran asks in the first chapter about Persian documents delivered before Jewish witnesses. The Gemara challenges: do we not require the document to repeat the substance in the final line? Ran responds that Persian scribes often leave many blank lines, so absent repetition the entire instrument remains susceptible to insertion and therefore defective. At first glance his answer works for the first chapter, where the Persian signatories are gentiles. But in the second chapter, the Gemara also discusses a Persian-language document signed by Jewish witnesses and again asks about the repeated final clause. Ran's answer seems less obvious there. If Jewish witnesses signed, they would ordinarily be careful not to leave excessive blank space. The proper explanation is that the two passages ask slightly different questions. In the first chapter, where validity depends entirely on delivery witnesses, Ran cannot explain the challenge merely as, "we do not derive from the final line." If that were the only defect, the delivery witnesses themselves could testify what stood there and cure the concern. Therefore he must say the Persian form leaves enough blank space that the entire document is capable of later alteration. That is a global defect, and delivery witnesses do not cure it. In the second chapter, no special delivery-witness framework is mentioned. There the question can be understood like Rashba explains: the statement "it is valid" sounds as though every line of the document is usable, yet without a proper repetition the final line is not. The answer is that the document is written in the ordinary corrected form. This distinction harmonizes Ran's comments. One can also read the second passage as a proof that, even with Jewish witnesses, the Gemara expects the ordinary document-closing form. Tosafot in Perek Get Pashut try to prove from that passage that even one blank line creates a problem. They note that the Gemara does not answer by saying there is simply one blank line left and therefore no real forgery concern. At first they treat this as a substantive proof, then they discuss the first-chapter passage and finally conclude that it is reasonable to decree even a spaced case because of an unspaced one. This sequence makes sense if the second chapter is the stronger source. There the witnesses are Jewish and ordinarily careful. If the law still insists on the formal repetition, the court must be applying a uniform rabbinic rule even where actual forgery risk is lower. Thus Tosafot's argument is not wasted discussion but establishes the breadth of the enactment. Accordingly, the practical distinctions are: A localized defect in the last line can be cured by delivery witnesses who establish what stood there. A document globally capable of alteration remains invalid even with delivery witnesses, except in categories such as gittin or certain acquisition documents where the law expressly permits reliance on them. And where Chazal enacted a uniform formal rule, actual absence of forgery risk does not always remove the requirement.
