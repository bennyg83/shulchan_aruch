The borrower's handwriting is valid, etc. The wording implies that if the notation is not in the borrower's own handwriting, it is invalid. This is difficult. The source is a responsum of Rosh. Rosh holds that an erasure or scraping in a document, even in the standard form, can invalidate where there is concern the holder erased and altered material. But Mechaber above in siman 44 follows Rashba, who validates erasures and scraping on the back of the document and in standard-form areas where they do not create a concrete suspicion of altered operative text. If so, although one should certainly avoid such erasures initially, why should the document be invalid after the fact merely because a payment notation on the back is not in the borrower's handwriting? Kenesset HaGedolah already raises this difficulty and does not resolve it. The answer, in my view, is that an actual payment notation changes the legal status of the reverse side. If there is no writing there, an erasure on the back is outside the operative body of the document. According to Rashba we do not assume that a receipt or condition was once written there. But once a payment notation is actually written on the reverse, that notation functions like a receipt. It becomes part of the evidentiary system by which the outstanding balance is calculated. Suppose the face of the document states one hundred. The back says fifty was repaid. When beit din determines the remaining debt, it no longer reads only the face of the document. It must use the reverse-side notation to know that only fifty remains. The notation is therefore functionally part of the essential operative text. An unexplained erasure or scraping within that payment notation is comparable to an erasure in the amount clause inside the document itself. The issue is no longer an abstract concern that perhaps something once appeared on the back. Something operative definitely does appear there, and the erasure may alter the amount still owed. Therefore the stricter law applies. If the notation is in the borrower's own handwriting, the evidentiary risk is reduced in the manner recognized by Rosh. If not, the erasure can invalidate the payment notation or create a defect in the document's operative amount. This reconciles Mechaber's acceptance of Rashba concerning ordinary reverse-side erasures with his citation of Rosh here. The difference is whether the erased area has become part of the document's substantive calculation of the debt. An empty reverse side remains outside the essential text. A written payment notation is itself the equivalent of a receipt and therefore becomes part of the essential evidentiary core.
