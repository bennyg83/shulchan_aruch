And if the witnesses wrote, etc. I already explained that writing the formula between the two witnesses on the same line does not always help, for the reasons stated above. If, however, "We witnesses..." is written on ordinary parchment between them, Tosafot indicate that so long as it does not occupy the equivalent of two full lines, the form can be valid. It follows that where an erased blank remains between two witnesses large enough for that formula, and the document and witnesses are all on erased parchment, the paper can be problematic even according to Ri in the name of Rabbenu Meir, who holds one can distinguish parchment erased once from parchment erased twice. The concern is that the explanatory words were originally written on ordinary parchment and then erased. The current equality of the erased areas would not reveal that earlier structure. This could explain why Mechaber in the preceding seif rules broadly that if a blank remains between the witnesses large enough for "We witnesses..." the document is invalid. Shach, seif katan 92, asks why Mechaber ignores Ribam's view that one can distinguish a once-erased area from a twice-erased area. According to the analysis above there is no question. The concern is not that the same erased area was erased once and then twice. It is that the explanatory formula may originally have been on ordinary parchment and later removed. Tosafot therefore insist that, in the valid structure, the formula cannot require two lines. Still, this explanation is somewhat forced, because the current paper itself may show whether the middle strip was originally ordinary parchment. For this reason, I am inclined to say Mechaber follows the many authorities who find Ribam's distinction too difficult to rely upon in practice. There is another serious problem. Even if the explanatory formula is safely written on ordinary parchment between the witnesses, why can the holder not later erase it and create a completely erased document and witness area? If the resulting blank is large, he can fill it with relatives or valid witnesses, claiming he merely added witnesses for honor. According to Tosafot, valid witnesses will not cooperate once they see a suspicious altered instrument. But what if he uses disqualified witnesses? If a valid witness already appears before them and the later added witnesses are invalid, perhaps the document can still remain valid under Rambam's broad position that relatives or disqualified witnesses do not necessarily invalidate regardless of where they sign. That creates a real fraud concern. Accordingly, Mechaber, who follows Rambam above, has stronger reason to prohibit placing the explanatory formula on ordinary parchment. Under Tosafot's stricter view that a disqualified middle witness invalidates, the fraud is self-defeating. Once the added invalid witnesses are discovered, the whole paper is invalid. But according to Rambam, that deterrent is weaker. Therefore, for Mechaber, the safest procedure is that the explanatory formula itself be written on erased parchment in a way whose physical erasure history can be checked. This also explains why Shach's objection to Mechaber is not decisive. Mechaber may deliberately reject Ribam's more delicate erased-parchment distinctions because, under his broader rule concerning added disqualified witnesses, those distinctions are insufficiently safe. One further question remains. If there is an erased blank between the witnesses, why can beit din not simply write something in that space and then compare the surfaces? If the area had previously been erased twice, perhaps the later writing would appear different. The answer is that the Talmudic distinction concerns the appearance of the parchment after erasure, not necessarily the appearance of fresh writing placed on it later. A line written today on parchment erased once and a line written tomorrow on parchment erased twice are not proven by the Gemara to remain visibly different. Indeed, the fraud cases themselves assume one can erase and rewrite in a manner capable of deceiving unless the physical parchment differences are examined directly. Moreover, the law cannot depend on a later court remembering to perform a special experimental writing. We do not suspect courts of basic legal error, but we do fear ordinary forgetfulness in performing extra procedural acts. This is similar to Sema's principle above concerning recording that an original document was torn. Where the validity of the paper would require a later affirmative act by beit din to reveal the truth, Chazal can invalidate the form rather than rely on that act being remembered. Thus the broad invalidation remains understandable.

It is valid. If the formula "We witnesses..." is written on ordinary parchment, I have already explained that its validity depends on the dispute between Rambam and Tosafot. All of that applies only where the blank between the witnesses is less than the measure of two full lines. If the gap can hold two lines, the document is certainly invalid. Tosafot explain why. The holder can cut away the original document together with the first witness and use the two-line middle area to create a one-witness document on erased parchment. He can similarly use the second witness to create another one-witness document. The two one-witness documents can then combine under the rule below in siman 51. Maharsha interprets Tosafot as limiting the concern when the middle writing is on ordinary parchment, but he himself records a marginal note saying this is strained because we should still fear the holder will erase it. That marginal observation is correct. Tosafot can be read more naturally. Their question is: perhaps the explanatory formula was originally written on ordinary parchment. If so, one might think that should actually be safer because writing on ordinary parchment does not conceal erasures. Tosafot answer that the solution still fails when the formula takes two lines. The holder can erase that area and divide it into two separate one-witness documents. Thus even ordinary parchment does not save a two-line gap. One might ask why the Gemara does not expressly say, "The formula must be written on erased parchment." The answer is that the Gemara's discussion follows Rav. Rav holds in Sanhedrin 30a that separate testimony regarding admission and loan does not combine unless each witness knows of the other. Two separate written witnesses may therefore not readily combine under his view. For Rav, writing the explanatory words on ordinary parchment can indeed be safe. For our accepted halachah, where separate one-witness documents can combine, the same arrangement becomes unsafe. This explains why the Gemara does not distinguish ordinary parchment from erased parchment categorically. The practical rule follows our law, not Rav's narrower combination rule. Accordingly, even if "We witnesses..." is written on ordinary parchment, a gap of two lines remains invalid because it can be transformed into two one-witness documents. This also clarifies Rama's preceding ruling that a blank between witnesses can sometimes be tolerated when there is no erasure at all. If both the witnesses and the document are on ordinary parchment, erasing the blank would produce a document on erased parchment with witnesses on ordinary parchment, which is visibly invalid. The holder cannot profit. But if the witnesses themselves are on erased parchment, a two-line blank creates the precise danger described above. Thus the material of the witnesses' signatures is decisive. In short: where witnesses are on ordinary parchment and there is no erased area, an ordinary blank can sometimes be safe; where witnesses are on erased parchment and the intervening area can hold two lines, the document is invalid because the holder can create two separate one-witness writings that later combine. This is the stronger reading of Tosafot and the marginal gloss cited by Maharsha. One can sharpen the distinction between Rav's view and our accepted law. Rav requires a stronger connection between separately given testimonies before they combine. If two witnesses testify at different times or in separate forms without awareness of one another, he can limit their joinder. Under that framework, two fabricated one-witness fragments may not automatically create one enforceable claim. The Talmudic drafting rule therefore does not need to prohibit every ordinary-parchment gap. But once we rule that separate one-witness testimonies can combine, the same physical form becomes dangerous. Halachah can therefore be stricter in practice than the bare wording of the sugya appears to require according to Rav. This is not changing the Talmudic rule. It is applying the same anti-forgery principle under the halachic rule of joinder that we actually follow. There is also a difference between a blank and a written explanatory clause. Where the two-line space remains blank, the fraud is obvious: it can later be filled. Where the explanatory clause is already present on ordinary parchment, one might think the completed writing neutralizes the concern. But because ordinary parchment can itself be erased and rewritten, the completed clause does not help if the area is large enough to generate two independent fragments after alteration. Thus the critical factor is not whether something is presently written there. It is whether the holder possesses enough manipulable space to create two separate claims. This is why the marginal gloss cited by Maharsha is persuasive. The law must examine the physical potential for fraud, not merely the current presence of ink. Accordingly, the rule stated above is firm: where the witnesses are on erased parchment and the intervening area has the measure of two lines, the document cannot be validated merely because "We witnesses..." is presently written there on ordinary parchment.
But if they wrote this before their signatures, etc. Shach, seif katan 33, explains the concern as follows. The holder may erase the explanatory words and leave the space blank. A single blank line between the document and witnesses does not invalidate, so the manipulation may not be visible. Shach therefore concludes that the measure of the phrase "We witnesses..." is only one line. Tosafot in Bava Batra 164b, however, state explicitly that this phrase ordinarily occupies no fewer than two lines. The matter requires explanation. Even if the phrase occupies two lines according to the handwriting used when the original document was written, a forger can rewrite the new document in larger, wider letters with generous spacing. What originally required two lines can then be compressed, relative to the new writing style, into the visual measure of one line. He can leave that space blank and avoid making the forgery obvious. This resolves part of the difficulty. The measurement of blank lines is always relative to the writing style of the document now before us, not some lost earlier script. Thus two earlier lines can become one later line if the forged document is written in larger script. There is, however, a deeper difficulty in Tosafot. They first state that the phrase requires at least two lines. Later they say that the only plausible case is where one witness signs at the beginning of a line, the other at the end, and "We witnesses..." is written between them. That seems to mean the phrase fits in less than one complete line. How can both statements be true? The answer lies in what exactly is being written. If the witnesses are not signed immediately adjacent to the operative document, then the explanatory formula must itself identify the document with precision. It must say something like: "We, the witnesses testifying to this document, whose amount is such-and-such, dated on such-and-such day, concerning the loan of so-and-so from so-and-so, are signed on erased parchment, while this document is written on ordinary parchment." That is necessary so that no one will say the witnesses signed merely to attest to the erased area itself. Such a full identification naturally requires at least two lines. This is the ordinary case discussed by Tosafot. But if the two witnesses are physically signed on the same line, one at each edge, immediately adjacent to the document, their signatures visually read together with the instrument. The Yerushalmi says that where witnesses can be read together with the document, they are treated as part of it. In that configuration, they need not repeat the amount, date, parties, and substance. It is enough to write briefly between them: "We witnesses are signed on erased parchment; the document is on ordinary parchment." That short statement can fit within half a line. This explains how Tosafot can say the full formula generally requires two lines yet recognize a same-line edge-to-edge configuration where a shortened formula fits between the witnesses. It also explains why simply writing the formula before the signatures can be insufficient. If an explanatory statement appears between the body and the witnesses, one could argue the witnesses signed only to attest to that statement, much like witnesses who sign beneath an ink smear may be taken as signing the smear rather than the document. Tosafot say this regarding court certification as well. To prevent that ambiguity, the explanatory statement must identify the underlying document itself in enough detail that the witness signatures clearly refer to the whole instrument. That is why the normal version is longer. This also answers why, under Rashbam's reading, one might leave a blank instead of writing the formula. Where the middle area is erased parchment, the witnesses can sometimes sign close enough to the document that a separate explanatory clause is unnecessary. The physical format itself reveals what they attest to. Where the area is ordinary parchment, however, the relation between the erased witnesses and ordinary text needs explicit explanation. Maharsha asks why one cannot simply erase the ordinary parchment where the explanatory words appear. One answer is that scribes deliberately seek ordinary parchment for the explanatory clause because an erased clause would itself generate further forgery concerns. Erasing that ordinary strip afterward would create a visibly abnormal configuration. Still, the stronger answer is structural. If the explanatory clause is placed on ordinary parchment and the full two-line formula is needed, erasing it would leave a two-line gap and invalidate the document. The holder therefore cannot safely erase it. If the short same-line formula is used, the witness placement itself limits what the holder can do. There is another possible construction. Why not simply leave the explanatory area blank but erase it twice, so that later attempts to erase and rewrite would produce a detectable third erasure? The answer is that after the first witness signs, the court may be relying on only one witness's integrity for the technical status of that area. One witness cannot ordinarily extract money by himself. We accept the arrangement only because he is afraid to falsify a technical matter that can later be exposed and render him disqualified. If the difference between one erasure and two is subtle enough that he might escape detection, the deterrent weakens. With two witnesses jointly supervising the form, the concern is smaller. This may explain why the law prefers an actual explanatory writing over merely relying on an unmarked twice-erased strip. The same point resolves another question. Where there are three witnesses, could the holder leave a blank and later fill it with relatives? If the blank is ordinary parchment, he can add disqualified people without creating a new erased-parchment signal. But where the safe configuration requires repeated erasure, added signatures would have to interact with the physical erasure history and can expose the manipulation. Thus the rules are not identical. The conclusion is: the full explanatory formula ordinarily takes the measure of two lines because it must identify the document sufficiently to show the witnesses attest to it; in the unusual case where the witnesses are signed edge-to-edge on the same line and visually connected to the text, a shortened formula can fit between them; the anti-forgery law is measured according to the writing style and physical arrangement of the document actually before us, which allows an originally two-line formula to be converted visually into one line by a forger using larger script; and therefore Shach's basic concern about erasing the formula remains real even though Tosafot ordinarily assign it two lines.
Or after them, etc. Why not write the explanatory formula on the reverse side of the document? If the reverse side is ordinary parchment while the witnesses are on erased parchment, this would seem to eliminate the immediate concern. If the holder later erased the reverse-side writing, perhaps the entire document should become invalid because we would suspect that a receipt or payment notation had been written there and removed. At first glance this seems to support Rashba's view that an erasure or scraping on the back of a document does not automatically create a presumption that a receipt had been erased. Tosafot at the beginning of Perek Get Pashut also seem to support this. They ask why, in a plain document, the holder cannot cut the document and write new text on the reverse side opposite the witnesses, thereby converting the paper into a folded instrument. Tosafot answer that the reverse side opposite the witnesses can be erased or smeared to prevent that fraud. If every such erasure created a presumption that a payment notation had been removed, the cure would be self-defeating. Still, this is not a decisive proof for Rashba. Tosafot may be speaking of visible scratching or smearing that clearly never contained writing. Or, because document practice itself regularly requires smearing opposite the witnesses to prevent conversion into a folded document, no one suspects a payment notation in that conventional area. People know the erasure was placed there as an anti-forgery device. There is another and stronger answer. The original document can begin on one side of the page, continue around to the second side, and end near the witness signatures, just as documents commonly use both sides of a sheet. A forger can then erase the entire text on both sides, including the explanatory words on the reverse, and write a new extended document beginning on the second side and continuing onto the first until it reaches the surviving witnesses. The signatures will then appear to attest to the fabricated document, all on erased parchment. Thus a formula written merely on the reverse does not truly solve the problem. The only reliable location is between the witnesses themselves, where cutting or erasing cannot detach the explanation from the signature structure. Accordingly, the law requires the explanatory formula to be integrated between the witnesses rather than placed elsewhere on the sheet.
