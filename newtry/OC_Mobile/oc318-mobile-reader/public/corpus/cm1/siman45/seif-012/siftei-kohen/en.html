According to the first view in siman 36, this means that the valid witness knew of the other witness’s disqualification, such as where they were relatives or regularly associated. Baal HaIttur and Nimukei Yosef add that when the witnesses are unavailable, the deed is not invalidated from doubt, since perhaps the valid witnesses did not know of the defect. This remains so even if it is known that all three sat together to testify. When they are present, they are questioned and believed. According to the later view in siman 36, however, the deed is invalid even where the valid witnesses did not know of the disqualification.

This ruling is not clear. Bahag states that if a relative or disqualified witness signed first, the remaining valid signatures may authenticate the deed, because the first signer may have been included merely for honor or to fill the space. If the disqualified witness signed in the middle or at the end, the deed is invalid, since there was no such need and the signers appear to have joined in one testimony. Ramban rejects this distinction and argues that later disqualified signatures are certainly no worse, citing the laws of gittin and a folded get. I respectfully disagree. The passages concerning a get address either initial practice or signatures required to complete the form of a folded get, where the relatives sign for completion rather than testimony. They do not establish that a needless relative or disqualified signer at the end of an ordinary deed is harmless. The Tosefta expressly speaks of the first signers, and the Yerushalmi supports taking that wording literally. In an ordinary deed witnesses may sign at different times. Therefore, when disqualified persons appear first, we may say that room was left for more senior signers and that they signed only to fill the space. When they appear later, after the deed was already complete, their signatures indicate participation in the testimony. Rif’s wording, the responsa of Rashba, and several other authorities can be reconciled with Bahag in this manner. Accordingly, the principal law is that earlier disqualified signatures may be disregarded where the circumstances show they were added for honor or completion, but disqualified signatures in the middle or at the end invalidate the deed unless there is clear proof that they did not join as witnesses. This distinction also preserves the rule that a document containing an internal defect cannot be authenticated through the remaining signers.
