Even though the witness who signed the document first is disqualified, etc. Shach, seif katan 23, argues extensively for the dissenting authorities. He concludes that if the disqualified witness signed first, the document can be valid, but if he signed in the middle or at the end it is invalid. I have already stated many times that where the early authorities dispute, I do not consider myself one who can simply decide that one tradition is proper and the other improper. All the more so here, where even the great masters themselves revised their positions. Rabbenu Tam famously retracted because of the force of the difficulties raised against him. I therefore will not attempt to declare one view superior on authority alone. However, Shach claims a decisive proof from the Yerushalmi and, in order to make that proof work, he emends, adds, deletes, and reverses several parts of the text. In my view there is no need for such extensive emendation. Only one word appears to be an obvious scribal carryover, and once removed, the Yerushalmi reads smoothly and actually supports Rambam. The Yerushalmi in the first chapter of Gittin says: The Mishnah teaches that any get bearing the signature of a Samaritan witness is invalid, except certain gittin of women. The Yerushalmi asks: then even one Samaritan witness should make it invalid. R. Elazar answers: establish it where a Jewish witness signed last. R. Yochanan says this follows the view that witnesses may sign in each other's absence. According to the view that witnesses sign only in each other's presence, even if the Samaritan signed last the document is valid. The Yerushalmi then says: A document signed by four witnesses, where the first two are found to be relatives or disqualified, is valid and the testimony is established through the others. R. Elazar in the name of R. Yochanan says this follows the view that witnesses sign only in each other's presence. According to the view that witnesses may sign even in one another's absence, even if the disqualified one signed last, it is valid. That is the text before us. Shach objects because the Bavli appears to say the reverse regarding the Samaritan witness. He therefore rewrites the Yerushalmi extensively, changing whole clauses from valid to invalid and reversing the order of the positions. But even after his emendation, the final phrase about a Samaritan signing last does not fit the second discussion, which concerns ordinary relatives and disqualified witnesses, not Samaritans. The simpler explanation is that the final word "Israel" in one clause is a scribal remnant carried over from the earlier discussion of Samaritan and Jewish witnesses. Delete that one stray word, and the text makes sense without changing its structure. There is historical support. Razah in Gittin cites a version close to Shach's reconstructed reading, and Ramban in Milchamot apparently also had a different text. But Rambam's ruling in Hilchot Avadim strongly indicates he had our present Yerushalmi reading. Rambam rules that a get with one Samaritan witness can be valid without limiting the case to the Samaritan signing first and the Jewish witness signing last. Lechem Mishneh asks why Rambam omitted the Bavli's limitation. If the Samaritan must be a known chaver, moreover, then why should the rule be unique to gittin? The answer is that Rambam holds the initial Bavli establishment, requiring the Jewish witness to sign last, was rejected after the Gemara answers that get witnesses sign only in each other's presence. Once they sign together, the Jewish witness knows who will sign with him. A valid Jewish witness will not join with a Samaritan known to be untrustworthy. Therefore, so long as the Samaritan is presumed to be a chaver, the order of signatures no longer matters. Razah himself says that gittin were treated like the practice of the punctilious people of Jerusalem, who would not sign unless they knew who was signing with them. This is exactly Rambam's reasoning. The Yerushalmi states it expressly: according to the view that witnesses must sign together, even if the Samaritan signed last the get can be valid. That is the ruling Rambam adopts. Mechaber in the laws of slaves follows Rambam and likewise does not make validity depend on signature order. This strongly supports the accuracy of our Yerushalmi text. Now apply the same reasoning to the second half concerning four witnesses. The Yerushalmi says: If a document bears four witnesses and the first two are relatives or otherwise disqualified, it can still be valid through the remaining witnesses. The implication is that if the disqualified witnesses signed last, the matter may differ. Why? Not because a disqualified person's mere intent to testify automatically invalidates valid witnesses. The valid witnesses become disqualified only if they actually joined as a single witnessing unit with the invalid ones. This is the principle of Rif at the end of siman 36. If the valid witnesses signed first and the disqualified witnesses later added their names without the valid witnesses' knowledge, the original valid testimony should not be destroyed. That is almost self-evident. Two valid witnesses who already signed a proper document cannot be invalidated merely because some relatives later write their names beneath them without permission. But if witnesses are required to sign only in each other's presence, then when the disqualified witnesses sign last the valid witnesses necessarily know they are joining the same signing session. Since the disqualified witnesses sign last, their signatures are plainly intended as testimony rather than as mere filler. The valid witnesses knowingly join with them, and the entire witnessing unit becomes invalid. By contrast, if the law permits witnesses to sign in each other's absence, even a disqualified witness signing last does not prove the valid witnesses knew of him or joined with him. They may have completed their testimony earlier and never known the invalid person later added his signature. Therefore the valid witnesses remain valid. This is exactly the Yerushalmi's closing statement once the stray word is removed: according to the view that witnesses may sign separately, even if the disqualified witness signed last, the document is valid. Thus the Yerushalmi reads perfectly as written. And this is our halachah. Except for gittin, ordinary document witnesses may sign even outside one another's presence. Chazal did not decree ordinary documents because of gittin in this respect. Therefore, even if a disqualified witness signs at the end, we can say the valid witnesses never knew of him and never joined with him. The document remains valid through the valid signatures. Consider the simple case. Two competent witnesses sign a document completely and deliver it. Later, without their knowledge, a relative of one party adds his signature at the bottom. Can that later act retroactively destroy their valid testimony? That is unreasonable. The whole law of "one relative or disqualified witness invalidates the group" depends on actual joining into one witnessing unit, not on the physical coexistence of names on one page. The Yerushalmi therefore supports Rambam's broader approach. Shach lists many authorities who invalidate where the disqualified witness signs later. Even so, where the early authorities divide, the possessor can certainly say kim li like the minority. And this minority is weighty: Rambam, Ramban, Rav Hai Gaon, and Rivash. According to the reading explained here, the Yerushalmi itself also supports them. Therefore one certainly cannot remove property from the possessor against this view. There is also a parallel from the Samaritan-witness case. There, the entire significance of signing in one another's presence is that the valid Jewish witness knows whom he is joining. If he signs first and the Samaritan later signs without his knowledge, there is no joining. If he signs while aware the Samaritan will sign, the shared act creates the problem unless the Samaritan is one whom the Jewish witness could legitimately join. The same principle governs relatives and disqualified witnesses. The decisive question is not merely order. It is whether the valid witnesses knowingly joined the invalid witness in one act of testimony. Signature order is only evidence of that joining. Where signing can occur separately, later order proves nothing. Accordingly, the Yerushalmi's distinction is conceptually precise. One should therefore not rewrite its text to fit a preconceived rule. Its plain structure gives a coherent legal theory and aligns with Rambam and Mechaber. Thus, despite Shach's attempt to establish the opposite as definitive, the possessor has ample basis to rely on the validating authorities even where the disqualified witness signed last.
Unless the last two, etc. The wording implies that if disqualified witnesses signed in the middle, the document is still valid. Shach, however, cites the majority of authorities as holding that middle signatures of disqualified witnesses also invalidate the document. Beit Yosef cites Tosafot in the second chapter of Gittin as invalidating even where the disqualified witnesses signed in the middle. He was troubled that Rosh seemed to disagree and validate such a case. In Even HaEzer he treats them as disputing; in Choshen Mishpat he tries to reconcile them. Maharit already objected to that reconciliation, and Get Pashut discusses the difficulty at length. Rama in Even HaEzer also requires explanation. Tur first says that where the husband says, "All of you sign," two are treated as witnesses and the rest as satisfying a condition, so if those additional signers are disqualified the get can still be valid. Tur later says that if two disqualified witnesses sign first, the get is invalid. Darkei Moshe infers that the earlier validation referred only to disqualified witnesses signing later, and asks that if signing first invalidates, all the more so should signing later invalidate. He answers that Tur follows Rabbenu Tam's first position, before Rabbenu Tam retracted. This is difficult because here in Choshen Mishpat Tur explicitly follows Rabbenu Tam after his retraction and says that Rosh agreed with him. Maharsha likewise asks against Tosafot in Bava Batra 163b. Tosafot, following Rabbenu Tam, say that a disqualified witness signing first can sometimes be valid but signing last is invalid. Maharsha asks from Gittin, where the implication appears to be that only signing first invalidates, and answers that signing in the middle can be valid according to Rosh. Yet Tosafot in Gittin 18b explicitly say after Rabbenu Tam's retraction that even middle signatures can invalidate. The proper resolution is to distinguish the reasons for invalidity. Where disqualified witnesses sign after the first two valid witnesses, there are two possible concerns. First, their signatures may have been intended as actual testimony. Once the valid witnesses and invalid witnesses join in one testimonial act, the group is impaired. Second, people normally authenticate a document from its last witnesses. If disqualified witnesses appear at the end, a court may mistakenly authenticate the document through them. The second concern applies specifically when the invalid witnesses are last. It does not apply when they are in the middle because the court can authenticate from the final valid witnesses. The first concern, joining as testimony, operates differently in gittin. Ran explains that in a get the decisive intention is the husband's. We presume the husband does not deliberately designate disqualified persons as witnesses. Therefore a disqualified signer in the middle need not be understood as joining the witnessing unit. In ordinary monetary documents, by contrast, even if the parties did not intend the invalid signer as a witness, the signer himself may have intended to join the testimony. That is enough to create the problem discussed by Ran at length. Accordingly, in a get the primary concern can be authentication from the final signers. There the problem is specifically where the invalid witnesses are last, while middle invalid witnesses can be tolerated. This explains Rosh's answer in Gittin and Maharsha's reconciliation. But in ordinary monetary documents, the concern of testimonial joining remains even for middle signatures. Tosafot in Gittin, when discussing the Bava Batra document case, therefore say that even middle disqualified witnesses can invalidate. Thus Tosafot and Rosh need not disagree at all. They are speaking about different document categories. Tur in Even HaEzer can likewise be understood without forcing him into Rabbenu Tam's abandoned position. His statement that later disqualified signers can be valid refers to the get setting, where middle signers do not necessarily create the same testimonial joining problem. His Choshen Mishpat ruling can remain stricter for ordinary documents. This is also the better practical law. At first I was troubled because Tur here says "the last two" invalidate, which sounds as though only the final signers matter. His abridged rulings likewise speak of disqualified witnesses signing "at the end." But Beit Yosef himself notes that Tur's wording does not clearly decide the middle case. The word "last" can be used loosely to mean any signers after the original first witnesses, not necessarily the final two at the bottom. Indeed, in Rosh's abridged rulings to Gittin, he writes that if a relative signed "after the first two" the get is valid. That phrase clearly includes a middle signer, because the actual last signer may still be valid. This supports reading Tur's "last" language in Choshen Mishpat as excluding only the first position rather than defining the exact final position. Accordingly, the correct rule is as Shach states: in ordinary documents, a disqualified witness who signs in the middle also invalidates when his signature is part of the testimonial unit. There is a possible proof from Tosafot in Bava Batra concerning a document whose text is on ordinary parchment and whose witnesses are on erased parchment. Tosafot ask why the witnesses need to write, "We witnesses are signed on erased parchment." Why not simply leave blank space? In a document with only two witnesses, one can understand the concern. But if there are three witnesses, leaving a gap between the first and the last two creates a possibility that the holder will later fill the blank with relatives or disqualified persons. If middle disqualified signatures were always harmless, that would not be a serious problem. This supports the view that in ordinary documents they can invalidate. One can answer that the Tosafot case may have different anti-forgery features, as I explained in my novellae. In particular, where the document text is on ordinary parchment and witnesses are on erased parchment, any opening for later manipulation can make the original paper improper because the holder could later cut, erase, and reconstruct it in a different configuration. But the broader point remains. The distinction between gittin and monetary documents gives the cleanest reconciliation of the early authorities. There is another way to understand Tosafot's concern. Suppose two borrowers borrow jointly from one lender and sign a handwritten statement on ordinary parchment while their signatures are on erased parchment. Even if that document lacks the full force of a formal witness-signed note, the borrowers may trust the lender not to alter it. He could later erase and rewrite the document so that the borrowers' own signatures appear as witnesses to a different person's debt. The danger exists because the signers themselves may be valid witnesses relative to that third person. Tosafot's statement that people do not hand over an instrument capable of such fraud can therefore refer not only to professional witnesses but to ordinary Jews generally. One does not intentionally place a stumbling block before another person. This explains why the anti-forgery rules remain strict even where the exact witness-order question is disputed. In practical halachah, however, the distinction stated above is sufficient: for gittin, middle disqualified signatures can be tolerated under the relevant theory because the husband's intent controls and the main concern is authentication from the end; for ordinary civil documents, middle disqualified witnesses can invalidate because their own intent to testify creates a problematic testimonial joining. This is the conclusion supported by Shach and by the stronger reading of Tosafot.
The last ones, etc. The principal reason is that documents are ordinarily authenticated from the final witnesses. If the last witnesses are disqualified, the court may end up authenticating the document through disqualified testimony. Shach discusses this at length. One difficulty remains. Rosh asks: if relatives can sign, should we not fear that the document will later be authenticated through them, just as the Gemara fears in the case of a folded get? He answers that ordinary documents are authenticated only from the final witnesses, and those final witnesses must always be valid. But then consider the Gemara's concern where witnesses left a one-line gap between the text and the signatures. The holder may cut away the original text, write a new one-line document together with one witness, and say that the additional signatures were merely added to increase the number of witnesses. The Gemara answers that in such a case the court authenticates from the upper witnesses. Why does that solve the problem? The forger could write the one-line document and sign disqualified witnesses in the added area. Then when the court authenticates from the upper witnesses, it would authenticate through disqualified witnesses, which is exactly Rosh's original concern. If we answer that the court always investigates whether the witnesses are valid, then Rosh's entire concern disappears, as Ramban already notes. Even if relatives are not suspected of deliberately signing a fabricated document, the question remains from witnesses disqualified because they are presumed unreliable. The answer is that the two cases involve different procedural postures. When the debtor claims the document is forged, beit din investigates the witness identities carefully. A person ordinarily does not brazenly call a genuine document forged, and the Torah itself does not strictly require formal document authentication absent a challenge. Once he does challenge it, the court scrutinizes the signers and will discover whether they are disqualified. Therefore the forged one-line document does not create the danger. Rosh's concern arises in a different case. Suppose the debtor admits that the document was genuinely written but claims some other defense. Under the rule that one who admits the writing may still require authentication in order to eliminate his migo, the creditor seeks technical authentication. In that setting, the court already knows the underlying document is real. It may perform only the limited authentication needed to remove the debtor's migo and may not investigate every signer's personal qualification with the same intensity. If the final signatures are relatives or disqualified persons, the court can mistakenly authenticate through them and deprive the debtor of his migo. That is the exact concern of Rosh. Thus there is no contradiction. When forgery is alleged, the court investigates the signers and does not rely blindly on invalid witnesses. When authenticity is admitted and the only need for authentication is procedural, the court may rely on the final signatures unless the document form prevents that. For this reason the final witnesses must be valid. The distinction also explains Tosafot's wording. Their concern is not that a competent court will knowingly accept disqualified witnesses. It is that ordinary document practice directs authentication to the final signers, and people may thereby come to rely upon a facially regular but legally improper configuration. The rule is therefore structural. Accordingly, the law that the final witnesses must be valid stands even though a court faced with a direct forgery claim would investigate more deeply.
