The disqualified witnesses were the first ones. The Shach concludes that specifically where the first witnesses are found related or otherwise disqualified can the document remain valid, because they may be treated as having signed merely to fill space. If the later or middle witnesses are disqualified, they appear to have signed as witnesses. He interprets the Jerusalem Talmud accordingly. I find his explanation difficult. If the qualified witnesses knew the later disqualified witnesses would sign, then their own signatures can be understood as made on the assumption that the later signatures were for honor or condition, for otherwise the whole testimony would be nullified. But if they did not know the disqualified witnesses would later sign, then they did not intend to join them at all. Even according to those who disqualify when the qualified witnesses did not know the other's defect, that is where they intended to testify together. Here the qualified witnesses already signed before the disqualified ones and never joined them in one act of testimony. I would therefore preserve the Jerusalem Talmud's ordinary reading: where witnesses may sign one without the other being present, even a disqualified person who signs last need not nullify the document, because we can presume that the qualified witnesses did not sign together with him. In other documents, where signatures need not be simultaneous, a later disqualified signer can therefore be treated as separate. Rivash likewise explains Rabbi Shimon bar Chanokh to mean that where all signed together, a disqualified middle or later signer can nullify the group, but where a document comes before us and we do not know they signed together, we presume a valid sequence and preserve the document. The Shach rejects this distinction, but in my view Rivash's approach is convincing. Since the signatures are the witnessing act, witnesses who did not sign together did not join into one testimony. The baraita's focus on disqualified first signers includes gittin, where the witnesses do sign together and therefore only the first signers can be explained as having signed merely for filling space.
The later witnesses are valid. The Tur writes that in a document with many witnesses, validation should be based specifically upon the later signers, because perhaps relatives signed first. Taz asks why this matters. Once valid later witnesses signed, the document itself is valid. Chacham Tzvi answers that perhaps the creditor forged two valid names and then caused relatives to sign first merely for filling. I do not find that convincing. If fraud is suspected to that extent, even a document with only two signatures would be suspect. Why should the creditor deliberately add relatives first and thereby make the forgery easier to expose? It appears instead that the Sages were concerned that one should not come to rely upon disqualified signatures even where valid signatures also exist. In the case of a folded get, Rabbi Yochanan permits only one relative because otherwise one might come to validate the document through two relatives and one valid witness. Likewise, a document can be invalid as internally forged even though valid delivery witnesses exist, because people might later rely on the disqualified signatures. Therefore the Tur requires validation through the later signers, who are certainly the operative valid witnesses. According to Rambam, however, the Sages imposed this concern primarily in gittin. In monetary documents, if valid witnesses are present, the document can be validated through any of the signatures. This explains the Mechaber's position.
