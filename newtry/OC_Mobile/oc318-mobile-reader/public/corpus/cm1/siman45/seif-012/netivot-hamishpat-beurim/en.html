His text requires several corrections. Ramban's principal proof is from the Jerusalem Talmud in Gittin: one passage implies that if a disqualified witness is first the deed is invalid, while another passage validates where a disqualified witness is found among many because perhaps the qualified witnesses signed first and the disqualified persons later added themselves. Ramban understood that a disqualified witness added at the end does not necessarily invalidate an already completed deed. The Shach offers several reconciliations and ultimately tends toward a stricter rule. Rivash, however, writes that a document appearing before us with some disqualified signers can remain valid where it is unknown whether they joined the qualified witnesses at the original signing. We may assume the qualified witnesses completed the deed first and the disqualified signers added themselves afterward. The Shach resists this because, in his view, the witnesses were presumptively joined from the outset. In my view a special feature of signed documents supports his concern. Signed witnesses do not complete their testimonial function merely at the moment of ink on paper. Their signatures become the functional equivalent of delivery witnesses when the deed is delivered. At that point the document emerges from the obligor's hand bearing all the signatures, and the parties effectively join those signers as the testimonial body. Therefore a disqualified signer can infect the testimonial unit even if he physically added his name later. This also explains Rambam's difficult language in the laws of divorce: a get with an early disqualified signer can be invalid because ordinary deeds would be invalid in the same manner, while the special validity of a get containing many signers depends on the primacy of delivery witnesses. In a get supported by proper delivery witnesses, the signature witnesses serve principally to prove authorization to write, and if the qualified witnesses completed that proof before the disqualified person later added himself, his signature need not invalidate the divorce. Ordinary monetary deeds depend more directly on the signed body at delivery, and therefore the rule can be stricter. As to middle signatures, if it is known the disqualified witness intentionally signed as part of the witness block, there is strong reason to invalidate. If the document merely appears with some disqualified signatures in the middle and there is a plausible explanation that qualified witnesses below had already signed and the others later filled space, the matter is more complex. The common concern that readers will assume every adjacent signer was accepted as a witness also supports caution.

Mechaber's wording implies that an ordinary document that comes before us is valid because we may assume the qualified witnesses signed first below and the disqualified persons added themselves afterward. But if it is known that the disqualified witnesses signed first above and the qualified witnesses knowingly signed afterward alongside them, the document is invalid. That is consistent with Rosh, Ran, and Rashba. Where there is clear evidence that all sat together for the purpose of signing, the invalidity is obvious. Likewise, if the disqualified witnesses deliberately signed first in the initial lines, their purpose was plainly testimony, and the qualified witnesses who later signed knowingly joined them. This is effectively the same as all having sat together to sign.
