Seif 2. Until the witness reads it. I wrote elsewhere, printed in my novellae to Even HaEzer siman 66, concerning witnesses who customarily sign documents although they cannot read them. If the person obligated by the document also signs it himself, or hands the pen to the scribe with authority to write all customary clauses strengthening the document and an acknowledgment that a public kinyan sudar was made, the problem is considerably lighter. He knowingly accepts whatever the scribe will prepare. Even without his own signature, if he knows that the witnesses cannot read and knows that pursuant to the kinyan they will write and sign a document, he has accepted that procedure. One cannot say that he relied on the assumption that such a document could not be valid because witnesses who cannot read are not permitted to sign. Nor need purchasers disregard the public notice created by the kinyan on the ground that such witnesses could not sign. According to everyone, they can sign if the document is read before them publicly, as I wrote elsewhere, and according to many authorities it is enough that two people read it to them even without a public gathering. Thus, at minimum, the obligor has knowingly accepted a legally possible procedure. By virtue of that acceptance, one may perhaps say that according to all opinions they may sign initially even if it was not actually read before them and even if the scribe is not a public appointee and is under no special fear, because there is no genuine concern of false testimony when the contents can immediately be verified by reading the document. Some authorities, however, hold that a matter destined to be revealed does not suffice in monetary law, and the principal basis for reliance is that the scribe is a person whom the community trusts. Elsewhere I discussed the custom in appraising movable property to rely on a beit din messenger, with the judges signing even when they have not presently heard the appraisal from him. At first glance, Rashi's language in siman 45 seems to indicate that reliance on a matter destined to be revealed is permitted only when the judges already know the underlying event and need merely ascertain the wording of the writing. In an appraisal, by contrast, the signatories do not even know the appraised amount. Nevertheless, the judges' custom is defensible. A matter that will certainly become known can itself suffice, at least after the fact, as appears from the Shach there, the Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 130, and Urim VeTumim. The objection initially is principally because a document has the character of testimony and Torah law rejects testimony received from another witness. Beit Yosef indicates that a formal act of beit din is treated more leniently, and an appraisal document is in any event not ordinary witness testimony. There is also room to distinguish an ordinary loan document, since a borrower usually borrows privately and the document is intended to endure for many years, so its facts are not so immediately destined to become known. An appraisal, however, will certainly be publicly verified in the near future. In such a case even Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel may concede initially. Beit Yosef there also records that according to the first Tanna even one reader can suffice. One may further compare the matter to the scribes of Rav Nachman, where fear of consequences created reliability. A beit din messenger is at least concerned that he will no longer be employed if a discrepancy is discovered. Moreover, since this custom was established when the authority over beit din messengers was much stronger, comparable to the authority of the Exilarch, its continuation does not create a concern merely because that fear is now weaker. This is analogous to the custom cited by Beit Yosef and Shulchan Aruch there that where the practice is to rely on one person, reliance is effective. The same reasoning applies to a synagogue attendant who cannot read the customary documents. Once the practice has been established, there is no concern. A warning is necessary in countries where governmental law recognizes documents only when written in the vernacular language and script. Even if the scribe is Jewish, a witness who signs must have two valid witnesses read the document before him if he does not himself know the script and language clearly and precisely. The scribe, if qualified as a witness, may join with one additional witness. Likewise, if the signing witness can understand the document after one person reads it to him and can see that the writing corresponds to what was read, that is sufficient even if the reader is not himself qualified to testify, provided the witness is thoroughly certain of the matter. When issuing a ruling, care must also be taken that wording adopted merely for effectiveness in secular court not distort the legal consequences required by Torah law. An obligation may also be created by an acknowledgment, and in a beit din document it is enough to write language that the matter was effected in every legally effective manner.
