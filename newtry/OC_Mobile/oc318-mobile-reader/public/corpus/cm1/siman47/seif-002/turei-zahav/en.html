(See there: It contains a clause of credibility.) I do not understand this distinction. The creditor was believed only so long as he said that he had not been paid, or at least remained silent. Here he expressly says he was paid. The borrower can say: What can I do if you refuse to believe me? I truly paid the creditor. If the answer is that he should have taken back the document, the same answer applies without a credibility clause. Moreover, the principal advantage of such a clause is that the creditor need not swear before collecting, yet after payment he must in any event swear as stated in siman 71. The borrower can therefore say that he paid relying on the creditor’s unwillingness to swear falsely afterward. This requires careful study. The Tur’s apparently unnecessary case of a creditor who says the document was one of accommodation may teach that even if he truly owes others but presently has sufficient property, his admission is accepted. We do not suspect that he anticipates future impoverishment and therefore admits now to preserve this document. Only when the other creditors can presently collect solely from this instrument is his admission rejected.
