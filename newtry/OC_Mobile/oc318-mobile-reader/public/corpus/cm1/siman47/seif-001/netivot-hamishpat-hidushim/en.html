If the first borrower has property, the creditor does not collect from the second debtor because of the resulting loss, just as one does not collect from encumbered property when free assets are available, Shach. Accordingly, if the free assets are destroyed, the creditor may collect from the second debtor. See Beurim. Credibility. The Shach concludes that even if the document contains no special credibility clause and the first creditor refuses to swear, the document remains in force. His refusal to swear is no stronger than if he expressly said, "It has been paid," which he is not believed to say to the detriment of the second creditor because Rabbi Natan's lien is from Torah law. Even if one witness testifies that it was paid, that does not help, and the first creditor may collect his debt from the second debtor:
And the document was established before beit din. Meaning, beit din saw it in his possession before the dispute arose. But if immediately when they see it in his hand he says within the immediate span, "It has been paid," he is believed through a migo that he could have refrained from showing it. The Ramban and Maggid Mishneh disagree and hold that even with such a migo he is not believed against the presumption that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him. The Shach concludes that although we generally rule that what is in a person's possession is presumed his, that applies to money or movable property. Here, even if the debt document is in his hand, the money represented by it is not physically in his possession, and therefore he is believed through the migo. The Tumim likewise rules this way. If he previously showed the document before witnesses and at that time said it was paid, he is believed. Likewise, if he possesses a receipt written in the lender's own hand, that helps. Even if he said nothing at all before the witnesses, he is believed when he comes to beit din and immediately says that it was paid, provided the second creditor was not present when the document was shown to the witnesses. If the second creditor was present at that time and warned the second debtor not to pay the first creditor, and only afterward the first creditor comes to beit din and says the document was already paid, he is not believed. See Beurim. If he possesses an acknowledged receipt, it helps. If the debt document is in the hands of a third party, he is not believed even if he personally holds an undated receipt, unless the receipt states that its writers actually witnessed the payment:
He is not believed. He is not believed through a migo that he could have waived the debt, because a lien under Rabbi Natan cannot be waived. If he says regarding documents in his possession that he designated them for his own creditor, that designation has no effect. If he says that particular money in his possession is money his fellow lent him, that also does not help, because loan money is given for expenditure, and even if the same coins remain, they belong to the borrower. But if business funds or merchandise are found in his possession, he is believed to say that they belong to a particular person when it is known that he handles investment funds for others, through a migo that he could have handed them to that person. The Shach objects that since he is permitted to use the money, it has the status of a loan in his hands. The author of Beit Meir on Even HaEzer and the Tumim disagree, holding that such funds do not become a loan. Even if he already used them, they do not become ordinary movable assets in the hands of his heirs, and his personal creditor does not collect from them:
To the detriment of others. Even if at the time of his admission he still owned other assets, he is not believed if he now lacks assets, because Rabbi Natan's lien applies even when the first debtor originally had assets. The Mechaber's wording that the lack of assets became known only afterward refers to a circumstance in which he could not simply leave beit din, return the money to the second debtor, and then say, "He has now repaid me."
