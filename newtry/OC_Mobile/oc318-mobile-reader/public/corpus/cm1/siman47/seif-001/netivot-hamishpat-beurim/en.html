And the document was established in beit din. The Shach cites an opinion in siman 99 that once the document has been established in beit din, the holder cannot later be believed through a migo to say it belongs to someone else, because that migo stands against the presumption that what a person holds is his. Ketzot HaChoshen asks why a borrower can nevertheless weaken a document through a migo of forgery, or why a custodian can claim return through a migo of unavoidable accident, without being defeated by possession presumptions. The distinction is that where the borrower is himself the possessor of the money or property and has a migo together with a presumption of possession, that combination can overcome the weaker opposing presumption. Even if an object was seized before witnesses, the possessor can sometimes say, 'I seized my own,' through a migo of return despite the general presumption that what was under the other person's hand belonged to him. But where a creditor says about property in his own hands, 'This is not mine,' he has no personal presumption of ownership supporting his migo. There the contrary presumption, that property in a person's hands is his, defeats the migo. Nevertheless the Shach concludes that in our present case the principal practical law follows Mechaber.
In beit din. If the creditor showed the document to witnesses and told them it was paid, he can still be believed, as Urim VeTumim explains. This differs from cases in simanim 72 and 133 where a person who admits before witnesses that an item he holds is collateral cannot later change his explanation. There those admissions determine the oath framework. Here, so long as the document had not yet been formally established in beit din, the creditor still had the power to destroy it and thereby remove the debt evidence. Therefore an admission made while that migo existed can retain force. The same applies where the borrower holds the creditor's handwritten receipt, or a witnessed receipt recording the creditor's admission rather than an actual witnessed payment, provided the receipt was created before the document was established in court. If the creditor had already shown the document to witnesses but immediately came to beit din and said it was an accommodation or paid document, he can still be believed where he retained the practical ability to go to the borrower first, return the document, and arrange matters consistently with his claim. But if the debtor's creditor had warned him in the witnesses' presence not to pay the original creditor because Rabbi Natan's lien applied, he could no longer lawfully take that step, and the migo disappears. If the document is in a shalish's hands, an acknowledgment receipt is not enough unless it records an actual payment, because the creditor never had the migo of destroying the document.
He is not believed. Sema discusses one who says, 'Money in my hands belongs to so-and-so.' The proper distinction is as follows. Responsa Rosh speaks where the amount of an iska is known, for example because there is an iska deed, and the only question is whether particular movables derive from that iska. He can be believed through a migo that he could have transferred those movables to the investor as repayment, because a later creditor who collected first keeps what he collected. Sema's case is where the exact amount of outside deposits is not known, but it is known that the person does hold deposits for others. That fact weakens the ordinary presumption that everything under a person's hand is his. If he says particular money is a deposit belonging to someone else, he may have a migo that he could simply have handed it to that person. Even if witnesses saw the property in his hands, once the property had actually been transferred to the alleged owner, the general possession presumption would not be enough to remove it from that possessor, because the presumption was already weakened by knowledge that the debtor holds deposits. Thus the debtor can sometimes be believed while still holding it through the migo that he could have transferred it. This differs from a situation where it was never known that the debtor held anyone else's deposit. Then the presumption that everything under his hand is his is strong, and a migo does not overcome it. If he already delivered property to the alleged owner at a time when his assertion was not believed, that person may still have to return it. But where it is known that he holds deposits, the weakened presumption allows actual transfer and can also support the migo. Sema brings proof from a wife who says property in her hands belongs to a third party and can be believed through a migo of giving it to him, and from a husband who says clothing was purchased for his wife. The underlying principle is the same. However, once property is already in a creditor's hands, the debtor cannot use a retroactive migo to extract it; the migo existed only before the transfer. This issue will be explained further in siman 99.
