That it was an entrusted document. Whether this applies only where the witnesses themselves did not know it was entrusted, or even where he claims that the witnesses acted improperly, provided that he has a migo that he could have destroyed the document, see below in siman 82 in the general rules of migo, where I discussed this principle at length.
Or paid. The same applies to any matter that would nullify the document, Shach.
And he has nothing from which to pay, etc. Although some hold that R' Nasan's lien applies even if the debtor has other assets, here, where we say that his admission is ineffective because it harms others, the case must be that he lacks assets from which to pay. Otherwise, how is there harm to others? Shach therefore discussed this unnecessarily in seif katan 2. Bach writes that the same applies if he has assets but is a violent person from whom collection is difficult, so that the lender prefers to collect directly from one who owes his debtor under R' Nasan's rule. Shach objects, and he is correct.
And the document contains a clause of trust. This applies to the claim that it was paid. Had the lender denied payment, the borrower would have been able to demand an oath, and until the lender swore he could not collect. Now that the lender himself admits that it was paid, he cannot collect, because had he denied it the borrower would certainly have demanded an oath. But with a claim that the document was entrusted, no trust clause is necessary, because in any event the borrower cannot impose an oath on the lender, as explained in siman 82. Accordingly, even without a trust clause the lender is not believed to say it was entrusted. See Tumim, however, regarding a case where he has a migo based on payment. There he may be believed to say it was entrusted through a migo that he could say it was paid. See the general rules of migo.
And the document was established before beit din. Sema explains this as validated by beit din. Shach correctly objects that validation does not remove the lender's migo that he could have burned or hidden the document. Rather, it means that beit din saw the document in his possession, after which he can no longer credibly claim that he could have concealed or destroyed it. Shach writes that if the very first time beit din saw it he said it was entrusted or paid, he is still believed, because until that moment he could have suppressed it and not shown it to beit din. The rule that he is not believed applies only where he first showed it without qualification and only afterward claimed that it was entrusted or the like. This is correct. See Tumim, where I inclined to the view that even if witnesses, rather than beit din, saw it in his possession and he then told those witnesses that it was entrusted or paid, he is also believed. This is unlike the rule below in siman 133, seif 3. See there. Sema writes that even where he is believed he must at least take the oath of "I have nothing," as explained below, and should include in that oath that his claim of payment is true.
He is not believed. But where he has a migo he is believed. We do not say that this is a migo against the presumption that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him. That presumption applies to money, but documents are not themselves money. The underlying debt therefore remains in the presumptive possession of the borrower, who is not considered obligated to the lender. So writes Shach, and this is correct. Although Shach treats it as a doubtful question of law, the Mechaber's conclusion is correct and conceptually compelling.
He is not believed. We do not say that he has a migo because he could waive the debt, since below in siman 86 we rule that under R' Nasan's lien he cannot waive it, Sema and Shach. Sema's distinction between a debt whose due date has arrived and one whose due date has not belongs properly below in siman 86, where I clarified it with Hashem's help.
He is not believed. Rosh writes in a responsum that he is likewise not believed to say that documents in his possession belong to another person and that he designated them for that person, because such designation changes nothing. If he says that money in his possession belongs to someone who lent it to him, even if he has a migo that he could simply hand the money over, that does not help. Even if he is telling the truth, since money lent is given for expenditure, the lender has no greater proprietary right in those coins than other creditors, and collection follows the ordinary rules of creditor priority. But if he says that the money is a deposit belonging to a certain person, and he has a migo that he could give it to that person, or says it is that person's investment capital, he is believed, and other creditors have no right to it. So write Sema and Shach. Shach objects regarding deposited money that the custodian is permitted to use, arguing that it is then like a loan and the depositor has no proprietary right. This does not appear correct, as I wrote at length at the end of siman 73. See there.
And it later became known, etc. Even if at the earlier time he had other assets and only now lacks them, he is not believed. R' Nasan's lien applies even while he has other assets. It is only that when the lender can otherwise collect, there is no reason not to believe a debtor who harms only himself. Once he now lacks assets and his admission would cause a loss to others, he is no longer believed, Shach. See what I wrote in siman 86.
He also causes a loss to himself, etc. Nevertheless, he is believed with respect to his own share. The borrower need pay only the one hundred that is owed to others, unless the lender later claims that his earlier admission was made deceptively, in which case the Mechaber's rule applies that we examine the circumstances of the admission, etc., Sema.
