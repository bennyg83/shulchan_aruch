Credibility. The Sema writes that if the document contains no clause of credibility, the creditor is believed to say that it was paid even where this harms another creditor, because he could not collect on the document without taking an oath, and he now says that it is paid. The Shach questions the distinction between a document that contains such a clause and one that does not. In his view, even without a credibility clause, if the debtor demands that the creditor swear and the creditor refuses and admits that the debt was paid, the document nevertheless remains effective for the other creditor. Refusal to swear cannot be stronger than an express admission that the debt was paid. Conversely, what does the credibility clause accomplish when the creditor himself expressly nullifies it by saying the debt was paid? Rather, since this document is subject to Rabbi Natan's lien, which is Biblical, while the oath required for collection on a document is rabbinic, the creditor's refusal to swear is ineffective. The Shach brings several proofs and concludes that it is clear that the creditor does not lose the debt merely by refusing to swear, since even an admission of payment would not be believed to the detriment of the other creditor. The Taz also questions what a credibility clause accomplishes, since it was granted only so long as the creditor maintains that he was not paid or remains silent, not when he expressly admits payment. He leaves the matter requiring careful resolution.
And it was established. The Sema explains this to mean that the document was authenticated by beit din. The Shach disagrees. Rather, the phrase means literally that possession of the document had already been established before beit din and they had seen it in his hand. Otherwise he has a migo that he could have hidden or burned it. Moreover, 'established before beit din' means that they saw it in his possession before the dispute arose. If the first time beit din sees it is when he immediately says that it has been paid, he certainly has a migo, since he could simply have refrained from showing it to beit din. The Shach further writes that the Ramban and Maggid Mishneh disagree with this entire ruling and hold that even while the document is in his possession he is not believed through any migo, because whatever is in a person's possession is presumed to belong to him and no migo can overcome that presumption. It is surprising that the Mechaber did not state their view explicitly here, although he cites it briefly in the Beit Yosef to siman 99. As practical halachah, although we generally rule that what is found in a person's possession is presumed his, that applies to money or movable property whose body is itself monetary value. Here, although the document is in his possession, the money is not. The Mechaber likewise appears to distinguish this way in siman 99. Therefore the matter appears to remain a doubt in law.
He is not. The Sema asks why the creditor should not be believed through the migo that he could forgive the debt. He answers that the Mechaber rules in siman 86, seif 5, that he cannot forgive it to the debtor. The Sema had distinguished there that this applies when the debt he himself owes has already fallen due, whereas if its due date has not yet arrived, perhaps he may still forgive the debt owed to him. Here, however, the case concerns a due debt, as implied by the distinction at the end of this siman between whether he made the statement when his own creditor demanded payment or at another time. The Shach writes that the Sema appears to derive this from the Ran, but in siman 86 he explained at length that even according to the Ran this is not compelled. The Sema further cites the Rosh that if a person says that documents in his possession have been specifically designated to Levi because Levi lent him money, his designation has no effect. Likewise, if cash is found in his possession he cannot say that it belongs to Levi because Levi lent him those very coins, since a loan is given to be spent. But if merchandise or investment property is found in his possession, he is believed to say that it belongs to another person when it is known that he conducts business with others' property, because he has a migo that he could have handed it over to that person. The Sema adds that only where he says that the cash was lent to him is he not believed; if he says it is a deposit he should be believed through a migo, like the investment property. The Shach rejects this. The proof from Even HaEzer does not apply, because with a married woman even money that she borrowed, if still intact, must be returned to the lender. Here, by contrast, the Rosh specifies investment property. Tosafot write that even deposited money that remains intact need not be returned in specie and may be replaced with other money, giving it the legal character of a loan. Perhaps the Sema refers to coins tied and sealed as a distinct deposit, but his wording does not imply this.
