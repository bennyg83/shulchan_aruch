And it contains a credibility clause. The Shach writes at length against the Sema: what does a credibility clause help once the creditor himself admits that he was paid? He therefore concludes that no special credibility is needed here at all. A credibility clause matters in the sale of a promissory note, because the seller can otherwise say, 'I was paid,' through a migo that he could have waived the debt. Under Rabbi Natan's lien, however, where the original creditor cannot waive the debt, he is not believed to say it was paid because his statement harms another and we suspect collusion. See what I wrote in siman 86, seif katan 7, defending the Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and Sema.
Credibility. Tumim writes that the need for a credibility clause applies to a claim of payment, but where the creditor says the document was merely a trust document there is no oath in any event, as explained in siman 82. He understands this as applying where no migo of payment exists, because otherwise the creditor claiming a trust document would possess a migo that he could have claimed payment. Urim similarly writes. This is incorrect. A claim that the document was a trust document never creates an oath, even where the claimant has a migo to say it was paid. This is implied by the language of Shulchan Aruch in siman 82, and Urim himself writes there that even where a migo of payment exists, no oath is imposed on a trust-document claim because there is a presumption against interest and trust arrangements. This is straightforward.
And the document was established. The Shach writes that once the document was established before beit din before the dispute arose, the holder no longer has a migo that he could have burned it, because that would be a retroactive migo. But if, at the very moment he shows it to beit din, he says that it is paid, he is believed through the migo that he could have withheld it from beit din. The Shach disputes Torat Chayyim at the end of chapter Zeh Borer, who writes that once the document is physically before beit din there is no migo. Torat Chayyim would allow the migo only if the person does not produce the document at all but says he has such a document in his possession and that it is paid, because he could have remained silent. The Shach's position is correct. Yet this migo that he need not have shown the document applies specifically when he appears before beit din. If he displays the document before witnesses and says it is paid, he is not believed through a migo that he could have burned it. A third-party custodian cannot weaken a document where he was not previously granted credibility, as the Shach explains in siman 56, seif katan 1. There is therefore no migo, because migo is asserted in beit din, not outside beit din, as appears from Shulchan Aruch siman 133 and Rama siman 72. See what I wrote in siman 133 concerning migo before witnesses.
In the hand of a third-party custodian. Ramban and Maggid Mishneh at the end of chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh disagree and hold that even a third-party custodian is not believed through any migo, because there is a presumption that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him, and we do not say migo against a chazakah. At first glance this is difficult. If even with a document one is not believed through migo because the chazakah says it belongs to the holder and was not paid, how do we rule that one who admits he wrote a document can require the claimant to authenticate it, based on the migo that he could have claimed forgery? That too would be a migo against the chazakah that the document belongs to the holder. One can distinguish between a chazakah independently established by witnesses and a chazakah known only through the borrower's own statement. In the latter case we may say migo against the chazakah, because the entire basis for recognizing the document is his own admission: the document is not yet authenticated, so the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted. Ramban makes a similar distinction in his novellae to chapter HaMokher et HaBayit. See what I wrote in siman 90, seif katan 12. There is still a difficulty from a deposit document, where the custodian is believed to say, 'I returned it,' through the migo that he could have said it was lost through unavoidable circumstances. There the document is fully authenticated before us, yet the migo is accepted and we do not say it stands against the chazakah. Perhaps the chazakah applies only to a loan document, where no exemption for accidents exists and we say that a document in the creditor's hand is certainly unpaid. A deposit, by contrast, can be extinguished by an unavoidable loss, so this chazakah does not apply with the same force. See what I wrote in simanim 99 and 255 concerning migo against this chazakah.
Alternatively. The Sema cites a responsum of the Rosh: if a person says, 'These funds belong to so-and-so, who lent them to me,' the statement has no effect, because a loan is given to be spent. But if he holds an iska and says the funds are from so-and-so's investment, he is believed through a migo that he could have delivered them to that person. If he says money in his hand was deposited with him by so-and-so, he is likewise believed through the migo that he could have handed it to the depositor, similar to merchandise. The Shach objects to the Sema. What appears from the Shach, that only the deposit half of an iska belongs to the investor, is incorrect. We explained in siman 99, seif katan 2, that against the recipient's creditors the entire iska, including the half legally treated as a loan, belongs to the investor. As to the Shach's statement that money deposited in unsealed form, where the custodian has permission to use it, makes him a borrower, the Gaon Tzemach Tzedek already objected in siman 73. So long as the custodian has not actually spent the money, he has no right to replace it with other money. The depositor takes his very money. See there, for his words are clear.
