[" And he has nothing, etc. Below in siman 86, seif 2, seif katan 5, I conclude that although Rabbi Natan’s lien applies even when the first debtor owns other property, nevertheless for purposes of disregarding an admission that a document is an amanah or paid, or otherwise causing loss to the second debtor, the rule applies only when the first debtor lacks other property. The reason is that the lien of Rabbi Natan certainly takes effect by Torah law even when the first debtor has property, but just as one does not collect from encumbered property when unencumbered property is available, despite the Torah status of liens, because of tikkun haolam, so too here, where forced collection from the second debtor would cause him loss, the first creditor has the status of one who seizes from another and may collect from the second debtor only when his own debtor lacks property. Accordingly, the first creditor may collect from the second debtor only after swearing that his own debt has not been paid, like anyone collecting from purchasers, as explained below in siman 114, seif 4. Rambam states this explicitly at the end of chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh, Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 51, part 2, cites him, and Semag at the end of positive commandment 93 writes the same. I discuss this further below in siman 86, seif 2, seif katan 5:"]

[" And the document contains a clause of credibility. The Tur and Darkei Moshe write this, though their wording is not fully precise. The Gemara presumably discusses every type of document, even one without such a clause, for example where the debtor did not demand that the creditor swear. In that circumstance an ordinary creditor need not swear, as explained below in siman 82, seif 2. Many Talmudic passages similarly discuss ordinary documents without a special credibility clause and without a demand for an oath. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 21, end of part 2, explicitly implies that only where the debtor demands an oath does the absence of a credibility clause potentially cause loss to the first creditor. The Tur and Darkei Moshe therefore mentioned only one common case. The substance of Baal HaTerumot and the Tur’s distinction between a document with a credibility clause and one without it, however, requires study. In my view, even when the document contains no credibility clause, the second debtor demands an oath, and the first debtor refuses to swear and admits that the document was paid, the document nevertheless remains in force. One might distinguish between a simple admission that it was paid when the debtor does not demand an oath, and refusal to swear when an oath is demanded. But this is not persuasive. Refusal to swear is no stronger than an express admission that it was paid. And what is gained by a credibility clause? The creditor himself says the document was paid, thereby expressly nullifying his own credibility clause. If witnesses proved payment, the clause would certainly be ineffective, and a litigant’s admission ordinarily has the force of one hundred witnesses. Rather, once this document is encumbered to the first creditor by Rabbi Natan’s law, the debtor’s admission has no force. If so, refusal to swear likewise has no force and the document remains valid. Moreover, Rabbi Natan’s lien is Torah law, whereas the oath required on documents is rabbinic. Further, once the debt is encumbered by Torah law to the first creditor, he is the true adverse party with respect to this debt, as stated in Ketubot, and the second debtor’s right to demand an oath from the intermediate creditor cannot defeat him. We should also be concerned for collusion when the intermediate creditor refuses to swear. This explains why Rambam, Semag, and the other poskim do not distinguish here between documents with or without a credibility clause, or whether an oath was demanded. They simply state that where Levi says he paid and Shimon admits it, we disregard Shimon’s admission because perhaps they are colluding. Accordingly, it appears that even if one witness testifies that the document was paid and the intermediate creditor agrees, this does not defeat the first creditor, because the document is already encumbered to him by Torah law under Rabbi Natan’s rule. Responsa Mahara ibn Chaim, siman 81, touches on part of this issue. His proposed resolution, however, does not fully answer it in my view. He writes that the cited poskim permit the intermediate creditor’s statement where there is no credibility clause because they hold he has a migo that he could forgive the debt, whereas when the document contains a credibility clause the debt is considered already collected as against the first creditor, so the intermediate creditor has no role in collection and is like a stranger. This too does not appear correct for the reasons just stated. Moreover, the Tur and Mechaber, who distinguish here based on a credibility clause, expressly rule below in siman 86, seif 5, that the intermediate creditor cannot forgive the debt. We nevertheless learn from Mahara ibn Chaim that where forgiveness is legally unavailable, even a document without a credibility clause that requires an oath cannot be defeated by the intermediate creditor’s statement that it was paid. This accords with my conclusion. Since we rule that he cannot forgive, as I explain below in seif katan 6, he is never believed to say it was paid. Independently, the logic is clear: once he is not believed to say the debt was paid, why should refusal to swear accomplish what his direct admission cannot? Ramban, the Rosh, and Ran at the end of Kol HaNishbaim similarly write regarding one who sold a debt document: if the debtor says he paid the seller, the seller must swear; if the seller refuses to swear, the purchaser loses because the seller is believed to say the debt was paid by a migo that he could forgive it. This shows that refusal to swear harms the purchaser only because the seller’s statement of payment is itself believed. If he were not believed to say payment occurred, refusal to swear would not cancel the document. Responsa Rashba cited by Beit Yosef below in siman 66, new material 22, likewise states that where a purchaser of a debt document is told by the debtor, ‘I paid the seller, let him come swear that I did not,’ the debtor’s demand is ineffective, and even if the seller comes and admits payment he is not believed. Below in siman 66, end of seif 33, seif katan 106, I prove that this responsum concerns a case where the seller separately owed the purchaser, so that the sold document was encumbered to the purchaser under Rabbi Natan’s rule. Even according to Mahara ibn Chaim’s reading of that responsum, under which the seller himself is not believed, Rashba’s further point remains clear: refusal to swear does not defeat the purchaser once even an admission of payment would not be believed. This appears clearly correct. See also what I write below in siman 66, seif 14, seif katan 33, and seif 76, beginning of seif katan 51:"]
[" And the document was established in beit din. Sema explains this as meaning formally authenticated by beit din, and writes the same in his Derishah. This is incorrect. It means literally that the document had already been seen and established in beit din as being in his possession. Otherwise he would have a migo that he could conceal or burn it, as Tosafot and the Rosh in the second chapter of Ketubot and the other poskim write. This is explicit in Tosafot at the end of Zeh Borer and in the Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia. In the latter context a third party remains believed even when the document was already seen by beit din, but that is because a trusted third party is believed even without a migo, as the Rosh and other poskim explain, and as I write below at the beginning of siman 56, seif katan 1. That passage nevertheless proves that ‘established in beit din’ means that beit din had seen it in his hand. It further appears that beit din must have seen it before the dispute arose. The Rosh writes explicitly that ‘established in beit din’ means that beit din saw the document in her hand before she claimed it was paid, so she can no longer rely on a migo based on what she could have done earlier. Beit Yosef cites this below at the end of siman 65. But if the first time beit din sees the document the holder immediately says, ‘It has been paid,’ he plainly has a migo that he could simply have refrained from showing it to them. This is the structure of every migo in the Talmud. A person may show an object to beit din and say, ‘It is yours, but you owe me such and such upon it,’ and is believed by a migo that he could have withheld it entirely. Although Torat Chaim at the end of Zeh Borer interprets Rav Nachman as saying that once she physically brings the document before beit din she cannot claim a migo that she could have burned it, because at the moment of her claim she no longer has the power to destroy it, and Raavan appears to explain similarly, this does not appear correct to me. If so, no migo would ever exist once the object at issue is shown to beit din. Nimukei Yosef at the end of Zeh Borer supports my reading, connecting ‘established’ to the rule of ‘seeing’ an object in the possession of the fraudulent craftsman of Pumbedita. In that context we rule below in simanim 132 and 134 that even if beit din now sees the object, the craftsman retains his migo so long as they did not see it before the dispute arose. Perhaps Raavan would disagree even there, but our halachah does not. Rivash, siman 343, explicitly confirms my interpretation. He explains that his statement that a document already in beit din destroys a migo refers only to a case where the document was seen by beit din before the parties’ claims and dispute, such as when it was brought for authentication or some other purpose. If the defendant produces the document as part of his current response and makes his claim at once, he remains believed by a migo that he could have withheld it. Rivash emphasizes that he never intended otherwise. This is clear. The need for explanation arises only because the Tur, Mechaber, and other poskim use the abbreviated phrase ‘established in beit din,’ which can mislead, as happened to Raavan and Torat Chaim. We therefore must clarify that our ruling does not follow them. Maharshal at the end of Zeh Borer and Mahara ibn Sasson, siman 72, may also appear otherwise, but the rule is as I have stated:"]
[" In beit din or in the hand of a third party. Ramban and Maggid Mishneh at the end of chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh disagree and hold that even when the document remains in the creditor’s own hand, he is not believed by any migo to say it is not his, because there is a presumption that whatever is in a person’s possession belongs to him, and migo is not asserted against that presumption. Maggid Mishneh writes that Rambam appears to hold the same. Shiltei Giborim in the second chapter of Ketubot rules likewise. It is surprising that the Mechaber omitted their view here and mentions it only briefly below in Beit Yosef to siman 99, seif 10, rather than explaining it here. Rambam’s own language indeed supports Maggid Mishneh: ‘If he had debt documents against Levi and said, This is an amanah document, or, It was paid, we disregard him,’ which implies that the documents are in his own possession. Rambam’s unqualified wording further implies that even if beit din had not previously seen the documents, he is not believed. The objections raised by Gidulei Terumah against Maggid Mishneh’s rationale do not disturb me. Ramban and Maggid Mishneh hold that because everything in a person’s possession is presumed his, this document too is presumed to belong to him. Semag at the end of positive commandment 93 likewise adopts Rambam’s wording and omits Tosafot’s qualification that the document had already been established in beit din or was held by a third party, even though Semag regularly cites Tosafot. Rashba also appears to support this, and in printed Responsa Rashba, siman 1047, he states explicitly that whatever is in a person’s possession is presumed his, and if he says it is not his he is not believed even with a migo, citing the rule that a creditor who says his own document is an amanah is not believed when this harms others, although he could theoretically forgive the debt. For practical law, however, although we rule that property found in a person’s possession is presumed his, as I conclude below in siman 99, seif katan 6, that presumption applies to money, movable property, and similar items whose bodies themselves have monetary value. Here the paper document may be in his possession, but the money represented by it is not. The Rama and Mechaber appear to distinguish on this basis, as I write there. The present matter therefore remains a doubt in law:"]
[" He is not believed. Sema correctly rejects Ir Shushan’s explanation that the creditor lacks a migo of forgiving the debt merely because he does not intend to waive and lose his own claim. Although Tosafot and the Rosh give that answer in one approach, we do not rule that way. We follow their latter answer, as the Tur and Mechaber expressly rule below in siman 86, seif 5, that once Rabbi Natan’s lien has attached the intermediate creditor cannot forgive the debt to the second debtor. I likewise decide below in siman 86, seif 5, seif katan 11, that this is the primary rule. Although the Rosh in his pesakim to the second chapter of Ketubot does not cite Tosafot’s second answer, in Responsa Rosh, klal 68, din 3, he writes that even if the widow wished to forgive the orphan’s debt she could not, because all who owe her have already become encumbered to her creditors under Rabbi Natan’s rule, as shown by the rule in Ketubot that a creditor who says his document is an amanah is not believed where he thereby harms his own creditors. Rosh repeats this in klal 69, din 3. It is therefore clear that in his responsa the Rosh retracted what might have been implied by his pesakim, and the Tur either knew this or independently regarded it as primary. Bach’s attempted distinction, that Rosh’s pesakim concern an admission made before it became known that the person owed others, when Rabbi Natan’s lien had allegedly not yet attached, while the responsa concern a later admission after the debt to others became known, is not persuasive. The pesakim themselves appear to concern a case where Rabbi Natan’s lien applies. Moreover, even if the debt to others was not known at the moment of admission, the pesakim imply that once it is later discovered that he already owed others at that time, Rabbi Natan’s lien is treated as having attached then. The responsum itself expressly discusses the Talmudic case in the second chapter of Ketubot and cites Tosafot’s second answer. The primary rule is therefore that where Rabbi Natan’s lien applies, the intermediate creditor cannot forgive. If it is discovered only afterward that he was already indebted to others when he purported to forgive, Rabbi Natan’s lien had already attached and the forgiveness accomplished nothing. The Tur below in siman 86 likewise implies this. I discuss and prove it further there. There is another answer to Tosafot’s question why the creditor is not believed by a migo that he could forgive the debt: if he actually forgave it, he would commit a wrongful act against his own creditor by destroying the latter’s claim and causing him monetary loss. By merely saying the document is an amanah or paid, however, he may expect his own creditor to believe that he is speaking truthfully. Ran suggests a similar point. Ran then struggles with the rule at the end of Shnayim Ochazin that when a woman admits a found receipt, it is returned to her husband, and the Gemara invokes Shmuel’s rule that one who sold a debt document may afterward forgive it, apparently using the migo that she could forgive. Ran answers that toward her husband she would certainly be willing to forgive and would not be embarrassed. In my view this is unnecessary. There the objection that she would be ashamed to act wrongfully toward the purchaser cannot apply, because by her present position she is doing something even worse, having sold him a ketubah that was already paid. For practical law, however, none of these secondary explanations is needed. The primary rule is that once Rabbi Natan’s lien attaches, the intermediate creditor cannot forgive, as I conclude below in siman 86, seif 5. Mabit, part 1, siman 204, ruled that where a person obligated himself to his son and one witness testified that the obligation was merely formal, and afterward the son forgave the obligation, the forgiveness was effective even according to those poskim who say one cannot forgive where it harms others. That does not appear correct. According to the rule stated here, the son’s forgiveness is ineffective. Sema further writes that this inability to forgive applies only once the second debtor’s due date has arrived, but before then it is possible that the debt may still be forgiven. He apparently derived this from Ran. Below in siman 86, seif 5, seif katan 11, I explain at length that even according to Ran this is not compelled. I conclude there that even a ketubah, whose obligation is not presently fixed and whose due date has not arrived, cannot be forgiven once the husband is encumbered by Rabbi Natan’s rule. All the more so a debt document whose due date has not yet arrived cannot be forgiven:"]

[" He is not believed, since he comes to harm others, etc. Responsa Rosh, klal 79, siman 11, cited by Beit Yosef here, states that if a person says, ‘These coins belong to so and so, who lent them to me,’ the claim is ineffective, because borrowed money is given to be spent and becomes the borrower’s property subject to repayment. But if he holds an iska and says that the money derives from so and so’s investment, he is believed by a migo that he could have handed it to that person. Sema, seif katan 4, infers that the rejection applies only where he says the money in his hand was lent to him, but if he says, ‘These coins belong to so and so, who deposited them with me,’ he should be believed by a migo that he could have returned them, just as with the commercial goods of an iska. Sema seeks proof from Even HaEzer, siman 85, where a woman is believed by migo to say that money found in her possession belongs to another. This does not appear correct to me. The proof from Even HaEzer is no proof. Even where the woman herself borrowed money, if the actual coins remain intact she must return them to the lender, as Rambam writes at the end of chapter 26 of Hilchot Malveh, as do the other poskim, the Rama in Even HaEzer 91:4, and Sema himself below in siman 132, seif katan 3. Deposited money in her hand is therefore no stronger than borrowed money. The present case of iska is different. The Rosh speaks specifically of an iska, which is half loan and half deposit, as stated in HaMekabel and explained below in siman 108, seif 4. The deposit half always remains the investor’s property, and all profit arising from that half belongs to the investor. The recipient has no right to use the deposit half for his own purposes. With an ordinary deposit of money, however, Tosafot at the beginning of Elu Metziot write that even if the exact coins remain, the depositary need not return those same coins where he has permission to use them; he becomes like a borrower and may repay with other coins. This is also evident in HaMafkid. Thus, although the money was originally deposited with him, once he has permission to use it, the coins in his possession are legally his and the arrangement has the law of a loan. Perhaps Sema refers specifically to coins bound and sealed, where use is forbidden, but his general language does not imply that limitation. See what I write further below in siman 99, seif 1, seif katan 7 and 8:"]
