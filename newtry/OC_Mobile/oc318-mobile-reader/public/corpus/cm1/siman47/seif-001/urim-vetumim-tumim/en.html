And it contains a credibility clause. Shach, seif katan 4, says the wording is imprecise because the Gemara speaks generally, even where no credibility clause appears. For example, if the debtor does not demand, "Swear to me," the creditor need not swear even without such a clause. This objection is not understandable. The present case is one where the creditor himself cries out that the debt is in fact paid and the document is worthless. The point is not that the debtor currently says, "Swear to me." Rather, absent the creditor's admission, the debtor could have demanded an oath. The creditor then could not have collected without swearing. Now, because the creditor himself admits he cannot honestly swear that the debt remains unpaid, why should the debtor be placed in a worse position? Had the creditor denied payment, the debtor would indeed have demanded the oath. This is straightforward. There is also no difficulty from the Gemara's general wording. As I explained in Urim, the need for an express credibility clause concerns specifically a claim of payment. A debtor can ordinarily demand an oath on payment. But concerning an amana claim, where the defense is that the document was never meant to represent an actual debt, the relevant migo and procedural rules differ. The Gemara speaks there about one who says, "This is an amana document," not specifically about payment, so it had no need to distinguish whether a credibility clause was written. Shach and Taz ask a stronger question. If we accept that without a credibility clause the creditor can say "paid" and thereby prevent collection, why should adding a credibility clause make him less believable? The debtor may say: "I trusted this creditor precisely because I considered him trustworthy. I relied on his honesty. Now he himself comes to beit din and says the debt was paid. Why should my having trusted him cause me to lose?" This has force. Shach also cites Ramban, Ran, and Rosh concerning a seller of a debt document. If the debtor claims payment, the seller must swear that the debt was not paid. If the seller says it was paid, he is believed only because he possesses a migo that he could simply waive the debt. This implies that absent that migo, the seller's statement of payment would not be believed even though the original document contained no special credibility clause. In a case governed by R. Natan, where the creditor owes money to another creditor and therefore cannot waive the debt, he should likewise not be believed even without a credibility clause. This is a strong challenge. To defend Baal HaTerumot, one must begin from a basic principle. Strictly speaking, once a creditor's debt is pledged to another creditor under R. Natan, the first creditor should not be believed to say "paid," because he thereby harms another person's established right. Ordinarily one who harms third parties by his admission is not believed. But if so, what did Chazal accomplish by instituting the rule that a creditor who collects on a document must swear when the debtor demands an oath? People frequently repay written debts without immediately destroying the paper. If a dishonest creditor wanted to avoid the oath, he could simply borrow money from someone else until R. Natan's lien attached to his own receivable. He would then say, "I owe another creditor; my statement that this debt was paid cannot harm him, so I need not swear," and the second creditor would collect from the original debtor. The first creditor and second creditor could then divide the proceeds. The rabbinic oath enactment would be nullified. Therefore Chazal strengthened their oath requirement. Where the creditor himself cannot or will not swear that the debt remains unpaid, the document does not collect even if another creditor claims through him under R. Natan. Otherwise the entire oath enactment could be defeated by a simple contrivance. This applies only where the debtor retained the right to demand the oath. If the debtor expressly granted the creditor credibility and waived the oath, then he voluntarily abandoned the rabbinic protection. There is no longer any enactment to safeguard against circumvention. The law therefore returns to its ordinary rule: where the creditor's statement would harm an attached third-party right, he is not believed. This is the purpose of the credibility clause in Baal HaTerumot. A second explanation is possible. A document is presumed unpaid. If the debtor gives the creditor explicit credibility and the creditor presents the document without any notation of payment on it, that very presentation is equivalent to an earlier assertion that the debt is still outstanding. The debtor expressly trusted that assertion. When the creditor later says, "It was paid," he is effectively retracting from testimony or admission already embedded in the operation of the document. Once he has made the earlier operative representation, he cannot simply reverse it after third-party rights have attached. This is less persuasive than the first explanation, but it also helps. Shach's proof from the seller of a debt document can be distinguished. A sale of a debt document is public. Rosh writes in Perek HaKotev that such a sale has a voice. If the debtor had already paid before the sale, he would ordinarily hear of the transfer and immediately protest: "How can you sell a debt I already paid?" His silence therefore supports the purchaser's position that the debt remained unpaid at the time of sale. Conversely, payment of a written debt itself often has a public trace or practical consequences, and purchasers are reluctant to buy a claim already rumored to be paid. Therefore we are less concerned that the creditor will sell a paid debt merely to evade the oath enactment. The sale context is not an easy mechanism for defeating the rabbinic oath. R. Natan's rule is different. The creditor need only owe money to another person. No public sale of the underlying document is necessary. If every such indebted creditor could bypass the oath requirement, circumvention would be easy and common. Thus Baal HaTerumot can distinguish the cases. There is strong support for Baal HaTerumot from Bava Metzia's discussion of a found receipt. The Gemara asks why a found receipt should be returned. Perhaps the woman already sold her ketubah and is now colluding with her husband to defeat the purchaser. The Gemara answers by invoking Shmuel's rule that one who sells a debt document and later waives it can extinguish the purchaser's right. Maharshal asks: perhaps she owes money to another creditor. Under R. Natan she cannot waive away the second creditor's right. His answer, that R. Natan does not apply to a ketubah, is insufficient in broader found-receipt cases involving ordinary debts. The Mishnah also discusses receipts generally. Why return a receipt when the creditor admits it if perhaps he owes another creditor and his admission would wrongfully defeat that third party? The answer must be Baal HaTerumot's rule. If the original document lacks a special credibility clause and the creditor says it was paid, the attached creditor cannot collect either, because the first creditor could not honestly take the oath required to collect. The found receipt therefore causes no new harm to the second creditor. Even without the receipt, the first creditor's admission of payment blocks collection. We do not ordinarily fear the unusual possibility that the document contains a special credibility clause, just as elsewhere we do not fear an uncommon clause such as an express assignment to the creditor and anyone deriving rights through him. Accordingly, the Mishnah can state its law generally. The Gemara's question from a sold ketubah is different. As explained, a sale creates different presumptions, and the seller is not automatically believed to say "paid" absent the migo of waiver. There is also a special reason in ketubah cases. The sale ordinarily occurs while the woman is still married. At that point the ketubah is not yet due, and even those who generally allow repayment before maturity recognize that a ketubah may never become payable at all if the wife dies first and the husband inherits her. The usual oath-on-payment structure therefore does not arise in the same way. This explains why the Gemara specifically worries about sale and does not solve the entire passage merely through the creditor's admission. The same framework resolves Tosafot in Bava Batra 144b. Tosafot, following Ri's position that a seller who waives a sold debt must reimburse the purchaser for the full value, ask why a found receipt should be returned. If she uses the receipt to defeat the buyer, she may owe the buyer the full amount anyway, so waiver is not an effective migo. At first glance one could answer that the sale and the found receipt occurred at the same time, or that the case concerns a woman still married, so other concerns disappear. But Tosafot's question becomes precise under the present analysis. They accept that R. Natan can apply to ketubah obligations and therefore still worry about third-party claims. The found-receipt case must then be one where the woman is already divorced and the ketubah due, so an oath claim can arise. The sale, however, would ordinarily have occurred earlier while she was married. Thus the sale and the found receipt are not the same event, and the purchaser's right can intervene. This is why Tosafot need their full analysis. It also explains another Tosafot passage. They ask whether the woman could have transferred her ketubah through ma'amad sheloshtan, a three-party transfer, and they prove at length that such a transfer cannot simply be waived. Why not ask more simply against Abaye, who says the physical ketubah document remains in her hands: perhaps she transferred the debt through ma'amad sheloshtan while retaining the paper? The answer is that perhaps, absent a credibility clause, the woman could still say "paid" and prevent collection through the oath mechanism. The Gemara's concern would therefore arise only in the unusual case where the ketubah contained special credibility language. Abaye can say that since the actual ketubah document is still in her hands, the court can inspect it and see whether such unusual language appears. Tosafot therefore cannot formulate the objection so simply. Their extended proof becomes necessary. All of this supports Baal HaTerumot. The rule is therefore: without an express credibility clause, the creditor's inability or refusal to swear can defeat collection even where another creditor seeks to collect through him under R. Natan, because otherwise Chazal's oath enactment would be easily circumvented; with an express credibility clause, the debtor waived that rabbinic protection, and the ordinary rule returns that the creditor cannot harm third-party rights by later saying the debt was paid.
And it was established, etc. Shach already explains that this means the creditor showed the document to beit din and, at that moment, did not immediately say that it was an amana document or otherwise defective. I would add the following. If witnesses saw the document in his possession before he came to beit din, and at the time they saw it he said, "This is an amana document," his statement should also be effective. Below, siman 133:3, there is an implication that a declaration before ordinary witnesses may not suffice in the case of the garments of Pumbedita. But Rosh explains there that the issue is that the claimant remains subject to an oath, and only beit din can impose and adjudicate that oath. Here there is no independent oath requirement. Sema's statement that an oath may still be necessary refers only to the broad oath, "I have no other property," under the Geonic enactment, not to an oath determining the truth of the amana assertion itself. Therefore, where no such substantive oath is required, a contemporaneous declaration before witnesses that the document is an amana document should be enough to preserve his credibility. This appears correct.
They are not believed. In Urim I explained that they do not possess a migo of waiver, because under R. Natan's rule one cannot waive a debt that is already attached to one's own creditor. Shach cites another answer from Ran in the second chapter of Ketubot. Ran says a creditor may be embarrassed to waive the debt outright because he would thereby actively destroy the lien belonging to his own creditor. But he is less embarrassed to say the original document was an amana document or already paid, because perhaps he is simply telling the truth. Ran asks from Bava Metzia's rule concerning a found receipt. The Gemara says we need not fear that the woman sold her ketubah and is now colluding with her husband, because under Shmuel she could simply waive the sold debt. But if she is embarrassed to waive, how is that a valid migo? Ran answers that toward her husband she would certainly be willing to waive and would not be embarrassed. Shach asks: what was Ran's question? If she returns a receipt after selling a debt that had really been paid, that is itself a grave wrong against the purchaser. Why should she be more embarrassed to waive than to produce a receipt that destroys the purchaser's right? In my view Ran's reasoning is sound. First, as explained above in siman 66, the receipt need not cover the entire ketubah. Indeed, if the full ketubah document remains in her possession, a receipt for the whole amount would be odd. She could simply surrender and tear the ketubah. And while she remains married she generally cannot waive the entire ketubah in the ordinary way. The case therefore concerns a receipt for part of the ketubah. When she later sells "my ketubah and all my rights under it" without specifying the exact balance, the purchaser acquires only whatever valid claim she actually possesses. If the receipt later appears, she can tell the purchaser: "I never sold you the portion already paid. A person cannot sell what he does not own." She is not necessarily embarrassed at all. She can claim the buyer misunderstood the amount of her remaining right. An outright waiver after the sale is different. There she openly destroys a right she admittedly sold to the purchaser. That is far more embarrassing. Thus Ran's migo question is well founded. Second, Ran may also be addressing Maharshal's objection discussed above. Perhaps the woman did not sell her ketubah at all but merely owes money to another creditor. Under R. Natan, that creditor claims directly against her ketubah right. Ran, in this answer, holds that R. Natan can apply even to a ketubah. If she merely says, "The ketubah was already paid" or "it was an amana arrangement," she can present herself as telling the truth and feels little embarrassment. But if she deliberately waives the debt to defeat her own creditor, she actively destroys his lien and would be embarrassed. Then the migo of waiver would appear weak. Ran therefore answers that because the potential waiver is in favor of her husband, she would still be willing to do it and would not be embarrassed. The husband-wife relationship makes the waiver plausible despite the third-party harm. Thus Ran's answer addresses the exact psychological weakness of the migo. There is no contradiction. Accordingly, where an ordinary creditor owes money to others, the migo of waiver can fail because of embarrassment at directly harming their liens. But in the ketubah case, waiver in favor of the husband is sufficiently natural that the migo remains available.
And if after he said, and so forth. I am uncertain whether this law applies where he said that the note has been paid. Since he is not believed to prejudice others, may he nevertheless afterward collect again from the lender? Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber mention this law only where he says that it is an accommodation note, and nowhere use the case of a paid note. With an accommodation note it is obvious that an ordinary note is not presumed to have been written merely as an accommodation, as Baal HaTerumot writes. Therefore, he cannot require an oath on that claim, and the presumption is that the note is genuine. He can accordingly retract afterward and say that he admitted it only in order to drive away his creditors, and his explanation is plausible. But where he says the debt was paid, a note certainly stands to be paid eventually. Were it not for the contractual credibility clause, he would be believed to say it was paid even where his statement harms others, as explained above. Regarding himself, however, he expressly admitted that it was paid. How can we rely on a slight conjecture that he admitted only in order to evade creditors and thereby extract money from another? At minimum this is a doubt, and the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. Therefore, it appears that he may not collect again. This is all the more so where the borrower himself expressly claims that he paid him. The matter nevertheless requires study, because I have not found it explicitly stated by the authorities.
