If he seized, etc. The Sema writes that this speaks where there are witnesses who saw the object in his possession, for otherwise he is believed to say, “I seized my own property,” by a migo that he could have said, “I returned it to you.” Indeed, the Ramban states this explicitly in Bava Batra, chapter Chezkat HaBatim, and the Ran writes likewise. Yet this requires explanation in light of the Mechaber’s ruling below, siman 364, concerning one who seized gold coins before a single witness, where the witness does not know how many there were and the seizer says there were twenty and they were his own. There he must return all twenty, and we do not say that he has a migo, for he could have said there were only two, and thereby be believed regarding the remaining eighteen. The Sema explains there, seif katan 7, that this is a migo against a chazakah, since whatever is in a person’s possession is presumed to be his. How, then, can a migo help here when it is likewise against that chazakah? In truth, the source of the ruling concerning the twenty gold coins is the Rambam, and it is possible that the Rambam indeed disagrees with the Ramban here. Accordingly, the practical ruling requires study. Alternatively, one may distinguish that there, regarding one or two coins, he certainly must return them by virtue of the witness. He has therefore already been established as a robber, and once that is so his migo that the remaining coins were his is ineffective against the chazakah, as the Sema writes. Even if that chazakah alone is not strong enough to extract property from one in possession, his claim has also been weakened because he has already been established as having seized what was not his with respect to those two coins. By contrast, where he has a migo regarding all the coins, namely that he could have said that he returned them, we say that he is telling the truth that he did not seize his fellow’s property at all, since the chazakah that whatever is in a person’s house is his is not strong enough by itself to extract property from the current possessor. According to this, if one seized two objects and there are witnesses who saw one of them, although there were no witnesses who saw the second and he could claim that he returned the second, that migo would no longer help, because he has already been established as having seized another’s property with respect to the first object. This is also reasonable independently, for what migo is there to say that he returned it? It is more advantageous for him to claim that what he seized was his, because according to that claim he retains even the object that was seen, whereas if he admits that he seized his fellow’s property but claims to have returned it, he loses the object that was seen and is compelled to return it. See the rules of migo, rule 38, whether this falls within the category of a migo for half a claim.
