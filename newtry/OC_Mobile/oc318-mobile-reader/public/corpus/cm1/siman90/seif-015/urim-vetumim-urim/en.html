Likewise, if he snatched, etc. Above, at the beginning of the siman, the witnesses to the taking did not see exactly how and under what circumstances he took it. Here, however, they saw him actually snatch it, and all agree that he must return it. This is provided that there are witnesses who subsequently saw the article in his possession. Otherwise, even if two witnesses saw him snatch it, he is believed to say, 'I snatched what was mine,' through a migo that he could have said, 'I returned it.' See Tumim. If he snatched two articles and there are witnesses of subsequent possession only regarding one of them, he nevertheless no longer has a migo even regarding the second article, for which there are no such witnesses. See there for what I explained precisely in this matter.
He must return it. This is the case of the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba mentioned in the Gemara. Whenever he does not contradict the single witness, the witness is treated like two witnesses, and he must return the article. This applies specifically where he snatched an article concerning which, had he contradicted the witness, he would have been obligated to take a Torah oath. Therefore, when he admits the witness's testimony, he is one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and must pay. If, however, he snatched a promissory note, regarding which no Torah oath applies, he is believed to say, 'I snatched what was mine,' since there is no Torah oath obligation. See the rules of migo.
And he took an article in the owner's absence. Here too, had he contradicted the witness, he would have been obligated to swear. Although the owner himself does not make a definite claim and knows only through the witness, he may nevertheless require him to take a Torah oath on the basis of the witness. Since the defendant cannot swear because he admits the witness's testimony, the witness is treated like two witnesses. With two witnesses he must return the article, and the same follows from one witness in this case.
