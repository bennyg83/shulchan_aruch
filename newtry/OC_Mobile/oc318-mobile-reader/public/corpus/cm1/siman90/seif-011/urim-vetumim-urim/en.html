And the witnesses see, etc. Meaning, they saw him enter empty handed and leave carrying objects. Otherwise he is believed by a migo that he could say, "These objects were already beneath my garments when I entered." The same law applies if the witnesses know that he took objects from the house but do not know under what circumstances. This entire seif concerns a case where he did not say that he entered in order to seize collateral, and the homeowner was present in his house.
And the homeowner takes a hesset oath. The wording is reversed. He first returns the objects, and afterward the homeowner takes hesset. A hesset oath always serves to exempt, not to enable collection, Shach.
But objects ordinarily lent, etc. See above, siman 72, seif 19, where this was discussed at length.
Known to belong to him. Meaning that it was previously known to be his. This is why the text says "the object ordinarily lent," with the definite article.
The homeowner claims they were stolen, etc. He has a migo that he could claim they were borrowed. Sema holds that this applies specifically in the present circumstances where witnesses saw the other person enter empty and leave with objects. Ordinarily, however, with objects normally lent out, a migo is ineffective because it would be a migo used to extract property. In Tumim I wrote that there is no basis for this distinction. The reason it is not a migo to extract property is that an object ordinarily lent remains presumptively in its owner's possession and therefore its recovery is not called extracting from the possessor. Thus, where the object is not ordinarily lent but all the other adverse circumstances are present, the homeowner is believed to say it was borrowed, yet he is not believed to say it was stolen by virtue of that migo, because in that case the object is presumptively in the possession of the current holder and the migo would be used to extract property. By contrast, for objects ordinarily lent, everyone agrees that they remain presumptively in the homeowner's possession. Consider this carefully.
Its law is like one who claims they were borrowed. See Tumim, seif katan 8. It appears that if the object is ordinarily lent but this particular person is not accustomed to lend to this holder, it is treated like an object not ordinarily lent, as explained above in siman 72, seif 19. Nevertheless, if all the other adverse circumstances are present, it is as though he regularly lends to him, and the homeowner is believed to say it was stolen by virtue of the migo that it was borrowed. See there, where I used this to resolve Tosafot.
And if he is established, etc. Sema holds that all the adverse circumstances are required even for objects ordinarily lent when the homeowner claims theft and the holder has an established reputation for theft. Shach holds that if witnesses saw him enter empty and leave full, this alone suffices to prevent the claim from being considered a migo used to extract property. According to both views, the comparison to a claim that the objects were borrowed is therefore not exact. In Tumim I wrote that the comparison is exact: with objects ordinarily lent we do not regard the migo as one used to extract property. See there.
Its law, etc. If the other adverse circumstances are absent and the objects are not ordinarily lent, so that the person who left with them is believed, we do not administer a hesset oath to him when he is established as a thief, because he is suspect concerning an oath, and the Sages do not enact one remedial enactment on top of another. Mordechai.
Like one who claims they were borrowed. Sema writes that the comparison is not complete. Where the homeowner claims theft, he swears while holding a sacred object, as Rambam writes in Hilchot Geneivah. Where he claims the objects were borrowed, he takes only hesset, as Rambam writes in Hilchot To'en. Shach writes that the requirement to hold a sacred object is a scribal error. This requires study, because the expression "he swears and collects" implies an oath while holding a sacred object. If it were hesset, the text should first have said that he collects and afterward takes the oath.
