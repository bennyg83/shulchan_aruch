And the witnesses see. Meaning that they saw him enter empty handed and leave carrying articles. Otherwise he is believed through a migo that he could say, "I brought in my own property and took it back out." The same applies if witnesses did not see him enter empty handed but there are witnesses that the articles he removed belonged to the owner and are items ordinarily lent out. Otherwise he is believed through a migo that he could say that he had purchased those articles earlier:
And the owner swears a hesset oath. The wording is reversed. It should read that after he returns the property, the owner takes a hesset oath:
He claims they were stolen. See the Beurim. Even where witnesses did not see him enter empty handed, if the articles are ordinarily lent out he is believed to say that they were stolen through a migo that he could say they were borrowed. This is not a migo used to extract property, because articles ordinarily lent out remain in the presumptive ownership of their owner. By contrast, with articles not ordinarily lent out, where additional suspicious circumstances exist, he is not believed to say they were stolen through a migo that they were borrowed, because that would be a migo used to extract. The principal law is explained in siman 357:
And if he is established, and so forth, as a thief. The Sema holds that all the suspicious circumstances are required even with articles ordinarily lent out where the owner claims they were stolen and the possessor is established as a thief. The Shach holds that if witnesses saw him enter empty handed and leave carrying property, this alone suffices to prevent the claim from being considered a migo used to extract, and no further suspicious circumstances are needed:
