He claims they were stolen. See Sema seif katan 34, through his statement that witnesses saw him enter empty-handed and leave with utensils. Sema appears to write this only according to Tosafot in Shevuot 46, who in the discussion of one recognizing his utensils hold that even property normally lent out cannot be recovered through a migo of "they were borrowed," because that would be a migo used to extract money. Sema therefore explains that here it is not a migo to extract, since witnesses saw the person enter empty-handed and leave with the utensils. Shach writes similarly in siman 22. But according to Tosafot's other explanation there, even utensils ordinarily lent or rented that are found in another person's house can support a claim of theft through a migo of borrowing, because this is not regarded as a migo to extract. Tosafot derive this from the law of roaming animals, and Sema himself rules accordingly in siman 357 seif katan 3. On that approach, even if no witnesses saw him enter empty-handed, the migo is not considered one to extract. The distinction is that property not ordinarily lent or rented remains under the possessor's monetary chazakah. Therefore, even where there are suspicious circumstances that allow the owner to say "borrowed," he cannot escalate that to "stolen" through a migo of borrowing, because that would use the migo to extract property from its possessor. But property ordinarily lent remains under the prior owner's chazakah even when physically in another's possession. A migo concerning such property is not a migo to extract. According to the Tosafot view that even ordinarily lent property would involve a migo to extract, the roaming-animal case can still be explained. The special need for a theft claim supported by migo applies where the owner says, "You stole them from my house," so that the items reached the possessor through a prohibited act. Calling a person a thief requires stronger grounds, and the claimant needs a migo. Roaming animals, by contrast, may have entered the possessor's control permissibly at first. When he later holds them and claims purchase, rejecting that claim does not initially label him as someone who acquired them through theft, and no special migo is needed. The same is true where an owner says an item was lent and the possessor says it was purchased: if the item initially came into his hands permissibly, his false purchase claim does not create the same presumption that he stole it.
