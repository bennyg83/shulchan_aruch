One who entered the house of the householder, etc. In the Derisha and Prisha I explained the approaches to this sugya and its variant readings. The Mechaber follows the reading of the Rif, Rambam, and Rosh, as explained there.
And what he writes, 'and the witnesses see him,' means that witnesses saw him enter empty-handed and also saw him leave with utensils concealed under his garments. By this entry alone he was not established as a robber, because the case is where they did not hear him announce that he was entering to seize collateral without permission.
And after some time he sued him, etc. The Mechaber precisely says 'after some time,' because if the owner's claim is, 'Give me the utensils I lent you,' it would make little sense for him to make that claim immediately upon the person's exit. If he had only just lent them, the circumstances would be different.
And behold, the witnesses. The definite article refers to the same witnesses who saw him enter empty-handed and emerge carrying utensils. He therefore cannot deny the entire event or claim purchase by virtue of a migo that he could have denied possession. The phrase also implies that witnesses saw the utensils presently in his possession before the lawsuit, so that he cannot say, 'I already returned them.' Witnesses to present possession are required here, as the Tur and Mechaber explain in seif 12.
But if the householder is a person who ordinarily sells his utensils, etc. Then we say that the householder sold them and asked the buyer to take them out concealed so that he would not be embarrassed by the sale of his household goods.
And likewise if he left with them openly. The same applies to the other adverse indications. If these are utensils normally carried concealed, or if the person is modest and ordinarily carries everything concealed, then even if the householder does not usually sell his utensils, the one who left with them takes an oath and is exempt. The Tur writes this as well.
But articles that are made to be lent or rented. Above, siman 72, the Tur and Mechaber, seif 19, explain which articles are considered ordinarily lent or rented.
Since he has witnesses that this article which is made, etc. The correct reading is 'this article which is made to be lent.' The word 'known' does not mean that witnesses testified the article originally belonged to him, for no such testimony was mentioned. Rather, because witnesses saw the other person enter empty-handed and leave with these articles, and the articles are of a type ordinarily lent, it is as though their ownership by the householder is known.
He claims that they were stolen. He is believed by virtue of the migo that he could have said, 'I lent them to him.' It is possible this is not considered a migo used to extract property, because the witnesses saw the other person enter empty-handed and leave carrying the utensils, so the possessor does not have ordinary established possession against the householder.
And some say, etc., that the householder is not believed, because we do not presume people to be thieves even where the householder has a migo. It is also possible that the reason is that this would be a migo used to extract property.
And if this person has become established and publicly known as a thief, then even when the householder claims the articles were stolen, his law is like one who claims they were borrowed. Meaning, it is like a claim of borrowing even regarding articles not normally lent or rented, where the householder can be believed, provided all the adverse circumstances mentioned above are present. The Rif, Rambam, Rosh, and Tur support this. There are nevertheless differences in the oath required: Rambam requires the stronger oath while holding a sacred object for a theft claim, whereas for a borrowing claim hesset may suffice. The Mechaber's comparison at the end of the seif appears intended primarily to teach the householder's credibility, not necessarily to equate the oath in every detail.
