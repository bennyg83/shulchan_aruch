And the witnesses see him. The Sema explains that the witnesses saw him enter empty handed, and also saw him leave with utensils concealed beneath his garments. That is, otherwise he would be believed through a migo that he could have said, I brought in my own utensils and then took them back out. The Bach writes the same. The same applies even if they did not see him enter empty handed, where there are witnesses that these particular utensils belonged to the householder, as below in seif 12.
And the householder takes a heset oath, etc., and they return, etc. The wording is reversed. The law is that the possessor must first return the householder's utensils, and only afterward does the householder take a heset oath. Before the return there is no need for the householder to swear, because we find no heset oath by which one swears and collects except through reversal, as the Rambam writes in chapter 1 of Hilchos To'en. The Rif likewise writes in Perek Kol HaNishba'in, Shaar 4, that the possessor must return the utensils to the householder, and the householder then swears that he did not sell him anything. Likewise, when the Rambam and Mechaber write below in seif 14 that there is no oath here, they mean before the return. Afterward, however, the householder must take a heset oath, for they write that after the possessor returns the utensils he may again sue the householder, and the law between them follows the usual rules. The Rambam's presentation is clearer because he does not place these two laws next to one another. He writes the present law in chapter 9 of Hilchos To'en, whereas the law below in seif 14 appears in chapter 4 of Hilchos Gezeilah. The proof is that there, in chapter 4 of Hilchos Gezeilah, the Rambam writes that even if the possessor says, I took it against a debt owed to me, he must return it, and there is no oath here. The Mechaber cites this language below in siman 364, seif 2. Why should the householder not be required to swear that he owes him nothing? Rather, the phrase "there is no oath here" plainly means before the return. His explanation that witnesses saw what he stole only answers a different point. Immediately beforehand, in the law of one who was robbed, the victim swears and collects before return, even with a sacred object, whereas here the householder takes the property without an oath. The reason is that in the robbery case the witnesses did not see what was taken, while here they did see what was taken. The Rambam there is not addressing heset. He therefore writes that after the possessor returns the property he can sue the householder, and the law between them follows the usual rules, relying on what he already wrote in chapter 9 of Hilchos To'en that the householder must then take a heset oath. The Tur likewise implies that even where the householder takes the utensils in the possessor's absence, he must afterward take a heset oath. This is clear.
And some say that this was stated only where he claims that they were borrowed. The Sema writes that the householder is not believed to say they were stolen, because we do not presume people to be thieves even where he has a migo, and perhaps the reason is that this would be a migo used to extract property. But the Rosh writes that through a migo of borrowing or rental we do not presume people to be thieves. This implies that where the possessor is already established as a thief, the householder is believed to say the items were stolen through a migo that he could have said they were borrowed or rented. The Ran in Perek Kol HaNishba'in likewise appears to hold this. If so, this cannot be considered a migo used to extract property, because witnesses saw him remove the utensils from the house. Even according to the view of Tosafos that I cite below at the end of siman 133, under one explanation, that such a migo is a migo used to extract, this case is different because witnesses saw him remove utensils from the house. Accordingly, where the items are ordinarily lent or rented, and the possessor is established as a thief, the householder is believed through a migo even without the other suspicious circumstances mentioned earlier. Had he claimed the items were borrowed or rented, he would be believed even without those circumstances. So too, when he claims they were stolen and the possessor is established as a thief, he is believed through that migo. This is also clear from the Tur, who writes that if the possessor is established as a thief there is no distinction between a claim that the items were stolen and a claim that they were borrowed. Thus, regarding items ordinarily lent or rented, a householder who claims theft against one established as a thief is believed even without the other suspicious circumstances. Otherwise there would still be a distinction between a theft claim and a borrowing claim. Rabbeinu Yeruham, Nesiv 31, chelek 4, likewise writes that ordinarily, if the householder says the items were stolen, he is not believed even regarding items ordinarily lent or rented, because the presumption favoring borrowed items is weakened when he himself alleges theft. But he then writes that with items not ordinarily lent or rented there is a distinction where the possessor is established as a thief. His meaning is that regarding items ordinarily lent or rented he had already written that there is no distinction, and the householder is always believed. So too, where he alleges theft against one established as a thief, the householder is always believed through the migo of borrowing or rental. This is contrary to what appears from the Sema in seif katan 35 and the following seif katan. The Rif in Perek Kol HaNishba'in, Shaar 8, the Rambam in chapter 5 of Hilchos Gezeilah, and Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 49, beginning of chelek 1, do not distinguish this point and write generally that where the suspicious circumstances concerning the possessor exist, and the householder alleges theft against a person established as a thief, the householder is believed. Even so, we do not abandon their general language in favor of the explicit words of the Rosh, Ran, Tur, and Rabbeinu Yeruham, as explained above.
And if this person has become established and publicly known as a thief, etc. See the Sema, who eventually writes that the Tur and Mechaber's general wording at the beginning of this siman concerns a case where the entrant claims purchase, etc. I already challenged him on this in seif katan 7. It seems to me that the Mechaber did not intend such a distinction. Since the Rif, Rosh, Tur, Mechaber, and other poskim write generally that a person established as a thief is treated like one against whom the householder claims borrowing, it follows that the law is equal in every respect, and the householder swears only heset. This must also be the Rambam's view. What difference is there between a possessor who is not believed to claim purchase because the items are presumed borrowed, and a possessor who is not believed to claim purchase because the items are presumed stolen and he is established as a thief? In either event, the presumption of sale is weakened. The Rambam's statement there that the householder swears while holding a sacred object appears to be a scribal error. Alternatively, the Rambam may be discussing a case where witnesses saw him remove utensils concealed beneath his garments but do not know which utensils he removed. Since he is publicly known as a thief, he has the legal status of a robber, and the householder therefore swears while holding a sacred object and collects whatever he claims, even if the thief does not admit that he removed that amount.
