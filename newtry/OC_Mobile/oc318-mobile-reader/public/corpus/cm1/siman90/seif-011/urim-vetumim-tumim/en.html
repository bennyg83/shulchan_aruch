And if he is established, etc., as a thief, etc. The Sema holds that even if a person is known as a thief, the owner cannot ordinarily claim that objects in his possession were stolen merely by a migo that he could have said they were lent, because that would be a migo used to extract property. Therefore, even with objects ordinarily lent, a theft claim requires the additional suspicious circumstances listed in the seif. The Shach objects that the Tur compares the theft claim directly to the loan claim. If the Sema were correct, there would be a major difference: with objects ordinarily lent a loan claim needs no further suspicious circumstances, while a theft claim would. The Shach says the migo is not one used to extract because witnesses saw the alleged thief enter empty handed and leave carrying hidden objects. Even this does not fully resolve the comparison. A loan claim can succeed with an object ordinarily lent merely because the owner identifies it, without any such suspicious entry and exit. A theft claim would still differ. The clearer explanation is what I wrote above: an object ordinarily lent remains, for evidentiary purposes, in the original owner's chazakah. Possession in the other person's hand does not create the usual ownership presumption. Therefore, when the owner says, "I lent it," he is not extracting from a full possessor. The same applies to a migo used to say, "you stole it," where the alternative loan claim would itself return the object without payment. Tosafos can therefore treat the theft claim like the loan claim. Where the owner says that other people stole the item and sold it to the present possessor, the situation changes because the market enactment applies. The owner concedes that the present possessor received the object through a purchase and cannot recover it without reimbursing the price. To recover the object without paying, the owner would now be extracting value from a purchaser. That is a genuine migo used to extract. The Rosh does not necessarily accept Tosafos's entire distinction, as I explained above. Although the Rif mentions the additional suspicious circumstances, it can be speaking specifically of objects not ordinarily lent. With objects ordinarily lent, the governing principle is that they do not acquire an ordinary chazakah in the holder, so the same extra circumstances are not required.
Even if the homeowner says they were stolen. The Sema says the homeowner is believed by a migo that he could have said they were lent, and explains that this is not a migo used to extract because the alleged thief was seen entering empty and leaving with objects. From his wording one might infer that even Tosafos permits a theft claim for an object ordinarily lent only when the additional suspicious circumstances are present. That does not fit Tosafos. Tosafos proves the theft migo from the law of cattle and similar property that has no normal chazakah: the owner can say, "you seized it from me," even without suspicious entry or concealment. The wording also indicates that with objects ordinarily lent, the special suspicious circumstances are unnecessary. The conceptual distinction is that objects not ordinarily lent stand in the current holder's chazakah. There a migo cannot ordinarily extract them. Objects ordinarily lent remain in the original owner's chazakah, and the holder's physical possession alone does not create the same evidentiary barrier. This is why Tosafos compares them to animals that wander, which likewise do not create a normal chazakah merely through possession. Tosafos's separate answer concerning a person who recognizes his books in another's hands and claims they were stolen through third parties must be read differently. There, the owner concedes that the present possessor did not steal them and instead bought them. As long as the purchaser has not been reimbursed, his purchase gives him a protected monetary interest under the market enactment. The owner's claim would therefore extract money from him. That is why Tosafos calls it a migo used to extract even if the books themselves are ordinarily lent.
And with this. This also explains the Rosh's disagreement. Tosafos infers that with objects ordinarily lent the owner may say "stolen" by migo of "lent" because the Gemara's limitation that a theft claim is not believed appears before its later discussion of objects ordinarily lent. The Rosh rejects the inference because it is generally implausible to brand a person a thief merely through migo. There is still a question why the Gemara did not place the theft limitation after the discussion of objects ordinarily lent if it applied there too. The answer is that the Gemara's unqualified phrase "stolen" naturally refers to the claim that the present holder stole them. With objects not ordinarily lent, the owner needs all the suspicious circumstances to establish even a loan claim, and if he shifts to "stolen" he is accusing this person directly, something the Gemara rejects because we do not casually establish a person as a thief. With objects ordinarily lent, however, no suspicious entry or exit is needed for the loan claim. If the owner merely says "they were stolen from me," that can mean unknown thieves stole them and they eventually came to the present holder. That does not brand the holder a thief. Therefore the Gemara cannot state an unqualified rule that "stolen" is not believed after the objects ordinarily lent clause. In that setting some theft formulations may indeed be more plausible. If the owner expressly says "you stole them," then the Rosh's concern about branding the holder a thief remains. If he says "others stole them and sold them to you," the market enactment and the purchaser's monetary interest become relevant. This explains the placement of the Gemara's language without accepting every inference made by Tosafos. The disagreement also illuminates the case of a recognized item purchased in the marketplace. According to Tosafos, once the owner's claim concedes a valid purchase by the present holder, recovering without reimbursement is extraction. According to the Rosh, who does not fully accept that conceptual limitation, the analysis can proceed differently. This resolves difficulties raised by Maharsha and later responsa concerning the Tosafos proof. There is another way to understand Rashi's reading, which requires the suspicious circumstances together with the object's ordinary susceptibility to lending. Rabbeinu Tam makes two things relevant: the nature of the object and the owner's personal habit of lending. A person cannot become "accustomed to lend" before some initial occasion. If no prior lending practice is known, the owner can always say, "this was the first time I began lending to him." Ordinarily that is too weak. But where the suspicious circumstances exist, such as entering empty and leaving full, concealment, and an owner not normally selling such items, those circumstances can establish that this was indeed the beginning of a lending relationship. They substitute for proof of prior habit. This works only if the object itself is of a type reasonably lent. If its very nature makes lending highly unlikely because it will be damaged or otherwise compromised, no number of these secondary circumstances overcomes the basic chazakah. Thus Rashi can require both an object of a lendable type and the suspicious facts when the owner's personal lending habit is not yet established, while agreeing that if both the object and the owner's habit are already known, no extra suspicious circumstances are needed. This explains the Gemara's proof from orphans holding objects ordinarily lent. There, the object was lendable and the owner was known to lend such things. The additional suspicious facts were therefore unnecessary. It also resolves the difficulty from the Mishnah concerning recognized books. Rashi may say that books are not always within the category ordinarily lent in the technical sense, or that the owner's habit was not established. Thus the special rules remain necessary. The same framework can explain Rabbeinu Chananel's reading that the phrase "objects ordinarily lent" focuses on the owner's conduct rather than merely the object's physical nature. He can agree in practical law that one needs the object's lendability and a personal lending pattern, while interpreting which element the Gemara's phrase emphasizes. Rabbeinu Tam, by contrast, reads the phrase more broadly and therefore does not adopt Rashi's exact formulation. These approaches remove the apparent contradictions among the early authorities.
As though he claimed they were lent. This means that with objects not ordinarily lent he cannot simply say "they were stolen" even though the suspicious circumstances might have allowed a loan claim. If the object itself is of a type ordinarily lent, however, and the owner also has the relevant lending habit or the suspicious circumstances establish the beginning of such a habit, a theft claim can receive the same evidentiary treatment as a loan claim according to Tosafos. Because Rabbeinu Tam says the law depends not only on the object's nature but also on whether this owner is accustomed to lend, one can say that where the object is ordinarily lendable but the owner is not yet known as a lender, the three suspicious circumstances may substitute for that personal habit. They show that the present incident likely was a loan or another unauthorized removal. This provides additional support for distinguishing between the object's inherent lendability and the owner's personal conduct.
Therefore. The preceding analysis also resolves the difficulty in Rashi's version. Rashi requires both the suspicious circumstances and an object ordinarily lent. Tosafos and Rosh asked how the Gemara can then bring proof from the orphans, since there the extra suspicious circumstances were not present. The answer is that in the orphans' case both relevant positive factors were already independently known: the type of object was lendable and the owner was accustomed to lend. No substitute suspicious circumstances were needed. In the earlier case the suspicious circumstances are required only to establish or substitute for the missing personal lending habit, not because they are always an independent fourth requirement. This also explains why Rashi struggled with the case of recognized books. If books are ordinarily lendable and the owner is ordinarily willing to lend, the owner should not need a public report of theft. Therefore Rashi must understand that the Mishnah's ordinary books do not automatically have both qualities. Once this is recognized, the reading of Rashi, Rabbeinu Chananel, Rabbeinu Tam, Tosafos, and Rosh can be aligned more coherently. The practical distinctions depend on whether the object itself is lendable, whether this owner is known to lend it, whether special suspicious circumstances substitute for that knowledge, and whether the theft claim accuses the present holder or concedes a protected marketplace purchase.
