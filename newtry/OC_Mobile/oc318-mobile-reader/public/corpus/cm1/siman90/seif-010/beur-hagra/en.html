One deposited, etc. This is stated in the Yerushalmi cited by Rif and Rosh concerning the enactment for one whose property was damaged by fire. A person deposited with another a tied sack, and an unavoidable accident occurred. The custodian said that it contained slag, while the depositor said that it was full of silk. The case came before Rabbi Ami, who ruled that the depositor swears and collects. Ri Migash writes that the custodian cannot be asserting with certainty that it contained slag, for if so the defendant should swear and be exempt, like one who was sued for wheat and admitted barley. Rather, he says that perhaps it contained slag. Although if he said, 'I know it contained gold, but I do not know how much,' he would ordinarily be one who is obligated to swear but cannot and therefore must pay, as the Yerushalmi states at the end of chapter HaDayanim, here the claimant sued for wheat and the defendant admitted barley, so no oath obligation exists between them at all. This accords with the view at the end of chapter HaManiah. Nor can one require a hesset oath that he does not know. Such an oath applies where there is no established monetary basis, but here there is an established monetary basis, since the custodian admits receiving something and is liable to pay, though he does not know what it was. This resembles the mishnah at the end of Bava Kamma, which distinguishes between one who says, 'I do not know whether I borrowed,' where there is no established monetary basis, and one who says, 'I do not know whether I repaid,' where, in a contest between certainty and uncertainty, the certain claim prevails. The cases are not identical in every respect. Rather, the Sages applied the robbery-victim enactment to a deposit, just as they did where one who caused a fire does not know the amount of the loss. This accords with Tosafot in the name of Ri regarding an informer. See Rosh there and Sefer HaTerumot, gate 39, part 2. See also the cited references 4, 50, and 63.
Provided that he claims, etc. The claim must be of the type recognized for a robbery victim and for one whose property was burned, as stated in Shevuot and Bava Kamma. This is unlike the Yerushalmi, which asks concerning the tied sack whether the depositor may claim items not normally suited to him. The Yerushalmi attempts to resolve the question from the incident involving Bar Ziza's tenant, who deposited a litra of gold with another, reasoning that some people do not publicize their wealth. Rif cites the incident, but the law does not follow the Yerushalmi where it conflicts with the Babylonian Gemara. See Rif at the end of Eruvin. Rosh attempts to reconcile them by distinguishing the Bar Ziza case, where the money remains presumptively in the depositor's ownership, from the Babylonian cases, where one seeks to extract money from the tortfeasor. The Yerushalmi's wording, however, does not support that distinction, for it asks generally whether such a claimant may assert unusual items and answers from the Bar Ziza incident. The Yerushalmi in chapter Kol HaNishba'in likewise asks this question regarding a robbery victim and cites Rabbi Yohanan's statement that a person may appear wealthy in the marketplace yet poor at home, or wealthy at home yet poor in the marketplace, and that one person becomes wealthy while another does not become impoverished. It then recounts the same Bar Ziza incident. Hagahot Ashri raises this objection. It appears that Rosh means only that the Babylonian Gemara does not dispute the actual Bar Ziza ruling, which is why Rif cites it. Ketubot 85b likewise supports the ruling, as Tosafot explain, but specifically because two considerations were present, including that the claimant supplied an identifying sign. Rosh proves this at length. See also the cited reference 91.
Some say that he swears, etc. Raavad objects that the claimant sued for the sack and its silk contents, while the custodian admitted one of them. He should therefore be liable under the rule governing partial admission, as stated in chapter HaDayanim and explained above in siman 88. Ordinarily, one who is obligated to swear but cannot do so must pay. Raavad answers that this rule applies only to a matter the defendant should have known, where the claimant says with certainty, 'You know,' and the defendant is presumed to be evading. Where the defendant could not have been expected to know, he swears that he does not know and is exempt. This applies even if he says, 'I know there was gold in it, but I do not know how much.' At first glance, this answer would apply to all the preceding clauses, but that is incorrect, for the correct ruling is that the depositor swears and collects. So rules the gloss in siman 388, seif 1, and likewise seif 7 there. This is Rabbeinu Tam's view in Tosafot to Bava Kamma. See also above, siman 87, seif 6, and the notes to siman 75, seif 17. The first opinion in siman 388, seif 7, does not necessarily disagree with Rabbeinu Tam. Ri disagrees there only because he understands the unresolved question to include a doubtful claim and therefore rules leniently, while Rabbeinu Tam holds that in such a case the enactment certainly applies and Rav Ashi's uncertainty concerns only a definite claim. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch in siman 388, seif 1, write, 'If the tortfeasor knew,' which shows a different approach. Raavad maintains that whether the custodian says, 'Perhaps it contained slag,' or, 'I know it contained gold but do not know how much,' the claimant swears and collects. He states this in his glosses to chapter 5 of the laws of Borrowing and Deposit, explaining that the defendant in the ordinary case should have known, while the custodian here need not have known. This is also why the Yerushalmi's tied-sack case says that the claimant swears and collects rather than collecting without an oath. Although Raavad's second argument, that the claim lacked a fixed measure, was rejected from the Yerushalmi at the end of chapter HaDayanim, the first argument, that the defendant was not expected to know, was accepted. He repeats this at the end of the laws of Injury and Damage, comparing the case directly to the tied sack and rejecting Rambam's distinction. Rosh likewise rules this way at the end of chapter HaKones, and Tur cites him here: Rif writes that the enactment for a robbery victim applies to a custodian, so the depositor swears and collects; Rosh adds that this is true even when the custodian says, 'Perhaps it contained slag.' Rambam writes that if the custodian knows there was gold but does not know how much, the ordinary rule applies, whereas Rosh says that because the custodian was not expected to know, the depositor always swears and collects. Tur states the same in siman 298 and cites Raavad. The gloss to siman 388, seif 1, and Shulchan Aruch there, seif 7, likewise follow this view. The distinction between what the defendant should and should not have known is explained by Rosh and by Tosafot in Bava Kamma 46b from the discussion in Shevuot 47a. A reason is required for why the father is liable while heirs are exempt. Otherwise, why would the Gemara ask how the case differs when the uncertainty is his rather than theirs? Tosafot answer that the injured party's claim is certain while the tortfeasor's uncertainty concerns a matter he was not expected to know, and therefore the rule of one who cannot swear and must pay does not apply. The first opinion relies on Bava Metzia 116b, where liability follows when there is an oath obligation even though the defendant was not expected to know, and on Bava Batra 134a, which draws a proof from the case of 'you owe me a maneh.' Were Raavad's distinction correct, those proofs would fail, since even where an oath obligation exists all would agree that such a defendant is exempt. Tosafot reject those proofs. Rambam is not contradicted by the Bava Kamma discussion because he requires the claimant to say, 'You know,' as explained below in siman 400, seif 1. The passage in Shevuot may even support Rambam, since the special exclusion of heirs implies that the father is liable even in this type of case. Rif writes that the contrary discussion follows Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov. Ramban challenges the earlier distinction based upon an established monetary basis, noting that in the mishnah concerning a borrowed and rented animal there is also an established monetary basis, yet the Gemara still derives that one who says, 'You owe me a maneh,' and the other says, 'I do not know,' is exempt unless there is an oath obligation. Rashba answers that in the animal case, the defendant's statement that it was rented creates no independent liability, while here even slag has value and the custodian admits liability for that value. See Ran to chapter HaDayanim. See also the cited references 50 and 63.
