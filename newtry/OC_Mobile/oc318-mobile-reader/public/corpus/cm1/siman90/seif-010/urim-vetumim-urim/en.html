Perhaps it contained dross or sand. See Tumim, where I concluded that this applies specifically where the dross or sand is worth at least one perutah. There is then some monetary basis to the claim, and he therefore holds that the depositor swears and collects, because the Sages extended the enactment for a robbed person to a deposit. But if the contents are not worth a perutah, there is no monetary basis at all and they did not institute the robbed person's enactment. One cannot object that this should be partial admission and therefore a case where one who cannot swear must pay, because the admission is not of the same kind as the claim: the claimant demanded gold and the defendant admitted dross. This must be where the claimant did not separately demand the sack. If he also demanded the sack, there is partial admission and the defendant is one who must swear but cannot swear, so the claimant need not swear at all.

The depositor collects, etc. This is because there is partial admission, and one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. Although the defendant had no reason to know what was inside, Rambam nevertheless holds that the rule that one who cannot swear must pay applies.
And some say that he swears that he does not know. There are two ways to explain this view. Either these authorities hold that the rule requiring payment by one who cannot swear does not apply when he had no reason to know, and they also hold that the special enactment for a robbed person was not extended to deposits. Or they hold only that the robbed person's enactment was not extended to deposits, and therefore disagree with the first ruling here, because there is no Torah oath at all: the sack itself was not demanded, and dross is not an admission of the same kind as a claim for gold. But in an actual case of partial admission they agree that one who cannot swear must pay. Shach writes that the latter explanation appears correct, because below in siman 298 the Mechaber rules that the robbed person's enactment was not extended to deposits, while he does not mention there at all the view that payment depends upon whether the defendant should have known. Rama, however, although he rules without qualification both here and there, in siman 388, seif 1, decides in accordance with those who hold that where the defendant had no reason to know, we do not apply the rule that one who cannot swear must pay.
