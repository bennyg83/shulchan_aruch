The owner of the deposit swears. Ri Migash asks why the depositor swears and collects. Ordinarily one who says, "You owe me a maneh," and the defendant says, "I do not know," is exempt. He answers that here the custodian admits the existence of a real monetary relationship and is uncertain only about its amount. Where there is an objective monetary basis, the claimant can swear and collect. Rambam follows this approach. Raavad objects from the sugya of the two oxen. There too the injured party says with certainty which ox damaged and the damager is uncertain, yet the claimant does not simply swear and collect. Kesef Mishneh recognizes this as a powerful objection. The correct answer is that Rambam's rule here is not a general principle of certain claimant against uncertain defendant. It rests on the special enactment modeled after the robbed person's oath concerning a deposit. Rosh cites the Geonim that the Sages strengthened the depositor so that custodians would be careful with deposits. Once the custodian admits the deposit relationship but cannot identify what happened to the property or its value, the depositor swears and collects. No such special enactment exists in the ox case. This is also the better understanding of Rambam's ruling.
