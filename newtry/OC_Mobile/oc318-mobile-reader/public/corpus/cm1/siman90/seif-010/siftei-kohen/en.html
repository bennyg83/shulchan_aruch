The owner of the deposit swears and collects. Ri Migash asks why the claimant should swear and collect. The case appears comparable to one who says, “You owe me one maneh,” and the defendant answers, “I do not know,” where the defendant is exempt. He answers that there the defendant admits no underlying monetary obligation, whereas here he admits liability for the deposit and is uncertain only about its contents. The Maggid Mishneh says Rambam agrees, but the Beit Yosef correctly objects that Rambam elsewhere rules that if wheat is claimed and the defendant says, “I do not know whether it was wheat or barley,” he swears that he does not know, pays barley, and is exempt from wheat. Raavad challenges Rambam from the Gemara's discussion of two oxen, which compares a certain claim by the injured party and an uncertain response by the tortfeasor to Rabbah bar Natan's wheat and barley rule. Ri Migash can answer that his rule applies where the defendant maintains an actual admission but may have been jesting. The Gemara's challenge is that if a barley answer to wheat may be mere deflection, the tortfeasor should likewise be able to say that his uncertain admission was a deflection. Moreover, in the two-ox case the tortfeasor was not expected to know which animal caused which damage, making deflection especially plausible. According to Rambam and the Mechaber, however, the principal answer is different. Here the Sages enacted the special rule of one robbed with respect to a deposit. Although ordinary law would allow the custodian to swear and be exempt, the Geonim established that the depositor swears and collects so that custodians will guard deposits carefully and not act negligently. The Rosh says that one should not depart from their ruling, and the Tur, Rif, and Maharshal likewise recognize this enactment. This removes Raavad's objection and reconciles the law with Rambam's wheat and barley ruling. The Beit Yosef's conclusion that the enactment applies only to hidden property damaged by fire and not to deposits is therefore difficult. The Rosh and Tur expressly apply the enactment to deposits, and Rif repeats the Jerusalem Talmud's teaching at the end of the chapter HaMafkid after already citing it concerning fire, indicating that the same robbed-person enactment applies to a deposit.
And he collects, etc. According to this ruling, the sack itself is not treated as a partial admission. Raavad and Rosh disagree and consider it partial admission. See what I wrote above, siman 72, seif 12, seif katan 21.
The depositor collects without an oath. According to Raavad, Rosh, and Rashba, he swears and collects. The Tur rules this way in simanim 32 and 33, and the gloss below, siman 388, end of seif 1, does likewise. It is therefore surprising that the Rama is silent here and in siman 298. As a matter of halachah, however, I concluded above, siman 72, seif katan 54, like Rambam and the Mechaber. Baal HaTerumot cites Raavad regarding one who deposited a gold ring without weighing it. The custodian did not know its weight and was negligent. The depositor claims it weighed two gold dinars. The custodian says, “I do not know whether it weighed even one dinar, and I do not know whether its interior was copper with only gold on the outside. You yourself know that I had no knowledge of it.” The depositor swears and collects. It appears that all authorities agree in this case, because the claimant admits that the defendant truly does not know, as I wrote above, siman 72, seif 12, seif katan 51.
And some say that he swears that he does not know, etc. The Sema writes that this “some say” refers to what was stated at the beginning of the seif. It therefore applies to the first interpretation, and not to the second interpretation that the Sema gives below in siman 298. The meaning is that the Sages did not extend the enactment made for one who was robbed to a case involving a deposit. The Mechaber considers the view of these authorities primary, for he ruled accordingly in the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 298. See there and examine carefully. See also Even HaEzer, folio 104b.
