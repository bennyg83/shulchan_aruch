The owner of the deposit shall swear, etc. All the later authorities explain that the reason is that the Sages extended the enactment made for one who was robbed to the law of a deposit. The Beit Yosef, however, ruled otherwise, and the Shach treats the matter as a doubt in the law. In my view, when the authorities say that the enactment for one who was robbed was extended to a deposit, they mean only a case that resembles an admission of part, except that the defendant could not reasonably have been expected to know the missing amount, or where the admission is not of the same kind as the claim so that there is no ordinary oath of one who admits part. In such circumstances the Sages created a special remedy by analogy to the robbed person. But where the defendant denies everything, such as where he says uncertainly that perhaps the deposit was not worth even a perutah, there is no enactment for the robbed person. In the robbed person case itself there is at least some established basis for monetary liability. If no witnesses saw the defendant take anything at all, the Sages did not institute the claimant’s oath. The enactment applies only where witnesses saw that he took something, so there is at least some concrete monetary basis. If so, even where the law is extended to a deposit, it should apply only where a comparable concrete basis exists. Why should we assume that the deposit enactment is broader than the very robbery enactment from which it is derived? There is a proof from the Raavad in chapter 5 of Hilkhot She’elah U’Pikadon, halakhah 6. He disagrees with the Rambam’s rule that we do not apply “since he cannot swear, he must pay” where the defendant could not have been expected to know. The Raavad brings proof from the Yerushalmi’s case where one deposited a tied sack with another. The depositary says, “I do not know what was inside,” while the owner says, “It contained gold ornaments.” The Yerushalmi requires the owner to swear and collect rather than saying that the depositary, who cannot swear, must pay. This shows that the Raavad understands the case as one concerning a deposit. Baal HaTerumot, Sha’ar 35, part 3, likewise cites the Raavad regarding a depositary who was negligent and then says that he does not know the value of the deposit, ruling that the depositor swears and collects. It is therefore clear that the Raavad accepts an enactment analogous to the robbed person for deposits. If so, at first glance the Raavad’s criticism of the Rambam in the next halakhah is difficult. The Rambam rules that where a tied sack was deposited, the owner swears regarding its contents. The Raavad writes that the reason is that this is an admission of part, since the owner claims both the sack and its contents, while the depositary admits the sack. But according to the Rambam, the Raavad says, the case resembles one where the tortfeasor says uncertainly and the injured party says definitely. Why is that a problem? The Raavad himself accepts that the enactment for the robbed person was extended to deposits. He should simply explain the Rambam on that basis. According to what I wrote, the matter is clear. Where there is an admission of part, and the ordinary rule would be “since he cannot swear, he must pay,” but the defendant could not have been expected to know the amount, the Sages created a special deposit enactment and allowed the owner to swear and collect. But if the dross or sack creates no legally relevant admission at all, because the case is treated like one who claims wheat and receives an admission of barley, then the depositary is effectively denying everything. In a case of complete denial the Sages made no such enactment. The same point is explicit in the Rashba’s novellae to Bava Kamma at the end of HaKones. He asks how the Yerushalmi’s case should be understood. If the claimant demanded both the sack and what was in it, then the depositary admits the sack and says “I do not know” regarding the contents. This should trigger the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot must pay. If the claimant demanded only the contents, then the case resembles one who claims wheat and receives an admission of barley. The Rashba answers that the claimant did demand the sack as well, but nevertheless the ordinary “cannot swear, must pay” rule does not apply because the depositary could not have been expected to know. The reason the depositor swears is that the Sages extended the robbed person enactment to a deposit. At first glance, once the Rashba invokes that enactment, why does he need to say that the claimant also demanded the sack? Even if the claim concerned only the contents, the deposit enactment should allow the depositor to swear. Necessarily, the enactment applies only where there is at least an admission to the sack, meaning a concrete basis similar to an admission of part, but where there is no such admission and the case is equivalent to wheat claimed and barley admitted, the Sages did not extend the robbed person enactment. This is clear. This also explains Rabbi Yosef HaLevi’s statement that the claimant swears because where one party is certain and the other uncertain, the certain claim prevails when there is a concrete monetary basis, even though we ordinarily do not rule like Rav Yehudah that certainty automatically prevails over uncertainty. The authorities objected that the Rambam himself rules that if one claims wheat and the defendant responds, “I do not know whether it was wheat or barley,” the claimant receives only the barley amount. How then could the Maggid Mishneh say that the Rambam follows his teacher Rabbi Yosef HaLevi? More briefly, if certainty alone prevailed, why would any oath be required? In every place where we truly say that certainty prevails over uncertainty, the claimant should recover without an oath. The answer is that Rabbi Yosef HaLevi is also speaking about a rabbinic enactment. Because one party knows and the other does not, the Sages required the party who knows to swear and then collect, but only where there is already a concrete monetary basis. The general Talmudic rule remains that in a case of certainty against uncertainty the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, even where the defendant’s uncertainty is connected with negligence. The classic case of an animal that was borrowed or rented proves this. If the custodian does not know whether the animal that died was borrowed or rented, there can hardly be greater negligence than failing to know the status under which he held it, yet the Gemara still rules that he is exempt. The Ramban himself raises this point. Rabbi Yosef HaLevi therefore distinguishes. The Sages enacted that where one party is certain and the other uncertain, certainty can prevail after an oath only where there is a concrete basis for liability. Where there is no such basis, they made no enactment. In the borrowed versus rented animal case there is no prior concrete monetary basis of the sort required, as the Rashba and the Ran in Shevuot explain. The wheat and barley case likewise lies outside the enactment. Thus, as a matter of strict law, certainty does not automatically prevail even where there is some monetary connection. The result here comes entirely from the special enactment, and the apparent contradictions disappear.
