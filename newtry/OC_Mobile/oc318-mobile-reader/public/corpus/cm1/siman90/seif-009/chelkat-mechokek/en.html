But if she lent from nichsei melog and died, etc. The reason is that she acted improperly by lending away the principal of her nichsei melog, because the principal is under the husband's rights. This is no better than a sale, where the husband can remove the property from the purchasers. So writes the Rosh. This implies that if she possessed nichsei melog in which the husband had no right to consume the fruits, as explained above in siman 85, she would be permitted to lend them to whomever she wished. In that case the debt might properly be considered prospective with respect to the husband, like a loan already owed to her by others before marriage. One can reject this, however. Since marriage gives the husband the status of a purchaser, even if he has no right to the fruits, he still inherits her upon her death. She therefore has no right to defeat that inheritance and turn presently held property into prospective property against his will. Wherever she lacks authority to sell, she likewise lacks authority to lend so as to make the property prospective. Rashba's statement that if she lends property over which the husband has no rights, the husband does not inherit that loan, refers to a case where he wrote to her, "I have no claim or dealings regarding your property," so that she is permitted to sell it. If she can sell it, she can likewise lend it. See Bach.
