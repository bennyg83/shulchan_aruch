So too, the owner's custodian. Rabbi Akiva Eiger writes that even where the custodian is not personally liable to pay for the utensils and therefore is not financially interested, he must nevertheless swear; see Teshuvot Chut HaShani, siman 7. His wording implies that the custodian's oath may be relied upon even when the custodian is interested. Shaar HaMishpat, however, cites Teshuvot Mahari HaLevi, siman 23, who writes that the custodian's oath is effective only when the owner does not suspect the custodian of having stolen the items. If the owner suspects him, the custodian cannot be the one who swears and enables recovery because he is interested. Shaar HaMishpat questions why this should differ from the owner himself, who is plainly interested yet is believed. He suggests that where the owner himself distrusts the custodian, it is improper to remove money from the alleged robber on the basis of that custodian's oath. According to this reasoning, even if the owner does not suspect him but the custodian would have to pay and is therefore interested, one could not rely on his oath. This requires study.
