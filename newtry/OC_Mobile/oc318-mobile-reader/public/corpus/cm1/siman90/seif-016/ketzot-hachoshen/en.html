Beit Shmuel writes that if a man says, "You are betrothed to me with this ring," and witnesses do not see the actual transfer but a moment later see the ring in the woman's hand under circumstances that make transfer virtually certain, the kiddushin can be valid according to the view that kiddushin testimony is treated like monetary testimony. Tumim objects that in monetary law witnesses serve primarily to establish truth, while in kiddushin the Torah requires witnesses as a constitutive condition of the act. Even certain knowledge that the transfer occurred should not substitute for actual witnesses. This objection is not decisive. Ran explains the view that Rabbi Eliezer recognizes signatures on a get even though delivery witnesses are the ones who effect the divorce. The signatures work because once the signed get is seen in the woman's hands, the witnesses to the signatures effectively establish with certainty that the husband delivered it. They are therefore treated like delivery witnesses. This shows that sufficiently direct evidentiary knowledge can count as witnessing the operative act. Nevertheless, Beit Shmuel's precise case is still doubtful. At the moment the ring was given, neither the man nor woman knew that witnesses were observing enough to establish the transfer. This resembles kiddushin performed while witnesses stand behind a fence, where even if they later know what happened, the parties did not act in the presence of witnesses and may not have intended legally complete kiddushin. With a signed get, by contrast, the husband knows the document itself will make the delivery provable once it is in her possession. That awareness makes the act equivalent to delivery before witnesses. Thus certain circumstantial proof can be testimony, but kiddushin may still fail where the parties themselves acted as though no witnesses were present.
