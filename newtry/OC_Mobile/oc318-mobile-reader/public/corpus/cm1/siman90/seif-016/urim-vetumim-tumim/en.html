The injured party, etc. I have room to doubt whether here too he is believed to say that he already paid for the injury. The plain implication of the authorities, who do not state that the injured party must have warned him, “Do not repay me except before witnesses,” suggests otherwise, for if repayment were a valid defense he should be believed by a migo that he could have said, “I paid you,” just as in the case of one who was robbed above. This is especially suggested by Mahari, who was asked about one who admitted that he struck another but claimed that the other began against him, and Mahari ruled that he was believed by a migo that he could have denied striking him at all. He does not mention any migo that he could have said that he paid him. This suggests that even if there were witnesses to the injury, the defendant would not be believed to say that he paid, perhaps because the claim is like an obligation whose time has not yet arrived, since until beit din assesses how much he must pay, he does not know what or how much to pay. Yet this creates the difficulty raised by the Ramah, that the Mishnah could have taught a case where one says to his fellow, “I injured you and paid you,” where he would be believed, but if there are witnesses he would not be believed. Perhaps the Ramah holds that injuries are uncommon, as stated above in siman 1, whereas ordinary robberies are common, although robbery committed through an injury is uncommon according to Tosafot. The Raah writes there that the Mishnah does not choose an uncommon case when it can state a common one. Thus the Ramah’s difficulty is only from ordinary robbery and not from injury, and in truth one who caused an injury is not believed to say that he paid. Nor can one answer that repayment of an injury is uncommon but that if he claims it he is nevertheless believed, while he lacks a migo because we do not say a migo based on an uncommon claim. If so, how did the Ramban prove from the Ramah’s ruling and from the passage in Ketubot that a robber is believed to say that he returned the item in the case analogous to the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba, where there are witnesses who saw the object? Perhaps repayment is itself uncommon and therefore no such migo exists. I later found in the Tosefta to Shevuot, and likewise in the Yerushalmi, that Rabbi Yehudah says: so long as they are still striking one another, the injured party swears and collects; after some time, if one says, “You injured me,” and the other says, “I did not injure you,” they are like ordinary litigants. Perhaps the meaning is that while they are still in the quarrel, the defendant is presumed not yet to have paid and therefore has no migo; afterward, however, he can say, “I paid you,” and therefore has a migo, so they are like ordinary litigants. This tends toward what I wrote, that he is believed to claim repayment and has a migo. The authorities therefore did not cite this Yerushalmi separately, because they stated the law here in the same general terms as the case of the robbed person, meaning where he lacks a migo based on repayment. This requires study.
He swears and collects. If the injured party wishes to reverse the oath, the language of Tosafot at the beginning of chapter Kol HaNishba'in implies that all those listed in Shevuot are cases where the opposing party is not fit to swear. Here too, because it is evident that he injured him and there are grounds supporting the claim, as Tosafot write in Bava Metzia 34b, the defendant is treated within the category of one suspect with respect to an oath. It is possible, however, that this is itself part of the rabbinic enactment. If the defendant says, “I do not want the enactment,” perhaps he may reverse the oath. This requires study.
However, I have a difficulty. As stated above in siman 34, one who raises his hand to strike another is disqualified from testimony and from taking an oath, and certainly one who actually wounds another is so disqualified. According to Rabbi Yehudah, who requires an admission of part of the claim, why is this special enactment necessary at all? The defendant should already fall under the enactment concerning one who is suspect with respect to an oath, under which the opposing party swears and collects. One cannot answer that he paid, for that would amount to a denial of the entire claim, as Tosafot write regarding the robbed person, and heilech is not available according to the opinion that Rabbi Yehudah obligates an oath even with heilech. According to those who hold that the injured party need not have a denial of two silver ma'ot, there is no difficulty, as written above at the beginning of the siman, but according to the Rambam’s teachers there is a difficulty. Based on what was written in siman 87, that where the suspect party has one witness supporting him, the opposing party does not collect by oath, one could say that there is one witness supporting the defendant that he injured the claimant only once; nevertheless, by virtue of the special enactment for an injured party, the claimant may swear against that witness and collect. That would not derive from the rules of a suspect litigant. This answer is strained. Rather, one must say that there were no witnesses, and a person does not become legally suspect through his own admission. From here there is proof against what I wrote in siman 92, that even on the basis of the defendant’s own admission the claimant may say, “Your own mouth has answered against you that you are suspect, and I shall collect,” for if that were so there would be no need for the special enactment concerning an injured party. He could always collect by oath without this enactment. Alternatively, the injury may have been inflicted inadvertently. For purposes of liability for damages a person is always forewarned, but he does not thereby become suspect when the act was inadvertent. This resolves the matter.
And if there is an indication, etc. This is what is called testimony based on knowledge without direct sight. The author of Beit Shmuel considered the following case: a ring was in a man’s hand and he said, “You are betrothed to me with this ring.” The witnesses did not actually see the transfer, but almost immediately the ring was in her hand, such that it could not reasonably have arrived there unless he had given it to her. He reasoned that according to the view that witnesses to kiddushin do not require formal examination and interrogation because kiddushin are compared to monetary law, just as here in cases of injury we extract money on the strength of such knowledge, so too she is betrothed. See Even HaEzer 42:12. If his proof is from here, however, it is answerable. In monetary law, witnesses were created only to resolve falsehood. If the defendant admits that he injured the claimant, no witnesses are needed at all. Therefore, where the circumstances provide knowledge of this sort, we are ourselves witnesses that the matter is true, and no further sight is necessary. Kiddushin, by contrast, effected without witnesses do not take effect at all by the Torah’s decree, because the Torah required two witnesses. Even if the facts are certainly true, if no witnesses saw the act there is a divine decree that the kiddushin do not take effect. One might ask that, if so, why do we compare kiddushin to monetary law regarding examination and interrogation? Perhaps it does matter whether the witnesses know the precise day and time. The answer is that even if they cannot now answer every question of examination and interrogation, at the moment of seeing the kiddushin they did know what occurred. Kiddushin depends on the witnessing at that moment. This distinction also clarifies Tosafot in Makkot 6a, who challenge Rashi’s explanation that monetary law differs from capital law because of the verse requiring the congregation to judge and save. Tosafot ask that the Torah says there is one law for both, and answer that although monetary law also requires two witnesses and compares three witnesses to two, one witness in monetary law can at least impose an oath, unlike in capital law. Yet the question remains why the Torah itself distinguishes the cases, and one might also say that Rashi accepts Tosafot’s technical answer but supplies the reason why the Torah was stricter in capital cases, namely the special requirement to seek acquittal. It therefore appears that Tosafot understood a simpler distinction. In monetary matters, witnesses are needed only to clarify truth. If the defendant admits, he obligates himself. Thus, if three witnesses testified and one is related, leaving two valid witnesses, even though the formal testimony of all three is invalidated by their joinder, beit din nevertheless knows from the two valid witnesses that the account is true. When the matter is clarified to be true, money may be extracted even through a compelling circumstantial inference; earlier generations even extracted money on the basis of strong presumptions. Capital law is different. Even if the accused admits that he killed, he is not executed without valid witnesses. Even if beit din is certain, as in the incident of Shimon ben Shetach who said, “Either I killed him or you killed him,” his blood is not entrusted to beit din without testimony. When the disqualified witness joined the others, their testimony was void, and mere certainty is insufficient. Thus there is a fundamental difference, and the comparison of “one law” does not create a difficulty, just as we would never infer from capital law that an admission in beit din cannot create monetary liability. Rashi’s need for another reason therefore indicates that Rashi holds that Rabbi Yosi’s distinction concerning joined witnesses applies even in areas classed with monetary law where a litigant’s admission itself is insufficient, such as gittin and kiddushin, which in some respects are treated as monetary law although a matter of ervah requires two witnesses. Tosafot there discuss whether gittin and kiddushin should be treated like capital or monetary law for this purpose. According to Rashi they are monetary law, which also answers Tosafot’s question about how people could ever validly effect kiddushin in the presence of relatives. Since admission cannot substitute for testimony in these areas, Rashi had to provide another reason, namely the special requirement of saving in capital law. Tosafot objected because the Torah still says “one law,” and because even monetary law speaks of two witnesses. Their answer that one witness can impose an oath in monetary law cannot apply to those matters where the defendant’s own admission is ineffective, since there one witness would have no meaningful oath to impose. Tosafot therefore reject Rashi’s approach and hold that gittin and kiddushin are treated like capital law in this respect, as they state explicitly. Then the distinction is straightforward: monetary law needs witnesses only to establish truth, so a powerful inference can function where formal testimony is lacking. This is clear in Tosafot. Nevertheless, there is room for the Beit Shmuel’s practical ruling, as the Mordechai ruled, though not for the Beit Shmuel’s stated reason that kiddushin are compared to monetary law. Even if they are compared to capital law, Tosafot in Shevuot 34a prove from the Gemara that any sufficiently compelling inference accepted in monetary law can also be used in capital law. Thus, if the pattern of a bite on a person’s back is enough to impose monetary liability for injury, the same sort of evidence could establish in a capital case that the victim became a tereifah through a penetrating injury, where the circumstances amount to having witnessed the killing. The witnesses can then be examined as to the day and hour when those circumstances occurred. The same can therefore apply to kiddushin, and the Mordechai properly ruled that the kiddushin are valid according to all opinions. The Rashba, who disagrees, does not accept Tosafot’s resolution. See the discussion in Beit Shmuel on Even HaEzer at length. Radbaz, responsum 179, writes that where a robbed person dies and his heirs say, “Our father instructed us that he was robbed and had not been repaid,” the special enactment for a robbed person was not extended to heirs. Nevertheless, if an heir claims with certainty, “You robbed my father of a maneh,” he swears and collects, not by virtue of the Mishnah’s enactment, but because the opposing party is suspect regarding an oath. If the heir does not claim with certainty, they may impose a ban. His words require study. From his formulation that their father instructed them that he had not been repaid, it appears that there were no witnesses who saw the property, for where there are no such witnesses the special oath of the robbed person does not apply at all, as explained several times above in this siman. Moreover, when he writes that the heirs make a definite claim, he must mean that they themselves know that the defendant took that amount, for otherwise how can they swear and collect from one who is suspect? An oath merely that “our father did not instruct us otherwise” cannot suffice to collect from a suspect litigant. Such a proposition should be rejected entirely. If instead he means that they personally saw him take the property, it is difficult why they should be worse than a guardian or the guardian’s wife, who may swear and collect. Why should the guardian’s sons or heirs be unable to do so? This requires study.
