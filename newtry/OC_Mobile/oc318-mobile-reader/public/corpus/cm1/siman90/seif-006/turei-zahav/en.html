(Seif 11, and he says, “They are purchased”) Beit Yosef cites Sefer HaTerumot that if he claims, “You delivered them to me as security for my debt,” this is equivalent to claiming, “They are purchased in my possession.” I am surprised that he did not cite an even broader ruling stated there, gate 49, seif 9: even the claim, “I seized them myself as security,” has the same status, and he is believed wherever he would be believed to say, “I purchased them.” The Rambam likewise rules in chapter 7 of the Laws of Robbery that although he admits taking the pledge without authorization, if there are no witnesses he takes a stringent oath and retains the pledge. It is clear to me that the same applies if he says, “They were deposited with me by you,” for he is believed by virtue of a migo that he could say, “I purchased them.” Although we generally presume that whatever is in a person’s possession belongs to him, a migo may still establish such a claim. The Talmud and authorities repeatedly state that one who holds property without witnesses is believed through a migo that he could have denied the entire event. The same applies here where witnesses are present, provided he has a migo of purchase. The Tur writes: “Therefore all these distinctions likewise apply where one entered another’s premises to take a pledge. Wherever the person carrying out the articles is not believed here,” etc. Sema asked that this implies that if there is no defect in the circumstances of the person leaving with the articles, he is believed even though he entered to take a pledge and now claims purchase, whereas at the beginning of the siman, if he says, “The articles you saw me remove are mine,” the alleged robber is never believed. Sema follows his own interpretation that “they are mine” means, “They were deposited in your house.” He therefore distinguishes between a claim of purchase and a claim of prior ownership. This is astonishing. If so, even on the claim “they are mine,” he should be believed through a migo of purchase. Moreover, where he did not enter expressly to take a pledge, it is obvious that he is believed when saying, “They are mine,” for this is no weaker than saying, “I seized them for my debt.” The Tur expressly writes that wherever the person removing the articles is believed here, one who entered to take a pledge is also believed. The Tur’s intent is simple. Any claim that contradicts the witnesses, in the manner I explained in seif 1, causes the person to be deemed a robber by virtue of the testimony; in that case the dispossessed owner swears and collects. But where the claim does not contradict the witnesses, such as “I purchased it,” and there is no defect in the manner of removal, the holder is believed. Consequently, he is likewise believed to say, “They are mine and were deposited with you,” through a migo of purchase. Even the claim, “I seized them myself for my debt,” does not render him a robber where there are no witnesses that he entered to take a pledge. The only distinction concerns the oath required from the dispossessed owner when he is believed. According to Rabbi Yosef Halevi, where the alleged robber says, “I took them as payment,” or “They were deposited with you,” and is disbelieved because the manner of removal is suspicious, the owner must take a stringent oath, because the other party accuses him of robbing many articles. Here, by contrast, where the possessor says only that a particular object was lent to him and the manner of removal is suspicious, the owner need take only a hesset oath and may recover that object. This appears to me to be the Tur’s view. I have seen other interpretations, but they are not correct in my opinion. Maggid Mishneh, however, appears to disagree with the Tur. In chapter 4 of the Laws of Robbery he writes: “There is indeed a distinction between one who enters expressly to take a pledge and thereby reveals his purpose, and one who enters without such declaration. One who reveals that he came to take a pledge cannot claim that the articles were purchased, and this is agreed upon.” This implies that even where there is no suspicious manner of removal, so that one who entered without declaration would be believed, one who entered expressly to take a pledge is not believed to claim purchase. This also appears to be the Mechaber’s view, for he did not cite the Tur’s ruling that all these distinctions apply to one who entered to take a pledge, and Rama likewise omitted it. This appears correct to me.
