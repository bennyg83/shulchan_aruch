Because he is suspected, etc. One may ask why the Mechaber does not simply say that the robbed person’s claim is uncertain and that an oath is not imposed upon an uncertain claim. We cannot answer that this is like the case of an open chest, where the owner may make a definite claim that a specified amount is missing from the house, as the Sema writes in seif katan 15, because the Mechaber himself did not accept that view above at the end of siman 75. It is the Rama who cites the authorities who hold that way. In the Rambam, which is the source of this ruling, the matter is clear. His ruling continues the preceding case in which the worker or household member of the owner was present in the house. The owner therefore makes a definite claim on the basis of that person’s report. Were the defendant not suspected, he could be required to swear in response to the statement of the owner’s worker or household member. Since he is suspected, however, he cannot take the oath, while the robbed person cannot himself swear and collect because the robbed person did not personally know the facts, and the reporting witness cannot swear in order to enable the owner to collect. The owner therefore loses. The Mechaber, however, states the law as an independent clause, which suggests that it is not necessarily dependent upon the preceding case. Perhaps he reasoned as follows. The Maggid Mishneh, in halakhah 12 there, is uncertain whether the Rambam holds that a report by a single witness can itself make the owner’s claim sufficiently definite to generate an oath. If this case conclusively depended upon such a principle, the Maggid Mishneh should have resolved his doubt from here. Therefore the Mechaber may have understood the Rambam as speaking of a different scenario. The defendant himself says, “It is true that I took twenty utensils, but they were mine on deposit,” or, “I took them for my debt.” The owner now knows with certainty that there was no such deposit and no such debt. On the defendant’s own account the owner therefore has a definite claim. There is no Torah oath in that case, because the owner did not initially make a definite claim. If he acquired certainty only after the defendant’s admission, then the admission preceded the definite claim. But heset can still apply once a definite monetary denial now exists. The Rambam, wishing to explain why the owner nevertheless cannot recover through any oath mechanism, says that the defendant is suspected. This interpretation is possible but strained. It therefore appears preferable to retain the first explanation: the owner’s claim is made definite through the report of the worker or household member. The Maggid Mishneh’s uncertainty concerns only whether such a report can create a Torah oath. The Rambam may nevertheless agree that it is sufficient to require heset. This distinction is straightforward. This also produces a further clear rule. Where the owner’s worker or household member actually testifies that the defendant took the property, and the defendant says not, “I took no more,” but rather, “I took it for my debt,” he must return the property without an oath. Once the worker or household member is accepted as a single witness for this purpose, the defendant is not contradicting the witness as to the taking. A single witness whom the defendant does not contradict has the force of two witnesses concerning the admitted fact. As explained above, where one witness testifies that the defendant took property and the defendant admits the taking but says he took it for a debt, he must return it. This is simple.
