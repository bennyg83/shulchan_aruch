Likewise, if she had a loan in the hands of others when he married her. Mordechai at the beginning of chapter Yesh Nochlin writes that where a loan is secured by a pledge, everyone agrees that a firstborn takes a double share because a creditor acquires a pledge. See Choshen Mishpat 278:7. It appears that all those rules apply here as well. A husband inherits a loan secured by a pledge because it is treated as already collected, even if the pledge was taken at the time of the loan. If the wife had a debt owed by her husband before marriage, and he later married her and she died, it would seem that the debt is actual property in his possession and he therefore inherits it, even though regarding a firstborn Choshen Mishpat 278 leaves unresolved the case where the father had a loan in the hands of the firstborn. There the uncertainty is whether the firstborn takes a double share because the money is already under his control, or does not because he inherits it from his father and it never came into the father's possession. Consequently, the firstborn does not take the extra half-share out of doubt. But that case is different because Scripture requires that the property be "found with him," meaning the father. Here, however, it should not be called prospective when the money is physically in the husband's possession. Nevertheless, Responsa Rashba cited by Beit Yosef rules that the husband inherits only half, as in the firstborn case. See there also the distinctions between a pledge belonging to a gentile and one belonging to a Jew, and between a place where land pledged as security can be redeemed and one where it cannot. If the wife's nichsei melog were stolen and not returned until after her death, that is prospective property. If land was robbed from her, land cannot halachically be stolen in the same manner. Likewise regarding stolen books, see Choshen Mishpat in the cited siman. Wages earned but not collected before death are also called prospective.
