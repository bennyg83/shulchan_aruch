He therefore swears, etc. The Rambam writes in Hilkhot Gezelah: if one witness testifies that the defendant entered and took utensils under his cloak but does not know what they were, and the defendant says, “I did not rob any utensils,” or says, “I took them for my debt,” then since the witness does not know what was under his cloak, the defendant swears while holding a sacred object that he did not rob, etc. Regarding the first clause, if “I did not rob” means that he flatly contradicts the witness and says he took nothing, it is obvious that he must swear in order to contradict the single witness. The Bach appears to infer that the Rambam’s conclusion, “he swears that he did not rob,” refers only to that first clause, while in the second clause, where he admits taking the objects for a debt, he does not actually swear. This is difficult. Why would the Rambam present two alternative cases at the beginning and then conclude with a ruling addressing only one, leaving the second wholly unresolved? If he intended no ruling on the second case, he should not have introduced it. It therefore appears more likely that “I did not rob” does not mean “I did not take.” Had that been the intent, the Rambam should have written “I did not take.” Rather, it means, “I did not commit robbery, because what I took was mine.” Thus the first plea is, “I did not rob, for I took my own property,” and it is legally parallel to the second plea, “I took the property for my debt.” The Sema likewise understands the passage this way. The explanation is that the witness necessarily establishes that the defendant took at least one utensil. The Maggid Mishneh writes that this is why the Rambam specifies that the witness does not know what the utensils were. If the witness knew exactly what the defendant took, and the defendant admitted the taking while claiming the object was his, he would have to return it. A single witness whom the defendant does not contradict has the force of two witnesses for that fact, and with two witnesses such a claim cannot retain the object. Therefore, as to the smallest possible utensil necessarily encompassed by the witness’s testimony, the defendant must return it without dispute. The entire controversy concerns the additional utensils regarding which there is no witness testimony and where the special rabbinic enactment ordinarily allows the robbed person to swear and collect. That enactment was not extended to a case supported by only one witness. This also explains why the Rambam stresses that the witness does not know the exact contents. The Maggid Mishneh asks that even if the witness did know, the defendant could still contradict him. According to what was explained, the defendant’s phrase “I did not rob” does not contradict the witness at all. It means only, “I took what was mine.” The issue is therefore precisely whether the witness identifies the property, because identification would obligate return of that amount. Accordingly, the Rambam must hold that one who becomes obligated through the necessary implication of his own plea can be treated as one who admits part, even though verbally he denies the entire claim. Here the defendant’s own position, together with the witness, necessarily obligates him at least for the smallest utensil. Although his words are framed as a total denial, the legal consequence of his plea is that he must return some property. He is therefore equivalent to one who admits part of the claim, and a Torah oath applies regarding the remainder. That is why the Rambam requires an oath while holding a sacred object. There is no exemption of heilech. Even according to those who treat a deposit as heilech, that is only where the defendant admits the deposit. Where he denies the claimant’s ownership, there is no heilech, as I explained at length above in siman 75. Certainly according to the Rambam, who holds that a deposit is not automatically heilech in any event, no such exemption exists. The Rambam’s ruling is therefore exact: the defendant takes a Torah type oath while holding a sacred object. Initially I thought that “I did not rob” meant “I did not take,” and that the oath in the first clause was simply the Torah oath imposed to contradict a single witness, while the oath in the second clause, “I took them for my debt,” was the later Geonic oath imposed upon one who seizes property for a debt. I withdrew from that explanation. The two rulings would then be fundamentally different: in the first case the defendant swears and pays nothing, while in the second he would have to swear and in any event return at least the smallest utensil necessarily established by the witness, as explained above and as appears from the Maggid Mishneh. It is much more coherent to read both clauses as one legal category, as in the first explanation. The Tur, however, writes that the defendant takes only a heset oath. He too must understand “I did not rob” as the Sema does, meaning, “I took my own property.” Otherwise the first case would plainly require a Torah oath to contradict the single witness. The difficulty is therefore why the Tur does not consider the defendant obligated in part through the necessary consequence of his own plea and thus subject to the Torah oath of one who admits part. The Perishah writes that according to the Tur even the smallest possible utensil need not be returned on the strength of the single witness. Although two witnesses would compel return, one witness would not. This cannot be accepted. The Tur uses the Rambam’s own formulation that the witness “does not know what they were.” If a single witness never required return of anything, it would make no difference whether he knew the identity of the utensils or not. The very limitation shows, as the Maggid Mishneh explains, that whatever the witness specifically identifies must be returned. Therefore the original question remains. We must say either that the Tur holds that becoming obligated through the implication of one’s own plea is not called an admission of part, as discussed above in siman 75, or that he holds that even though the defendant denies ownership, the smallest utensil is treated like a deposit and therefore like heilech. The admitted or legally established portion would then not generate an oath. The latter possibility is difficult, but it is available according to those who broadly apply heilech to a deposit. According to the Rambam, however, no deposit is heilech automatically, so his requirement of the stronger oath is consistent. As for why the defendant who says, “I took it for my debt,” is not required to take the special Geonic oath imposed upon a person who seizes property for a debt, the Sema answers that the defendant claims the owner transferred the property to him absolutely in payment of the debt. This does not seem correct. If so, the plea is simply an outright purchase or transfer, and the Sema himself in seif katan 5 distinguishes a debt seizure from a purchase. On his explanation there would be no meaningful distinction. Rather, the Ran’s distinction should be used. The Geonic oath applies where the artisan or possessor is the one demanding payment from the owner. If the owner is the claimant and the possessor merely defends himself by saying that he holds the property against a debt, the Geonic oath was not instituted in that form. Here the alleged robber remains passive and the robbed person is the one making the claim. Therefore that special oath does not arise. As a matter of practical law, the stronger oath while holding a sacred object appears correct, because the Rambam states it explicitly, while the explanations needed to reconcile the Tur’s lesser heset oath are strained.
