How does the robbed person collect? Knesset HaGedolah cites Radvaz that if the robbed person dies before suing and his heirs say with certainty that their father told them he was robbed of a particular amount, while witnesses saw the robbery but not the amount, the heirs can swear and collect. I am surprised. Even the robbed person himself could collect only by taking the special oath. If he died without swearing, how can heirs inherit a right that was never collectible without his oath? Tur and Shulchan Aruch in siman 108 expressly say that heirs of an employee or robbed person do not swear and collect. There is, however, one exception. If the son of the robbed person personally saw the robbery and knows its amount with certainty, he can take the severe oath and collect. Even during the father's lifetime, where the father himself was absent from the event but the son saw it, the son can serve as the person with direct knowledge for the special enactment. All the more after the father's death, the son inherits the underlying right and has the direct factual knowledge necessary to swear. Other heirs, such as a brother or sister who did not occupy that position during the original event, cannot acquire the special oath right merely by inheritance. Thus the rule differs from the employee case because in robbery the son can sometimes stand in the evidentiary place of the robbed person himself.
The householder swears while holding a sacred object. The shepherd case in Bava Metzia at first seems to fall under the special enactment for a robbed person. Witnesses proved he had consumed two animals. Why did Rabbi Zeira seek to impose Rabbi Chiya's oath on the remainder instead of simply letting the owner take the robbed person's oath and collect? The answer is that the robbed person's enactment applies where the uncertainty itself arose before the witnesses at the time of the wrongful act. They saw the wrongdoer remove property but did not know how much. There is then an objective monetary uncertainty independent of the parties' later claims, and the Sages allowed the victim to swear and collect. In the shepherd case the witnesses saw only two animals eaten. The owner's assertion that additional animals were taken before or afterward creates a new uncertainty solely through the parties' claims. The special enactment was not made for such a case. Therefore Rabbi Chiya's partial witness oath is needed. This also explains the baraita concerning an injured person. So long as the parties are actually striking one another and witnesses see the event but cannot determine the full extent, the injured person can swear and collect. A later claim that an additional injury occurred at some earlier or later time is treated as an ordinary claim. Under our practical law, where the witnesses saw him whole and immediately afterward injured, the objective uncertainty itself is enough and the injured person's enactment applies.
