How does the robbed person proceed? See Shach seif katan 1, through his citation of Lechem Mishneh's question. At first glance this is the same as Tosafot's question in Shevuot 46 concerning one who claims a utensil, where they ask that this is simply Rabbi Yehudah's rule. Tosafot answer that the case is one in which the defendant already returned one item. The Shach could not use that answer according to Rabbi Yehudah. Rabbi Yehudah requires a partial admission. If one item had already been returned, the defendant would already be disqualified as a robber, and the opposing party would swear and collect even without the special enactment for a robbed person. The case must therefore be one of heilech, for Rabbi Yehudah obligates even with heilech although he otherwise requires a partial admission, as is evident from the worker sugya. According to the Sages, however, Tosafot's answer is necessary: if the case were heilech, the question that this is merely Rabbi Yehudah would remain. Thus for the Sages the case is where he already returned one item, while for Rabbi Yehudah the Shach's explanation, that it is heilech, is required.
And he does not know what they are. See Shach seif katan 50, who writes that the defendant must return the items without an oath. He says this only according to Rif and the Mechaber. The Tur holds that an oath is required and that the owner claims the items were stolen, as Shach writes in seif katan 7. The Shach further writes that if the items were not seen, the defendant is believed through a migo. Tumim objects: what migo is this? Even if the defendant could have claimed that he returned the very items witnesses saw him take, and would have been believed, the robbed person would still swear and collect regarding additional items that he says were also taken, because Tosafot hold that even after some items were returned the robbed person can swear and collect. There is no difficulty. The defendant has a broader migo. He could have admitted the claimant's entire account, that he took all the alleged items, and then said that he returned all of them. In that case he would certainly be exempt, because even if witnesses saw him take all the items claimed, one who robs before witnesses need not return before witnesses. Therefore he is believed when he says that some of the items the witnesses saw him take were his own. Once beit din accepts his claim through the migo that his own property was taken, he is not judicially established as a robber at all. The Sages penalized only a person who stands before beit din as a robber. If he is believed by migo and thus never acquires that status, the claimant does not receive the special right to swear and collect concerning additional items. Where the items were seen and the defendant lacks a migo, however, the items are removed from him and he is established as a robber with respect to the witnessed taking. The robbed person may then swear and collect even for additional items not seen by the witnesses. Ketzot HaChoshen correctly writes that the enactment applies only where the dispute itself has a derara demamona created independently of the parties' pleadings, such as witnesses who saw a robbery but do not know how much was taken. It does not extend to wholly unrelated claims. On this point his analysis is correct.
The owner swears while holding a sacred object and collects. See Shach seif katan 7. The Shach found the Tur difficult: if, where the defendant did not enter to seize collateral but there are several suspicious circumstances, the owner takes only a shevuat heset, then certainly when he did enter to seize collateral and there is an additional suspicious circumstance, why should the owner require the more severe oath while holding a sacred object? Bach answers that the Tur's severe oath concerns a defendant who says, "I took what was mine." The Shach answers that the case is where the owner says the property was stolen. If there was no entry to seize collateral, such a theft claim would be believed only against one already established as a thief. Here the very entry to seize collateral supplies the needed basis. The Shach's additional statement, that only in the law of the robbed person did the Sages establish an oath concerning items the witnesses did not see, while here the robbery itself is known, is another explanation of the Tur. The Tur's phrase that "this is what stands between them" means that the robbed person needs the severe oath only regarding items not seen by the witnesses, while as to items the witnesses actually saw he need not swear. In my view Sema's position can be defended against all the Shach's objections. The Shach asks why one who says, "I took it for my debt," should not be believed, since that claim does not contradict the chazakah. The objection is indeed strong if the chazakah means that no debt exists, because then even the claim, "You delivered it to me for my debt," would contradict the same chazakah. Rather, with respect to the very items the witnesses saw, the defendant can be believed to say, "I took them for my debt," by a migo that he purchased them, because such a debt claim does not inherently contradict a chazakah, as Baal HaTerumot and the other poskim cited by the Shach write. But that credibility does not prevent the robbed person from swearing and collecting with respect to additional items that the defendant denies taking. Even if the defendant is believed through migo that the witnessed items were taken for a debt, he admits that he seized property without permission to satisfy a debt and is therefore a robber. The Sages penalized a robber by allowing the robbed person to swear and collect concerning the disputed additional items. Indeed, even if the claimant himself admits that a genuine debt existed, or the defendant holds a document proving the debt, unauthorized self-help seizure can still give him the status relevant to the enactment. The robbed person can swear and collect regarding the additional disputed items where there is derara demamona. This is certainly so where the defendant says, "I took my own property," but lacks a migo. Once witnesses establish a taking that beit din treats as robbery, the robbed person swears and collects. Therefore, when the defendant admits the witnessed taking and merely justifies it as collection of a debt, the special rule should also apply to the remaining disputed items. The Shach appears to disagree only because he understood Sema to deny the migo even as to the witnessed objects themselves. On that point the Shach is correct: the defendant can be believed regarding the witnessed objects. But as to additional items he denies taking, even the Shach should agree that the robbed person may swear and collect if the defendant's own story leaves him as one who seized collateral unlawfully. If, however, the debt arose from wages or another claim that legally permits seizure of collateral, so that even according to the defendant's story he is not a robber, and he has a migo supporting that story, then the robbed person does not receive the special oath. The Shach's criticism of Sema concerning a defendant who says, "I took my own property," is tied to the Shach's broader assumption that if the items later were not seen, the robbed person cannot swear and collect even for additional items. Sema apparently does not accept that assumption. The requirement that the items be seen is stated by the poskim only to remove the witnessed items themselves from the defendant despite his migo. It is not necessarily a condition for the robbed person's special oath concerning additional property. This is consistent with siman 99, where a borrower who says that property in his possession belongs to another is not believed through migo because the migo stands against the chazakah that what a person possesses is his. There the opposing party also has a monetary chazakah. Here, where the witnessed taking itself is established, the analysis differs. Still, if one snatches from another before witnesses and later, when the item is no longer seen, says, "I took what was mine," the later migo that he could have said he returned it may prevent extraction of that particular item, but it cannot retroactively remove his status as suspect for an oath. At the moment of the witnessed snatching he had no migo and was established as a robber if his justification is not accepted. A later migo cannot retroactively qualify him. Otherwise even where the item was seen and beit din removed it from him, he could later seize it without witnesses and acquire a migo that would retroactively restore his credibility, which is untenable. Moreover, the later migo itself does not clear his prior status, because its alternative claim is "I returned it." Even if he had returned the robbery, a robber remains disqualified until the legal requirements of restoration and repentance are met. Therefore, once he is certainly a robber, the Sages' penalty allowing the robbed person to swear and collect concerning the additional disputed items remains even if the robber later acquires a migo. Otherwise, even where the witnessed item is seen, the additional items that the claimant says were taken were by definition not seen, and the robber could always assert the same alternative of return. The enactment would collapse. The distinction is where the defendant already had a migo at the moment of the witnessed event, such as one seen entering empty-handed and leaving laden with utensils, where he can say they were purchased, or that they were his, supported by a purchase migo. In that case the witnesses never establish him as a robber. Since he never receives robber status before beit din, there is no basis for the special penalty. Therefore the poskim and Mechaber require "seeing" only in seif 11 for purposes of extracting the very utensils witnessed, where the defendant combines possession with migo. They do not state that seeing is a condition in the earlier rule allowing the robbed person to swear and collect concerning unobserved items. Thus Sema's core position is sound. A migo can protect the defendant from losing the specific utensils in his possession, but a later migo against a chazakah does not necessarily erase his status as a suspect robber and therefore does not prevent the robbed person from swearing concerning other items. On one point, however, the Shach is primary: a purchase migo that already existed at the time of the observed possession can establish that he took his own property and prevent him from becoming suspect at all. But a mere later migo of return does not accomplish that. Tumim reaches the same conclusion, and this is the principal rule. The Shach's proof from the herdsman case is not compelling, because there the witnesses actually saw a forcible seizure that appeared as robbery, whereas here the witnesses did not see the act of snatching itself and the defendant can explain his possession as a purchase.
That the defendant takes a shevuat heset. See Shach seif katan 10, who holds that one is not deemed disqualified merely because he says, "I took what was mine," or "I took it for my debt." Although one who snatches before witnesses and says, "I snatched my own," is ordinarily disqualified when he is not believed, here the case concerns one who entered to seize collateral for a known debt. Even though he is not believed to say that he abandoned his original purpose and took only his own property, we do not regard him as more culpable than one who takes collateral without permission, who is not disqualified because people do not perceive the prohibition clearly. This is also evident from Shach seif katan 1, where the reason he does not become disqualified is that people do not perceive the conduct as prohibited. But where the debt itself is not known, all agree that he becomes suspect if he says, "I took my own," or "I took it for my debt," and his claims are not believed.
