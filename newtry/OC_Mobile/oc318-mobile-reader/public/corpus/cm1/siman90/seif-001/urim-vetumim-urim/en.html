Witnesses saw him, etc. This means that the homeowner did not authorize him to enter and take collateral, and he likewise had no legal authority to seize collateral without permission, as explained below in siman 97. He is therefore deemed a robber. Even if the homeowner owes him money and he took the property toward the debt, Rambam holds, and the Mechaber rules below in siman 354, that he is a robber. See Tumim, seif katan 1. I asked that if so, he is suspect concerning an oath, while witnesses testify against him concerning part of the claim, which should obligate him to swear under the rule of Rabbi Chiyya's first teaching. I gave several resolutions there. See there.
To take collateral without permission. Rambam and Tur write that this is a penalty imposed by the Sages upon the robber. Therefore, Shach writes that if the robber dies, the penalty was not imposed on his son, requiring the son to pay whatever amount the robbed person swears to and collects. A penalty is not imposed on the offender's son after him. This differs from the enactment for a hired worker, where the employer's son remains liable, because that rule is not a penalty.
And they do not know what they were. They must at least know that he took an object. If they do not know whether he took an object or merely stones and dirt, the claimant does not have the special status of a robbed person at all. So write Sema and Shach.
Whether he says, "I never entered," etc. This language also appears in Tur. Sema asks that Tur holds that when a defendant contradicts witnesses by saying he never entered at all, and witnesses testify that he did enter, even if they did not see him take any objects, he is established as a denier contradicted by witnesses, and the robbed person swears and collects. Sema answers that "I never entered" means, "I never entered to take collateral, but entered for other matters." This is strained. The straightforward reading is the reverse. Tur means that one might have thought that when he contradicts the witnesses and is established by them as a false denier, and they also saw him taking things, the robbed person should be believed without an oath. Tur therefore teaches that he nevertheless collects only with an oath.
In all these cases, etc. Regarding objects that the witnesses actually saw him take, the robbed person need not swear at all, provided the taker has no migo that he returned them. If he does have such a migo, the robbed person cannot collect without an oath, and it requires study whether he may collect even with an oath. This applies where the taker claims in beit din that he took his own property. If he claims that he took nothing, what migo does he have? Saying he took nothing contradicts the witnesses, and he cannot then claim that he returned it, because one who says, "I did not take," is equivalent to saying, "I did not return," nor may he claim that he purchased it. See Tumim.
He swears. See Tumim, seif katan 2. Even if the taker returned what the witnesses testify that he took, the robbed person still swears and collects concerning the remainder.
While holding a sacred object. Shach holds that if there is testimony that the claimant currently sees some of the objects in the taker's possession, such testimony is not required with respect to every object. Once the taker has been established by witnesses as a robber concerning some of them, no further evidence of present possession is required for the rest, and the robbed person swears and collects. See Tumim, seif katan 2, where I wrote that this does not appear correct. With respect to every object that the robbed person claims and seeks to collect by oath, there must be evidence that prevents the taker from claiming, "I returned it." It need not literally be witnesses who presently see the object. Any circumstance that prevents a return claim suffices, such as a warning, "Do not return it to me except before witnesses," or the like. But if the taker could have claimed that he returned the object, the Sages did not institute that the robbed person collect it by oath. This applies where the taker is not independently obligated in a Torah oath, for example because there was no partial admission by his own mouth or testimony creating such an oath, as where what he admits is immediately tendered. But if he is subject to a Torah oath, then because he is suspect, as stated in seif 5, the robbed person swears and collects even with respect to objects concerning which the robber has a migo. See there.
Whatever he claims. Sema holds that this applies specifically when the taker says, "I took nothing," or, "I took something, but it was mine because it had been deposited with you," or, "I took it as collateral for my debt." In all those cases, the robbed person swears and collects under all circumstances. But if the taker claims, "I purchased the objects from you in your house when I entered saying I intended to take collateral," Tur requires all the conditions mentioned in seif 11: the homeowner is not accustomed to selling his possessions, the manner of carrying the objects is unusual, and the other conditions stated there. If any of those conditions is absent, the robbed person does not swear and collect. Thus, the Mechaber's unqualified wording here does not contradict Tur, and Sema considers Tur's view authoritative. Shach disagrees. He understands Tur to require the conditions of seif 11 regardless of what the taker claims. Without them, according to Tur, the robbed person never swears and collects. Therefore, since the Mechaber ruled here without qualification, it follows that the law does not follow Tur, as Shach himself writes in questioning Tur. See Tumim, seif katan 3, where I concluded that Sema's view is correct with respect to a claim of "I took nothing" or "It was my property deposited with you," for these do not require the later conditions. Regarding a claim, "I took it for my debt," it remains doubtful whether Sema is correct that Tur agrees even there, and this requires study. There is also room to question Tur himself: when he requires the conditions below, does he require them even for the objects that witnesses actually saw, or only for additional objects that the witnesses did not see? In the former category he may certainly have to return the objects, while for what the claimant seeks beyond the witnesses' testimony the later conditions are necessary. See there, where I agreed with this approach because Rav Hai Gaon likewise writes so.
Whatever he claims. See Tumim, seif katan 3, where I demonstrated from Rambam that this applies specifically when the witnesses did not see the act of taking inside the house. If he took the property before witnesses and says, "There were twenty objects, but they were mine," even though the witnesses did not see how many there were, the robbed person receives all of them and requires no oath at all. Here an oath is required even in the case where the taker says, "I took my own," because the witnesses did not see the actual taking. See there carefully.
That people deposit. If the robbed person admits that the object was not deposited with him but was his own, he is not believed even though he has a migo to say it was a deposit, because that would be a migo to extract property and would also run against the presumption. Maharshal writes that if he says it was a deposit, he must clarify from whom he received it so that the matter can be investigated and the depositor asked whether this is true.
He takes a hesset oath. Rav Hai Gaon holds that one does not become suspect merely by entering another's premises to seize collateral. Although he committed a prohibition, people do not regard themselves as acting dishonestly on that account.
Rather, we impose a ban. According to this view he is suspect. This is also the conclusion of most authorities, and so appears from the Mechaber below in siman 355.
