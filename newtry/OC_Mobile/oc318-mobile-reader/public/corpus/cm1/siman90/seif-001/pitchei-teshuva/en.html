So too, the owner's custodian. In Rabbi Akiva Eiger's glosses to the Shulchan Aruch he writes: "Even where the circumstances are such that the custodian is not liable to pay for the utensils and therefore has no financial interest, he must nevertheless swear. See Responsa Chut HaShani, siman 7." His words imply that the custodian's oath is effective even if he does have a financial interest. Shaar Mishpat, however, does not appear to hold so. He cites Responsa Mahari HaLevi, brother of the Taz, siman 23, who writes simply that the custodian's oath is effective only where the owner does not suspect the custodian of having stolen them. If the owner suspects the custodian, the custodian cannot swear and collect because he has a financial interest. Shaar Mishpat comments: "I do not understand how this differs from the owner himself, who swears and collects even though no one has a greater financial interest than he. Nevertheless, perhaps since the owner suspects the custodian and does not trust him, it is forbidden to take money from the robber on the basis of uncertainty by relying on the custodian's oath. This appears correct." According to this, the same would apply even where the owner does not suspect him, but the custodian is required to pay and therefore has a financial interest: one could not rely upon his oath. This requires study.
