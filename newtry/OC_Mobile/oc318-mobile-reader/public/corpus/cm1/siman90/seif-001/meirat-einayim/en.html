We also find cases where taking a pledge is permitted by authority of the Sages even against the owner's will, such as where he owes wages for the claimant's labor, animal, or utensils, as the Tur and Mechaber write below in siman 97:14. See also siman 354:6, where the Tur cites the Rambam that even one who seizes a pledge from another without authorization of beit din is called a robber, and all the more so one who enters his house to take a pledge. The Raavad disagrees and says he is not thereby called a robber, and that the Mishnah concerning one who was robbed speaks where it turns out that no debt was owed. According to the Rambam, whose view we follow as the Mechaber rules in siman 359, our case concerns one who enters to take a pledge without authorization of beit din and is therefore deemed a robber and disqualified, even if the debt itself truly exists. The owner swears while holding a sacred object and collects. See the Tur in seif 20, where he writes that even in a case of one who entered another's home to take a pledge, the owner is not always believed. Specifically, the owner must not ordinarily sell his utensils, the entrant must be a person who behaves immodestly, he must have carried the utensils concealed, and the type of utensils must not ordinarily be carried concealed. If one of these indications against the entrant is missing, the owner is not believed to swear and collect, and the entrant is believed. Yet here the Tur, Mechaber, and other authorities write without qualification that the robbed owner swears and collects. The difference must be that there the entrant claims, "I bought them from him," whereas here he says, "They were mine," or "I took them for my debt." A claim that the items were his is contrary to the presumption that everything found in a person's house belongs to that householder. Therefore the owner is believed even without the additional indications against the entrant. This is why the Tur, Mechaber, and other authorities carefully formulate the entrant's response as "I took what was mine" or "I took it for my debt," and none states that the same Mishnah applies when he says, "I bought it from you."
