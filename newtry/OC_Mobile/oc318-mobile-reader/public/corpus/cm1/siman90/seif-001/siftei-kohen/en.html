How does the law of one who was robbed apply, etc. The Tur, Rambam, and Semag state that the victim swears with an oath resembling a Torah oath and collects, and that this is a penalty imposed by the Sages upon the robber. They say this to teach that if the robber dies, the penalty was not imposed upon his son, just as other rabbinic penalties are not inherited. This differs from the workers' enactment, where the employer's son may remain liable, because that enactment was not a penalty but rested on the superior plausibility of the worker's claim due to the employer's preoccupation. Lechem Mishneh asks why a special penalty is needed. If witnesses establish that the defendant removed at least some utensils, or the defendant admits some, he would appear to owe a Torah oath as a partial admitter. Since a robber is suspected concerning oaths, the opposing party should swear and collect under the ordinary rule for a suspected oath-taker. One answer is that the admitted or witnessed objects remain present and are treated as “here it is,” so no Torah oath would otherwise exist. The Sages nevertheless transferred even this rabbinic oath to the victim. Another answer is that the defendant is not yet conclusively a suspected oath-taker, because he claims that he took his own property or seized for a debt. People may not perceive such conduct as robbery. The enactment teaches that the Sages nevertheless treated him like one suspected of an oath.
That there were utensils, etc. The witnesses must know that he removed at least some utensils, though they do not know which ones. This appears from Tosafot and the Rosh. If the witnesses do not know that he removed utensils, the victim does not swear and collect even if the entrant is otherwise suspected, because he may have carried stones or pebbles under his clothing. The Sema and Bach rule accordingly. Even Rashi can be understood consistently with Tosafot and the Rosh.

Whether he says, etc. The victim swears and collects only where the articles were seen in the entrant's possession. Otherwise the entrant is believed through a migo that he returned them. Through that same migo he may say, “I took them for my debt,” or, “I took my own property.” Ran writes that all the laws in this discussion assume witnesses who saw the objects. Without such sight, the migo of return applies. A claim that the objects were purchased may sometimes be believed even where witnesses saw them, as below, seif 11, but here such a claim is unavailable according to Raavad, Maggid Mishneh, and other authorities because the entrant revealed that he entered to seize a pledge. Baal HaTerumot likewise says that even an established robber or one who entered without permission to seize property is believed through a migo of return, and that all these oath rules concern a case of sight.

The homeowner swears while holding a sacred object and collects, etc. The Sema's explanation that the entrant cannot say, “I seized for my debt,” because this contradicts a presumption is difficult. Such a claim does not contradict any presumption. Baal HaTerumot expressly equates a claim of purchase with a claim that the property was seized for a debt in all situations where the possessor would otherwise be believed, such as where the homeowner commonly sells his utensils, the objects were carried openly, the entrant is ordinarily discreet but carries such objects openly, or the homeowner alleges theft against one not established as a thief. Likewise, where the entrant could say “purchased,” he can say, “I took my own,” or, “I seized for my debt.” The Tur here does not discuss a case where the entrant has a migo. Later, in seif 20, the Tur explains the circumstances in which the entrant may claim purchase, and in those circumstances he may likewise claim that he took his own property or seized for a debt. The Bach also understands the Tur this way. The Tur does not distinguish between claims of purchase, ownership, and seizure for debt. His statement that the victim must take an oath resembling a Torah oath concerns a person not previously established as a robber, whom the Sages treated as a robber because he entered without permission to seize property. By contrast, against one already established as a thief, the owner takes only hesset. The Mechaber and other authorities omit the situations where the entrant is believed because they follow Rambam, Raavad, and Maggid Mishneh that a person who entered openly for the purpose of seizing a pledge cannot later claim purchase. Once he disclosed that purpose, he is not believed to say that he took his own property or seized for a debt through a purchase migo. This is the principal law. Tosafot and the Rosh similarly rule concerning one who forcibly seized property in the presence of witnesses that he cannot later say it was purchased or taken for a debt.
