The robbed person , how so? The Rambam and the Tur write that this is a penalty imposed by the Sages upon a robber. The Shach and the Lechem Mishneh ask why any special enactment for the robbed person is needed. The defendant is already a person suspected regarding oaths. He denies everything, while witnesses testify that he took at least some property. This is the first rule of Rabbi Chiya: testimony by witnesses to part of a claim has the same effect as an admission of part and creates a Torah oath. Since the defendant cannot swear because he is suspected, the opposing claimant should swear and collect under the general enactment governing a suspected oath taker. Why do we need a separate enactment for one who was robbed? The Lechem Mishneh and the Shach answer that with respect to the utensils the witnesses actually saw and which remain extant, even though the defendant says, “They are mine,” his denial is ineffective and those objects are considered heilech. The Shach gives another answer, that the very designation of the robber as suspected is itself part of the special penalty imposed upon him. The second answer is difficult in light of the law below in seif 5, where the supposed penalty could result in the defendant being exempt without paying anything. The robbed person could simply say that he does not want the penalty. The first answer does not fit the Rambam either, because the Rambam holds that a deposit is not heilech merely because the object exists. It becomes heilech only when the defendant places it before beit din and says, “Take it and go.” Here the defendant denies the claimant’s ownership entirely. Tosafot already raised the underlying question. What is the difference between the Sages and Rabbi Yehudah, who says that the special rule applies only where there is an admission of part? Even according to the Sages, the witnesses’ testimony should itself be equivalent to an admission of part under Rabbi Chiya’s first rule. Tosafot answer that the practical difference is a case where the defendant already returned the utensils that the witnesses saw him take. This is a good answer, and Tosafot did not answer like the later authorities that the witnessed property is heilech. Had that been their answer, Rabbi Chiya’s own view would create a problem, because Rabbi Chiya holds that witness testimony can generate an oath and also holds that heilech does not exempt. How then could the Mishnah be established according to him? Still, Rabbi Yehudah’s view remains difficult. He requires an actual admission of part and does not apply the special enactment where the witnessed property has already been returned. Why is a special enactment for the robbed person needed even in his case? Once the defendant admits part of the claim, he is an oath suspect who cannot take the Torah oath of one who admits part, and therefore the opposing party should swear and collect under the ordinary rule governing a suspected oath taker. It is strained to answer that, according to the view that heilech does not exempt, Rabbi Yehudah is speaking where the claimant demands a large gold lamp and a silver cup and the witnesses saw only the silver cup, or the defendant admits only the silver cup, so that the admission is of a different type than the claim. One would then have to assume that Rabbi Yehudah holds that one who claims wheat and barley and receives an admission to only one of them is exempt. According to our ruling that heilech does exempt, one can establish Rabbi Yehudah’s case as heilech, as the Ran writes, although I explained in the preceding siman that Rabbi Yehudah himself obligates an oath despite heilech. In any event, there is no compelling difficulty according to every opinion. In truth, there is no difficulty at all. The enactment permits even the wife of the watchman to swear and collect. There can therefore be no Torah oath in the ordinary sense, because the claimant himself does not make a definite claim based upon his own knowledge. A definite claim reported through a person who is disqualified from testimony does not create a Torah oath according to any view. Moreover, even if there were a Torah oath that the suspected defendant could not take, the ordinary rule is that his opposing litigant swears and collects. If that litigant cannot swear, can a witness swear in his place? Can his wife or members of his household swear for him? Such a proposition should be entirely rejected. Yet here, under the special enactment, the wife of the watchman may swear based upon what she observed and the robbed person may collect. That itself demonstrates the substantial independent penalty created for a robber. Tosafot’s question concerns only the wording of the Mishnah. Rabbi Yehudah says, “until there is an admission of part,” implying that according to the first Tanna the witnesses’ testimony is not itself regarded as the equivalent of an admission of part. Tosafot therefore ask from Rabbi Chiya’s first rule and answer that the case can concern property already returned. Their point is terminological, not that the special enactment is wholly unnecessary. I nevertheless do not understand Tosafot’s answer that the defendant already returned what the witnesses saw him take, and the Mordechai gives the same answer. Rabbi Yehudah says that there must be an admission of part, giving the example that the claimant says, “You took two,” and the defendant says, “I took only one.” If the defendant already returned the admitted item, what admission of part remains? In the hired worker context, if the employer admits that he fixed a certain wage but also says that he already paid it, this is not an admission of part capable of generating a Torah oath. If the defendant did not return the witnessed property, however, then even if he denies everything the witnesses establish part of the claim and Rabbi Chiya’s first rule applies. Rabbi Yehudah should therefore have said merely that some of the property must remain unreturned, not that an admission must come from the defendant’s own mouth. The admission as such would add nothing. One could say that Rabbi Yehudah disagrees with Rabbi Chiya’s first rule, because he follows Rabban Gamliel, who obligates one who claims wheat and receives an admission of barley, while Rabbi Chiya’s rule is not accepted in that framework, as appears in Bava Metzia. But then Tosafot’s question never begins. It is strained to say that Tosafot’s point is only that if the Sages follow Rabbi Chiya they too would always require the functional equivalent of an admission of part, whereas Rabbi Yehudah says that an actual admission is necessary, so the first Tanna should have said that witnesses suffice. To preserve Rabbi Yehudah’s wording, Tosafot would then establish the first Tanna even where the witnessed items were already returned, while Rabbi Yehudah himself could simply disagree with Rabbi Chiya. This is possible but strained.
In truth , were it not for Tosafot’s wording “for example, where he returned it,” I would have explained them differently. The Mishnah itself says that the defendant claims, “I did not take,” so an actual prior return is awkward. Tosafot may follow the Sema’s view above in siman 75 that Rabbi Chiya’s first rule applies only where the witnesses establish that repayment could not have occurred, such as where they never moved away from the debtor. If they merely testify that a loan occurred but do not know whether it was repaid, then although the defendant’s denial of the loan is legally treated as an admission that he did not repay, Rabbi Chiya’s special rule does not apply. As I explained there, it is possible that Tosafot and the Rosh follow this approach. If so, the witnesses here testify that the defendant took the property, but he could have claimed that he later returned it, because a robber who took property before witnesses need not return it before witnesses. Only because he says, “I never took it,” is his own mouth treated as saying, “I never returned it.” Yet the witnesses themselves do not establish a present debt, and Rabbi Chiya’s first rule does not arise from their testimony alone. This may be Tosafot’s intent when they say that the case can be established “where he already returned it,” meaning that according to the content of the witnesses’ testimony a return remains fully possible. The defendant’s own denial is what removes that possibility. Rabbi Yehudah therefore requires that the defendant himself admit, “I took one,” and since he does not claim to have returned it, he expressly admits a presently outstanding portion of the claim. This is a complete admission of part. This explanation is correct according to the Sema’s approach, although Tosafot’s word “for example” remains somewhat difficult because in fact the property need not actually have been returned. This requires study. Nor can one object that the witnesses saw the defendant take property without knowing its exact quantity, and therefore their testimony should not count because an admission must concern something of defined measure and number. Since the witnesses say they do not know the exact quantity, they nevertheless establish with certainty at least the minimum value of a perutah. Their testimony is effectively an express statement that he owes no less than a perutah. This accords with the Ramban’s resolution rather than the Rosh’s, as I wrote above in siman 72, Tumim seif katan 25. Examine that discussion. Finally, the Shach’s and Lechem Mishneh’s question arises only according to the Rambam’s teachers, who require a denial of two silver ma’ah even in all these special categories who swear and collect. According to the Rambam himself, who requires only a perutah for these special oaths, there is no difficulty in any event. If we sought to derive the claimant’s right solely from the ordinary rule that an oath suspect’s opponent swears and collects, the underlying Torah oath would require a denial of two silver ma’ah beyond the portion established by witnesses. The special enactment for the robbed person therefore has independent practical force even on that basis.
He swears while holding a sacred object. The Shach writes that whatever the witnesses affirm he took must be returned without any oath. This is simple and correct. He also cites Baal HaTerumot and the Ran that in all these cases where the claimant swears and collects, there must be witnesses who saw the property still in the defendant’s possession. If there are no such witnesses, the defendant is believed to say, “I returned it,” because one who robs another before witnesses need not return before witnesses. He then has a migo: since he could have said, “I returned it,” he may say, “I took what was mine.” These are correct principles. The Shach in seif katan 3, however, explains that the requirement of witnesses who saw the property applies only to the specific utensils about which the witnesses testified, meaning to the portion they actually saw. According to him, no such witnesses are needed for the remaining utensils, because once the defendant has already been established as a robber he must answer the remainder under the special enactment. I am surprised by this. Even if he returns what the witnesses saw, for example where they testified with certainty that he took two utensils and did not know about the rest, and he then returns those two, the robbed person must still swear concerning the remaining items. Tosafot state this explicitly, as I cited in the preceding seif katan, explaining that the Sages’ case can be one where the defendant already returned what the witnesses saw. The Mordechai says the same. Reason also requires this. Otherwise, the enactment for the robbed person would be nullified. Since the defendant must return what witnesses actually saw without any oath by the claimant, why would the robber not immediately return those items so that the claimant could no longer swear and collect regarding the additional property? He has to return them in any event. Rather, even after he returns them he remains a robber. “The wicked man takes a pledge,” and the status is not erased merely by repayment. As explained above in siman 46, even a robber who performs genuine repentance does not regain full testimonial qualification until beit din has evidence of his rehabilitation. See also the end of siman 34. Thus, returning the witnessed property does not remove his status, and the robbed person can still swear and collect regarding the rest. If, however, the defendant has not returned the witnessed items but says, “They are mine,” and is believed through a migo that he could have said, “I returned them,” while as a consequence the robbed person could not swear and collect the additional items, that would destroy the migo. What migo from the claim “I returned them” would he have, if upon actually returning them the robbed person could then swear and collect the rest, whereas under his present claim the robbed person could not? Necessarily, even if he returns the witnessed items, the robbed person still swears and collects regarding the rest. If so, the authorities’ requirement of witnesses who saw the property would become meaningless according to the Shach. One cannot answer that witnesses who saw the property are required only as to the items expressly identified by the first witnesses, while once such items establish him as a robber no witnesses are needed for the other items. If returning the first items does not remove his status as a robber, why should witnesses who saw the remaining items be necessary? And if returning them does remove that status, then even witnesses who saw the remaining items would not help, because he would no longer be subject to the special oath mechanism. Therefore the correct rule is clear. As to items that witnesses testify he took, witnesses who saw them are needed if the robbed person wishes to recover them without an oath. As to additional items claimed beyond what the witnesses saw, the robbed person cannot swear and collect unless there is likewise no migo of return concerning those additional items. The Sages did not institute the special oath where the defendant has a valid migo. The reason is that the special enactment is directed at the fact that the defendant is a robber. If he admits that he robbed the claimant but says, “I later returned the objects,” the Sages did not create a special oath allowing the claimant to overcome that repayment defense. The same applies where the defendant has a valid migo that he returned them. By contrast, if the additional objects themselves were seen in his possession, so that no return migo exists as to them, then the robbed person may swear and collect, even if the objects expressly identified by the original witnesses were not later seen. Returning those original items does not erase the robber’s status, as explained. It is possible that with respect to the very items about which the witnesses testified, the claimant cannot use the special oath if the defendant retains a valid return migo, because the enactment was made only where no such migo exists. At minimum, however, it is clear that regarding the remaining items, if there are no witnesses negating a return migo, the robbed person does not collect, contrary to the Shach. This is also explicit in Baal HaTerumot, Sha’ar 49, part 7. He writes that because witnesses testify that the defendant took property, he has the status of a robber, and because he has no migo he is not believed, so the Sages enacted that the robbed person swears and collects. The plain sense is that concerning the very property for which the claimant swears, the defendant lacks a migo. Baal HaTerumot likewise writes in part 12 of that sha’ar that all the rules stated there concerning the robbed person’s credibility and the defendant’s inability to say, “I seized it for my debt,” are presumed to refer to a case where the property was seen. His words point directly to the requirement that every disputed item recovered under the enactment be outside the defendant’s return migo. “Seen” need not always mean literal observation of the specific utensils, which can be difficult where the very dispute is whether certain utensils were taken. The same result can arise where the owner warned the defendant upon entry not to return the property except before witnesses, where the owner was absent so that a return could not realistically have occurred, or in other circumstances that eliminate a plausible return defense. All this applies where there is no independent Torah oath, whether through an admission of part or through Rabbi Chiya’s first rule, as discussed in seif katan 1. If the robber himself is subject to a Torah oath as one who admits part, then even if he has a migo concerning the remaining utensils, he remains obligated to swear. Ultimately he admits part of the utensils, and it makes no difference whether regarding the remainder he says, “It never happened,” “They were mine,” or “I returned them.” None of these defenses removes the Torah oath generated by the admission of part. Since he is suspected and cannot take that oath, the opposing party swears and collects, as explained in the preceding seif katan. Examine this carefully.
And he collects. Know that the Tur, in this siman, letter 20, cites the statement concerning witnesses who saw a person enter another’s house and later emerge with utensils concealed under his cloak. He requires the three conditions stated in the Gemara and set out below in seif 11. The Tur then writes that all these conditions and distinctions apply as well to the case of one who was robbed, where someone entered his house to seize a pledge. The difference is that in the ordinary robbed person case the claimant must swear while holding a sacred object, in a form resembling a Torah oath, whereas in the case of one seen leaving with the owner’s utensils, whenever the owner is believed he collects without such an oath, although heset is still required. This follows Rabbi Yosef HaLevi and the Ramah. Rav Hai Gaon, however, writes that there is no distinction between the cases, and even in the latter case the owner collects only after taking an oath resembling a Torah oath. The Shach objects to the Tur’s position that even where the entrant expressly revealed that he was entering in order to seize a pledge, the owner is believed only under the same conditions, namely that the owner is not accustomed to selling his utensils, the entrant is not ordinarily one who conceals objects, and the utensils themselves are not of a type normally concealed. The Shach argues from the case of the cattle keeper where witnesses saw the defendant seize the animals. Tosafot and the Rosh write there that once the seizure is witnessed he is no longer believed to say that the items were purchased or were seized for a debt. How then can the Tur disagree with Tosafot and the Rosh? In my view the stronger objection would have been from the case of the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba. The defendant admitted, “Yes, I snatched it, but I snatched what was mine,” and he was not believed. The Tur himself cites that law. It is strained to say that the ingot case means only that he claimed the claimant owed him money and therefore he seized it, because the words “I snatched what was mine” do not naturally mean that. This is especially difficult according to the Shach, who holds that there is no legal distinction between saying, “It was mine,” and saying, “I seized it for my debt.” We must therefore understand the Tur carefully. What precisely is the subject of the distinction he draws between the ordinary robbed person and the case where witnesses saw someone leave with utensils under his cloak? If he refers to the very utensils that the witnesses saw, the Rif expressly writes that those items are recovered without any oath because witnesses establish the taking. How could the Tur add a requirement that the owner swear while holding a sacred object? If he refers to additional utensils that the witnesses did not identify, it is difficult to say that in the ordinary case of one seen carrying utensils under his cloak the owner may claim further utensils not seen by the witnesses. No authority states such a rule except Rav Hai Gaon in Sha’arei Shevuot, and there too he requires at least an oath resembling a Torah oath. For if we permit recovery of items not actually seen by witnesses, it must necessarily be through the Mishnah’s special enactment for the robbed person, under which the robbed person swears and collects. Once we invoke that enactment, the claimant must take the Mishnah oath. What basis would any authority have to allow recovery of unobserved property merely with heset? The Bach’s distinction between saying, “You took them for your debt,” and saying, “You purchased them,” was already rejected by the Shach, and rightly so. More fundamentally, why should the person who openly announced that he entered to seize a pledge, and is therefore a clear robber, be in a better position so that the robbed person must take a stringent oath, while in an ordinary case where one is merely seen leaving with utensils the owner could recover with only heset? The correct explanation of the Tur is therefore that he is speaking in two different respects. In the Mishnah’s robbed person case, the special oath concerns utensils whose precise identity or quantity is not established by the witnesses. As to whatever the witnesses themselves identify, no oath is needed, as the Rif and Shach write. In the separate case where witnesses saw a person leave carrying concealed utensils, all of the owner’s special credibility relates only to the utensils the witnesses actually saw him carrying. Regarding those very utensils the three conditions are required: the owner is not accustomed to sell his possessions, the entrant is not ordinarily one who conceals, and the objects are not normally concealed. The Tur then says that the same sorts of conditions which in the ordinary concealed utensils case apply to items actually seen by the witnesses also apply in the robbed person case to the additional items the witnesses did not see. In other words, the evidentiary considerations that operate there regarding the observed utensils operate here regarding the unobserved remainder. The difference is that in the robbed person case, because the claim extends beyond what the witnesses actually saw, the claimant must swear while holding a sacred object. In the concealed utensils case, where the recovery is limited to the items seen by the witnesses, the owner does not need that oath, only heset. This is why the Tur carefully writes, “wherever the owner is believed,” meaning regarding items actually observed by witnesses. There is no practical situation there requiring the stronger oath because he is not recovering beyond what was seen. Rav Hai Gaon, by contrast, holds that there is no distinction between the two settings. In every case, as to what witnesses directly saw, whether the defendant entered to seize a pledge or was simply seen carrying objects, the owner recovers without any special oath. If the owner seeks more than the witnesses saw, then in every setting, whether the defendant entered to seize a pledge or was merely observed leaving, the owner must swear while holding a sacred object before collecting. This is what the Tur means by saying that according to Rav Hai there is no difference between the cases. As to witnessed property there is no oath at all, and as to additional property there is always the stronger oath. See Rav Hai’s Sha’arei Shevuot, where this is explicit.
Accordingly , on this interpretation the Shach’s objection from the cattle keeper falls away entirely. The Tur agrees that regarding the very items seen by witnesses, the defendant is not believed to say they were his, and the special conditions discussed by the Tur are not needed for those items. The Tur imposes those details only concerning the additional items for which the robbed person seeks to recover by oath. The cattle keeper case therefore proves nothing against him. Indeed, even apart from this explanation, the Shach’s objection can be answered by examining the Rambam. In chapter 4 of Hilkhot Gezelah VaAveidah the Rambam rules that where witnesses saw a person enter another’s house in order to seize a pledge and then leave carrying utensils, and the alleged robber says, “I did not take the owner’s property, the objects under my cloak were mine,” the robbed person must nevertheless swear before collecting. This is so even though the witnesses saw that he entered empty handed and left carrying utensils, from which it necessarily follows that he took them from the owner’s house. Yet in that same chapter, halakhah 15, the Rambam rules that if one witness, and certainly two, saw a person snatch gold coins, although they did not know the number of coins, and the snatcher says, “There were twenty, but they were mine,” he must pay all twenty and the victim collects without any oath. Why should the first case require an oath? There too the witnesses do not know the exact quantity of the utensils, and the defendant admits taking the stated quantity but claims that they were his. The Sema in seif katan 3 explains “I took what was mine” to mean that his own objects had been deposited in the victim’s house. Yet the witnesses saw him leave full after entering empty. Whatever his explanation, the objects necessarily came from the house. If the parties agree on the number and dispute only ownership, why not permit collection without an oath just as with the gold coins? The Shulchan Aruch itself rules like the Rambam’s gold coin case below in siman 364, seif 5. We must distinguish between witnesses who are present at the actual act of seizure and witnesses who are not. Where they witness the snatching itself, and before snatching the defendant did not announce, “See, I am taking what is mine,” the property remains in the victim’s established possession, and the victim need not swear. In the house case, however, the witnesses were not inside when the taking occurred. They merely heard the defendant say before entering that he intended to seize a pledge and later saw him emerge carrying utensils. Since they did not witness the taking itself, it remains possible that his threat was exaggerated and that he had a specific deposit of his own property inside the house, so that the items came into his hands without an actual forcible seizure. The owner therefore collects only with an oath. The two cases are not comparable. On this basis, neither the cattle keeper case nor the silver ingot case creates a difficulty for the Tur, because those involved witnessed seizure. Here the actual taking was not seen. One could therefore understand the Tur to require the three conditions precisely because the seizure itself was not witnessed. Even the objects the witnesses later recognize might require an oath. This would not contradict the Rif’s statement that what witnesses saw is recovered without an oath, because the Rif may be speaking where the defendant says, “I took nothing.” One who says he took nothing effectively concedes that none of the objects were his, and his statement directly contradicts the witnesses who saw him carrying them. In such a case the objects are returned without an oath. Tosafot likewise ask that the witnesses’ testimony concerning some utensils should invoke Rabbi Chiya’s first rule, which implies that those particular items are returned without an oath. Otherwise, if the robbed person still had to swear even for the witnessed part, it would not resemble Rabbi Chiya’s rule. But that is all in the Mishnah’s case where the defendant says, “I took nothing.” If instead he admits taking them but says, “They were mine and had been deposited there,” and the witnesses do not contradict that detail, it is possible that the robbed person must swear even regarding the items certainly carried out, despite the defendant’s lack of a migo. Since ownership is uncertain and we do not presume a person to be a thief without basis, some oath is required. Here, where the defendant had previously announced an intent to seize a pledge, he is not as firmly established as a thief as one who is caught in an unexplained seizure, as the Shach himself notes. This yields another possible direct reading of the Tur. His later discussion may concern only utensils actually seen, yet still require the conditions and the oath because the witnesses did not observe the actual seizure. In fact the case where he merely announced an intent to seize a pledge may be less severe in this respect, because the evidence is indirect, and therefore the owner must take the stronger oath. In the ordinary case where a person was simply seen leaving with utensils and the owner claims they were borrowed or stolen, the defendant may be more firmly established as one who wrongfully removed them, so the owner needs only heset. This reading fits the Tur’s plain language more comfortably, but I am troubled by his concluding statement that Rav Hai Gaon sees no distinction and requires the owner here too to swear while holding a sacred object. If all the discussion concerned the exact objects seen by witnesses, Rav Hai actually holds that as to those objects the owner recovers without any oath. The Tur should then have written that Rav Hai requires no oath, not that he requires the same stronger oath. For this reason, the first interpretation appears preferable. The Tur agrees that property actually identified by witnesses is recovered without oath, consistent with Rav Hai Gaon, the Rif, and Tosafot. At any rate, the Shach’s objection does not stand. We may now return to the Sema. The Sema found it difficult that at the beginning of the siman the Tur states without qualification, as does the Shulchan Aruch, that the robbed person swears and collects regardless of the defendant’s precise formulation, while later in letter 20 the Tur says that all the detailed conditions, such as the owner not being accustomed to selling and the entrant not ordinarily concealing objects, apply to the earlier robbed person case. Why were these qualifications not stated immediately at the beginning? The Sema therefore distinguishes between different defenses. If the defendant says, “It is true that I took your objects, but I bought them from you there in your house,” the three conditions are necessary. That claim does not contradict the witnesses and does not contradict the usual presumption that property found in a person’s house belongs to him, because the defendant admits the property was originally the owner’s and claims only that a purchase occurred. But if he says, “I took nothing,” or, “I took my own property that had been deposited there,” his assertion runs against the surrounding presumption, and he is not believed even without the three conditions. The Sema adds that the same is true if he says, “I seized them for my debt.” In that case too he is not believed without regard to the three conditions, and the robbed person swears and collects. The Shach objects that there is no relevant presumption distinguishing a debt claim from a purchase claim. Why should “I seized them for my debt” be weaker than “I purchased them”? He also asks why the defendant who says, “They were mine,” should not have a migo that he could have said, “I purchased them.” If the answer is that such a purchase claim would oppose the presumption that whatever is in a person’s house belongs to him, then even witnesses who later saw the objects should be unnecessary, because the defendant would have no return migo either, since that too would allegedly oppose the same presumption. Baal HaTerumot also writes explicitly in Sha’ar 49, part 9, that where the three conditions are absent, the defendant is believed even to say, “I seized them for my debt.” These are strong objections to the Sema’s stated rationale. Nevertheless, the Sema’s substantive distinction appears correct, though not for the reason he gives. A person who openly enters another’s house before witnesses announcing that he is going to seize a pledge, and then after returning home claims that he actually purchased the property from the owner, is presenting an inherently uncommon story. Precisely because such a purchase is so unusual, most authorities other than the Tur appear to rule that he is not believed even without the three conditions of seif 11. The Tur, however, holds that rarity alone does not strip a possessor of his ordinary credibility. Since he presently says, “I purchased it,” who can remove the property from him unless the additional suspicious circumstances are present? But if he says, “The objects were mine all along,” and seeks to rely on a migo that he could have claimed a purchase, that migo rests upon an extremely uncommon alternative. A migo based upon a claim that people do not ordinarily make is not accepted, as explained in the rules of migo. This is not comparable to a migo of return. Returning property is common and therefore supplies a genuine migo. A post hoc claim that the openly announced seizure was actually an on the spot purchase is too unusual to serve as the alternative claim supporting a migo. Rav Hai Gaon makes this distinction explicit. He first presents the first category, one who enters another’s house to seize a pledge, and then a second category, where witnesses see someone conceal utensils and leave. He sets out the detailed conditions required in the second category and afterward writes that there is a difference between the two. In the first, the person entered by way of taking a pledge, the witnesses do not know exactly what he took, and he denies the taking. In the second, he says the property was his. Therefore one must investigate the detailed conditions before the owner may swear and collect. Rav Hai clearly distinguishes the first case because the defendant denies the taking, while in the second he admits possession and claims ownership, which is the purchase type defense described immediately beforehand. If in the first case he too claimed a purchase, the same detailed conditions would be needed. The difference lies between denial and an affirmative acquisition claim. The Shach’s migo question must therefore be answered as above. The defendant cannot support “they were mine” by a migo of “I bought them,” because the latter is an uncommon claim in these circumstances. Rav Hai’s words thus support the Sema’s practical distinction, and it is clear that the Tur did not invent the requirement of the detailed conditions. He relied upon Rav Hai Gaon, whose teachings are received tradition. Rashi, however, appears to read the sugya differently. In Shevuot, where Rabbi Yehudah explains the Mishnah by speaking of one seen taking utensils under his cloak, there was an older text reading, “Rabbi Yehudah said,” and Rashi deletes the attribution and reads simply, “They said.” If the Tur’s plain approach were correct, making the concealed utensils rule part of Rabbi Yehudah’s explanation of the Mishnah itself, the older reading attaching it to Rabbi Yehudah would fit well, because the same three conditions would be part of the Mishnah’s robbed person rule. The Rif too writes in Sha’ar 4 that Rabbi Yehudah’s statement concerning one seen leaving with utensils is a separate matter and not simply the rule of the Mishnah. How then could the Tur state the matter broadly unless he had a major source? The answer is that he relied upon Rav Hai Gaon, who expressly distinguishes between denial and a claim of acquisition in the manner explained. As for the Sema’s additional statement that the same relaxed rule applies even when the defendant says, “I seized them for my debt,” I remain uncertain. If a person in possession of a particular object who says, “It is collateral in my hand,” is believed only because he has a migo that he could have said, “I purchased it,” then the Sema is correct that here the migo fails because the purchase claim is too uncommon. Baal HaTerumot’s statement does not refute this, because he discusses the ordinary case of someone seen leaving with property, not one who openly entered to seize a pledge. There, where the special suspicious conditions are absent, a purchase is itself common enough, and “I seized it for my debt” can be believed through a purchase migo. But perhaps possession itself gives the holder independent credibility to say, “I hold it as collateral,” or, “I seized it for my debt,” just as possession gives credibility to say, “I purchased it,” without any need for a migo. If so, the Sema’s extension would be incorrect. Nor is there conclusive proof that the Tur himself meant to include the debt claim. At the beginning of the siman he does not explicitly discuss one who says, “I seized it for my debt.” The Sema inferred the matter from the Tur’s later statement concerning one witness and the defendant’s claim that he seized for a debt, where the Tur says he does not acquire the status of a robber through one witness. The Sema inferred that with two witnesses the robbed person would recover under the initial rule. But this is not conclusive. The Tur may simply have stated a clear rule for the one witness case, whereas with two witnesses the result would still depend upon whether the three conditions are present. Therefore the point remains doubtful. Since in any event this entire area is a rabbinic enactment rather than Torah law, every unresolved doubt should be resolved leniently for the defendant.
