And he did not have... and when he leaves. This is the language of the Rif and Rambam. Therefore they do not write that he actually took a pledge, because according to the Gemara's conclusion it is enough that witnesses saw him leave with articles.
They saw that there were... and they do not know. This is the first version in the Rosh, following Rashi.
Whether... This is Rambam's wording in the middle clause. In some versions of the Tur as well, the first clause concerns a defendant whose denial is contradicted by witnesses, so the claimant swears even without the special enactment for a robbed person. The witnesses' contradiction itself obligates him, like the shepherd case according to those who hold that where one partially admits but says he does not know the amount, the claim is sufficiently quantified to obligate an oath. The second clause, where he says, "It is mine," is learned from the case of Rabbi Abba's silver bar, for if there were two witnesses he would be treated as a robber and would have to return the known amount. The third clause is Rabbi Yehudah's statement, as explained in the Rif's Shaarim.
Even the witnesses... This is the second version in the Rosh, following Tosafos, and the word "even" teaches that there is evidence that the witnesses saw these articles and no more, as the Tur explains.
He swears while holding a sacred object. Yerushalmi, as above in siman 89.
And they shall be... Since the Gemara says "when he claims him for articles..." rather than merely "with articles," the claimant must specify his claim.
And some say that we require... This is based on the end of Perek HaKones, Bava Kamma 62a, where the enactment for a robbed person is applied and Rav Ashi asks about one who claims a pearl. The Rashba explains that the uncertainty is whether an item of that sort would ordinarily be placed there. Tosafos understands the passage differently. In siman 388 seif 1 in the gloss, the view that even an unusual item may be claimed follows Tosafos, whereas Rambam and Shulchan Aruch there follow the Rashba that without witnesses one is believed only for an item ordinarily placed there. Rambam, however, appears to interpret the question as applying even to an item known to be unusual, asking whether the enactment grants the claimant credibility despite his negligence in placing such an item there. Hence the language that because he was negligent he is not believed. But where witnesses establish the item, payment is required even for an unusual article. Thus the Ramah here follows the Rashba, while Rambam's rationale of negligence does not apply in the same way. The matter is a doubt in law, which explains the ruling that if the claimant seized, he may retain it.

The first view holds he is not considered suspected, because people do not necessarily understand taking collateral for a debt to be prohibited, just as Bava Metzia 5 says concerning "You shall not covet."
