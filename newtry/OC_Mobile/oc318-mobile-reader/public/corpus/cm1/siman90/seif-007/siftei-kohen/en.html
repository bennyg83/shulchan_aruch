Everything he is estimated to possess, etc. The Rosh distinguishes this law from the incident involving the steward of Bar Ziza, where property was returned to the steward's heirs even though he was not wealthy. There, the question was whether to preserve money in the presumed possession of the depositor, and we say that a person may conceal his wealth to avoid appearing rich. Here, however, the claimant seeks to extract money from the tortfeasor, so we follow ordinary estimation. Maharshal rules accordingly. Hagahot Ashiri objects from the Jerusalem Talmud, which appears to infer from the Bar Ziza incident that even one who is not estimated to own such property may swear and collect, because some people avoid publicizing their wealth. The answer is that the Babylonian Talmud disagrees. In both the case of hidden property destroyed by fire and the case of one robbed, Rav Ashi says that the claimant swears and collects only if he is wealthy enough to own the claimed silver cup or is trusted enough that others deposit such objects with him. Otherwise his claim is not accepted. The Maggid Mishneh likewise rules that our Gemara is controlling despite the Jerusalem Talmud. To reconcile our Gemara with the Bar Ziza incident, one must distinguish as the Rosh did. Alternatively, in the cases of one robbed and hidden objects damaged by fire, the victim's right to swear and collect is itself a rabbinic enactment. The Sages limited the enactment to a claimant whose possession of such objects is plausible. The Bach suggests this, and it may also be the Rosh's intent.
In the manner that will be explained in siman 418. This applies specifically where one set fire to another person's house, because every type of property is ordinarily kept in a house, and the injured party is believed concerning whatever he claims. If one set fire to another's stack of grain, the claimant is believed only regarding objects ordinarily kept in a stack, such as threshing implements and cattle tools. He is not believed concerning other objects. The Tur's language implies that if witnesses establish the presence of unusual objects, the tortfeasor must pay for them. This follows the Jerusalem Talmud cited by the Rosh, that in the presence of witnesses the Sages agree with Rabbi Yehudah. See below, siman 388, seif 1.
