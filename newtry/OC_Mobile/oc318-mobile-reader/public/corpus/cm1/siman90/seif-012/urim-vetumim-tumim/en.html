Through a migo that he could have said, “I returned it to you.” The Sema writes that this proves that one who robbed before witnesses does not have to return before witnesses. Otherwise, what migo of return could there be, since repayment itself would have to be witnessed? The Shach writes that if so, the same proof would arise from the Rambam here. Yet the commentators understand the Rambam as holding that a robber who robbed before witnesses must return before witnesses. Necessarily, this passage is no proof, because the owner’s actual claim is that he lent the objects to the possessor, and in a loan everyone agrees that one who borrowed before witnesses need not return before witnesses. The Derishah already anticipated this objection. His proof is instead that the law equates a claim of theft with a claim of loan. Just as the loan claim requires witnesses who recently saw the property so that there is no migo of return, the theft claim likewise requires such witnesses. That would seem to show that even a thief can otherwise say, “I returned it.” At first glance, therefore, the Shach’s rejection fails.
Nevertheless, there is still no proof from here. Where the objects are not normally lent and the owner says, “They were stolen,” even after the defendant has been established as a thief the owner is believed only where all the additional suspicious circumstances are present, such as that the owner is not accustomed to sell, that the objects were concealed, and that the possessor is not ordinarily one who conceals. If the owner says “stolen” while the possessor says “borrowed,” the possessor is plainly believed. The suspicious circumstance that the owner is not accustomed to sell has no force against a loan claim, because the possessor does not claim to have purchased the objects. Concealment likewise has no force against a loan claim, since as Rashi says people are embarrassed to carry borrowed objects openly and may conceal them. Thus the possessor has a migo: he could say, “I did not steal them, I borrowed them and later returned them.” That alternative is believed. Therefore witnesses who recently saw the items are needed even without deciding whether a robber ordinarily must return before witnesses. The entire proof disappears. The Knesset HaGedolah sought to bring a proof from the case of the cattle keeper who seized the orphans’ cattle. An older reading of the Gemara says that there were witnesses who saw him seize them after the father’s death. The Ran explains that witnesses specifically to a postmortem seizure are needed, not merely witnesses to some earlier possession, because otherwise the keeper would still have a migo that he returned the cattle. Therefore, he argues, the witnesses must eliminate the return migo. Tosafot and the Rosh omit the words “after death,” which might suggest that they did not share the Ran’s concern about a return migo because they held that property seized before witnesses must be returned before witnesses. This too is no proof. If the whole point of requiring a postmortem seizure were to eliminate a migo of return, why would the Gemara omit the primary alternative claim and mention only the secondary one? The Gemara says that because he has a migo that he could say “I purchased them,” he may say that he seized them during the father’s lifetime. If the decisive migo were really “I returned them,” that should have been the Gemara’s own formulation. Necessarily, witnesses had recently seen the property, so a return claim was already unavailable. The only remaining migo was purchase. Once that is so, Tosafot and the Rosh could properly omit “after death,” because the special timing was unnecessary. There is therefore no proof from their text either way. There is, however, clear evidence from the Rosh at the beginning of the last chapter of Bava Kamma. The Mishnah says that if a robber died after leaving the stolen property to his sons, they must pay, but if the adult sons say, “We know that our father made an accounting with you and nothing remained owed,” they are exempt. The Rosh explains that this refers to a case where we know the stolen property came into the father’s hands only from the heirs’ own statement. If witnesses had established that the property came into the father’s hands as stolen property, the heirs would not be believed to say that their father later paid, because the property was known to have come into his hands through robbery and to have remained in his possession. The father himself would not have been believed to say, “I returned it to you and then purchased it from you.” Similarly, regarding a deposit, if witnesses saw the object in his possession after knowing that it had been deposited with him and it was still in his hands, he cannot say, “I returned it and then purchased it.” The Rosh explicitly equates robbery and deposit. Just as in a deposit case witnesses must have seen the object still in his hands in order to defeat a return claim, so too in robbery. His repeated emphasis that the stolen object is “still in his hand” shows that if witnesses only established the original robbery but did not later see the object, the robber would be believed to say that he returned it. The Shach likewise rules this below in siman 361. Tosafot there offer two answers, and the Rashba’s novellae also indicate that witnesses who recently saw the stolen property are necessary, otherwise a return claim is believed. One should not object from the ruling above in siman 37 concerning Reuven who robbed Shimon of a garment and sold it to Levi. If Reuven dies, Shimon may testify for Levi because even if the garment remains with Levi, Shimon cannot recover both the garment and its value from Reuven, who is dead. The implication is that if Reuven left assets from which Shimon could collect, Shimon would be disqualified as an interested witness. Why? The heirs could seemingly say that perhaps their father repaid Shimon, if a robber is believed to say he returned the property. The answer is that regarding disqualification for interest in testimony, we take even remote possible interests into account. Shimon may have warned Reuven, “Do not repay me except before witnesses.” If so, the heirs could no longer plead that their father paid without witnesses, and Shimon would retain a financial interest in the testimony. Therefore that passage proves nothing about the ordinary burden of pleading repayment. The Ramah brings another proof that one who robbed before witnesses need not return before witnesses. In Ketubot the Gemara asks why the Mishnah does not teach the case, “You lent me one hundred and I repaid you,” where the defendant would be believed through the principle that the mouth which created the obligation is the mouth which removes it. The Gemara answers that the Mishnah wishes to continue, “but if there are witnesses,” and a borrower who borrowed before witnesses need not repay before witnesses, so witnesses to the loan would not destroy the repayment defense. The Ramah infers that the Mishnah could still have used the example, “I robbed you and returned it.” If a robber who robbed before witnesses had to return before witnesses, then in the first clause, where he alone admits the robbery, he would be believed through the mouth that prohibited, while in the second clause, where witnesses establish the robbery, he would no longer be believed to say he returned it. Since the Gemara does not offer this example, robbery too must be repayable without witnesses. The Ramban praises this proof. The Giddulei Terumah, however, asks that according to Rabbah’s original view in Bava Batra, before retracting, a person who deposits property before witnesses must receive it back before witnesses. How would Rabbah answer the Gemara in Ketubot? The Ramah’s question would remain applicable to a deposit. The Giddulei Terumah apparently assumed that even one who holds that a deposit must be returned before witnesses may nevertheless agree that an ordinary loan need not be repaid before witnesses, and therefore regarded the deposit example as an independent challenge. But this assumes the reverse of Tosafot’s analysis in Bava Batra. Tosafot there make clear that anyone who holds that a loan may be repaid without witnesses certainly holds that a deposit may be returned without witnesses. Their only uncertainty is the opposite direction: even according to the opinion that a loan must be repaid before witnesses, perhaps a deposit need not be returned before witnesses because the depositary has a migo of unavoidable accident. Thus the Giddulei Terumah’s premise does not stand. Moreover, Tosafot in Ketubot themselves already ask how the Gemara can use the repayment rule according to the opinion that a loan made before witnesses must be repaid before witnesses, and they give two answers. The same answers would resolve the deposit case as well, because deposit cannot be stricter than loan on this point. The Giddulei Terumah and the Lechem Mishneh further object that Tosafot’s answer, that the Tanna preferred the field example in order to teach Rav Huna’s rule that one may not acquire a minor’s property by chazakah, would equally answer the Ramah’s robbery proof. If so, the proof disappears. There is no difficulty. Why does the Gemara itself not give Tosafot’s answer that the field case was chosen to teach Rav Huna’s law? Instead it answers that the final clause with witnesses would not work in an ordinary loan because a loan before witnesses need not be repaid before witnesses. The reason is that the anonymous Gemara rules in accordance with the halakhah that repayment need not be witnessed. It therefore will not give an answer that suggests that, but for Rav Huna’s collateral teaching, the Mishnah could have used a loan example in which witnesses would destroy the repayment defense. That implication would be false according to the halakhah. The Gemara therefore gives the more fundamental answer that the loan example simply does not fit. Tosafot’s alternative answer is needed only for the minority view that requires repayment before witnesses. According to that view the anonymous Gemara’s own halakhic answer is unavailable, so Tosafot explain why the Mishnah still chose the field formulation. But if a robber who robbed before witnesses did have to return before witnesses, then even according to our halakhah the Ramah’s robbery example would remain available. The Gemara’s loan answer would not resolve it. Since we would necessarily have to invoke Tosafot’s secondary answer involving Rav Huna even according to the accepted halakhah, the Gemara itself should have said so. Its failure to do so indicates that robbery, like loan, need not be repaid before witnesses. The Ramah’s proof therefore remains strong. The order of Tosafot’s own discussion supports this. After giving the answer that the Mishnah wished to teach Rav Huna’s law, they ask why the Mishnah did not say, “Rabbi Yehoshua concedes where the claimant says, ‘Do not repay me except before so and so.’” This question appears to address all the preceding answers, not merely the first one. According to what was explained, that is precise. A stipulation requiring witnesses is valid according to the halakhah itself. Therefore the question is directed even against the anonymous Gemara’s answer. If such a case would make the witnesses clause work, why did the Gemara not use it? This demonstrates that the Gemara prefers a formulation consistent with the actual halakhah and that the Ramah’s inference from robbery has force. The Knesset HaGedolah cites the Mabit and his son the Maharit as holding that even the opinion requiring witnessed repayment is limited to a robber who robbed before witnesses. Since he committed his wrongdoing publicly, his return must likewise be public so that his correction and repentance become known. A robber who stole without witnesses need not return before witnesses. If so, one might think the Ramah’s proof disappears. The Mishnah could not say simply, “I robbed you and returned it,” because if the robbery was admitted only by the defendant and was not witnessed, then even after the admission he would be independently believed to say he returned it. The exemption would not depend specifically on the principle that the mouth which created the obligation can remove it. If the case instead means, “I robbed you before witnesses,” then there is no meaningful migo, because according to this view everyone knows that a publicly witnessed robbery must be returned publicly. The defendant could not plausibly say that he returned it privately. Thus the proposed example might fail for reasons unrelated to the general rule.
But this is not correct, because the Maharsha already addresses a related difficulty. The Gemara answers that the Mishnah cannot use the case of a loan and repayment because a loan before witnesses need not be repaid before witnesses. The Maharsha asks why the Gemara needs that reason at all. Even without it, the defendant is believed to say “I repaid,” so there is no need for a migo from the fact that his own mouth created the loan. He answers with difficulty that Rabbi Yehoshua’s statement is fundamentally taught for the latter clause, “but if there are witnesses.” Although it is true that the latter clause is central, the first clause too is plainly framed as an application of the mouth that created the obligation. The Gemara itself contrasts Rabbi Yehoshua’s usual rejection of one migo with his concession to this migo. If repayment is independently believed, what concession is there and what role does the migo play? The correct explanation is that the rule “a borrower before witnesses need not repay before witnesses” means that after a valid loan has been established, the borrower is believed to say that he subsequently repaid it. But where a defendant appears before beit din and gives a formal, unqualified admission, “So and so lent me one hundred,” he cannot later say that he had already repaid the loan before making that admission. Why would he have formally acknowledged an outstanding loan if it had already been paid? His unqualified admission means that he presently owes the money. If in the same breath, within the time of his statement, he adds, “and I repaid it,” he is believed because the mouth that created the obligation immediately limits it. The situation is exactly like the verse from which the concept is derived: “I gave my daughter to this man.” The first words establish a prohibition and the identification of “this man” immediately determines its permitted object. Thus, when the defendant says, “So and so lent me one hundred,” he creates a present admission. If he adds within the same statement, “I repaid it,” he removes the obligation through the same mouth. But if he waits beyond the immediate statement and then says, “I had already repaid before I admitted the debt,” he is not believed. Even a migo would not help, because such a plea contradicts the force of his own formal admission. The Gemara therefore truly needs its answer concerning the latter clause. If witnesses establish only the historical loan, the borrower may still say that he later repaid, because a loan before witnesses need not be repaid before witnesses. Therefore the witness clause would not destroy the defense, and the Mishnah could not use the loan example. The migo concept remains necessary in the first clause because the defendant’s own formal present admission would otherwise foreclose a claim of earlier repayment. The same reasoning restores the Ramah’s robbery proof even under the Mabit and Maharit distinction. A person may voluntarily say, “I robbed you,” and within the same statement add, “and I returned it before this admission.” He is believed through the mouth that created the obligation, even though if he waited until later to say that the property had already been returned before his admission, he would not be believed against his own earlier unqualified confession. But if witnesses establish that he robbed publicly, and if public robbery had to be returned before witnesses, then he could no longer say that he returned it privately. The two clauses would fit the Mishnah perfectly. Since the Gemara does not use this example, the Ramah’s inference that public robbery also need not be returned before witnesses retains force. One may still reject the proof on a different ground, based on what I explained in the rules of migo, seif katan 82 and 83, regarding Tosafot’s intent. The Mishnah’s latter clause seeks witnesses analogous to the witnesses of the bridal canopy in the first case, meaning two witnesses specifically. In a robbery case, however, even one witness would be enough to prevent the defendant from retaining the property if he does not contradict that witness. The case would be exactly like the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba. Therefore the Mishnah could not formulate the contrast as “but if there are witnesses,” implying two are uniquely needed. The same answers why it did not say, “You lent me one hundred within the repayment period and I repaid it.” In that setting too a single witness could affect the result, depending on the precise plea, whereas the Mishnah sought a case parallel to its first clause where two witnesses are the decisive evidentiary threshold. One might object that Rav and Shmuel disagree with the silver ingot principle. This is not a difficulty for the Rambam’s method, because Rav and Shmuel can concede that the matter is disputed among Tannaim, as Tosafot explicitly write regarding the Mishnah of borrowed and rented animals, which is established according to the Tanna who applies “since he cannot swear, he must pay.” The same is possible here. Thus the Ramah’s proof is not conclusive from the Mishnah’s formulation alone, even though it remains suggestive. In practice, however, all the later authorities agree with the Ramban and the Ramah that one who robbed before witnesses need not return before witnesses, and most early authorities also hold this way. I nevertheless have not fully understood the conceptual basis. We rule above in siman 79 that if a defendant tells beit din that he paid a particular hundred and witnesses then establish that he had not paid it at that time, he becomes established as a false denier regarding that same hundred and is no longer believed later to say that he paid it. If a person’s attempt to delay payment by a false statement before beit din is enough to remove his future credibility regarding that money, then certainly one who has been conclusively established by witnesses as an actual robber of that very money or object should seem to lose credibility to say that he later returned it. One might answer that in siman 79 the decisive fact is not merely wrongdoing but being caught in a false statement. A robber is established as one who takes another’s property, not necessarily as one who lies about later events. But this is not fully satisfactory. A robber is disqualified from testimony and from oaths, which shows that halakhah does treat him as lacking ordinary credibility. Why then should he be believed regarding the very object he was proven to have stolen? Perhaps the rule in siman 79 is not a pure evidentiary principle at all but a penalty imposed by the Sages. A person who brazenly lies before beit din about payment is penalized through the authority of beit din and loses the ability to make a later payment plea regarding that obligation. A robber, though wicked, did not necessarily commit that particular affront to beit din, and the Sages did not impose the same procedural penalty. This remains strained and requires study.
