The Sema infers that the Tur holds a robber who took property before witnesses need not return it before witnesses, and therefore can later say, "I purchased it," through a migo that he returned it. The Shach agrees. Knesset HaGedolah cites Tosafot in the case of the orphans' shepherd as proof that a robber who took before witnesses must return before witnesses. Tosafot says that once witnesses establish forcible seizure, the person can no longer claim purchase. If return did not require witnesses, why would he not have a migo that he returned it? The case must be read with present observation. The disputed ox was seen in his possession when the case came to court. If there had been no present observation, even animals that ordinarily lack a possession presumption could be defended through the migo of return. The Gemara's question from Reish Lakish therefore itself proves that the ox was presently seen. Once the court both knows the earlier forcible seizure and sees the same object still in his hand, the migo of return disappears. This reconciles Tosafot and Rosh with the Tur and Sema. A robber need not generally return before witnesses; the problem in that sugya was present observation. The same answers the question from a Rosh responsum concerning one who seized another's object because of a claimed debt. Such a person is not automatically treated as a robber and may still say he returned it where there is no present observation. The apparent contradiction disappears.
