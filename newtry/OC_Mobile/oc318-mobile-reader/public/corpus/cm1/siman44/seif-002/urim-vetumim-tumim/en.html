Mechaber's wording appears to say that even where the document is signed by witnesses, if the writing itself is in the debtor's handwriting it is valid. That is difficult. In a witness-signed document we must fear collusion against purchasers. Suppose the witnesses leave blank space and the debtor later adds language favorable to the creditor. Or suppose the document was prepared in Nisan for a contemplated loan that did not occur until Tishrei. The debtor can later alter the amount from one hundred to two hundred in his own handwriting, or alter the date to an earlier time and confirm the erasure in the last line. Because the writing is genuinely his, ordinary handwriting verification will not expose the manipulation. The creditor can then collect improperly from purchasers. Rashba's responsum appears to speak only of a document entirely in the debtor's own handwriting, without witness signatures. Such a handwritten obligation ordinarily collects only from the debtor himself and lacks purchaser-lien force. There, once we know the additions are truly in the debtor's handwriting, the ordinary forgery concern largely disappears. But a formal witness-signed instrument has third-party consequences. The debtor and creditor can collude to alter it to the detriment of purchasers. Accordingly, Mechaber's broad wording should be limited. The leniency is sound for an ordinary handwritten obligation enforceable only against the debtor. For a witness-signed document intended to collect from encumbered property, substantial concern remains and the matter requires study.
