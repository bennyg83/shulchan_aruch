They must then both sign again. A later signature by only one witness does not suffice. This differs from the case below, siman 45, seif 21. There the deed itself is written on paper, and the notation between the witnesses is merely for the debtor’s protection, to prevent the creditor from forging the instrument. Since no present defect appears and no forgery can result, one signature may suffice. Here the notation benefits the creditor. Perhaps the deed was originally written entirely on paper, or perhaps the erased places contained a clause adverse to the creditor, and only the first witness signed the corrective notation. Rashba’s proof from Bava Batra 164a is not decisive. That passage can be explained as requiring the witnesses to write, before signing and on the paper itself, that they signed on paper while the deed was written over an erasure. If such a declaration is absent, the deed is invalid. According to Tosafot as well, the Gemara could have answered that the declaration was written above on the erased portion. Rivash records that the Raah disagrees with Rashba and appears inclined to his view. The Raah’s own answer is strained, whereas the explanation given here appears clear.
