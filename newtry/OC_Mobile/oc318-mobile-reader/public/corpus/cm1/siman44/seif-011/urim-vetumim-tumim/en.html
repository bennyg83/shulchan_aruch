And both of them should sign again. Mechaber writes that ideally both witnesses should sign the confirmation, because even Rashba agrees in responsum that this is preferable and removes all doubt. But after the fact, if the confirmation is written between the first witness and the second and is in the handwriting of the second witness, Rashba validates it. Certainly, where the holder of the document is already in possession, he can say kim li like Rashba. Shach's attempt to reject Rashba does not appear correct. Rashba's principal proof is from Bava Batra 164b. The Gemara discusses a document written on erased parchment with the witnesses signing on ordinary parchment. It asks why such a document cannot be validated by writing between the first and second witness: "We, the witnesses, signed on ordinary parchment and the document is on erased parchment." Rashba reasons: What would that accomplish? Even after the notation, perhaps the entire original document had been written on ordinary parchment and the holder later erased it and rewrote it. The only thing establishing the special arrangement is the notation written by one witness between the two signatures. If one witness were not believed for this type of technical document confirmation, the notation should accomplish nothing. Therefore, in a matter whose function is only to establish the physical condition and integrity of the document, one witness can be relied upon. Shach answers that there is no proof. Perhaps the Gemara means the notation should be written before either witness signs, so that both witnesses subsequently sign beneath it. Then the confirmation is included in the signed document and there is no reliance on a single witness. This answer is strained. The baraita is teaching the status of a document that comes before us in a particular physical form: the document on erased parchment and the witnesses on ordinary parchment. It is not principally teaching how to draft a document prospectively. If we are free to redesign the document before signing, countless easy solutions exist. The debtor or the witnesses could write a line from the body of the document in handwriting above the signatures. Or they could use a special formula, mark the parchment, or otherwise make repeat erasure detectable. The baraita's point is the legal status of the form before us, not a catalogue of drafting fixes. The Gemara's question therefore means: Why not permit the case where, between the two existing witnesses, one witness writes the explanatory confirmation? That is how Rashba reads it. There is another indication. The Gemara frames the comparison through the first clause and second clause of the baraita: a document on ordinary parchment with witnesses on erased parchment is valid; a document on erased parchment with witnesses on ordinary parchment is invalid. It asks why the second case cannot be repaired in the same structural manner. If Shach's reading were correct, the answer would merely be that in the second case both witnesses had already signed without adding the needed formula. That would not explain the asymmetry in the document forms themselves. Rashba's reading better preserves the Gemara's question. Raah, who disagrees with Rashba, does not give Shach's simple answer. He says only that the Gemara could in principle have established the case differently. That omission is significant. It suggests the obvious "write it before both signatures" answer was not viewed as satisfactory. There is an even deeper reason. Anything written before the witnesses' signatures as part of the instrument's validation belongs to the document itself. A clause such as "not as an asmachta" or other protective wording is considered part of the document's operative text. If the sentence, "we the witnesses signed on ordinary parchment while the document is erased," is written before the signatures, it too becomes part of the document. Then the baraita's description, "the document is on erased parchment and the witnesses are on ordinary parchment," is no longer exact if that validation clause appears on ordinary parchment. Part of the document itself would be on ordinary parchment. The baraita sounds as though the entire document text is on the erased area and only the witness signatures are on ordinary parchment. Thus the explanatory clause must itself be placed within the witness-signature area, not in the operative body before both signatures. This directly undermines Shach's reading and supports Rashba. Tosafot's discussion nearby can also be read in Rashba's direction. They ask whether one can manipulate a document whose text is on ordinary parchment and witnesses on erased parchment by erasing and rewriting. Their answers rely on the physical difference between first and second erasures and on how the witnesses sign. This shows that the legal system does rely on technical physical indicators of document integrity. A single witness's notation about such an indicator can therefore be credible even though one witness cannot independently extract money by ordinary testimony. This is because the witness is not supplying the underlying debt testimony. The signed document already does that. He is only establishing a technical fact that removes a suspicion of forgery. The Torah's rule that one witness cannot extract money does not mean every factual matter related to a monetary document requires two witnesses. Where the court merely faces a possible forgery scenario, the presumption that a Jewish witness is upright can suffice to remove the suspicion. This becomes clearer from another possible fraud. Suppose the first witness signed on erased parchment. Between the first and second witnesses there is enough space for the notation, "we the witnesses signed on erased parchment." The second witness writes that notation and then signs. If we say a single witness cannot be trusted for this technical confirmation, the entire structure fails. But the first witness himself is also involved. He does not ordinarily hand over a defective paper and blindly trust the second witness to complete it improperly. Witnesses are presumed to supervise one another in the document they jointly execute. This provides an additional presumption that the notation is genuine. Tosafot in Bava Batra 163b effectively rely on this concept when they say one witness does not ordinarily hand over a defective instrument expecting the second to fix it later without oversight. The wording there is singular because the first witness does not relinquish a defective paper. This mutual supervision strengthens Rashba's rule. Even if only the second witness physically writes the confirmation, the signed instrument as a whole was executed under the oversight of both. There is another proof from the first clause of the baraita. A document on ordinary parchment with both witnesses on erased parchment can be valid if the appropriate indication appears between them. Suppose we refuse to trust one witness regarding that indication. The holder could cut away the document together with one signature, erase the text, rewrite a larger amount, and separately manipulate the area between the witnesses. The only thing preventing this is the technical fact established by the second witness's writing and signature that the witness area had already been erased in the required manner. Again the document depends on one witness for a technical anti-forgery fact. If that were categorically insufficient, the baraita's validation structure could hardly function. One should distinguish carefully between "one witness cannot extract money" and "one witness is never trusted regarding a physical feature of a document." The former is a Torah rule governing substantive testimony. The latter is false. Halachah routinely relies on one trustworthy person for matters of identity, status, and technical verification where the substantive monetary obligation is already established independently. Here the underlying debt arises from the document and both signatures. The second witness's notation merely confirms that the apparent erasure is not a fraudulent alteration. There is no independent extraction of money based solely on his word. Rivash, responsum 49, places an important limitation on Rashba. The rule works only where the confirmation is recognizably in the handwriting of the second witness himself. Otherwise the holder could insert a forged statement between the signatures. That safeguard removes the principal fraud concern. Thus Rashba's ruling is not a blanket acceptance of any one-witness notation. It is a specific case where the notation is physically tied to the second witness's known handwriting and integrated into the witness-signature area. That is a strong evidentiary structure. There is also support from Tosafot's question about the size of the gap between the witnesses. According to Ribam, even where the document and witnesses are all on erased parchment, a substantial gap between the first and second witness can create concern that the document was originally on ordinary parchment and later erased. Tosafot answer by measuring how much room the explanatory notation occupies. Maharsha explains that if the gap were large enough for two full lines, the holder could create a new two-line document and one witness. Why do Tosafot focus on the size of the technical notation rather than simply say the second witness's presence is insufficient because he is only one witness? Because they accept that the second witness's technical confirmation can matter. Their concern is only whether enough blank space remains for an actual new forgery. This fits Rashba perfectly. One may still ask: If one witness is enough to establish a technical document fact, why should we require both witnesses to sign ideally? The answer is obvious. Two signatures remove all doubt. A technical presumption strong enough after the fact is not necessarily the preferred drafting method. Rashba himself says that ideally both witnesses should sign the confirmation. Mechaber therefore correctly requires both initially while validating Rashba's one-witness structure after the fact. This also explains why a document in which the confirmation appears only in the handwriting of an unrelated third person would not be comparable. The second witness is part of the original witnessing unit and his handwriting is physically integrated between the two signatures. An outsider's note lacks that connection. Similarly, if the notation were written long after the original signatures, one would need proof that the witness wrote it as part of the original execution rather than as a later private statement. The law is strongest where the structure of the paper itself shows contemporaneous integration. There is one more conceptual point. The decree requiring two witnesses for money limits beit din from believing one witness as the sole basis for imposing liability. It does not erase ordinary presumptions of honesty. When the court faces only a remote forgery concern, one valid witness can remove that concern because the monetary liability is not being created by him. This is analogous to many rules where one witness does not directly award money but can destroy a migo, clarify identity, or establish a procedural fact that allows an already-existing document or obligation to operate. The same principle applies here. Thus, even if one rejects some of Rashba's precise proofs, his underlying legal distinction is sound. Accordingly, I would rule as follows: Initially, both witnesses should sign the confirmation. After the fact, if the confirmation appears between the witnesses and is clearly in the handwriting of the second witness, the document is valid according to Rashba. The holder can certainly rely on that view where he is already in possession. Shach's rejection is not compelling. Indeed, Tosafot and the structure of the sugya provide substantial support for Rashba.
