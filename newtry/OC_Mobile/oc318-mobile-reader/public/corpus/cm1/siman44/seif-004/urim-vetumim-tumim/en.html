Where he wrote "valid and confirmed," etc. Shach devotes considerable discussion to this issue. Since we rule that failure to write "valid and confirmed" does not automatically invalidate a document, what does the formula accomplish? Perhaps the original scribe did not write it at all, and the holder later forged material in the final line and then added "valid and confirmed." Ramban already raised this challenge against Rashbam, and several authorities follow him. Yet Mordechai cites Raavyah as validating a document where the formula appears, while Raavan disagrees because sometimes scribes omit it and a forger could then add it. This shows Raavyah himself agrees that omission of the formula does not necessarily invalidate the instrument, yet he still treats its actual presence as significant. Rambam appears to do the same. He never says a document is invalid merely because it lacks "valid and confirmed." Nevertheless, in Hilchot Malveh 27 he rules that if an erasure in the line before the last is large enough to have contained that formula, the document is invalid, lest the holder have erased a detrimental clause and then written a validating formula. This strongly implies that when "valid and confirmed" appears in the proper place, the court can ordinarily rely on the final line. Bach likewise understands Rambam this way. How can we answer the forgery concern? The best explanation is the one given above from Ran concerning Persian documents. Even where scribes are not absolutely required to repeat the document or write a validating formula, prevailing practice matters. If a document comes before us bearing the customary closing formula, we presume the scribe followed normal practice and wrote it originally. We do not invent a scenario in which this particular scribe deviated from the overwhelming custom by leaving the space blank and the holder later forged both new content and the standard closing. Halachah regularly follows majority practice in such matters. The formula "valid and confirmed" was a deeply established scribal custom. Tosafot compare it to the familiar formula "according to the law of Moshe and Israel." The Mishnah at the end of Yadayim and the Yerushalmi cited by Ramban both show that this closing language was broadly used in documents. Therefore, when it appears, the stronger presumption is that the original scribe wrote it. This is all the more persuasive than Ran's Persian-document case. There, the foreign scribes may not even understand our protective document conventions, yet where the paper contains a clear concluding repetition Ran treats the ending as recognizable and does not automatically fear insertion above it. In a Jewish document, where professional scribes regularly use the standard closing formula, the presumption is stronger. One may ask: if the formula is not legally indispensable, why should a majority custom suffice against a possible forgery? Because documentary law constantly relies on normal scribal practice unless a concrete defect appears. We assume witnesses sign properly, dates are inserted according to ordinary rules, erasures are confirmed according to custom, and standard clauses were not secretly fabricated merely because deviation is theoretically possible. A bare possibility of forgery does not overcome established practice. Accordingly, where "valid and confirmed" appears in its usual location, we may treat it as the authentic closing of the original document. The last line before it can then be read according to the ordinary rules. Raavan's concern remains understandable. Because omission does not invalidate the paper, a particular scribe could in fact have left out the formula. He therefore refuses to rely on its later presence. But practical halachah can follow Raavyah and Rambam based on majority scribal usage. This is especially appropriate where local custom is well established. Ramban himself writes that when the halachah is uncertain, one should follow the prevailing custom. Here the custom that scribes close documents with the standard validating formula supplies the needed presumption. Thus the mere theoretical possibility that the holder forged both the substantive line and the closing formula is not enough to invalidate an otherwise regular document.
