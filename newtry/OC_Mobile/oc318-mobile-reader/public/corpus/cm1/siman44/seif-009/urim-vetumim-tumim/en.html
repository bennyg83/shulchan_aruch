Nowadays, etc. The wording is awkward because the Mechaber follows and copies the language of the Tur. The Beit Yosef understands the Tur's wording to mean that under the original Talmudic law the phrase "valid and established" did not help, because a document lacking that phrase was still valid. Therefore, even if the phrase appeared, nothing could be learned from the final line, since perhaps the holder himself added the phrase. Only nowadays, when the accepted and unvarying practice is to write "valid and established," do we learn from the final line. That explanation works for the Tur. The Mechaber, however, ruled above in seif 4 that even under the Talmudic law, if "valid and established" is written, one may learn from the final line. What then does "nowadays" add? One possibility is that although the Mechaber adopted that ruling, many authorities disagree. Scribes therefore previously did not place validations in the final line, because the document might come before a judge who followed the dissenting authorities, or the debtor might plead that he follows those authorities. Nowadays, however, established custom gives the practice full force, and one may validate a correction in the final line without concern. Alternatively, even under the rule that one may learn from the final line when "valid and established" appears, that rule may originally have depended on the majority of documents concluding with that phrase. We would assume that the phrase marks the actual end of the document. Yet the normal practice was also that any correction or validation had to appear before the concluding formula, because the formula itself completed the document. Therefore, although one could learn substantive content from the final line, a validation still had to precede the formal return clause. If it came afterward, the majority presumption was weakened, for most documents were validated before the concluding language. Maharsha explicitly explains Tosafos in Bava Batra 162 this way. Nowadays, by contrast, the concluding phrase is indispensable by custom, so a validation may be written even after the return clause without relying merely on a majority presumption. This requires study.
Therefore, etc. The Shach's view appears to be that if a document comes before us without the phrase "valid and established" and with one blank line before the witnesses, it is entirely invalid, because it is susceptible to forgery: one could write whatever he wishes in the blank line and then add the concluding formula. If there is no concluding formula but the witnesses are signed immediately adjacent to the text with no blank line, then according to the Mechaber and those who follow Ramban, who do not invalidate every document merely because it lacks a formal return clause, one might say that the document is valid but nothing can be learned from the final line. This requires study, because the Shulchan Aruch's language does not appear that way. It seems that nowadays the entire document is invalid. This is what the authorities write: nowadays a document lacking the concluding formula is invalid. Since the witnesses do not write the document themselves, but rather a scribe does, if we occasionally validate a document without the concluding phrase, scribes will rely upon that and omit it, assuming that the witnesses will sign closely. The witnesses, following ordinary practice, may leave space, and this will create an opportunity for forgery. The ruling therefore requires study, but the language of the Shulchan Aruch implies total invalidity. The Shach further writes that many people err by validating a correction after the words "valid and established." That certainly does not help. If the correction is detrimental to the holder of the document, however, one might argue that it should be effective, since there is no concern of forgery. Yet Tosafos in Bava Batra 161, discussing why a tied document must always conclude with the validating phrase, implies that even detrimental material written after the conclusion lacks force. Otherwise one could imagine that the original document ended properly, a blank line remained, and afterward a detrimental term was written there before the witnesses signed. The fact that Tosafos does not rely upon such a possibility suggests that once the concluding phrase is required as the formal end of the document, anything written after it, even to the holder's detriment, is not treated as part of the document. One could respond that a holder who writes a detrimental term in a place from which it can easily be cut away has harmed himself, and we are not responsible for protecting him. But this is strained, and the first explanation appears primary. The Bach was uncertain whether this custom applies only in a place where people are known to understand that a document lacking "valid and established" is invalid. The Shach writes that it obviously does require such a local presumption and criticizes the Bach. In my view the Bach's doubt is sound. Above, the matter depends on witnesses, whether they know to sign close enough to the document, and ordinary witnesses are not necessarily experts in document law. Here, by contrast, the requirement of the concluding phrase is the responsibility of the professional scribe. The scribe is specifically appointed to prepare documents correctly, and professional scribes are generally presumed to know their craft until the contrary is shown. The Shach equated witnesses and scribes, but they are not comparable. Witnesses may be whoever happens to be available, while a scribe is selected for this work. Certainly according to the Mechaber's view that a document is not intrinsically invalid merely because the phrase is absent, and the phrase matters only for learning from the final line, there is no need for a separate local presumption.
And if "valid and established" is written twice, the document is valid. Why do we not suspect that the original document ended one line earlier with the first concluding formula, and that the holder later inserted whatever he wished in the last line and added a second formula? The Sema explains that the case concerns only one blank line, not two. The Shach already objects that the Shulchan Aruch should not have stated the rule without qualification, especially according to the Sema's own view that even a one line gap may sometimes be prohibited as a safeguard against a larger gap. It therefore appears clear that when the Shulchan Aruch says the document is valid, it does not mean that the final line is necessarily usable. Rather, it means that the document itself is not invalid. Earlier the Shulchan Aruch said that a document lacking the accepted concluding formula is invalid in its entirety because it was not prepared according to the established form. Here, where two formulas appear, the scribe at least complied with the requirement to include the formula. Even if the holder later added the second one, the original document had been prepared properly. We therefore need not invalidate the entire document. At most, we refuse to derive new content from the final line because of the possibility of forgery. The document itself remains valid. This is also the proper understanding of Rabbi Yehudah Barceloni's rule. Where the last line merely repeats the preceding material and adds no new substance, the concern is only whether the scribe complied with the formal requirement. If there are two concluding formulas, the document as a whole remains valid even though the final line is not relied upon. This is straightforward.
