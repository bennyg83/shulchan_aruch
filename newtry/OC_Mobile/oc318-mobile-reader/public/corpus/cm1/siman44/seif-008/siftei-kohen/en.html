The size of the words 'firm and established.' Less than that amount is valid, because if those words had been written their erasure would be recognizable. Yet why should the law depend on the erasure being in their usual place or of their exact size? If the authentication of erasures appears in the final line, perhaps the witnesses left that entire line blank; an erasure anywhere in the deed, or even a smaller one, should then invalidate. The Talmud states this rule concerning a folded deed, where 'firm and established' was routinely written and the final line was usable. In an ordinary deed the difficulty remains. The Bach attempts to align the Tur and Rambam with Rashbam's view that the final line is usable when 'firm and established' appears, contrary to the Maggid Mishneh. But I have shown that the Tur and Rambam do not follow Rashbam and that even when those words appear the final line ordinarily is not used. The Maggid Mishneh is therefore correct that erasures cannot be authenticated in the final line. This restores the question: why only an erasure in the place or of the size of 'firm and established'? The Tur is especially difficult because he also decrees a spaced case because of an unspaced one. The entire section works readily under today's custom, where every deed must contain 'firm and established' and omission invalidates it, but its precise Talmudic formulation requires study.
Lest he erased, etc. The Sema says the deed is invalid even if no advantage to the holder is presently written in the erased place, because he may have erased an obligation above or could later insert a benefit, making it a document capable of forgery. That reasoning is unpersuasive: it is not presently capable of forgery, because any such alteration would itself invalidate it. If the erased content cannot be identified, the deed is invalid for the simpler reason that the erased matter may have contained a clause nullifying the instrument, while the authentication in the final line is ineffective. If the erased matter remains evident, for example it was crossed out with a pen, then only the final line's authentication is disregarded. The crossed out adverse clause remains effective against the holder, but the entire deed is not invalid merely because the holder could have altered it, since an actual alteration would invalidate it.
This applies where the witnesses are presumed knowledgeable, etc. It follows that elsewhere too we may fear they left two lines. The rule that only the final line is disregarded applies where the witnesses are known to understand the law; otherwise even two or more lines cannot be relied upon. Likewise, whenever the law assumes that witnesses acted properly, this applies only where they are presumed knowledgeable concerning the particular rule. The same applies in the next seif. The Bach's discussion does not persuade me.
