This applies when there is no blank space, etc. Shach asks against Tur. Tur holds elsewhere that even where one full blank line separates the last written line from the witnesses, we still do not derive law from the last line because Chazal decreed uniformly, lest we confuse a spaced document with one that is not spaced. Why then does Tur distinguish here and say the concern applies only when there is no blank line? The answer is that the two rules arise from different legal structures. The rule that we do not derive law from the last line is a formal rabbinic enactment. Once Chazal enacted that rights are not derived from that line, they applied the rule uniformly and did not distinguish between cases. Here, however, the issue is not a formal enactment against using the line. The document contains the customary formula "valid and confirmed," which ordinarily allows reliance upon the preceding content. The concern arises only because an erasure appears in the line before it. We fear perhaps the holder erased the formula itself and then wrote some favorable matter there. This is a factual suspicion, not a categorical decree. If one full blank line separates the suspicious erasure from the signatures, we effectively know the holder could not have erased the closing formula and replaced it in the feared manner, because scribes do not ordinarily leave two blank lines. The concrete concern disappears. Once it disappears, the ordinary rule governing a document properly closed with "valid and confirmed" returns, just as in a folded document where the closing formula allows reliance on the concluding area. Thus there is no contradiction. The last-line rule is a uniform enactment. The erasure concern is evidence-based and can disappear when the spacing itself excludes the suspected forgery.
