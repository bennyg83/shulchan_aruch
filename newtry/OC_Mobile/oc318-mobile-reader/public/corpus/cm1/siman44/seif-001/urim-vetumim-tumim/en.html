Because we do not derive law from the last line. Sema writes that even if one full blank line separates the text from the witnesses, we still do not derive law from the last written line. Although such a blank makes it clear that the witnesses did not crowd their signatures immediately beneath the text, Chazal made a uniform rule and did not distinguish. Shach says this depends on the dispute among the Rishonim. According to Rosh and those who hold that failure to repeat the substance of the document in the last line can invalidate the entire document because it was not executed according to rabbinic form, the last line is never relied upon. But according to Ramban and those who say repeating the substance is only good advice and does not invalidate the whole paper, Shach argues that where there is no realistic forgery concern, we may derive law from the last line. He says he discussed this at length elsewhere, though that work is not available to us. In my view Sema is correct. If Chazal's only concern were the physical gap, they could have enacted the simpler rule: leave one blank line between the text and the signatures. Witnesses already tend not to sign pressed tightly against the text. Instead, Chazal required the last line itself to repeat the substance of the document. That indicates a categorical legal rule concerning the evidentiary use of the last line, not merely advice about spacing. Shach cites Nimukei Yosef that the repetition was not enacted as an absolute condition invalidating the whole document but merely as protective advice. That does not prove the last line itself becomes usable when the whole document remains valid. There are two separate questions: whether failure to repeat invalidates the entire instrument; and whether independent new content written in the last line can be relied upon. Nimukei Yosef can reject total invalidation while still accepting the uniform rule that new rights are not derived from the last line. I find a strong proof from Tosafot in Bava Metzia 7b. Rashi says that, for some purposes, the operative core of a document does not have an advantage over the standard formula because the names of lender and borrower and the amount are repeated in the concluding line. Tosafot object: we do not derive law from the last line. Now suppose Rosh's theory were correct that if the concluding line failed to repeat the document's substance, the entire paper was invalid. Then Rashi's answer would be strong. When a document comes before us with the final line saying, "So-and-so borrowed from so-and-so such-and-such amount," we could infer that the same information must also have appeared above. Otherwise the concluding line would contain new matter instead of repetition, and the whole document would be invalid. Since witnesses are presumed to act properly, we would know the lender, borrower, and amount necessarily appeared in the main text. Thus one could indirectly learn the operative content through the final line even though one does not directly derive rights from it. Tosafot's objection would fail. Their objection therefore implies they do not accept the theory that failure to repeat invalidates the entire document. Indeed, Nimukei Yosef cites Ri, the author of Tosafot, as following Ramban that the omission does not wholly invalidate the paper. That is consistent. But this very Tosafot also supports Sema. If the legal rule were that we may rely on the last line whenever there is a blank line and no forgery concern, Tosafot could not simply object categorically, "we do not derive from the last line." The case in Rashi can readily involve a blank space, because witnesses ordinarily leave some room. The fact that Tosafot state the rule broadly shows they understand it as categorical. Accordingly, even under Ramban, where the whole document remains valid, new content in the last line is not ordinarily used to establish rights. One can therefore accept Ramban on total validity while agreeing with Sema on the evidentiary status of the last line. Shach's own later view actually points this way, for he writes that Rosh may not invalidate the whole instrument after all, yet still treats the last line as unusable. That should have led him to the same conclusion here. There remains a possible refinement. Where the last line contains material favorable to the debtor rather than the holder of the document, there may be stronger reason to use it because forgery by the holder is not suspected in the same way. That issue is discussed below. But as a general rule, the last line is not a source for new rights merely because a blank line happened to remain before the signatures. Sema's rule is therefore compelled by the structure of the enactment and by Tosafot.
Because we do not derive from it, etc. Shach cites Rashba that even if the original document witnesses themselves say, "We wrote the document and also wrote this last line," the additional matter has only the status of an oral loan because it is not evident from the document itself. In my view this depends on the dispute discussed below in siman 49 concerning two people with the same name. If the original witnesses say, "This is the person for whom we wrote the document," some authorities, following Rashba, are uncertain whether that oral identification can restore full written-loan status when the identity is not evident from the paper itself. Under that approach, the same should be true here. The witnesses can establish the last-line matter orally, but it remains only an oral claim. According to Rambam, however, and Mechaber below, the witnesses' clarification can create full documentary effect because they could write another proper document. Their original agency was not completed correctly, since they should have written the identifying or beneficial matter in a valid place. The same logic applies here. If the witnesses improperly placed material in the unusable last line, they did not fully perform the task entrusted to them. They should have written that material before the last line and then repeated the substance afterward. Therefore, if they remain competent to issue a replacement document, their testimony can support full written-loan status. The distinction is that below, in the same-name case, the document generally contains a kinyan. Then a corrected instrument can be written from the original date without becoming antedated. Here, if there was no kinyan, writing a fresh document from the original date would create an antedated paper. They can therefore write only prospectively. Accordingly, the last-line matter cannot gain the original lien date even if their oral testimony is believed. This is why Rashba can say it has only oral-loan status as to the original date. Thus the two positions can be reconciled substantially through whether the original lien can be recreated without antedating.
But if the last line is to the detriment of the holder, etc. Darkei Moshe cites this from Nimukei Yosef, though Tosafot already state it explicitly in Bava Batra 162b. Tosafot explain why scribes often write "valid and confirmed" at the end of a document. Sometimes a condition unfavorable to the holder must be written in the last line. If an erasure appears there, the court must know whether a detrimental clause was erased. Therefore the final validating formula is needed to confirm any erasure in that area. This indicates that matter unfavorable to the holder can be effective even in the last line. At first glance, however, Tosafot earlier in Bava Batra 161b seem difficult. The Gemara asks concerning a folded document: perhaps the holder will cut off the last line and thereby validate an otherwise problematic witness signature. Tosafot ask: but the last line merely repeats the substance of the document and we do not derive rights from it. Why would cutting it matter? They add that if it is cut off, the paper itself can become invalid because the required repetition is missing. Why did Tosafot not ask more simply: whether he cuts the last line or leaves it, nothing written there can have legal effect because we never derive from the last line? The answer must be that detrimental matter can indeed be written there. The holder may therefore wish to cut it away. One could say Tosafot's "cutting" refers not only to the final line but also to the line before it, but that is unnecessary. A better reading is that there are two stages. Before the document has reached its formal conclusion through the repeated clause, the last line can still contain substantive material, including something unfavorable to the holder. The witnesses then repeat the substance and thereby close the document. But once the document has already been formally concluded through the repetition, anything later is outside the instrument altogether. Even a detrimental notation written after that point is not derived from the document. The holder can say it was written as a joke, inadvertently, or for some unrelated purpose. This must also be the view of Rashbam, who says even detrimental matter is not learned from the last line in a completed document. Maharsha and Shach below in siman 45 imply that Rashbam's case is one where the holder does not admit the notation was intended seriously. If he admits it, his admission of course binds him independently. Thus Tosafot need not disagree sharply with Rashbam. They can agree that once the document has already been formally closed, later writing is outside the instrument. Their rule that detrimental material can appear in the last line applies before the final repeated clause has completed the document. This reading also explains Tosafot's earlier question. If the last line contains only the required repetition and the holder cuts it off, the document can become invalid because the witnesses did not properly close it. If the holder had written a detrimental clause there before the required repetition, that clause could matter and he might be tempted to cut it. Once the repetition follows, however, everything above it remains part of the document and everything below is outside. From this we learn a further point. Tosafot in 162b say the validating formula is needed because an erasure in the final area might have contained a detrimental clause. This implies that beneficial matter for the holder is not learned there even before the repetition, because of the ordinary forgery concern. Although the witnesses are expected to repeat the document's substance afterward, Chazal made a uniform rule against using the final line for holder-beneficial additions. Why? Because if we allowed such additions when a proper repetition later follows, we would have to distinguish carefully between cases where the repetition is genuine and cases where the holder forged the additional line and manipulated the ending. Chazal instead treated the last line uniformly. Detrimental material is different because the holder has no incentive to forge against himself. Therefore the anti-forgery decree does not apply in the same way before formal closure. This supports the broader principle explained above: we do not derive favorable rights for the holder from the final line, even where spacing appears safe. Now consider Ran's discussion of Persian documents in Gittin. The Gemara asks why such documents are valid if their scribes do not observe the rabbinic practice of repeating the substance in the last line. Ran answers that Persian scribes may leave several blank lines and are not meticulous about our spacing rules. If so, there is concern the holder can insert additional lines. But where the document itself contains an obvious repetition of its substance, that repetition marks the true end of the instrument. Anything written below it is suspicious. Anything above it can be read as part of the original paper. This seems to support using material before the final repetition even where ordinary Jewish witnesses left extra space. If Persian documents can be saved by an internal marker showing the real ending, all the more so Jewish documents, whose witnesses normally know the repetition rule, should be reliable above that marker. Still, Ran's rule requires study. Perhaps he speaks only in a setting where delivery witnesses independently establish the transaction, and the remaining concern is merely that the Persian scribes might have left space for later additions. If the repetition makes the true ending visibly recognizable, we no longer fear that particular forgery. That does not necessarily prove every line above the repetition has full evidentiary force under our ordinary document rules. Yet it gives substantial support to the idea that the repetition functions as a formal boundary. This also illuminates the dispute whether failure to repeat invalidates the whole document or merely prevents use of the last line. If the repetition is a mandatory legal condition, omission can invalidate the entire instrument. If it is primarily protective advice, omission leaves the paper valid but deprives the final line of evidentiary force. Either way, once the repetition is actually present, it strongly marks the endpoint. Accordingly, the proper synthesis is: Matter favorable to the holder is not ordinarily derived from the last line. Matter detrimental to the holder can be effective when written as part of the document before its formal conclusion, because forgery against oneself is not suspected. After the document has already been closed through its concluding repetition, later writing is outside the instrument, and even detrimental matter has no documentary force unless the holder independently admits it. This reconciles Tosafot, Rashbam, Nimukei Yosef, and Ran with minimal dispute.
