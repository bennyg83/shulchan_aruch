And if it was not confirmed, the erasures do not invalidate, etc. Shach, seif katan 14, objects to this ruling and says it does not appear correct in practice. Rashba brings proof from the Tosefta at the end of Bava Batra: an erasure in the essential body of the document invalidates, while an erasure outside the essential body does not. Shach explains that the Tosefta speaks where the erasure is recognizable. This is difficult. If the erasure is recognizable, why should even an erasure in the essential text invalidate? The Mishnah itself speaks of a document reading, "zuzim which are..." where the amount was erased. That is in the essential body of the document. Yet the law is not that the entire document becomes invalid; rather, the creditor receives only the minimum amount, two zuzim. If so, why distinguish between essential text and standard form? If one wishes to reject Rashba's proof, the better answer is that the Tosefta is not dealing with a concern that perhaps some condition or qualification was written and then erased. Rather, the original document witnesses themselves testify how they signed and state that there was no forgery. In that setting, an erasure in the standard form can be tolerated because there is no concern of falsification. An erasure in the essential operative portion remains invalid because, even with the witnesses' testimony, the matter is no longer evident from the document itself. Thus the Tosefta may address the documentary form rather than the fear of actual forgery. The dispute between Rashba and Tur appears to parallel the dispute of Rashi and Tosafot in Bava Metzia 7b. Rashi explains the case where one litigant is holding the standard form and the other the essential portion of the document as though neither has greater value, because either portion can still support collection. Tosafot object: how can one collect from the essential portion when the standard form is missing? Even if only one line of the document was erased, the paper can be invalid. All the more so when the whole standard form is absent. It appears Rashi follows Rashba. He holds that erasure or loss of the standard form does not by itself create a presumption that some condition or limitation was there. We do not invent such a defect, and therefore the essential operative portion can still support collection. Tosafot, however, follow Nimukei Yosef and Tur. Once part of the standard form is missing, we fear that a condition or other relevant matter may have been there. There is no material difference between a section being cut away and being erased. This also explains why Ramban, in Shitah Mekubetzet, agrees with Rashi's reading and does not raise Tosafot's objection. He is following the Rashba-type approach. Accordingly, since Rashi and Ramban support Rashba, Shach's rejection should not be treated as decisive. Rivash, responsum 383, tries to reinterpret Rashi differently, but his explanation is strained. See further what I wrote below in siman 52.
And some say that if there was an erasure, etc., it is invalid. One should not object against this view from Gittin 17b, where the Gemara worries that a husband might cut off the date from a get and then deliver it. If cutting away part of a document created a general fear that perhaps some condition or qualification had been written there, the get should become invalid. We generally do not assume a person will perform an act that makes the get invalid, and all the more so where the result is clearly invalid. Rabbenu Tam indeed proves from that sugya that cutting parchment from a get does not automatically invalidate it. Rashba similarly asks: if the date was cut off, how do we know a date was ever there? Perhaps some other text was removed. He answers that lack of a date does not invalidate the get after the fact. According to the stricter view of Nimukei Yosef, however, a missing section should itself create concern that a condition may have been written there. The proper explanation is that when the Gemara says he "cuts off the date," it means the cut itself shows that what was removed was the date. For example, the get originally said, "in the year 508 from creation," and he cuts away only the words indicating 508, leaving the phrase "in the year from creation." It is then evident that a date had been present and was removed. The exact day is unknown, so the get lacks a usable date, but there is no reason to suspect some unrelated condition was cut away. The same must be said in Bava Metzia concerning Tosafot's objection to Rashi. Tosafot say that when the standard form is missing, the document is invalid, so they interpret the valuation dispute as concerning the date, because removal of the date does not necessarily invalidate the whole paper. At first glance, however, why is that better? If the date is missing, perhaps a condition was written in the place now removed. The answer is the same: the cut is visibly in the date location and shows that only the dating words were removed. This also clarifies Rashba's own method. Rashba asks how the Gemara can assume a date was removed rather than some other text. His question shows he does not accept the explanation that the shape of the cut necessarily reveals the date. That fits his broader position: an erasure in the standard form does not invalidate the document from fear of a hidden condition. Thus his own views are internally consistent. According to Rashba, even if the whole standard form were erased, or most of it, the document can remain valid. That appears to follow from Rashi's case where one party holds the essential portion and the other the standard form. This is broader than Beit Yosef's responsum, siman 300, which invalidates where the entire standard form is missing. Perhaps Beit Yosef was stricter there because the case involved a get and severe marital consequences. Shach also asks from Tur and Rama. Here they rule that even an erasure in the standard form can invalidate, while in Even HaEzer 125:19 Mechaber and Rama quote Rambam that if one letter or one word of the get's standard form was erased and not confirmed, the get remains valid. Shach answers that there the erasure is only a single word, where there is no substantial concern that a condition was removed. That is a good answer. But then an erasure in the essential part should still invalidate even when its content is recognizable. This creates a difficulty within Rambam. Here he treats an erasure in the essential portion strictly, while in Even HaEzer 127:8 Mechaber rules that if the date was cut off and the get delivered, it is valid. Why should cutting differ from erasure? Indeed, Rashba says cutting is worse in some respects. The date is part of the essential form of a get. The only possible distinction is that an erasure in the essential text is invalid because witnesses should never sign over such an alteration. Chazal therefore decreed even a recognizable erasure because of an unrecognizable one. But if the witnesses originally signed a proper get and the husband later cuts off the date, the witnesses themselves did nothing improper. The get can therefore remain valid after the fact. This distinction is possible but somewhat strained. The matter still requires study.
