Tur cites Rashi that if both parties built the open field wall without making any ownership marker, it is presumed joint even if it later falls into one party's domain. Rabbeinu Yonah disagrees and says that if neither made a marker and the stones later fall into one party's domain, possession can determine the result. Beit Yosef explains the disagreement from the Gemara. Rashi reads the Gemara's question, "Why not let neither side make a marker?" as showing that even without markers the wall would ordinarily be divided. Otherwise the Gemara could have answered that both sides make markers specifically so the later ownership of the fallen stones will be clear. Rabbeinu Yonah says the Gemara could indeed have given that answer but chose the stronger explanation that one party might fabricate a marker. Taz and Shach ask why the Gemara did not simply establish the marker rule according to all opinions as protection in case the stones later fall into one party's domain. Ramban answers that the Mishnah says not only that the stones are divided but also that the place of the wall is divided. Possession of the stones can change when they fall, but the ground itself does not fall into one person's domain. Therefore the marker rule cannot be explained merely as protection against later physical possession. The marker must also clarify the ownership of the wall's location. This answer requires care, because the courtyard Mishnah likewise says both the place and stones belong to both, yet Tosafot says "place" can sometimes be stated incidentally with the stones. Why not say the same in the open field? The difference is contextual. In a courtyard the law itself always compels joint construction, so no one could mistakenly infer that absence of markers changes the ownership of the ground. The word "place" can therefore be carried along with the stones without misleading anyone. In an open field, however, the Mishnah specifically connects division to the marker arrangement. If lack of markers could leave the location itself uncertain, saying "the place and stones are divided" specifically because both made markers is significant. Ramban therefore had reason to treat "place" as substantive in the open field passage. Rabbeinu Yonah's position can still be defended by analyzing the Gemara's initial assumption. Rashi says the question, "Let neither make a marker," assumes that absence of a marker itself would be evidence that neither built alone, because a unilateral builder would have placed an outward marker. That evidence would protect even if the stones later fell into one side's possession. The Gemara's conclusion requires outward markers even when both built, lest one party make a deceptive marker and claim the whole. Once the law requires markers on both sides when both participate, simple absence of a marker is no longer reliable evidence. The absence may simply mean the required markers are missing. Accordingly, after the final enactment, if no markers exist and the stones lie in one party's possession, Rabbeinu Yonah can apply the ordinary rule that the possessor is stronger. The original negative inference from absence of a marker has disappeared. Indeed, Rashi's own wording later says that with a light reed fence, where no marker system is workable, witnesses should document whether one or both built. If joint ownership were automatically preserved even when the wall later fell entirely into one party's possession, why would witnesses be needed when both built? This suggests that once no reliable marker exists, later possession can matter, which actually supports Rabbeinu Yonah. Bach distinguishes a case where both did make outward markers. He says then joint ownership is exceptionally strong, so even a long later possession by one side cannot support an exclusive claim. But where neither made markers, a sufficiently long possession can support a migo of purchase. I do not think the marker itself defeats a genuine migo. Ramban and Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash explain that a marker is only a weak sign used to resolve an otherwise balanced uncertainty. It cannot overcome an established contrary possession or a strong migo. In the courtyard, a marker is disregarded precisely because the law already establishes definite joint ownership. All the more, where one party has a true migo that can support exclusive ownership, a marker cannot automatically defeat it. This also resolves questions raised against Tosafot's explanation of the courtyard wall. Tosafot says that in an open field, after long possession one can claim exclusive construction through a migo of purchase. That can remain true even where markers exist, because the marker is only an evidentiary device for uncertainty, not absolute proof. The decisive rule is that a marker clarifies a balanced doubt but does not prevail against a legally superior migo or certainty.
