(Supplement) If, etc. Ramban writes in brief: some ask what the Gemara's objection is, 'Let neither one make,' etc., since the marker helps if one later removes the stones into his own property. They answer that the Gemara challenges the statement, 'therefore, if it fell,' etc., which implies that a marker on both sides is effective for both parties. This is the latter view. Ramban writes that according to us there is no difficulty, for Rashi already explains, 'Let neither one make,' etc., that the absence of a marker itself serves like markers on both sides. Even if one later removes the stones, it does not help. Only regarding a courtyard wall was it necessary to teach that removal does not help, because the Sages did not enact a marker there and one might otherwise have been believed. The mishna teaches that he is not believed because they compel one another and it is known to belong to both. In a valley, although they are not compelled, he is not believed because it is known to belong to both from the absence of a marker. This is the latter view cited by the Tur in Rashi's name. It requires study, however, because the opening clause's 'therefore' was established as referring to one who removed the stones. If so, the latter clause can also be interpreted that way, and the original question returns: why does the Gemara ask, 'Let neither one make,' etc.? (End of supplement.)
