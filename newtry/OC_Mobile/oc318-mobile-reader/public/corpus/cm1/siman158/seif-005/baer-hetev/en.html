Both of them. The Bach writes: Certainly, a case in which neither of them made an ownership marker on the wall is not comparable to a case in which both made such a marker. Where both made markers, even if the fallen stones remained in one person's possession far longer than is usual, they still belong to both, and that person is not believed to claim that he alone made the wall by means of a migo that he could have said, "I purchased it," because this is a migo against witnesses. We are deemed to know that, since both made ownership markers, both built it. But where neither made a marker, the stones are not presumed to belong to both unless they remained in one person's possession no longer than the usual period. If they remained there longer, that person is believed by a migo that he could have claimed, "I purchased it from you." Rabbeinu Yonah understands from the Gemara's placement of the word "therefore" that, regarding a field in an open valley, the wall is not presumed jointly owned unless it fell into the possession of both. When the Gemara asks why neither party should make an ownership marker and does not answer that the Mishnah teaches that both make markers because of the case where it falls into one party's possession, that is because it preferred to answer that the rule was taught for the ownership marker itself. The Beit Yosef implies that according to Rashi there is no distinction between a case where both made markers and one where neither did, but this is imprecise. The Taz answers that, in his view, this opinion holds that making an ownership marker cannot help once the wall falls, because the marker would no longer be recognizable. Thus, the marker's primary function is only while the wall stands, and it is regarding that situation that the Gemara asks why neither should make one.
