Even if the enclosed owner himself built the third side, Beit Yosef writes that this reveals that the fencing is desirable to him and he must participate in the fourth side as well. Darkei Moshe objects from the Tur, who, according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak's interpretation, says that even if the enclosed owner fences opposite his own field on the fourth side, so long as part of that side remains open he has not yet received the benefit and is not charged. According to what I wrote earlier, however, Beit Yosef is correct where the wall stands on land belonging to both parties. Once the enclosed owner builds part of the fence and thereby reveals that he desires the structure, his courtyard acquires his share of the wall, and he becomes liable for its price as in an ordinary purchase. The Tur speaks according to the view that the encircling owner built entirely on his own property, so the enclosed owner acquires no part of the wall and is liable only for actual benefit. If the enclosure is still open, he has not yet benefited. Thus the two rulings concern different constructions.
Rambam requires that the wall stand on land belonging to both neighbors before the enclosed owner can be charged half the expense. Raavad objects that if the builder left all the ground for the other and built wholly on his own property, the enclosed owner should at most deduct the value of the ground and still participate in the construction. Maggid Mishneh explains Rambam: the enclosed owner is charged half the construction only because, when the wall stands on his land as well, his courtyard acquires half the wall. If the wall stands wholly on the builder's land, the enclosed owner does not acquire any of the wall merely by paying and is liable only for the value of the benefit he actually receives. This is also the reason Rashi interprets the Mishnah's "one who fenced" as fencing between himself and the enclosed owner, rather than between himself and the public domain. The Gemara's phrase, "You caused me extra enclosure," fits a wall between the two parcels, and only there can the enclosed owner's courtyard acquire half the wall. The Tur nevertheless cites Rambam and afterward cites Rabbeinu Yitzchak, who explains the wall as being between the encircling owner and the public domain. That appears contradictory because such a wall is necessarily on the encircling owner's land. Beit Yosef suggests that Rambam may distinguish between a ruin, where the other owner will eventually use the walls, and an open field, where he may not. Ramban indeed takes almost the opposite approach. He says that in an open field, if the wall occupies both parties' ground, the neighbor can object that he never wanted to lose planting space and would have preferred merely to employ a guard. If the builder first asks and the neighbor objects, the builder cannot later compel him merely by constructing the wall. Yet if the wall is built without prior protest in a field ordinarily suited for such improvement, the builder can be treated like one who improved another's field without authorization and may be reimbursed according to the ordinary rules of benefit. The more precise distinction is therefore that joint ownership of the ground allows the courtyard to acquire the wall only where the law recognizes the construction as one the recipient would accept. In an open field where fencing is not customary and he protests, the construction cannot be forced upon him. Where there is no protest and the circumstances show acceptance, reimbursement can follow from the benefit and acquisition principles.
The Gemara seeks to prove from Rabbi Yosei that one who benefits while the other suffers no loss is exempt, because the enclosed owner is charged only when he himself builds the fourth side. Tosafot answers that his building reveals that he desires the expenditure. Maharshal rejects a general rule that mere revealed desire creates liability in every case of benefit without loss. In my view the Mishnah can be explained without that general rule. Where the wall stands on land belonging to both parties, once the enclosed owner reveals that he desires the construction his courtyard acquires his share of the wall. From that point he is not merely a beneficiary of another's property. He has purchased part of the wall itself and must pay its price. This is exactly Maggid Mishneh's explanation of Rambam, and Nimukei Yosef says similarly regarding one who later relies upon a wall another built. This also vindicates Beit Yosef's ruling that if the enclosed owner builds even the third side, he can become liable despite not yet being fully enclosed. The liability is not necessarily for completed benefit. It can arise because his conduct reveals acceptance of the structure, his courtyard acquires his portion, and ordinary purchase liability follows. The Tur's contrary language belongs to Rabbeinu Yitzchak's view where the wall stands wholly on the encircling owner's property and the only possible obligation is for benefit. There, so long as an opening remains, no completed benefit exists.
