One who had a ruin, etc. The Tur states this law regarding one whose field lies among his fellow's fields and the fellow fenced three sides around it. The law is the same as Rambam's case of a ruin here. Perhaps Rambam chose a ruin as the greater novelty: although the enclosed owner derives relatively little benefit, once all four sides are fenced we apportion the entire expense to him. All the more so with a garden or field, where fencing protects produce and seed from trampling, animals, and the evil eye.
Up to four amot. This is the standard height of a wall or partition between courtyards according to all opinions, as explained at the beginning of siman 157. It appears that the same applies here to a ruin situated among residences, because visual damage applies. This differs from the dispute below in seif 3 regarding visual harm to a garden.
It appears to me that he need pay nothing. Rama writes this to exclude Beit Yosef's view at the end of this siman, that in such a case the enclosed owner must pay half the expense of the two sides that the surrounding owner fenced, because his own construction reveals satisfaction with the outer fencing. According to Beit Yosef, the Mishnah's statement that if the surrounding owner fenced three sides the inner owner is exempt teaches only that without any manifestation of consent, even three fenced sides do not obligate him. The Tur's view at the end of this siman is that he remains exempt. Darkei Moshe brings proof from the Tur. Beit Yosef himself writes that the Tur's view is not convincing. See the Perishah and Derishah.
Because he cannot use the walls. Maggid Mishneh writes that even if the surrounding owner offers him permission to use the walls after paying half their cost, he still cannot compel him. Since permission depends on the surrounding owner's will and the inner owner has no inherent right to use them even upon payment, he cannot be forced to pay against his will.
Likewise, if the enclosed owner, etc. This teaches that one should not say: since the surrounding owner did not fence all four sides, his work did not benefit the inner owner enough to obligate him; and because the surrounding owner had no claim for payment until the fourth side was completed, he must have despaired of recovery. The ruling teaches otherwise.
If it is a place where they are accustomed to fence. Meaning, between neighboring properties, because of the evil eye or so that one entering will be identifiable as a thief, even where there is no concern for animal traffic, as here where the surrounding fields are already fenced externally.
The enclosed owner need not, etc. He can say, 'I gained nothing from your fence, because I still need to fence between myself and the surrounding fields.'
One of the sides. Meaning, one of all four, while the surrounding owner fenced the other three. As explained in the preceding siman, until all four are enclosed the inner owner is not liable even if he himself fenced one side.
According to the extra enclosure he caused. The Tur, seif 16, cites Ramah that the calculation is proportional by area: calculate the total square amot of all the fields for which the external fences were built, then calculate the area of the interior field alone. If the inner field is one third or one fourth of the whole, he pays that proportion of the fencing expenses. Rama's phrase follows the Gemara. This measure applies where the outer fence surrounds all five fields externally. If the divider is built internally between the surrounding and inner fields, the inner owner pays half the entire expense of those internal divisions.
