If a person owned a ruin situated among his neighbor's ruins, and the neighbor fenced the first, second, and third sides, leaving this ruin enclosed on three sides, the owner of the inner ruin is not required to contribute anything, because he has not yet benefited; his ruin remains open to the public domain as before. Therefore, if the neighbor fenced the fourth side as well, so that the ruin became fully enclosed, the entire expense is assessed against the inner owner, and he pays half the expense incurred for all four sides, up to a height of four cubits. Rama: If the outer owner fenced two sides and the inner owner then fenced the third, while the fourth remained open, it appears that the inner owner need not pay anything until one of them fences the fourth side. This applies only when the site of the wall belongs to both. If the wall belongs entirely to the builder and was built on his own property, the inner owner is charged only a small amount as the judges deem proper, because he cannot use the walls. If the inner owner himself fenced the fourth side, he revealed that he desired the enclosure and must pay half the expense of the other three sides, provided the walls belong to both. Rama: All this concerns a wall built between the outer and inner properties. If the outer owner fenced along the exterior perimeter while the boundary between him and the inner owner remained open, then, in a place where it is customary to fence, the inner owner need not pay anything even if the outer owner fenced all four external sides. If, however, the inner owner fenced one of the sides, thereby revealing that he is pleased with the enclosure, he must pay according to the additional perimeter his presence caused the outer owner to build.
