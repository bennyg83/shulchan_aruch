Up to four amot. See Sema, seif katan 12, who explains that because the ruin stands in an area used for human residence, visual intrusion applies. At first glance this is difficult. If so, why does seif 9 say merely that one can object initially, and why does seif 7 say that if the enclosed owner fenced with reeds he pays only the cost of reeds? Where visual intrusion applies, one can compel construction of a proper stone wall so that repeated litigation will not be necessary. The Mechaber's language about minimal guarding expenses also seems out of place if the case concerns a residential ruin. Several seifim become difficult on that reading. It therefore appears that the Rambam's term "ruin" includes both ruins of courtyards and houses and uncultivated field ruins. He first discusses a person who owns three ruined properties surrounding the fourth owner's ruin. The surrounded owner is not charged at all, whether the ruins are of buildings and courtyards or fields, because while the fourth side remains open to the public domain the place is not suitable either for sowing or for ordinary courtyard use. The surrounded owner can also say that even without his own parcel the surrounding owner would have needed internal fencing against animals and people entering from the public domain.
Only a small amount according to what they assess. One should not object from the rule concerning a courtyard where a person builds a partition entirely within his own property and the neighbor need not contribute. There the builder protects the neighbor only from the builder's own visual intrusion, and a person does not have to pay another to stop harming him, since the person causing damage bears the duty to distance himself. Here, by contrast, the surrounding owner's fence also protects the enclosed owner from the interference of people coming from the public domain, so the enclosed owner must contribute according to the benefit he receives.
The enclosed owner need pay nothing. This is not comparable to siman 157, seif 10, concerning construction clearly intended to support a continuation, where the neighbor must contribute to the entire wall. There the circumstances show that he intends to place a roof or otherwise use the wall, and therefore intends to acquire a share in the wall's courtyard footprint. Nimukei Yosef and Rashba write that once he demonstrates that intent he acquires the relevant share and immediately becomes obligated to pay. Here, however, the circumstances do not show that the enclosed owner intends to place a roof or otherwise acquire a proprietary share. He therefore pays only when he actually receives benefit, as I explained above in siman 157.
Additional perimeter. See Sema, seif katan 19, through his statement that if the enclosed field represents one third or one quarter of the total expense, the enclosed owner pays accordingly. See Shach, seif katan 4, who calls the Sema's words difficult without explaining why. His intent appears to be this: suppose the combined fields form a square ten by ten, containing one hundred square units, and the enclosed field is eight by eight, containing sixty four units. The surrounding owner's own land is then only thirty six units, little more than one third as large as the enclosed field. On the Sema's wording the enclosed owner would pay nearly two thirds of the fence while the surrounding owner would pay only a little more than one third. Yet if the surrounding owner had simply built a fence along the boundary between them, the enclosed owner would never have paid more than one half. Why should moving the fence farther outward around the surrounding owner's own fields worsen the enclosed owner's position? He receives no added benefit from the greater distance. There appears to be a textual error in the Sema. It should read that the enclosed owner pays a corresponding share of one third or one quarter of the expenses, meaning one half of the portion attributable to the extra perimeter that his field caused. The reason is clear. The Gemara states that he contributes only for the extra enclosure his presence caused. For example, if the total square is ten by ten, one hundred square units, while the enclosed field is five by five, twenty five units, then the surrounding owner's land occupies seventy five units. If those seventy five units stood by themselves in the most efficient square, their sides would be a little more than eight and one half units and the total perimeter approximately thirty five units. With the enclosed field inside them, the outside square is ten by ten and the perimeter is forty. The enclosed parcel therefore caused only about five units of additional fencing, one eighth of the total. There is no basis to make the enclosed owner pay more than the share attributable to that increase. Likewise, in the earlier example where the enclosed field is eight by eight and the surrounding owner's land only thirty six units, a square of thirty six units alone would be six by six, with a perimeter of twenty four. Because the land is arranged around the enclosed field, its practical strips form a longer configuration and the surrounding owner bears approximately twenty seven and one fifth units, while the enclosed owner's portion according to the proper calculation is about twelve and four fifths units of the forty. The calculation is based on the proportional additional perimeter that the enclosed parcel caused, not simply the relative land areas. Thus, if the enclosed property represents a third or quarter of the relevant added burden, he pays half of that attributable portion, or a corresponding measure. Perishah does not appear to explain it this way, but the Bach's wording can be read accordingly, and this appears to be the Shach's intent.
