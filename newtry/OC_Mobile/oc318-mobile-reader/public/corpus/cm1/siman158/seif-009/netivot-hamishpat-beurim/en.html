Who told him before he fenced the fourth side. Ramban explains that Rav Huna's rule, requiring payment according to what was actually fenced, treats the field as one naturally standing to be fenced according to local custom. One might ask that even in a field naturally standing to be improved, Sema elsewhere allows the owner to say, "Take your wood and stones," and similarly where a ruin is involved. Why can the enclosed owner not say the same even after the fence was built? The distinction is that one who descends into another's property without permission may be told to remove his materials. Here both parties need the fence. This resembles partners, where if one protests in advance he may sometimes say, "Take your wood and stones," but if he does not protest and the improvement benefits both, he cannot later demand removal. The same applies here. Since the fence benefits both parties, silence prevents him from demanding removal. One should not ask why Ramban's comparison to a field standing to be improved does not require payment of the increased value rather than expenses alone. Increased value is paid where the improver intended to improve the other's property. Here his primary intent was his own benefit, not to improve for his neighbor. See Shach in siman 391, seif katan 20.
