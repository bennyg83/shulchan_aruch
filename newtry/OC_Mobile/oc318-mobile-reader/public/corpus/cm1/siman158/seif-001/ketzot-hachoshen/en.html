Where two partners own a garden and seek division, Sema says the Shulchan Aruch relies on the rules stated earlier for a courtyard. If the garden is large enough to be divided, one can compel the other to divide and build the appropriate partition. If it is not large enough, division requires both parties' consent, and where they made a kinyan on division one can compel the other to erect the partition.
Tur's wording suggests that in a garden the compulsory partition applies specifically where the garden has the legal minimum size for compulsory division. He writes: "Two who were partners in a garden and come to divide, if it has the law of division, they compel him to make a fence between them." Mishneh LaMelech therefore infers that if the garden has no compulsory division and they voluntarily agree to divide, one can say, "I agreed to divide only on condition that you build the fence from your own funds." The reason is that a courtyard presents a more serious visual harm. Even a local practice not to build partitions cannot override that harm. Therefore, when parties voluntarily divide a courtyard, we presume their agreement included the ordinary shared cost of removing the visual harm. A garden has a lesser visual harm. One party may therefore say that his consent to voluntary division did not include an obligation to share the fence expense. The Mishnah's phrase, "and likewise in a garden," can therefore refer only to the type of wall or construction material, while the garden itself may be discussing a case with a compulsory right of division. The Shulchan Aruch's phrase, "and they come to divide," likewise naturally indicates a garden that has the law of division. This supports Tur and Mishneh LaMelech.
