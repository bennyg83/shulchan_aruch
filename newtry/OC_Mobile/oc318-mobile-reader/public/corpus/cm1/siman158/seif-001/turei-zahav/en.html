(Seif 2, one who sells a garden, and so forth). According to the Maggid Mishneh's explanation cited by the Sema in seif katan 4, deriving this from siman 320, the ruling astonishes me, for there the case concerns a field, whereas here the Rambam writes after this ruling: “But if he sold land in an open valley without specification, we do not compel him to fence,” and so forth. It appears to me that the Rambam should be explained as follows. A garden contains planted species, and the prohibition of mixed seeds applies. Where one sold an unspecified garden, the purchaser has the inferior hand, and the seller may give him whichever garden he chooses. If the seller gives him a garden intermingled among the seller's gardens without any separating fence, the seller would be required to refrain from planting at the edge of his own garden a species prohibited as mixed seed with what the purchaser plants. If there is a fence, no distancing is required, as Tosafot write in Bava Batra, chapter Lo Yachpor, s.v. “one vineyard,” and so forth. The Rambam therefore writes “even in a place where the custom is not to fence.” This means that between two established neighboring owners there is ordinarily a boundary marker, or each has already acquired a right based upon the required distancing. That is unlike an intermingled garden with no separating boundary and where the seller's plants already reach the edge of the portion being conveyed. Presumably, he did not sell except on the understanding that a fence would be built. This resembles one who digs a cistern adjacent to his fellow's field in an area suited for cisterns, in siman 155, seif 17, who must distance even though he acts permissibly. Here too, the purchaser must contribute half the expense, and we do not apply the principle that a seller sells generously.
