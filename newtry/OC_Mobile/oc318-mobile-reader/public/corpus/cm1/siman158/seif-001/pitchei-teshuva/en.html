According to what he caused him. See Baer Heitev and Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, siman 24. Reuven authored a commentary on the orders Nezikin and Kodashim and contracted with Shimon the printer for a fixed price per page to print those two orders with Rashi, Tosafot, and Reuven's new commentary below. Printers ordinarily dismantle the arranged type after finishing each page so the letters can be used for another page. Shimon owned many letters and therefore left the type arranged, removed only Reuven's commentary from the bottom, and used the remaining setting to print for himself the two orders with Rashi and Tosafot. Reuven claimed that because he had borne the cost of typesetting, Shimon should not benefit freely from that arrangement and should reimburse him for his share of the typesetting, like one who benefits from another's labor, comparing it to an irrigation channel and to one who surrounds another's property on three sides. Shimon answered that the letters were his own and he could use them as he wished. Noda BiYehudah first explains that if, as the question's wording suggests, Shimon contracted for a fixed sum per page and Shimon himself had to pay the typesetters, press workers, paper, and all other expenses, Reuven has no claim. Shimon supplied other type for Reuven's continuing work and did not delay it, and can say that he agreed to the price precisely because he knew he could also benefit from the setting. In that arrangement Shimon, not Reuven, paid the typesetters. If, however, the original accounting separately allocated specified sums to the typesetters, press work, paper, and the balance for use of the letters and ink, so that Reuven in substance paid the typesetters, then the parties' competing arguments require analysis. Reuven's analogy to an irrigation channel is not exact, because Choshen Mishpat siman 170, seif 1, concerns parties who all come and need irrigation, not one who has already dug the channel. Nor is the case like one who surrounds another on three sides. Bava Kamma 20b explains liability there on the ground that he caused the other to incur additional fencing expense. Here the typesetters charge the same whether that setting is used for few or many impressions, so Shimon caused Reuven no additional typesetting expense. One might argue that since Shimon is willing to spend additional money on the press work and other printing costs, he thereby reveals that he values the printing, and that this disclosure should make him share the typesetting cost. Tosafot there might appear to support this. But Tosafot's rule operates where there is also some extra expense caused by the beneficiary. According to the Shach in siman 158, seif katan 4, following the Bach, even where he reveals his willingness he pays no more than the additional expense he caused. For what he did not cause, he need not pay merely because he benefited. Even according to Sema there, seif katan 19, that the inner owner pays his proportional share, that is because he caused at least some extra expense; his revealed willingness then obligates him proportionately for the whole. Where he caused no extra expense at all, revealed willingness concerning one expense cannot obligate him for all. Upon further consideration, however, Noda BiYehudah concludes that Shimon must pay his share. One who lives in another's courtyard is liable for all the benefit he received if he caused even a slight loss, even where the courtyard was not ordinarily rented, as stated in Shulchan Aruch siman 363, seif 7, in the name of some authorities. Here Shimon causes substantial loss, because had he not printed these orders himself, purchasers would have been more available for Reuven's books. According to that view, which is held by most authorities, Shimon must pay the full value of his benefit. Even according to the Ramah cited by Nimukei Yosef in chapter Keitzad HaRegel, who limits liability to the amount of loss actually caused, and in Noda BiYehudah's view the Rambam agrees, the loss here is not slight and in his estimation exceeds the typesetting expense. Shimon must therefore pay for the benefit he obtained from the arranged type in proportion to the number of copies he printed. See there.
