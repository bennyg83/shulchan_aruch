One who sells. See Beer HaGolah. The ruling follows the approach that interprets the Mishnah as concerning a purchaser, as in siman 218, seif 20, and treats an ordinary garden sold without specification like a place where fencing is customary. Some Geonim were pushed to this explanation by the Yerushalmi's statement that in a garden one can compel fencing even where local practice is not to fence.
Collected note: One who sells. Rambam likewise interprets the Mishnah's garden clause this way.
Collected note: One who sells. According to the older textual reading, the Gemara contrasts an ordinary garden with an ordinary field and concludes that an unspecified sale of a garden is treated as a place where fencing is customary. This can apply even where local custom generally is not to fence gardens, which resolves the Gemara's inference. Rambam can therefore be read as preserving both elements of the sugya.
Collected note: One who sells. Rambam writes that in an open field one compels fencing only where that is local custom. If the seller rule applied even against contrary field custom, a seller would paradoxically be treated more leniently than ordinary partners in some cases. This supports reading his law as above.
Collected note: One who sells. The Tosefta cited by the Rif and Rosh says residents of an open field can compel one another to make a ditch and secondary ditch, while the Mishnah regarding a seller requires space for a wall, ditch, and secondary ditch. Ramban answers that the Mishnah concerns a place where field fencing is customary.
Some say. The Yerushalmi can be understood as discussing a place where fencing is not customary, and its own contrasting clauses point in different directions. Therefore the exact scope remains disputed.
