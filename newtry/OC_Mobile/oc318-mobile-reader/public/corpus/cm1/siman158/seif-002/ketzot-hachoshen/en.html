Rambam writes that if one sells a garden without specifying further, the purchaser is compelled to build a wall between it and the seller's remaining garden, even in a place where gardens are ordinarily left unfenced. But if one sells an open field, the purchaser is compelled to fence only where the local practice is to do so. Raavad objects, and Kesef Mishneh explains Rambam from the Mishnah and from Rambam's law of sale. A seller of half a field ordinarily conveys on terms that require the purchaser to assume the place needed for the boundary structures. Rambam's commentary to the Mishnah says similarly that one who sells a garden without qualification, leaving it mixed among other gardens, requires the purchaser to build the partition even where the local practice is not to fence. A plain open field is different and follows local practice. On Rambam's understanding, the purchaser of the garden may have to build a full four cubit wall. Partners who merely divide a garden can satisfy the law with a ten handbreadth partition, but a purchaser must remove the seller's harm more completely. This answers the question posed to Rambam by the scholars of Lunel. They asked why he requires only ten handbreadths in his law of partners when the Mishnah says, "and likewise in a garden," apparently referring to the same four cubit wall as a courtyard. Rambam answered that the phrase "likewise in a garden" refers to the construction materials. According to his broader interpretation, the four cubit case concerns sale, while the partnership case needs only a lower partition. There is also a substantive reason for the distinction. Tosafot explains that even according to the view that visual intrusion is ordinarily not legal damage, once people have already become accustomed to private use, as after a wall falls, renewed exposure can be actionable. A seller who previously possessed the entire garden privately was accustomed to private use before the sale. The purchaser is the newcomer who creates the intrusion and can therefore be required to remove it. This resembles a house and roof, where the roof owner bears the full cost because the courtyard owner is not causing harm to the roof owner. Rambam's reading of the Mishnah is nevertheless surprising because the ordinary Hebrew phrase, "a garden without qualification is treated as a place where people fence," is normally understood as a garden in a locale without a known custom, not as "one who sells a garden without qualification." The Jerusalem Talmud provides support for Rambam. It states that in a garden, whether the local custom is to fence or not to fence, the parties can be compelled; in an open field, local custom controls. If so, the Mishnah's phrase cannot simply mean an unspecified local custom, because even a known practice not to fence does not remove the obligation in a garden. Rambam therefore interprets the phrase as an unspecified sale of a garden, meaning sale without special terms. This explains his unusual reading. Still, Rambam's commentary to the Mishnah says that an unspecified open field is not compelled even in a place where people fence, while his Mishneh Torah says that an open field is compelled where the custom is to fence. The latter is clearly supported by the Jerusalem Talmud and should be treated as his final practical ruling.
