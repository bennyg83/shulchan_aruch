And some say that he is not disqualified. See the Shach and Netivot HaMishpat, who agree with the view of Rivash, cited by Beit Yosef. Rivash rules that if one swore not to eat and then ate, he is disqualified because he committed an act; but if he swore to eat and then failed to eat, where no act was committed, he is not disqualified. Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot Beit Yosef, letter 26, indicates that he is nevertheless disqualified rabbinically, and Tumim also discusses this. See Responsa Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg, Choshen Mishpat, siman 5, regarding witnesses who accepted an oath not to disclose to any person a certain matter they knew and afterward testified about that matter before a beit din. He discusses both the status of the witnesses and the validity of the testimony they just gave, and concludes at length that because their oath was comprehensive, expressly forbidding disclosure to any person, it certainly took effect and prohibited testimony even before a beit din. By testifying they therefore violated their oath and became disqualified from future testimony. Although Shulchan Aruch records two views whether violation of an oath concerning future conduct disqualifies a witness, and Rivash rules that disqualification applies where the violation involves an act, while this case is a prohibition without an act, Knesset HaGedolah and Urim VeTumim establish that even according to Rivash the witness is certainly disqualified rabbinically. Nor can one say that these witnesses remain valid because they believed they were performing a mitzvah by testifying. Here one cannot plausibly say that they relied on such a belief to violate the oath, reasoning that the oath had never taken effect, because they had knowingly sworn specifically concerning disclosure of this matter. In a matter of this kind they certainly should not have acted without first obtaining a halachic ruling. This is entirely unlike those who buried a corpse, discussed in the preceding seif, who may have had no doubt and may actually have regarded their act as a mitzvah. These witnesses, by contrast, had originally sworn precisely because they did not regard disclosure as obligatory. As for the testimony they gave while violating the oath, it is clear that this testimony too is invalid. See the responsum at length.
