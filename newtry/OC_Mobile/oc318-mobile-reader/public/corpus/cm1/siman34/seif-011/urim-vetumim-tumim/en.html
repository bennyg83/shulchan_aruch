Because he thinks he is performing a mitzvah, etc. Tosafot in Sanhedrin 25b ask why extortionists are disqualified only rabbinically. They should be Torah-disqualified, since "you shall not covet" is violated even when money is paid, although people mistakenly think the prohibition applies only when the object is taken without payment. I do not understand the question at first glance. What difference does it make that their legal understanding is wrong? If the prohibition appears permitted to them because they misunderstand the law, that should resemble interest witnesses, who are not disqualified because the prohibition does not appear serious to them, even though in truth they violate a Torah prohibition. One might answer that this entire principle depends on the dispute concerning those who bury a dead person on a festival. Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua validates them because they believe they are performing a mitzvah. Rav Pappa disqualifies them, showing that mistaken belief in permissibility does not preserve qualification. Then perhaps our halachah that a person is not disqualified where the act appears permitted to him follows Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua. But the baraita adding extortionists to the rabbinic disqualifications may have been stated according to Rav Pappa, who rejects this excuse. This does not fit Tosafot's language. Their question is not presented as one only according to Rav Pappa. Moreover, Tosafot themselves derive from the baraita about an interest-bearing document that the witnesses are not disqualified because the prohibition does not appear serious to them. If Rav Pappa rejected that principle generally, the baraita would remain difficult according to him. The better explanation is that "the prohibition does not appear serious to them" is not an absolute rule that every person in the world is presumed ignorant of Torah law. There are scholars and knowledgeable people who certainly know the prohibition. If we are uncertain whether this particular person knew and sinned deliberately or erred in the law, how can we extract money on the strength of his testimony? The answer is that he has a presumption of fitness. We therefore assume he did not knowingly commit a disqualifying Torah offense and that his misconduct resulted from error. But this works only so long as his presumption of fitness is intact. Once Chazal themselves disqualified the extortionist rabbinically, we already know he violated at least a rabbinic standard serious enough to destroy that presumption. His chazakah is impaired. At that point, the Torah-level question reopens: perhaps he did know the Torah prohibition and violated it deliberately. Tosafot therefore ask that he should at least have the status of a doubtful Torah-disqualified witness. Money should not be extracted through his testimony without prior declaration as though he were merely rabbinically disqualified, and the other practical differences between Torah and rabbinic disqualification should apply. This is a strong question. But where we can maintain that he is not rabbinically disqualified at all because we preserve his original chazakah and assume he acted from error, the problem does not arise. There the presumption of fitness remains whole. I later found a similar principle in Panim Me'irot, part 2, responsum 105: once a person violated a rabbinic prohibition and thereby impaired his chazakah, we no longer automatically use his former presumption of fitness to say he would never violate a Torah prohibition. There is also support from the law that one suspected of a rabbinic prohibition can become suspected concerning an oath, even though false oath is Torah law. Once the presumption of upright conduct is impaired, suspicion can extend further. Accordingly, Tosafot's question is well founded without denying the general rule that where a prohibition genuinely appears permitted to a person and his chazakah remains intact, he need not become disqualified.
