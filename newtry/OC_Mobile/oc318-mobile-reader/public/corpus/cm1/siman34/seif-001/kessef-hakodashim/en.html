Seif 18, gloss. Who takes payment to testify. Payment for writing testimony is permitted. Whether accepting that writing fee from only one side creates a concern resembling bribery or financial interest was discussed by my father-in-law at length. Where payment is received only after the writing and the witness knows that his heart was not inclined toward gain, there is substantial room for permission, even more than in the case of a customary gift discussed in siman 9. I myself was once compelled to take a writing fee in such a case, although it was not formal direct testimony but a report of one witness from another in the style of a letter. Where a fixed fee is customarily charged for drafting a record of testimony, the fee is for the writing, not for the testimony. We do not suspect a Jew of suppressing truthful testimony unless paid. He would testify without payment; the payment compensates only the labor of writing, much like travel or accompanying the parties. Since the written record identifies the source and says what each witness stated, it is also a matter likely to be revealed and checked, even when sent to a distant city. Still, the best arrangement would be for the writing fee to come from a communal fund or another neutral source rather than from one party, thereby removing even remote suspicion. A paid agent who is also a witness appears clearly acceptable. Friendship and hostility do not invalidate testimony, because ordinary emotional inclination is not treated as disqualifying interest for a witness. Only concrete financial interest does. Thus the small affection that may arise because a person hired this agent, perhaps even paid generously, does not undermine his presumption of fitness. The payment is fundamentally for a permitted service, and a slight incidental sense of gratitude is no worse than ordinary friendship. The same explains the universal practice that witnesses to kinyanim and documents receive payment, often openly described as a signing fee. They may lawfully receive compensation for travel, attendance, preparation of the instrument, and the enduring writing itself. Although signed witnesses are regarded as though their testimony was investigated in beit din, the Torah did not require them to create a document designed to endure for many years. The prophetic writings themselves emphasize the preservation of documents, and the later writing labor may therefore be compensated. Because everyone knows that witnesses customarily receive a fixed fee for these services, the payment does not create a defect in their testimony. Financial interest is not an intrinsic personal disqualification like kinship; it disqualifies because it creates suspicion. Where the source and amount of the benefit are known and institutional, the ordinary presumption of honesty removes that suspicion. Moreover, one who makes a kinyan before witnesses knowing that the purchaser will pay the customary witness fee can be viewed as accepting their testimony despite that known benefit. Communal enactment can likewise establish that designated officials or witnesses are trusted despite ordinary forms of interest, and a long-established custom across Jewish communities may function as such an enactment. This may also help explain the established use of paid witnesses in marriage and related documents.
