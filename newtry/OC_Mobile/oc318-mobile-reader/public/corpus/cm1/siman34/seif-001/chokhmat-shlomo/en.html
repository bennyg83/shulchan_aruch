Seif 1. “A wicked person is disqualified from testimony.” Note: Mishneh LaMelech in the laws of lender and borrower expresses doubt where two witnesses saw Reuven commit a transgression but do not know whether he acted inadvertently or intentionally. Is Reuven believed to say, “I did it intentionally,” or do we apply the principle that a person cannot make himself wicked? Elsewhere I showed that his doubt seems contradicted by Tosafot in the first chapter of Bava Metzia concerning two witnesses who say, “You ate forbidden fat,” where Tosafot ask how the person can be believed to say, “I ate it intentionally,” when one cannot make himself wicked. Rashba and Ran concerning the oath of testimony also indicate that the rule applies in this setting. I have now reconsidered in light of Tosafot, Pesachim 63b, regarding uncertain warning. Tosafot explain Rashi to mean that a warning is ineffective where the witnesses themselves do not know whether the act will be intentional, because the warned person can later contradict them and say, “I acted inadvertently.” According to the opinion that an uncertain warning is still a valid warning, the case must in fact involve intentional action, or there could be no lashes. This appears to show that where the witnesses saw the act but uncertainty remains only whether it was intentional, the defendant can admit that it was intentional and be punished, despite the rule that a person cannot make himself wicked. That supports Mishneh LaMelech. The passages seem contradictory. It is difficult to distinguish between a case with formal warning and one without warning by saying that after warning inadvertence is unlikely, because the Gemara itself treats the uncertainty as genuine. Moreover, if that distinction were valid, several questions in the sugya would fall away. The statements of Tosafot therefore require reconciliation. There. “Even a valid witness who knows that his fellow is wicked,” etc. The Beit Yosef wonders why Rambam and Tur cite the verse, “Do not place your hand with a wicked person to be a corrupt witness,” when the Gemara derives the prohibition from “Distance yourself from falsehood.” It appears to me that both verses address different forms of the same wrongdoing. We rule that if one witness among a group is found related or disqualified, the testimony of the entire group is void. If a valid witness knows that his fellow witness is a wicked person whose disqualification is unknown to beit din, and nevertheless joins him, the court may rely on the pair and extract money. The valid witness has then literally joined a wicked person to function as a corrupt witness, in violation of “Do not place your hand with a wicked person to be a corrupt witness.” But suppose the valid witness knows that beit din will ultimately discover the other’s disqualification and will not collect money on their testimony. He joins only in order to cause their entire joint testimony to become void, perhaps to help the defendant or harm the claimant. In that case he is not producing a corrupt monetary judgment, so the verse “Do not place your hand” is less directly applicable. Nevertheless, he is presenting himself as a genuine witness while secretly intending to sabotage the testimony, and therefore violates “Distance yourself from falsehood.” The Gemara addresses that form, while Rambam and Tur address the ordinary case where the court would otherwise rely upon the pair. This also explains the Mechaber’s phrase “and it need not be stated” in the stronger case. If the disqualified person really witnessed the event but is a prior robber, then by joining him the valid witness causes his own otherwise valid testimony to be void and may bring about an improper monetary judgment. If the second person is not merely disqualified but is fabricating the entire event, one might have thought the valid witness’s own testimony remains independently true because they were not actually joined at the moment of seeing the event. Nevertheless it is obviously prohibited to join a liar. The Midrash on Eikhah, “The precious sons of Zion,” supports the language of Rambam and Tur: they would not sign a document unless they knew who was signing with them, in fulfillment of “Do not place your hand with a wicked person to be a corrupt witness.” Signing is the beginning of testimony, and the concern is not necessarily that the other signer lies about this event but that he is otherwise disqualified.
Seif 1. Regarding one who raises his hand to strike another, who is called wicked, Tosafot in Sanhedrin 78 and Choshen Mishpat siman 420 discuss his status. I found in Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hilchot Chovel UMazik chapter 5, that such a person is disqualified from testimony and the opposing party swears and collects. It appears, however, that merely raising his hand disqualifies him only rabbinically, because there is no lashes for that act, as it is a prohibition without a physical action, and he is not thereby a wicked person of monetary corruption. If he actually strikes the other person in a manner that does not cause a perutah’s worth of damage, Rambam rules that he receives lashes. He is then disqualified by Torah law. I later saw that the Rama in this siman, seif 2, explicitly indicates that merely raising one’s hand creates only a rabbinic disqualification. Yevamot 22 also supports a distinction. The Gemara asks why “I killed him” should differ from “we killed him” for testimony concerning a woman’s husband. If a person who actually participated in killing is liable to death, he is certainly wicked and disqualified. But where a group says “we killed him,” there can be cases where none is executed. Why should the witness be disqualified? It seems that participation in the assault itself can create a Torah disqualification through the prohibition of striking. One could still suggest a case where they administered poison without physical striking and were not death-liable, leaving room for difficulty. Further study is required.
