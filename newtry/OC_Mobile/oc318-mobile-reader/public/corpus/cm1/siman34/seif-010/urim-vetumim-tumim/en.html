Both the lender and the borrower, etc. The witnesses are omitted because they are not disqualified: the prohibition does not appear serious to them. Bach objects from Tosafot. Tosafot explain that the Mishnah's "interest-bearing lender" can refer to a rabbinic disqualification because even with fixed Torah interest, the lender may not appreciate the prohibition fully since the borrower pays voluntarily. Yet despite this lack of appreciation, the lender is still rabbinically disqualified. If so, Bach asks, why should the witnesses not at least be rabbinically disqualified? Panim Me'irot raises the same question against Beit Yosef, who follows Nimukei Yosef that an interest-paying borrower is only rabbinically disqualified because the prohibition does not appear serious to him. I do not understand the question. There is no universal rule that every person who violates a Torah prohibition which does not appear serious to him is automatically rabbinically disqualified. If so, the Mishnah could simply state: anyone who violates a Torah prohibition without appreciating its seriousness is rabbinically disqualified. Rather, rabbinic disqualification is itself an enactment. Chazal disqualified some offenders even where the Torah prohibition was not fully appreciated, and did not disqualify others. The scope depends on their judgment concerning the nature of the misconduct, the need of the time, and the protection of judicial reliability. Therefore one cannot create an analogy from lender and borrower to the witnesses. Even if Chazal disqualified the lender or borrower because of their participation in an interest transaction, it does not follow that they imposed the same disqualification upon the witnesses, or upon every other participant whose offense was not regarded by him as severe. Rabbinic decrees are not derived from one another by analogy unless the sources establish the comparison. Thus Beit Yosef's ruling presents no difficulty.
And some say that with rabbinic interest only the lender is disqualified, not the borrower. This raises a difficulty from Sanhedrin 25a. Rava says that one who borrows with interest is disqualified. The Gemara asks: but the Mishnah says "one who lends with interest." It answers: "a loan that comes through interest," meaning that even the borrower is included. According to Rama's cited view, this is difficult. Tosafot explain that the Mishnah lists rabbinic disqualifications, and with rabbinic interest the borrower is indeed valid. Rava's statement disqualifying the borrower must then concern fixed Torah interest. Yet the Gemara's answer includes the borrower in the Mishnah's category. If that refers to fixed Torah interest, why is the Mishnah listing this Torah disqualification among rabbinic disqualifications? If the Mishnah wished to enumerate Torah-disqualified witnesses, it could have listed false witnesses, Sabbatical-year violators, Shabbat violators, robbers, and many others. This itself appears to be a strong proof for Nimukei Yosef: even with fixed Torah interest, the lender and borrower are disqualified only rabbinically. Then the Gemara's question is exact. The Mishnah is listing rabbinic disqualifications, and therefore includes a lender even in fixed interest. Why omit the borrower? The answer is that "one who lends with interest" means a loan transaction involving interest and includes the borrower as well. The underlying prohibition is Torah law, but the witness disqualification is rabbinic. Accordingly, if Rama wishes to cite the view that with rabbinic interest only the lender is disqualified, he should also make clear that even with fixed Torah interest the resulting disqualification is only rabbinic, not Torah-level. Rama abbreviated where fuller explanation was needed. I am therefore surprised by Panim Me'irot, part 2, responsum 105, who treats Rava's statement as a difficulty against Nimukei Yosef. It is, on the contrary, a proof for him.
