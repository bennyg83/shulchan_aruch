A slave, etc. Rambam explains that a slave is disqualified from testimony because Scripture says concerning false witnesses, "as he intended to do to his brother." Just as "his brother" refers to those fully within the covenant, so too the witness must be one of them. Kesef Mishneh and especially Lechem Mishneh object from Bava Kamma 88a. The Sages there hold that one who injures a slave is liable for the ordinary damages because Scripture says, "a man and his brother together," and a slave is included among "his brothers" because he is obligated in mitzvot. The Gemara then asks: if so, why should a slave be disqualified from testimony? It answers through a kal vachomer from a robber and a woman. This seems explicit that according to the Sages a slave is included in "brother," and his witness disqualification comes from kal vachomer, not from the verse itself. Rambam was likely troubled by Tosafot's question. If we use a kal vachomer from a robber, then a mamzer should also be disqualified: a robber may enter the congregation yet is disqualified from testimony, so all the more a mamzer. One can answer that the robber's own actions caused his defect, while the mamzer's did not; but then woman can serve as the counterexample. Tosafot give an answer that Rambam apparently did not accept. Rambam therefore holds that a kal vachomer cannot uproot an explicit verse. A mamzer is certainly "his brother." Once Scripture includes him, no kal vachomer can remove him from testimony. Why, then, can the kal vachomer exclude a slave? Because "brother" has two plausible meanings. Ordinarily it can mean a fellow obligated in mitzvot, which would include a slave. But it can also mean a full brother in covenantal lineage. If there were no kal vachomer, we would read the word in its broader ordinary sense and include the slave. Once the kal vachomer strongly indicates that a slave should not be qualified, we use it to determine which meaning Scripture intended: "his brother" here means one fully within the covenantal brotherhood, excluding a slave. Thus the verse and kal vachomer work together rather than one overriding the other. In other contexts, where no kal vachomer compels a narrower reading, "brother" retains its broader meaning of one obligated in mitzvot, and the slave is included. This explains why Rambam records the exclusion as arising from the verse "his brother." The kal vachomer teaches how the verse must be read. There is therefore no contradiction.
