Or if he had relations with his wife, etc. Sema cites Rashba, responsum 1236, that if one says, "Your wife committed adultery with me," we divide his statement: we accept "your wife committed adultery" but not "with me." But if he says, "I committed adultery with your wife," Rashba says the statement cannot be divided. It is difficult that Mechaber omits this distinction here and in Even HaEzer siman 116, where one who says he committed adultery with another man's wife can render her prohibited to her husband. He does not distinguish between the two formulations. One might say Rashba follows the approach of Raavan and Tosafot, discussed above in siman 33, that dividing testimony means linguistically retaining one portion of the statement and rejecting another. According to Raavad's approach, by contrast, a litigant is simply outside the category of testimony concerning himself, so the wording is less decisive. But it is difficult to say Mechaber entirely ignored Tosafot, Rashba, and Raavan. The key is Rashba's own responsum. He asks why, even where the witness says, "I committed adultery with your wife," we do not divide the statement by saying the act was under compulsion. He answers that regarding a male participant, involuntary intercourse is difficult to formulate, since erection generally implies volition, and if non-Jews compelled him he was still obligated to die rather than transgress. Rashba then asks from Yevamot, where one who says "I killed him" can be believed regarding the death so that the wife may remarry. How can the statement be divided linguistically? Rashba answers that one can say he killed unintentionally or under compulsion. This shows that Rashba's limitation concerns the Gemara's capital case, where the witness and another combine to execute the woman. In that setting, one cannot say the witness acted inadvertently, because the case necessarily involved warning and a capital offense. He himself is testifying to a circumstance incompatible with his own claimed inadvertence. But in our practical case the issue is only whether the witness becomes disqualified for transgression, not whether anyone is executed. We can therefore divide the statement by saying that he sinned inadvertently while she acted knowingly. Examples are readily available: he thought the act was permitted, thought she was unmarried, mistook her for his own wife, or otherwise acted unintentionally while she knew the truth. Accordingly, even where he says, "I committed adultery with your wife," we can divide the statement for our purposes and treat him as having acted inadvertently. This is why Mechaber did not preserve Rashba's linguistic distinction. There is further support from the authorities in Yevamot and Ketubot who hold that even in cases of compulsion the statement can be divided more broadly than Tosafot in Sanhedrin allow. Also, Rashba's concern that one compelled by non-Jews still violated kiddush Hashem would not necessarily disqualify him under Rama in Even HaEzer siman 42, who rules that persons who transgress under such compulsion can remain valid witnesses. Accordingly, for the present law, both formulations can be divided.
Or if he sodomized his ox, he and another witness combine to disqualify him. Tur also records this rule, though it is difficult according to Raavad and Rosh. Raavad explains that when one says, "So-and-so sodomized my ox," we do not say a person is related to his property in the formal sense, but he is certainly financially interested in the animal. Since his testimony cannot be accepted regarding the ox, and one portion of the testimony is void, the entire testimony should be void even regarding the offender. Rosh agrees in the first chapter of Makkot. Why then does Tur record the rule without qualification? It appears that Tur is speaking in our time. When capital jurisdiction was operative, the testimony affected the ox directly, because if two valid witnesses testified it would be stoned. The owner was therefore financially interested in that portion of the testimony. Once his testimony was invalid regarding the ox, the rule that testimony void in part is void in full could apply to the testimony regarding the offender. But today, unfortunately, we do not adjudicate capital cases. The ox will not be stoned and remains permitted for ordinary use. There is therefore no present judicial consequence concerning the ox. More than that, testimony concerning the ox would require a beit din of ordained judges. Our present courts are not competent to receive that capital testimony. Just as an admission to a fine before an unordained court is treated as an admission outside beit din because the court lacks jurisdiction, testimony concerning the ox is likewise outside the operative judicial framework. Thus the words concerning the ox never become legal testimony at all, while the words concerning the person, for purposes of disqualifying him as a witness, do come before a competent beit din. Accordingly, the rule that testimony void in part is void in full does not apply. This explains why Tur preserves the practical rule for our courts even though Rosh agreed with Raavad in the original capital-law setting.
To testify, etc. This law requires study. If the litigant himself can testify to disqualify a person, all the more so should his relatives be able to do so. But Shevuot 37b appears difficult. The Gemara asks whether one who intentionally swears falsely concerning a deposit after warning can incur both lashes and an offering. Rabbah says such a case would involve denial in the face of witnesses, because the witnesses who warned him can also testify against him and make the denial ineffective. Why must those warning witnesses be valid for the depositor's monetary claim? Perhaps they are relatives of the depositor but unrelated to the custodian. Then they could not help recover the deposit, but could still testify to the custodian's wickedness and lashes, just as the litigant himself can testify to disqualify him. Likewise, the depositor and one other witness could give the warning. They should be believed for punishment even if the depositor cannot testify for his own money. Ran in his novellae to Shevuot cites this very question from Raavad. His answers revolve around the principle that we do not divide the testimony in that way. Ran adds that the witnesses remain interested, because fear that they may later testify and cause lashes could induce the defendant to admit and thereby benefit the depositor. Ran's answer is itself difficult where the oath is a voluntary oath of denial rather than a court-imposed oath. If the defendant fears witnesses, no one forces him to swear. He can simply deny without taking the oath. In any event, the original Gemara difficulty remains substantial. I think there is another complication from Bava Metzia. The Rishonim ask why one who denies a deposit becomes disqualified. Perhaps he has an old outstanding loan claim against the depositor and therefore withholds the object. Ramban answers that a mere possibility does not overcome the definite act of denial for purposes of future witness qualification. Another answer in Shitah Mekubetzet says that where witnesses establish the deposit, the denier knows beit din will force him to return it. His denial therefore cannot be explained as merely seizing his own alleged claim. Apply that here. How can lashes be imposed for the false oath? Even if witnesses know the deposit was entrusted to him, perhaps he has an old loan claim against the depositor. For an offering, he can later admit the facts and obligate himself. But lashes cannot be imposed where even a remote uncertainty remains. We must therefore rely on the second answer: where valid witnesses can recover the deposit for the depositor, the defendant's denial is exposed as wrongful. But if the warning witnesses are relatives of the depositor, their testimony cannot recover the deposit. Then the old-loan possibility remains and lashes cannot be imposed. This explains why Rabbah needs witnesses valid for the depositor's monetary claim. One cannot answer that the case involved two partners as depositors and each could testify regarding the other, because the resulting testimony would be fragmented and analogous to separate testimonies concerning different events. For lashes, which resembles capital procedure, such joined partial testimony is problematic. Accordingly, the rule that a litigant can testify merely to disqualify another remains difficult against the plain sugya. I discuss the matter further below in siman 92.
Provided that he derives no benefit from his testimony, etc. See Urim regarding Shach's objection to Sema's statement that, unlike Mordechai, Mechaber does not necessarily require that the witness received no interest payment. Shach cites Rivash, responsum 339. Rivash argues against Mordechai: even if the witness says, "He lent me with interest and I paid him the interest," we divide the statement. We accept that the lender lent with interest, but not that the witness himself was the borrower. If so, what financial interest remains? Shach rejects Rivash and argues that dividing testimony works only where the witness is not personally interested. If he stands to gain, we presume the entire testimony false. I believe Rivash has strong support. Rashba, cited by Beit Yosef here, explains the Gemara's case of one who testified, "He lent me with interest," as occurring before the witness had sued the lender for return of the interest. According to Mordechai, however, the case must involve one who never paid the interest or already waived it. If so, what lawsuit for return of interest could there be? Rashba's language therefore implies that even where the witness paid, we divide the statement. So long as he has not sued for repayment of the interest, he is treated as having removed himself from that monetary claim. This resembles the rule that with monetary interest we do not require qualification both at the beginning and end. Once he removes his interest, he can testify. Raavan states the matter even more explicitly. Where one witness to an interest-bearing loan is related to the lender or borrower, his testimony can be accepted against the unrelated party through division of the statement. If fixed Torah interest was paid and the borrower later sues the lender for return, the relative's testimony may disqualify the lender, but it cannot be used to recover money for his relative. That is exactly Rivash's principle. At first I wondered: if the lender is disqualified through this testimony and later wishes to regain qualification, must he not return the interest? If so, the witness or his relative still benefits indirectly, because the lender may repay in order to rehabilitate himself. But Raavan writes that in this case the lender's rehabilitation need not depend specifically on return of this interest. He can go to a place where he is unknown and demonstrate repentance by returning a lost object or similar conduct. Thus the witness does not necessarily gain from the disqualification. The practical issue depends on the two explanations in Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Makkot. According to Raavad's approach, a litigant is simply outside the category of testimony concerning himself. We do not reinterpret the words as "he lent with interest, but not to me." The statement still says the loan was to him. If so, Shach is correct: so long as the witness remains monetarily interested, dividing testimony cannot remove that interest. There is proof from Bava Batra, where the Gemara asks why two interested parties cannot simply remove their interest and testify. Tosafot ask that qualification is required both at the beginning and end, and answer that because they were litigants rather than witnesses regarding themselves, the usual beginning-in-disqualification rule does not apply. If mere division of testimony already removed their personal interest, why would actual removal be necessary? This supports Shach according to Raavad's framework. But according to the second explanation, the person does enter the category of testimony and we divide the words themselves: "He lent with interest," but not necessarily to this witness. Then Rivash is correct. Once the legal content of the testimony no longer gives the witness any right to recover the interest, there is no remaining financial interest. There is no greater removal than the court itself treating the testimony as referring to someone else. Rashba, as explained above, follows this second approach, and Raavan states it explicitly. They therefore support Rivash. Mordechai follows Raavad's approach and accordingly requires that the witness did not actually receive or retain a claim to the interest. Rama appropriately rules cautiously that we should not disqualify a person from testimony in a doubtful case, and therefore requires that the witness have no monetary benefit. If, however, the witness has already brought a lawsuit against the lender for return of the interest, everyone agrees he has become an actual litigant and cannot afterward testify against him in this matter. This is clear.
