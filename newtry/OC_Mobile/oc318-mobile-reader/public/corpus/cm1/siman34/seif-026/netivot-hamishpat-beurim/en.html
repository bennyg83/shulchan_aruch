You came in order to testify. Shach writes that if none of the people present intended at the time of seeing to testify, then if qualified and disqualified people later all testify before beit din, the disqualified members can invalidate the entire joined testimony. At first glance this seems inconsistent with the Mishnah, which conditions invalidation on intention to testify. The proper distinction is between joinder at the time of seeing and joinder later at the time of testimony. According to Rashbam, one who sees and intends to testify already receives the name 'witness' at the time of seeing. According to Rabbi Yitzchak, witness status arises only upon actual testimony. From Rabbi Yitzchak we can learn that even if no one had witness status at the time of seeing, once qualified and disqualified participants later testify as one joined group, the later joinder can invalidate all. Therefore, if only the qualified witnesses testify and the disqualified people never testify, the latter never acquire witness status and cannot invalidate the former. If the qualified witnesses intended to testify while the disqualified people did not, there is also no complete joinder because their statuses were unequal from the outset. Even according to Sema, who disagrees with Shach where no one originally intended to testify, I incline that if qualified and disqualified witnesses later testify within the same immediate testimonial unit, their joinder in the courtroom can invalidate the whole, much like partially disproved witnesses. Still, the principal law follows Sema that where none originally intended to testify, mere common observation does not itself invalidate the qualified witnesses. If a witness was qualified when he saw the event but became disqualified before testimony, the entire joined testimony can likewise be affected, because once the group was joined in observation and part later becomes invalid, the rule that partial invalidation can defeat the whole applies.
