That he lent to him at interest. The Rambam likewise writes, 'He lent to me at interest,' and the Sema understands that the borrower is nevertheless believed to disqualify the lender, rejecting the Mordechai's requirement that the witness received no benefit. This is astonishing. The Rambam uses the Gemara's exact language, and just as the Mordechai interprets the Gemara to mean that no benefit was received, the Rambam and Mechaber can be read the same way. Moreover, the Rama immediately equates the robbed party's testimony against the robber with the borrower's testimony against the lender, provided they derive no benefit, precisely as the Mordechai rules. The Rivash attacks the Mordechai, arguing that because a person cannot testify about himself, we divide his statement and treat him as having testified only that the other person lent at interest. He further argues that even one who says, 'He stole from me,' may join another witness to disqualify the thief, because testimony about oneself has no status as testimony at all. In my view this is incorrect. Division of a statement applies only where we do not suspect personal interest. Where the witness may benefit, we suspect that he testifies for that reason. An agricultural tenant cannot testify while produce remains on the land; we do not say that he is a party and simply divide his statement. The same applies here. Nor is the proof from removal of interest in communal litigation decisive, for removal is still required, and the Ramban and other authorities explain that passage differently. Even if a party is not called a disqualified witness concerning himself, he can still have been disqualified at the beginning of the matter through personal interest, just as one who observed testimony concerning his wife cannot testify after divorcing her. The Rashba's responsum that two robbed parties may join concerns a case where they gain nothing from their testimony, such as after waiving restitution. Otherwise they are interested witnesses. The ruling of Rabbi Yakar is therefore correct.
Provided that he derives no benefit. In the Shulchan Aruch printed in Hanau in 1617, the text reads, 'provided that there is no benefit.' The Levush reads, 'provided that they derive no benefit,' and this is the correct version. See below, siman 37, seif 16 in the gloss.
