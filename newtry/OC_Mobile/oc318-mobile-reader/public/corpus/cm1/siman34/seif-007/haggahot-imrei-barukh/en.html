(Taz, seif katan 7) Many question the Tur's expression of astonishment, etc. Note: I will explain the Taz's question and what appears to me to resolve it. The Tur, in seif 15, cites the Rambam that although a thief or robber returned the money he stole or robbed, and although a zomem witness paid the money concerning which he was rendered zomem, they remain disqualified. The Tur writes: "I do not know why they should not become qualified after returning the money. Perhaps he means that although they paid, since they paid under compulsion of beit din they remain disqualified until they return it voluntarily." The Taz asks that the Tur contradicts himself, because later, in seif 42, he again cites the Rambam that if two witnesses testify that a person disqualified by one of these sins repented or received lashes, he becomes qualified, but other witnesses disqualified because of money they extorted or stole require repentance even after paying. There the Tur raises no objection. The matter may be resolved simply. The Rambam in Hilchot Edut chapter 10 explains that the Torah disqualifies as a witness one whose conduct gives him the title "wicked." Thus one liable to lashes is disqualified, as Scripture calls him "the wicked one" in the verse concerning lashes, and certainly one liable to a court-imposed death penalty. Bava Kamma 60b, discussing David's question whether he could take Israelite barley for his animals intending to repay with Philistine lentils, cites the verse, "the wicked one shall restore the pledge, he shall repay what he robbed," and derives that even though a robber pays, he remains called wicked. This could explain why the Rambam disqualifies a robber who pays until he has actually repented. But the Tur's wording still requires precision. If his answer were merely that the Rambam speaks of payment compelled by beit din, he should have said explicitly, "Perhaps he means that he paid under compulsion." It appears instead that the Tur has a different intent. Why indeed is a robber who pays still called wicked? Ordinarily, when a prohibition is linked to a positive commandment, fulfillment of the positive commandment removes the force of the prohibition. Tosafot, Pesachim 29b, even write that one who retains chametz intending to destroy it does not violate the prohibition because the prohibition of possessing chametz is linked to the positive commandment of destruction. Although robbery may leave the separate sin of having caused distress, why should that alone give the Torah status of "wicked," especially in David's case where the owners apparently did not know of the taking and therefore suffered no immediate distress? The Rambam at the beginning of Hilchot Gezelah writes that one who robs a perutah violates "You shall not rob." He is not lashed because Scripture linked the prohibition to the positive commandment, "he shall return the stolen item that he robbed." Even if he burned the stolen object he is not lashed, because he must pay its value and a prohibition assigned to monetary payment does not incur lashes. The Rambam thus gives two distinct reasons: where the stolen object itself is returned, the prohibition is corrected by the positive commandment of return; where the object was lost or burned, the robber remains obligated in payment and therefore is not both lashed and required to pay. This follows the discussion in Makkot 16 and the explanation cited by the Ritva from the Ramah. Accordingly, the commandment of "he shall return" removes the robbery prohibition only when the robber returns the very object stolen, as implied by the words, "the stolen item that he robbed." If the object was lost or burned, the positive commandment of return can no longer be fulfilled with that object. The robber nevertheless pays its value, and the reason there are no lashes is the rule that a person does not receive lashes and make monetary payment for the same act. Conversely, when the actual object is returned, the exemption from lashes cannot rest on payment, because returning an object that always belonged to the owner is not a monetary payment in the technical sense. Sanhedrin 72 likewise explains that an existing stolen object remains the owner's property and is merely held as a deposit in the robber's hands. Therefore return of the object requires the reason that the prohibition was linked to a positive commandment, whereas payment after destruction relies on the rule against lashes together with payment. This also explains the Rambam's precise words, "this is a positive commandment": the positive commandment is specifically to return the stolen object itself. Once the robber does not return that very object, payment of its value does not erase the prohibition of robbery. He therefore remains in his wickedness and is still called wicked. That is the Gemara's precision, "although he pays for his robbery, he is wicked." By contrast, when the stolen object itself is returned, he is no longer called wicked, even if beit din compelled the return, because the positive commandment has been fulfilled and the prohibition has thereby been corrected. The Tur therefore understood from the outset that the Rambam's case involved compelled restitution, for if the robber returned the property willingly he would already be a complete penitent. The Tur's difficulty was that even compelled return of the actual stolen object should remove the prohibition, so why should he remain disqualified? Hence the Tur writes, "I do not know why they should not qualify after returning the money," using the language of return, which naturally means returning the very thing stolen. The Tur then answers, "Perhaps he means that although they paid." In other words, perhaps the Rambam's wording is imprecise and his case is not return of the stolen object, but payment of its equivalent in money. Such payment does not correct the robbery prohibition, and the robber remains wicked unless the payment itself was voluntary repentance. If payment occurred only under compulsion of beit din, he remains disqualified. But when the stolen object itself is returned, even by compulsion, he becomes qualified. Thus the Tur's entire comment concerns the Rambam's expression "although he returned it." Later, in seif 42, where the Tur cites the Rambam's separate formulation in Hilchot Edut, "although they paid," the Tur raises no objection, because he himself agrees that where the stolen object was not returned and only its value was paid, the offenders remain disqualified until they repent. Voluntary payment itself is praiseworthy and can constitute repentance. The Rambam's language at the beginning of Hilchot Geneivah also distinguishes return from payment: it is forbidden to steal even with intent to return, or with intent to pay, so that one not accustom himself to such conduct. Thus "return" and "payment" are not one and the same category.
