Who transgressed a rabbinic prohibition. See Pri Megadim on Orach Chaim, general introduction, letter 24, who writes that in his opinion one is termed a rabbinic rasha only where the transgression incurs lashes for rebellious conduct. Otherwise he is not so classified. This is indicated by the Rambam's language, “where the liability is of rabbinic origin.” Accordingly, scholarly investigation is needed to determine which transgressions incur such lashes and which do not, for in the latter category the person is not disqualified even rabbinically. See also Pri Megadim inside siman 328, seif katan 8. Urim VeTumim writes that one who has relations with a non-Jewish woman is also disqualified rabbinically, as the Sema states. If this is done publicly, or he takes her in a form of marriage, he is disqualified by Torah law because he violates “You shall not intermarry with them.” Netivot HaMishpat writes similarly. See Even HaEzer, siman 16. At first glance, however, the Sema himself appears to follow Beit Yosef that one who has relations with an idolatrous woman is disqualified by Torah law, and Baer HaGolah likewise cites this in his name. Birkei Yosef, letter 11, records that Maharshal in a responsum, siman 19, regarded such a person as rabbinically disqualified, and that Knesset HaGedolah in Even HaEzer, siman 42, and Ere'a DeRabbanan, letter 528, objected to this. Birkei Yosef further writes that one who violates a rabbinic prohibition passively, through failure to act, is not called a rasha and remains valid for testimony. Although the Gemara calls one who gives devious counsel a “cunning rasha,” he is not thereby termed simply a rasha. And although Yevamot teaches that one who violates the words of the Sages is called a rasha, one who does so through passive nonperformance is not called a rabbinic rasha and is not disqualified from testimony, as Responsa Chut HaShani, siman 18, rules.
