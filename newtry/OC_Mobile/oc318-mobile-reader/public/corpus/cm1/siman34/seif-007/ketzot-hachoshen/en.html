Even though he returned it. The Tur distinguishes between one who returned stolen property only through coercion and one who returned it voluntarily. In cases of habitual wrongdoing, such as usury, mere repayment may not be sufficient rehabilitation. But one who committed a single theft and returned it voluntarily can regain testimonial fitness. Rama follows this approach below in seif 29. Bach argues that even for a one time theft, once the thief has actually returned the property, he should regain fitness even if beit din compelled him. A difficulty arises from Bava Metzia 5 concerning the shepherd whom witnesses said had eaten two sheep. Rabbi Zeira says that if Rabbi Chiya's rule applies he should swear, but the Gemara objects that he is a robber and therefore disqualified. Since he must repay the two sheep, why should he remain disqualified? Bach could answer that rehabilitation occurs only once repayment has actually been made. At the moment beit din is determining whether he must swear, he has not yet returned the value. Since he is then disqualified, the rule becomes that the opposing party swears and collects. Later repayment does not reopen that already fixed procedural result. This resembles what I wrote in siman 69. Nevertheless, Tosafot explicitly rejects Bach's leniency. Tosafot says that repayment forced by witnesses is not a proper restoration because he pays only because he has been caught. That is why the shepherd remains disqualified, and why one who denied a deposit remains disqualified until genuine repentance. This applies even to a one time offense. Therefore Rama's rule is primary.
One who shares with the thief. Rivash ruled concerning one who took a share of property stolen from a Jew and was compelled by beit din to return it. He does not become disqualified as a witness merely because of that participation, comparing the case to one who tells an agent, "Go steal," where the sender is exempt because there is no agency for transgression. Tumim is astonished: this person knowingly holds another's money and refuses to return it. Why is he different from one who denies a deposit, who is disqualified even though the property first came to him lawfully? Tumim therefore suggests that Rivash speaks only after he returned the property, perhaps even through coercion, or after the owner's despair. Neither suggestion fits Rivash or Rama, who cite the ruling without such limitations. The true distinction is that a person who receives stolen property after the original theft does not thereby become the owner's robber. One who steals from a thief does not pay double payment because the Torah says, "stolen from the house of the man," and after the first theft the property is no longer fully within the owner's domain. Bava Kamma further records the view that before the owner's despair, if another person consumes the stolen property, the owner may sometimes recover only from the first robber because the second party never became his robber. We rule that the owner can recover from the consumer, but that is because the consumer damaged the owner's property, not because he became the original robber. If the second person merely takes the object and it is later lost without his damaging it, the special robbery obligation need not attach to him toward the original owner. The first robber himself pays according to the value at the time of his first theft, even though he continues holding the object afterward, because later possession is no longer a new act of robbery from the owner's domain. By contrast, a deposit remains fully within the owner's legal possession. One who denies the deposit becomes a robber of the owner and is disqualified. Therefore a person who merely joins the thief afterward and takes a share need not acquire the testimonial status of a robber toward the owner, even though the object itself must be returned if still extant. Rivash's ruling is therefore sound. There is one possible difficulty where the person originally participated in arranging the theft itself. Rivash discusses a case that may imply he first appointed the thief as agent. According to Rashi in Bava Metzia, where partners steal and one acts also for himself, agency for transgression can apply through the principle that because he acquires for himself he acquires for his fellow. Tosafot disagrees. Thus if the participant commissioned the theft as a true partner from the outset, his status may remain doubtful according to the dispute between Rashi and Tosafot. Rama's ruling can be understood as addressing one who only shared afterward.
