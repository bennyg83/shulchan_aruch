Payment for testimony. Mahari ben Lev is uncertain whether one who receives payment to testify is disqualified biblically or only rabbinically. This turns on a deeper question. If an interested witness is disqualified because we suspect he will lie for financial advantage, then a paid witness should likewise be biblically disqualified through the same concern. If an interested witness is disqualified instead because a person is legally considered his own relative, without any presumption of lying, then a paid witness can remain biblically qualified. In my view the second approach is correct, and a paid witness is biblically valid. Some support comes from the law of a husband who makes a defamatory accusation against his wife. Rabbi Yehudah derives that he is liable only where he "placed words against her" through payment, linking the verse to "you shall not place interest." Yet the continuation says that if the accusation proves true, the woman can be executed. Thus the Torah itself contemplates witnesses who were hired and nevertheless treats their testimony as capable of capital consequence, absent refutation. This suggests that receiving money does not itself create biblical testimonial disqualification. We must therefore explain the biblical invalidity of an interested witness differently. Mahari ben Lev suggests that personal interest disqualifies because a person is legally his own relative. He asks why, if so, a person is believed against himself, since relatives are disqualified both for benefit and detriment. He answers that an admission against oneself is legally a new undertaking or gift. This cannot be the true explanation. If a person first denied borrowing and witnesses later prove payment, he cannot now say, "I repaid," because his own prior statement, "I never borrowed," carries the implication, "I never repaid," and his own admission is stronger than witnesses. No new undertaking theory explains that. Likewise an ordinary admission of an old debt can be binding even without the formal language needed for a newly created obligation. Bava Metzia also derives the oath of partial admission directly from one's own mouth. If admissions worked merely as voluntary gifts or new undertakings, they would not generate the Torah category of partial admission. The correct principle is that Torah law gave every person special credibility concerning himself, despite all ordinary testimonial disqualifications. Rashi in Kiddushin states this explicitly from the verse, "when he says, this is it." The Torah's requirement of two witnesses applies when a person testifies about others. With respect to himself, the Torah entrusted his own statement, whether he is otherwise a relative, slave, woman, or single witness. Thus an interested witness is invalid regarding others because he is legally testifying about himself, while his own admission against himself is accepted under a separate Torah rule. This also explains the doctrine that one can make something prohibited upon himself through his own statement. It is fundamentally a rule of personal credibility, not merely a vow, for otherwise release by a sage should undo it. There remains a question why personal admission can overcome contrary witnesses. The verse shows that his own mouth is accepted in its own domain, but perhaps only absent witnesses. Yet the halachah demonstrates that the personal admission rule is itself stronger concerning the person than outside testimony. This is a distinct Torah grant of credibility, not an ordinary migo. Accordingly, the paid witness remains biblically qualified unless some other biblical defect exists, while the interested witness is excluded because his testimony is legally testimony concerning himself.
