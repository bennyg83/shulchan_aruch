No public declaration is required, etc. According to what the Sema writes below, seif katan 55, in the name of the Nimukei Yosef, that with respect to the very testimony through which a witness becomes disqualified no public declaration is needed, one must ask why the Rama gives the reason that this disqualification is unlike other disqualifications that require public declaration. The same is true of every disqualified witness with respect to the testimony through which he became disqualified. It must be explained in accordance with the Ran in the second chapter of Kiddushin. One who takes payment to testify is not disqualified only from that particular testimony; rather, every testimony he gives afterward is also disqualified, because he remains under a presumption that he takes payment to testify until it is established that he took no payment. One might therefore have thought that a public declaration is needed regarding future testimony, so that people will know that he is presumed to take payment and will not use him as a witness where they will later be unable to prove that he took no payment. The ruling therefore teaches that even for those later testimonies no declaration is needed, because his disqualification is not absolute, it can be repaired by returning the payment, and the entire disqualification is merely a penalty imposed by the Sages. The Rama abbreviated too much. He should first have cited the Ran's ruling that later testimony is also disqualified, and then stated that no public declaration is required. It also follows that because this is not an inherent status of disqualification, if three witnesses testified and one of them took payment, the testimony of the remaining two is valid. It is not treated like a case where one member of the group is found to be a relative or otherwise disqualified. Ritva writes this explicitly in the second chapter of Kiddushin. Likewise, if Reuven paid Levi to testify on his behalf in a dispute with Shimon, but Levi testified in Shimon's favor and against Reuven, Levi's testimony appears valid. By doing so he brought a loss upon the person who hired him and forfeited his payment. We do not allow a sinner to profit from his act, and if we disqualified the testimony he could also use this as a device for manipulation.
It is forbidden to accept it. The Sema asks from the incident in Perek Zeh Borer in which Bar Chama undertook to pay Rabbi Papi's royal tax for an entire year because Rabbi Papi had argued in his favor. The Sema answers in the name of the Rosh that Rabbi Papi was in any event exempt from the tax. Knesset HaGedolah above, siman 9, asks what benefit Bar Chama then provided. If Rabbi Papi was already exempt, his action accomplished nothing. If one says that Rabbi Papi nevertheless used to pay as an act of piety, the difficulty remains: how could Rabbi Papi abandon his pious practice and benefit from another person where there would be an appearance of bribery, which the Rama forbids? Knesset HaGedolah further asks according to Ramban below, siman 163, who holds that a royal tax imposed by the king upon a particular individual must be paid even by a Torah scholar, and who derives this very rule from the case of Rabbi Papi. According to Ramban, how could Rabbi Papi accept Bar Chama's benefit? It appears that the Rama's prohibition on accepting a gift by way of tax does not apply here because Bar Chama did not pay the tax out of his own pocket. He merely used his influence with the king to obtain an exemption for Rabbi Papi. Since by law Rabbi Papi should have been exempt, and the king had imposed an improper burden upon him, what monetary benefit was given here and what act of piety was surrendered? Bar Chama was independently obligated to try to persuade the king to remove an unlawful exaction. Ramban's difficulty is also resolved, because Bar Chama merely undertook to intercede with the king for an exemption. As Ramban himself explains, this did not increase the burden borne by the other Jews. It was therefore a case where one party benefits and the other loses nothing. More fundamentally, Ramban and the Rosh follow their respective positions concerning one who pays another person's debt without authorization. According to the view that the debtor is exempt from reimbursement, that rule is derived from the law that a person prohibited by vow from benefiting another may nevertheless pay his debt, even where the creditor presses for payment, and may contribute his half shekel even where the treasurer would otherwise seize assets. Such payment is regarded merely as driving away a lion. If this is permitted under the stringent Torah law of vows, all the more so should it be permitted here, where the concern is only the appearance of bribery or receiving compensation for a judgment. Thus, according to Ramban, Bar Chama actually paid the royal tax for Rabbi Papi without Rabbi Papi having instructed him to do so. This is payment of another person's debt without authorization, which is permitted even where benefit is prohibited by vow, and certainly here. The Rosh, however, follows his view below, siman 128, that where the creditor is pressing, one who pays another person's debt may recover from him, and such payment is forbidden where benefit is prohibited by vow, as the Tur states explicitly. It would therefore also be forbidden in the context of a judgment. The Rosh consequently needed to explain that a Torah scholar was exempt from the tax, and Bar Chama merely interceded with the king, so no monetary benefit was conferred. Each authority thus follows his own position. In my view this is correct. We may derive a stable principle from this: whenever an act is permitted under the laws of vows because it is not considered a prohibited benefit, it is likewise permitted in connection with a judgment, especially after the judgment has already been rendered, when there is no concern of actual bribery but only payment for judgment or belated bribery. Since the act is not legally considered a benefit in the law of vows, it is permitted here as well.
Because of suffering, etc. I do not understand why this rule appears in the laws of testimony. With witnesses such a case does not ordinarily arise, because testimony must be given before beit din. If beit din knows that the witness is acting under compulsion, how could it accept his testimony, and certainly a Jewish beit din would never torture him. If beit din does not know that he was compelled, how can he later say, "I was under compulsion," after the rule that once a witness has testified he may not retract? If there are other witnesses who establish the compulsion, then it becomes a case of two against two, as explained below in siman 46. The actual ruling in Maharik's responsum concerns a person who, after being tortured by a non-Jewish court, admitted that another person held money belonging to someone else as a deposit. They then sought to impose an oath upon that other person on the ground that the claimant had a definite claim based on information received from another. Maharik ruled that this is not considered a definite claim at all, because the statement made in the foreign court was extracted through torture and has no substance. It is therefore only an uncertain claim. This has nothing to do with the law of testimony and belongs instead in the laws governing claims and defenses. Perhaps the present rule concerns a case where the litigant accepted upon himself whatever certain persons would testify in the foreign court, and they then testified there under torture, as those courts commonly do when they suspect witnesses. The ruling would teach that such testimony has no force because he did not accept them under those circumstances. This explanation, however, is strained.
