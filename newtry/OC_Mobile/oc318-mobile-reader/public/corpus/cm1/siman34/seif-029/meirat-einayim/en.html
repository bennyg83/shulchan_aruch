Until it is known that they retracted, etc. Ran writes that according to Rabbeinu Chananel and Rif, a robber becomes qualified again only from the time witnesses testify in beit din that he repented. Any documents he signed in the interim are invalid. Ran calls this surprising: once witnesses establish that he had already become qualified when he signed, it is revealed retroactively that he was fit to testify then. The Mechaber's unqualified ruling here therefore requires study.
They have broken their bills in which they are called a rocky and abominable horn and a horn in their midst:
Even to a non-Jew. This law is stated in a baraita. Hagahot Maimoniyot explains that the case is where he accepted upon himself not to lend with interest even to a non-Jew, although lending a non-Jew with interest sufficient for one's livelihood is permitted. If the amount exceeded his livelihood needs it would already be prohibited. Perhaps even where he accepts only not to exceed that amount, it still shows repentance because he acknowledges the prohibition.
And he must return, etc. Meisharim writes that repentance for a borrower on interest requires that he accept upon himself not to borrow with interest even from a non-Jew. The requirement here to return interest or stolen property means that the sinner must do what lies upon him, while the recipient then follows the applicable law. A habitual robber may be one from whom the victim should decline repayment in certain repentance circumstances, whereas an occasional robber may be required to restore it and the victim may accept. In any event, one seeking complete repentance must satisfy Heaven and remove from his possession what is not his.
