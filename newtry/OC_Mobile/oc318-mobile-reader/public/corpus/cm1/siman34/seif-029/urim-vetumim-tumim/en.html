Once he received lashes, etc. Shach writes in seif katan 33 that one who was disqualified because of wicked conduct and later repented may testify concerning events he saw while still wicked, because for this kind of disqualification we do not require that the witness have been qualified both at the beginning and at the end. He states this as obvious. In my view the opposite is correct. Mordechai gives two reasons why, in a case of monetary interest, we do not require qualification at both the beginning and end. First, the witness can remove his monetary interest at any time. Second, he is not disqualified generally. He is disqualified only regarding the particular person or matter in which he is interested. Once the interest is removed, he is like an unrelated outsider. The second reason plainly does not apply to a wicked witness. A rasha is disqualified for everyone, more broadly than a relative, who is disqualified only for relatives. One might try to use Mordechai's first reason: a sinner can repent at any moment, just as an interested witness can remove his interest. But the second explanation is the primary one, and Ramban states it in Bava Batra. Moreover, the first explanation is itself difficult. A person who knew testimony while he was another's son-in-law and later the daughter died remains disqualified because the testimony began in disqualification. Yet he could theoretically have divorced his wife at any time, even against her will under the original Torah framework. Why does the ability to remove the relationship not help just as the ability to remove monetary interest does? One possible answer is that Mordechai follows Rambam, who treats marital relatives as rabbinically disqualified. Chazal patterned that decree after blood relatives, whose relationship cannot be removed. Therefore even though marital relationship can theoretically end, the rabbinic decree follows the Torah model and requires qualification at the beginning. But according to most authorities, who treat many marital relationships as full Torah disqualifications, even this answer is unavailable. There is strong proof from Ketubot. Rashi explains the Mishnah where witnesses say, "We were disqualified," as including gamblers. Raah explains that because gambling disqualification is rabbinic, the witnesses can say, "We were gamblers and later repented," without violating the rule that a person does not make himself wicked. R. Yosef ibn Migash says similarly even concerning Torah robbery: they can be believed to say they had sinned and then repented. Now the Gemara says that where their handwriting is not independently established, the Sages believe them, while R. Meir does not. Tosafot ask: if the Sages believe them, why is even an oral loan preserved? They answer that no oral-loan testimony remains because the witnesses were unqualified at the beginning, and testimony requires qualification both at the beginning and the end. According to Raah and R. Yosef ibn Migash, the witnesses are now repentant and qualified. If Shach were correct that wickedness does not require qualification at the beginning, there would indeed be an oral loan. One cannot say those authorities reject Tosafot and preserve an oral loan. Tosafot's point is necessary to the sugya. Otherwise the witnesses would lack a genuine migo to say the document was forged, because by claiming former disqualification they would still preserve an oral debt, whereas a forgery claim would destroy the debt entirely. Their alleged migo would not be equivalent. Thus even according to R. Yosef ibn Migash, a witness who was wicked at the time he saw the event cannot testify after repentance. Beginning in qualification is required. There is another proof from the case, "He lent to me with interest." The Gemara asks that the witness himself is wicked. According to R. Yosef ibn Migash, perhaps the witness means: "He lent to me with interest, but I repented afterward." He could be believed in this through a migo that he could have said simply, "He lent with interest," without identifying himself. If repentance cured a bad beginning, he would now be qualified to disqualify the lender. The Gemara does not answer this way. One may say the Gemara did not spell out every condition, just as under Mordechai it must also be assumed that the witness did not retain a monetary claim for return of the interest. Still, the simpler implication is that repentance does not cure the fact that the event was witnessed while he was disqualified. Likewise R. Yosef says, "So-and-so sodomized me under compulsion" is accepted, while "willingly" is not because the witness makes himself wicked. Why not distinguish within the willingly case: if he says he later repented, he should become qualified? The obvious answer is that even after repentance the testimony began while he was disqualified. One could object from Rava, who says a person is related to himself and cannot make himself wicked, so even a willingly participating victim can testify against the offender. Perhaps this shows bad beginning does not matter. But Rava's statement is aimed at R. Yosef's general rule. He says the self-incriminating portion is not testimony at all. Rava need not be addressing every later procedural consequence of a separately admitted repentance. This is not enough to overturn the clearer proofs. There is also support from Bava Kamma. The Gemara derives a slave's witness disqualification through a kal vachomer from a robber and a woman. If a robber did not require qualification at the beginning, the slave should likewise not require it, because it is enough for the derived case to be no more stringent than its source. Yet below, siman 35, seif 7, a slave who is later freed cannot testify concerning what he saw while a slave. This too suggests a formerly wicked witness cannot testify to what he saw while wicked. I later realized Shach may have relied on Rosh at the end of the second chapter of Gittin, citing Ramah, that an agent who received a get while wicked and later repented may deliver it after repentance. Tur and Mechaber rule so in Even HaEzer siman 141. But this actually proves the opposite. Rosh explains the validity by comparing the case to one who was sighted and later became blind. Yet such a person is not generally qualified for ordinary testimony after the defect arose. Tosafot and Rashba explain that the leniency is specific to agency in a get. If a repentant rasha were broadly valid for all testimony regardless of his initial disqualification, Rosh would not need to compare him to a case whose leniency is itself specific to get agency. The correct conclusion is that both cases are special to the rules of delivering a get. Mahari ibn Lev reaches the same conclusion. Most decisively, the Tosefta in Sanhedrin chapter 5 states: if one knew testimony while he was a dealer in Sabbatical-year produce and later repented, he is disqualified. If he knew the testimony before becoming such a dealer, then became disqualified and afterward repented, he is valid because his beginning and end were both in qualification. This is explicit. Even for a rabbinic wickedness disqualification, qualification at the beginning and end is required. All the more so for a Torah robber. This Tosefta was evidently not before Shach when he first wrote his ruling. I later saw that Shach himself, at the end of siman 35, retracted and inclined to the view that a wicked witness does require qualification at both beginning and end. That is the correct law.
