An apostate who returned, etc. See Urim, where I cited Sema's explanation. Shach writes in seif katan 20 that according to Sema this special rule would apply only to an apostate for idolatry. Shach rejects that and says every habitual sinner can become qualified immediately upon sincere acceptance to abandon the sin. His proof is Maharik, who derives the rule from a kohen who married a woman forbidden to him. The kohen vows, descends from the platform, performs the required service, and divorces her. Shach argues that this shows acceptance can rehabilitate every sinner. The proof points in the opposite direction. The kohen is not restored merely because he says, "I accept upon myself not to marry forbidden women again." He is required actually to vow. This shows we do not automatically trust bare acceptance. A visible confirming act is required. The reason is that we suspect he may not keep a simple promise. Once he makes an actual vow, however, we do not presume he will violate the vow. Thus, in the kohen's case, acceptance alone is insufficient because nothing outwardly demonstrates repentance. The vow is the objective sign. Likewise, with an apostate for idolatry, separating himself from the pleasures and social world of the non-Jews and returning to Jewish life is itself a visible act demonstrating genuine return. That is what Sema means. This is analogous to the kohen's vow. Accordingly, Maharik's source does not prove Shach's broad rule. It proves that some outwardly recognizable act of repentance is needed. There is an additional reason not to generalize. The kohen who married a prohibited woman is not necessarily Torah-disqualified from Temple service or priestly blessing merely because of that marriage. His exclusion is a rabbinic penalty. Chazal imposed it and can define the conditions under which it ends. A wicked witness who is Torah-disqualified under "do not place your hand with the wicked" stands on a different footing. Why should a bare declaration of future compliance automatically restore him? At most the kohen case is an analogy, not a full proof. All the more so where a person is deeply habituated to sin. Hagahot Mordechai in Perek Almanah Nizonet cites a responsum of Maharam of Rothenburg suggesting that even an apostate for idolatry requires clear and complete repentance. Although that particular case involved signs of fraudulent repentance, Maharam's proofs from Perek Zeh Borer and the case of one who stole a measure indicate that mere verbal acceptance is not always enough. Therefore even for an apostate for idolatry, the matter depends upon beit din's assessment that the repentance is genuine and outwardly evident. The practical rule should remain narrow. Where the change itself is unmistakable and objectively demonstrates return, such as abandoning the apostate life, immediate rehabilitation can be recognized. For other habitual sinners, and even for an apostate where the circumstances are doubtful, mere verbal acceptance is insufficient without clear acts of repentance. The matter is entrusted to the judgment of beit din.
