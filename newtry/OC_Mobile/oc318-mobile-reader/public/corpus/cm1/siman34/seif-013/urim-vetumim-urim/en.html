The principal rule is as Sema writes below: the prohibition of "do not covet" applies by Torah law even where full payment is given, but since ordinary people understand theft as taking without payment, one who pressures another to sell for full value is not disqualified from testimony by Torah law. They allow their animals to graze in other people's fields, etc. These cases concern grazing in fields. Therefore there is no distinction here between one who tends small livestock and one who tends large livestock. In a field, allowing either to graze is forbidden. At home, however, keeping small livestock may still be prohibited because they commonly escape and enter fields, whereas large livestock are different. So rules the Shulchan Aruch below in siman 409. The reason the Mechaber does not state here that one who keeps small livestock at home is disqualified is that this disqualification applies only in a place where most fields belong to Jews, such as Eretz Yisrael and Babylonia in earlier times, a circumstance not common among us. Below in siman 425 the Mechaber rules that shepherds of small livestock are neither lowered into danger nor rescued from it, implying that shepherds of large livestock are different. One may answer that there the case concerns tending other people's animals, where specifically small livestock creates the stronger concern, whereas here testimony is disqualified only when he lets his own animals graze improperly, and regarding his own animals the Sages did not distinguish between small and large livestock. Alternatively, because the law there touches on danger to life, the Sages were not as stringent unless small livestock was involved. Sema. As for Sema's statement that the prohibition against keeping small livestock at home does not apply today, one should ask why the Rosh in Bava Kamma and the Tur below in siman 409 still cite the prohibition. It must be that although today Jewish ownership of surrounding fields is not common, some doubt remains. Therefore we are stringent regarding the prohibition itself, but for disqualifying testimony we preserve the person's presumption of fitness.
