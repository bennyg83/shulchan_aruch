Without the owner's consent, etc. Sema writes in seif katan 32 that such persons are not Torah-disqualified because they violate "you shall not covet," but people understand that prohibition as applying only where no payment is made. He writes that this is the truth, citing Tosafot. This contradicts Mechaber below in siman 359, seif 10, and Sema's own words there in seif katan 14. There he correctly says that even one who pressures another into selling for full value violates the Torah prohibition of "you shall not covet," but because people do not understand the prohibition that way, the offender is not thereby Torah-disqualified. He even objects to Ir Shushan for writing that there is no Torah prohibition. Kesef Mishneh similarly comments on Rambam, Hilchot Edut chapter 10, who speaks of "one who transgresses rabbinic robbery, such as extortionists." Kesef Mishneh says this is because "you shall not covet" is popularly understood to apply only without payment. But Rambam himself writes explicitly in Hilchot Gezelah chapter 1 that one who brings many companions to pressure another into selling his property for money violates "you shall not covet." The correct explanation is therefore as follows. Torah indeed prohibits the conduct. But the prohibition is a separate prohibition of coveting, not the Torah legal category of robbery requiring restitution under "he shall return the stolen object." If the buyer acquired the object, Torah does not necessarily require return merely under the law of theft. Chazal, however, treated this conduct as a form of rabbinic robbery and imposed the laws of restoration upon it. Once they defined it as rabbinic theft, they also disqualified the extortionist as a robber. Thus Rambam's phrase "robbery of their words" is exact. The Torah offense exists, but its legal classification as robbery, with the associated restitution and witness-disqualification consequences, is rabbinic. This resolves Rambam and the law. Sema and Kesef Mishneh's wording, if understood as saying there is no Torah prohibition at all, requires study.
