Who stole the found object of a deaf-mute, an incompetent person, or a minor. This form of theft is prohibited only rabbinically for the sake of peaceful relations, as the Tur and Mechaber write below in siman 270. This applies specifically to a found object, where no competent owner intentionally transfers it to them, as explained there. Rambam and the Mechaber omitted this point here, relying on what they wrote in the laws of theft and lost property. It may also be that they relied on the following rule disqualifying those who train pigeons to bring them other pigeons, which likewise involves only rabbinic theft for the sake of peaceful relations, as explained below in seif 16.
Without the owners' consent. This is so even though they pay money. The meaning is that all who pay are not thereby transgressing the prohibition of coveting, because 'you shall not covet' implies taking without payment, as Tosafot indeed write. Rather, they do not seize by force but pressure the owners until they give it to them, even without adequate payment; see the Perishah.
Whether shepherds of small livestock, etc. Below, siman 409, the Tur and Mechaber distinguish one who raises animals at home: small livestock is prohibited because such animals commonly jump and leave the house for the fields and cannot be guarded, unlike large livestock. Here the novelty is that when the animals are actually pastured in the fields there is no distinction between large and small livestock. The Tur and Mechaber did not state that one who raises small livestock even at home is disqualified, because that disqualification applies only to one who raises them in Eretz Yisrael, or in Bavel when most of the fields belonged to Jews, unlike our times, as the Tur explains in siman 409. It is therefore treated as a law not currently practiced. The Beit Yosef writes likewise. See also the end of siman 425, where the Tur and Mechaber state that shepherds of small livestock are neither actively brought up nor cast down, and they do not mention large livestock. It appears that there they speak of one who shepherds others' animals, whereas here the animals are his own and he is suspected that for his own benefit he will allow them to graze in Jewish-owned fields. According to this one might say that the phrase 'his own' applies only to large livestock, but this does not fit the unqualified language of the Gemara and poskim. One must therefore say that for disqualification from testimony and oaths, even regarding small livestock they required that they be his own. Alternatively, concerning the extreme law that one does not save him from death, they were not as stringent except with shepherds of small livestock.
