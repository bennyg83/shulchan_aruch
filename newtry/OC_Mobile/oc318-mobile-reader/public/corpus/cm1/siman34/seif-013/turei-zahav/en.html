(See there in the Tur: the Rambam wrote that if two testified concerning one person that he is disqualified, etc.) The Bach asks why this differs from two pairs of witnesses who contradict one another. I suggest that with two pairs, when each pair later appears separately and testifies, the state of uncertainty differs from the original moment of contradiction, when the two pairs stood against one another together. By contrast, the individual concerning whom conflicting testimony was given has undergone no change from the time the testimony about him was received. This distinction, however, is not fully clear. Alternatively, with two pairs of witnesses, the testimony of one pair against the other is not initially accepted, since it is two against two, except in a case of hazamah, which is a special Torah novelty. But where two testify that a single person is disqualified, their testimony is certainly accepted. If two later testify that he is valid, he nevertheless remains in doubt. This is from Mahari. The gloss adds that the cases are fundamentally different. With two pairs, one pair is certainly valid. Since we do not know which, we preserve both in their prior presumption of validity. With one individual, there is no comparable certainty. Although in the law of a provisional guilt-offering the logic appears reversed, since all agree that uncertainty between two pieces creates liability whereas they dispute a single piece, the cases differ. There, the forbidden piece never possessed a presumption of permissibility, and a definite prohibition existed. Here, both pairs originally possessed a presumption of validity, and even after the doubt arose one pair certainly remains valid. Thus there are two favorable factors: both were valid before the doubt, and one remains certainly valid afterward. For a single individual there is only the original presumption. Since two witnesses stand against two witnesses, at minimum there is a rabbinic doubt, and he is therefore disqualified.
