Seif 7. In the gloss: “One who shares with the thief is not disqualified.” Note: I saw in Netivot HaMishpat that he cites Urim VeTumim’s question: why should such a person not be disqualified? Is he not comparable to one who denies a deposit? Netivot answers that because he did not himself remove the object from the owner’s domain, he is not disqualified, and derives from this a new rule that if he makes a physical change in the stolen object he would be disqualified. In my view this is not correct. Even without making a change, we rule below in siman 358 that one may not purchase property from a thief, especially where the owners may not have despaired and the purchaser is effectively benefiting from actual stolen property. Why should the fact that he did not personally remove it from the owner’s domain save him? He knowingly performed a prohibited act by acquiring from the thief. Even if that prohibition is only rabbinic, he should at least be rabbinically disqualified. Rather, the reason must be that ordinary people do not perceive the act as a serious transgression in this context, comparable to those who bury the dead on the first day of Yom Tov under circumstances where they do not realize the severity of the prohibition. People recognize the principal thief as the sinner but do not necessarily regard one who later buys or receives the property as committing an act that disqualifies him from testimony. If so, making a change in the object does not alter that perception. The person still did not steal it himself, and the thief already had power over the object to change it. Therefore he is not disqualified merely by making such a change. This appears to me the correct explanation.
