Those who fly pigeons in an inhabited area, etc. The Gemara gives two explanations. One is gambling: a person says, "If your pigeon reaches first, I will give you such-and-such." The second is sending one's pigeon to lure pigeons from others' dovecotes. The Mechaber follows Rambam's wording and treats luring other pigeons as the specific offense of pigeon-flying. The racing wager falls under gambling. Luring pigeons is not full Torah theft, because pigeons come from the world at large and multiply in dovecotes without the owner having acquired each bird fully. Nevertheless the Sages prohibited it for the sake of social peace, like taking found objects of a minor, deaf-mute, or mentally incompetent person. Rambam and the Mechaber specify an inhabited area to exclude the wilderness, where wild pigeons are ownerless. Racing pigeons as gambling disqualifies only when the person has no other occupation and presumably lives from the gambling proceeds. Luring others' pigeons, however, disqualifies even if he has other income, because the act itself places him within the category of a robber, albeit rabbinically. This also explains Rambam's continuation concerning racing other animals.
He is presumed to eat from gambling, which is a trace of theft. I proved in Perishah that Rambam does not hold that gambling is full theft because the stake is an asmachta and therefore never legally acquired. Rather, even if the money is acquired, the opponent does not transfer it with full willing intent but only through play and sport, so it is treated as a trace of theft. Yet because the theft aspect is only rabbinic, this alone does not disqualify him unless he also derives bodily benefit from it. Therefore, he must have no other occupation, so that he presumably eats from these gambling proceeds. If he has another occupation but it does not provide enough for his support and expenses, and he also lives from gambling, he is disqualified. Conversely, even if he has no occupation at all, if he possesses other wealth and does not support himself from gambling, he is not disqualified. According to Rashi and the Tur, however, the reason gamblers are disqualified is that one who has no occupation does not appreciate how hard people work for their money and therefore may treat false testimony lightly. Under that approach the result is the reverse: any other occupation, even small, prevents disqualification, while one who has no occupation is disqualified even if he has independent wealth. The Mechaber follows Rambam. It is surprising that Rama did not mention the other view.
