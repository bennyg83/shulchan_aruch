He is presumed to live from gambling, etc. Sema's explanation is somewhat difficult in its references to bodily benefit and growing up from prohibited money. The matter can be stated more clearly. The Gemara gives two reasons why a gambler is disqualified. Rami bar Chama says gambling involves asmachta, so the winner does not acquire the money validly and it is theft. Rav Sheshet says the reason is that the gambler is not engaged in productive worldly activity. Rambam, in Hilchot Gezelah chapter 6, rules that gambling is rabbinic theft. Maggid Mishneh therefore asks: if gambling is already rabbinic theft, why does Rambam in Hilchot Edut need the additional reason that the gambler has no occupation and is not engaged in productive activity? A rabbinic robber should already be disqualified. The answer follows Rama's rule above in seif 3 that for a rabbinic disqualification we require a rasha of monetary greed. An ordinary gambler is not necessarily driven by greed. Experience shows that gamblers often lose more than they win, impoverish themselves, and gamble primarily for entertainment and passing time. Therefore, if the game is only rabbinically considered theft, an occasional gambler who has an independent livelihood is not necessarily a rasha of monetary rapacity and is not disqualified. But if he has no occupation or livelihood other than gambling, then he is presumed to eat from gambling proceeds. His livelihood depends upon taking that money, and he is therefore a rasha of monetary greed. The rabbinic theft then disqualifies him. This is Rav Sheshet's point. Rami bar Chama, by contrast, holds asmachta does not acquire at all, making the winnings Torah theft. For Torah robbery, no additional proof of monetary greed is needed. Any robber is disqualified. Therefore even a person with another occupation is disqualified according to Rami bar Chama. Rambam rules like Rav Sheshet in the practical outcome: gambling money is rabbinic theft, and the gambler is disqualified only when he has no other occupation and is therefore presumed to live from it. This is the clear explanation, and I believe it is also what Sema intended, though his wording is compressed. Sema further writes that according to Rambam, even if the gambler has some income, he is disqualified if it is insufficient for his livelihood and he must supplement it from gambling. He is then still eating from gambling and is a rasha of monetary greed. But if he has enough capital to support himself, even if he is not actively engaged in business, he is valid according to Rambam because his subsistence does not depend on gambling. Rashi and Tur understand Rav Sheshet differently. For them, the disqualification is not primarily because gambling is theft, but because a person who has no productive occupation does not develop fear of sin and proper social responsibility. Sema quotes Tur as saying that money becomes light in his eyes. If that were the entire reason, perhaps he should remain valid for non-monetary testimony. Rashi's formulation is better: such a person lacks fear of sin generally. According to Rashi and Tur, one who has money to live on but does no productive work can still be disqualified, because the defect is lack of engagement in ordinary social life. Conversely, one who has even a modest occupation and understands ordinary commerce may be valid, even if the occupation does not supply all his needs. Another difference is gambling only with a non-Jew. According to Rambam, if the entire problem is rabbinic theft and there is no prohibited taking from a fellow Jew, such gambling would not create the same disqualification through this route. According to Rashi and Tur, he can still be disqualified because, regardless of whom he gambles with, he is not engaged in productive worldly activity. Rama did not repeat all these distinctions here because he relies on what he writes below in siman 370, seif 3, where he says the widespread custom follows the view that the primary problem is not theft but failure to engage in productive activity. From that later ruling, the practical differences follow automatically, including that one who has no occupation can be disqualified even if he gambles only with non-Jews.
