And it goes without saying if the offense carries a court-imposed death penalty, etc. It appears that any prohibition given as a warning for a capital offense disqualifies a person from testimony, even though there are no lashes for violating that prohibition. The absence of lashes reflects the severity of the offense, not its leniency. The same applies to a prohibition remedied by a positive commandment. It is not exempt from lashes because the offense is light, but because Torah requires correction of the violation. Indeed, if he nullifies the positive remedy or fails to fulfill it, he can incur lashes according to the relevant Tannaitic views. Such an offense therefore disqualifies from testimony because of its seriousness. By contrast, a prohibition lacking an act is not punished by lashes because Torah treats it more leniently where no prohibited act was performed. According to Rambam, such an offense does not create Torah disqualification from testimony. This resolves the difficulty raised by Kenesset HaGedolah and Mishneh LaMelech concerning witnesses who sign an interest-bearing document. Although the obligation may be subject to restitution, the offense is not thereby light; the requirement to restore does not prevent disqualification. If lashes are absent only because the warning was uncertain, the authority cited by Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 42 implies that according to Rambam the offender is likewise not disqualified. This must refer to a prohibition not explicit in Torah, because regarding an explicit prohibition Rambam follows the view that uncertain warning can still be valid in the relevant cases. Still, I remain uncertain. If a person was not warned at all, he can nevertheless become disqualified. Why then should uncertain warning be different merely because lashes are technically impossible? It is strained to say that in ordinary absence of warning the offense is intrinsically capable of lashes, whereas with uncertain warning it never can be. This requires study.
