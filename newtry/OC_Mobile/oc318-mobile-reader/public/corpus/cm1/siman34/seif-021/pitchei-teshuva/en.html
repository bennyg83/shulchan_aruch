The borrower. See Baer Heitev, who writes that this implies that the guarantor and the witnesses remain qualified. In Shach at the beginning of siman 52, the reason is explained in the name of Tosafot, the Rosh, and other authorities: ordinary people understand the verse "You shall not place interest upon him" as directed only to the lender and borrower; see there. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 5b speak only of lenders; see also Rabbi Akiva Eiger's Tosafot on the Mishna, chapter Zeh Borer. See Chavot Yair there, at the end of siman 104, who writes that this applies only when the witnesses are not before us. If they are present, we certainly ask them, and if they do not answer that they acted inadvertently we do not advance that claim for them, as stated above concerning those who buried the dead. Nevertheless, he writes that further study is required whether, if they themselves make this claim and they are learned men, we accept their assertion that they did not know an explicit law. See Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh 4:6, who cites Knesset HaGedolah in the name of Shiltei HaGibborim that if the witnesses who sign know there is a prohibition involved, they are disqualified according to everyone. That is obvious. The question, however, is whether the witnesses are believed to say, "We knew there was a prohibition and nevertheless signed." Perhaps they are not believed because a person cannot render himself wicked, or perhaps this differs. Where the person himself creates the prohibited act he is not believed, but here the prohibited act is already before our eyes; we had merely been inclined to assume that he did not know this act was forbidden. Perhaps he is therefore believed to say that he knowingly committed it. Mishneh LaMelech initially suggests a proof from Nimukei Yosef in chapter Zeh Borer concerning the gravediggers, but distinguishes between a conclusion forced by circumstantial evidence rather than by the witnesses' own mouths, and a case where the witnesses themselves testify about themselves that they intentionally violated the prohibition. In the latter case they may not be believed because a person cannot render himself wicked. See Masa'at Binyamin, siman 21. Eretz DeRabbanan, at the end of entry 17, cites this and writes that he does not understand the doubt. Where witnesses tell a person, "You ate forbidden fat," the person did not create the prohibition, and the witnesses could have assumed he ate inadvertently, yet he may say, "I did it intentionally." Tosafot in Bava Metzia 3b nevertheless ask that a person cannot render himself wicked. This shows that Tosafot hold that the rule applies even where he did not create the prohibition. Birkei Yosef, note 32, likewise writes that Rabbi Yosef Bechar David in Divrei Emet proved from the Rashba and Ran in chapter Kol HaNishba'in, regarding one suspected of an oath violation, that the rule applies even where he did not create the prohibition. All this is contrary to the above Chavot Yair. Above, seif 4, seif katan 9, I also cited Rabbi Akiva Eiger as disagreeing with Chavot Yair, and he cited Maharit, Even HaEzer, siman 43, to the same effect. See further Mishneh LaMelech there for several laws concerning witnesses to interest transactions, and what I write below at siman 52, seif 1. In Rabbi Akiva Eiger's gloss to Shulchan Aruch he notes that according to this, with Torah-level interest the borrower is disqualified only rabbinically. See Tumim.
