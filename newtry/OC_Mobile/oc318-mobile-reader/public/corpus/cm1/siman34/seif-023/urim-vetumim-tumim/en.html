Before he was publicly declared disqualified, the testimony is valid. See Urim, where I cited Sema in the name of Nimukei Yosef that for the very testimony through which a person becomes disqualified, prior declaration is unnecessary. Shach in seif katan 22 objects. Nimukei Yosef says this only during the Gemara's initial question concerning one who said, "He lent to me with interest." The Gemara asks that the witness himself is an interest borrower and therefore disqualified. Nimukei Yosef asks: but this is only rabbinic disqualification and normally requires prior declaration. He answers that for the very testimony through which the disqualification arises, no declaration is needed. The Gemara ultimately answers by dividing the statement: a person does not render himself wicked, so we accept the portion concerning the lender and disregard the self-incriminating portion. Shach therefore asks why Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Sema cite Nimukei Yosef's rule at all. According to the conclusion, the case from which it arose disappears. I do not understand Shach. If he means that the rule has no practical case, there are many. Suppose witnesses signed a document for payment on the second day of Yom Tov and took wages for doing so. According to everyone they are at least rabbinically disqualified, and if they acted for money they can even be classed as monetary wrongdoers. Without Nimukei Yosef's rule, the document itself might remain valid because their rabbinic disqualification had never been publicly announced. According to Nimukei Yosef, no announcement is required for the very testimony through which they became disqualified, so that document is invalid. Many similar cases can be formulated. If Shach means that the Gemara's conclusion rejects the legal principle itself, that is even more difficult. Once the principle was necessary to understand the Gemara's question, why assume it was later retracted? The final answer resolves a different issue, namely self-incrimination. It does not say the declaration rule was wrong. Had Nimukei Yosef stood alone, one might still hesitate. But Tosafot also require this rule. In Bava Metzia 5b, Tosafot ask why a bailee suspected of setting his eyes on the deposit can be made to swear it is not in his possession. If he covets it, he is at least an extortionist, who is rabbinically disqualified. But a rabbinic disqualification ordinarily requires declaration. Why should he be disqualified before declaration? The question makes sense only if, for the very testimony or oath through which the misconduct appears, declaration is unnecessary. Likewise in Bava Metzia, Perek Eizehu Neshekh 72a, Tosafot ask why witnesses on an interest-bearing document are not disqualified. They answer that the case involves rabbinic interest and that the witnesses do not profit. This implies that if they did profit, for example by taking payment to participate, the document would be invalid. Again, there had been no prior declaration. The only explanation is that for the very testimony through which they become disqualified, no declaration is required. This is precisely Nimukei Yosef's rule. Another practical case arises where a witness testifies that he gambled with someone and two other witnesses confirm that fact. The rule "a person does not make himself wicked" does not apply once two external witnesses establish the gambling, as Tosafot explain in Sanhedrin 9b and Makkot 6a. If the gambling renders him rabbinically disqualified, then ordinarily his lack of prior declaration would preserve the testimony in which he participated. But under Nimukei Yosef's rule, no declaration is needed for that very transaction, and the entire group can fail under the rule that if one witness is found disqualified the testimony is void. Thus the rule has many practical applications. However, although Nimukei Yosef and Tosafot support it, it is not unanimous. Rashba in his novellae to Bava Kamma, at the end of Perek HaKones, asks the same question concerning the oath that the deposit is not in the bailee's possession. If one suspects he coveted it, he should be a rabbinically disqualified extortionist. Rashba answers that he had not been publicly declared disqualified. This is explicit that even for the very matter through which the misconduct appears, prior declaration is required. Likewise, regarding the Tosafot question about an interest-bearing document, Rivash in responsum 502 cites some commentators who answer that the case is rabbinic interest and the witnesses had not been publicly declared disqualified. Again, they require declaration even for that same document. We therefore have a genuine dispute among major authorities. Nimukei Yosef and Tosafot hold that no declaration is needed for the testimony through which the rabbinic disqualification arises. Rashba and the commentators cited by Rivash require declaration even there. Accordingly, in monetary law the possessor can say kim li in accordance with the authorities who require declaration, as I explain in Kuntres HaTefisot. There remains a difficulty for the latter view from the Gemara's question, "Let him be disqualified because he is a shepherd," where the shepherd had not been declared disqualified regarding that particular matter. According to Nimukei Yosef one can answer that the person knew of his own shepherd status and entrusted him with the flock, so the disqualification arose in relation to that very matter without declaration. According to Rashba's view, the question remains. Perhaps, as Kenesset HaGedolah writes below in siman 92, declaration is needed only to invalidate testimony because otherwise unsuspecting parties suffer loss. To disqualify a person from taking an oath, no declaration is required, because the same third-party-loss concern does not apply. That can answer the Gemara. In any event, the practical law is not absolute. For testimony, the requirement of declaration in the very matter of disqualification is disputed, and possession can follow the stricter requirement. For oaths, there is stronger ground to say declaration is unnecessary. This requires careful application.
