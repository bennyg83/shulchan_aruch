Tumim asks that the case can be found where the witnesses signed a document on the second festival day, or signed a document containing interest, and so forth. See there. Tumim appears to understand "that very testimony" to mean that through the same testimony now being given, the witness reveals that he became disqualified by the underlying act, as in the case where he says, "He lent me money with interest," so that by his testimony concerning that event he shows that he himself became disqualified, or where he admits that he signed a document on the second festival day. But there is no reason to distinguish such cases, because they too can cause loss to purchasers. For example, a document signed on the second festival day is ordinarily treated as valid, just as a document dated on Shabbat may be valid in the circumstances discussed by the authorities. The creditor may sell the debt instrument in reliance upon its apparent validity, and witnesses may later establish that it was actually signed on the second festival day, thereby disqualifying it and causing loss. Shach therefore explains that "that very testimony" means testimony through which the witness disqualifies himself in the act of giving testimony itself, such as one who says, "He lent me with interest." There, with respect to the very oral testimony through which he disqualifies himself, there is no concern of loss to third parties, and therefore no prior public declaration is required. Shach's question is then well founded. According to Rashba's distinction, however, between one who says, "I committed adultery with your wife," where we do not divide his statement, and one who says, "Your wife committed adultery with me," where we do divide his statement and treat him as though he merely said, "Your wife committed adultery," one can still find a case under this rule. It applies where he says, "I borrowed from him with interest," for according to Rashba we do not divide that statement. It can also apply where a witness took payment to testify, although there no public declaration is required in any event, as stated in seif 18.
We maintain the person in his prior presumptively valid status. Ketzot HaChoshen asks why this differs from a mikveh that was measured and found deficient, where items immersed in it are treated as impure retroactively because there are two adverse presumptions: the impure item retains its prior status, and the mikveh is presently deficient. Here too there is a presumption favoring possession of the money, together with the fact that the witness is now found disqualified. In my humble opinion there is no difficulty. In the case of the mikveh, both presumptions are evidentiary presumptions that clarify the factual state, and together they support the conclusion that the mikveh had already become deficient earlier. A presumption of monetary possession, by contrast, is not an evidentiary presumption that clarifies facts. It is merely the rule that money cannot be removed from the possessor without proof, based upon logic or upon Scripture, as explained at the beginning of Bava Kamma, chapter Shor Shenagach. See also the discussion on seizure at the end of Ketzot HaChoshen, where he expressly writes this. These two types of presumptions therefore do not combine to establish retroactive disqualification.
