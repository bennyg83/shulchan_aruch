See Tumim, seif katan 1, who writes that a prohibition given as a warning for a court-imposed death penalty, for which the rule is that lashes are not administered, nevertheless disqualifies the violator. The absence of lashes there reflects the greater severity of the prohibition, not its leniency. The same applies to a prohibition corrected by a positive commandment. By contrast, a prohibition violated without an action, for which there are no lashes because of the relative leniency of the matter, does not disqualify by Torah law. See there and Netivot HaMishpat. Birkei Yosef, letter 9, also discusses whether one who violates a prohibition without an action might nevertheless be disqualified by Torah law even according to the Rambam. Maharit, Even HaEzer, siman 23, and Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot Beit Yosef, letter 15, incline that way, and a manuscript commentary of Rabbi Uri similarly infers from Darkei Moshe that violation of a prohibition without an action causes Torah-level disqualification. Birkei Yosef concludes, however, that the primary view is that according to the Rambam he is disqualified only rabbinically. The Rif expressly writes in a responsum that one is not disqualified for a prohibition without an action, for which there are no lashes, except in the case of an oath. Presumably the Rambam agrees. Maharit likewise appears to conclude this way, as do Knesset HaGedolah in a responsum printed in Edut BeYaakov, siman 17, the author of Beit Yosef there, siman 18, and Responsa Chut HaShani, siman 18. See Responsa Beit Shmuel Acharon, siman 17, regarding the rule that a positive commandment does not override a combination of a negative and positive commandment, and whether one who nevertheless performs the positive commandment is disqualified by Torah law or only rabbinically. He writes that this depends on a dispute. Tosafot in Chullin 141, citing Ri, hold that although the positive commandment does not override both, there are nevertheless no lashes because the negative prohibition itself is displaced by the positive commandment and only the positive prohibition remains. According to this, the person is disqualified only rabbinically. Mishneh LaMelech, by contrast, is understood there as holding that where an additional positive commandment strengthens the negative prohibition, even the negative prohibition is not displaced and lashes are incurred, which would produce Torah-level disqualification. Beit Shmuel Acharon argues at length that Rashi agrees with Ri and therefore concludes that the witness is disqualified only rabbinically. His attribution to Mishneh LaMelech, however, is difficult, for Mishneh LaMelech in Hilchot Shofar chapter 1 merely leaves the matter in doubt. More directly, one could cite the Rambam, Hilchot Nedarim 3:6, who rules that if one makes a vow forbidding himself the matzah of Pesach night, sitting in the sukkah on Sukkot, and the like, and then eats, sits, or performs the act, he receives lashes. Sefer HaChinuch explains that a positive commandment does not override the vow because a vow carries both a negative and a positive commandment, “He shall not profane his word” and “According to all that proceeds from his mouth he shall do.” This indicates that the Rambam does not follow Ri's view. See Shaar HaMelech there, Noda BiYehudah, Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 430, and Responsa Shlomo, Orach Chaim, siman 15, who discuss Ri's position at length. As to the practical law of testimony, it appears to me that in such a case he may perhaps remain valid even rabbinically, because he believed that he was performing a mitzvah, analogous to the rule below in seif 4, though this requires study. See also Beit Shmuel Acharon, siman 20, who discusses the Tumim concerning a prohibition serving as warning for capital punishment and a prohibition corrected by a positive commandment. Netivot HaMishpat writes that one who merely fails to perform a positive commandment does not become disqualified as a witness unless he actively violates the law or completely casts off the yoke of Torah. Chut HaShani, siman 18, rules similarly, as cited by Birkei Yosef, letter 10: nowhere do we find that one who fails to make a sukkah or take a lulav is thereby disqualified, even rabbinically.
