But concerning an oath about the future, etc. Rivash decides that if one swore not to eat and then ate, he is disqualified because he performed an act. If he swore that he would eat and then failed to do so, he performed no act and is not disqualified on that basis. Mishneh LaMelech rules like this distinction. In Tumim I conclude that today he is nevertheless disqualified rabbinically. I further explain that the rule exempting one who violates a prohibition without an act because there are no lashes applies to ordinary prohibitions. Where the transgression involves monetary desire, he can still be disqualified even without a lashes-bearing prohibition.

Or, “I was under duress.” If he claims that he was under duress already at the time of the oath, he is not believed to excuse himself afterward. But if he swore to pay by a fixed time, the time passed, and he did not pay, Shach says he is believed to say that he had no money. See Tumim, where I leave this rule doubtful. If Reuven swore that he had never waived Shimon's opening a window into his courtyard, and witnesses testify that Reuven physically assisted in opening it, then as a matter of property law we may infer waiver from his assistance. Nevertheless, he is not thereby rendered suspected regarding his oath, because it remains possible that he assisted but did not intend to waive. Sema, citing Rashba.
