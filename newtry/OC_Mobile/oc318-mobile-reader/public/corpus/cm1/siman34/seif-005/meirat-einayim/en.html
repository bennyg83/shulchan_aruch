Both a vain oath and a false oath. See below, siman 92, for their definitions.
And an utterance. This follows the verse's language in Vayikra: 'Or a soul who swears, expressing with the lips, to do harm or to do good.' 'To do harm' means, for example, swearing not to eat or not to sleep; 'to do good' means swearing to eat or to sleep.
Whom the community placed under a ban. One who violates bans arising from communal enactments is not thereby disqualified from testimony, for if so hardly one person in a thousand would remain qualified. So writes Chiddushei Agudah in chapter Zeh Borer. The same applies to one who refuses to enter into a communal enactment, as Maharik, shoresh 126, cited in Darkei Moshe, writes.
Specifically an oath concerning the past. For example, he swore that he had already eaten, when in fact he had not.
But an oath concerning the future, etc. The reason is that at the time he swore he intended to fulfill his oath, but later his inclination overpowered him and he violated it. Since the act was not independently prohibited but became forbidden only through his own oath, this does not establish that he would testify falsely, which is itself intrinsically sinful and grievous. By contrast, with an oath concerning the past the oath itself left his mouth falsely. The Tur at the beginning of siman 92 writes that Rabbeinu Tam too holds that one is disqualified even for violating a future oath, while Rivash rules that with a future oath he is disqualified only when he violates it through an active deed, such as swearing not to eat and then eating, but not by passive failure, such as swearing to eat and failing to do so. This differs from Ir Shushan, which combines and abbreviates the views where expansion was necessary.
He is not believed to say, 'I acted inadvertently.' Rashba explains the reason: otherwise no one would ever be established as suspected, even regarding an oath of testimony, for he could always claim, 'I forgot,' etc.
Or, 'I was coerced,' etc. The laws of coerced oaths for which one may swear are explained in Yoreh Deah, simanim 228 and 232. Likewise, if one assisted his fellow in opening windows or an opening into his courtyard, even though for purposes of chazakah this is considered immediate waiver, as explained below in siman 154, seif 12, and he later swore that he had not waived it, he is not thereby disqualified. It is possible that he is telling the truth, for perhaps he did not assist with intent to waive but intended to jest and expected the other person later to close it. So writes the Beit Yosef in seif 8 in the name of Responsa Rashba.
