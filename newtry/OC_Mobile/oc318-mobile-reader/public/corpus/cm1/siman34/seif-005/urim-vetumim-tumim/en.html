And some say that he is not disqualified, etc. Sema writes that Rivash distinguishes between one who swore not to eat and then ate, who becomes disqualified, and one who swore to eat and failed to do so, who does not. Shach cites a responsum of Masat Binyamin that the halachah follows this compromise. I am surprised. Rivash's reason is that violating a future oath passively involves no act and therefore carries no lashes. According to Rambam, one who violates a prohibition without lashes is not Torah-disqualified. But according to the second view in seif 2, even such a person is at least rabbinically disqualified. Accordingly, one who swore to eat and did not should still be rabbinically disqualified, even if not Torah-disqualified. The Mishnah in Shevuot does not list this case because it enumerates Torah disqualifications, not rabbinic ones. Perhaps Rivash himself did not accept the view that a non-lashable prohibition creates rabbinic disqualification. But Rama rules that it does. According to Rama's own ruling, the passive violator should at least be rabbinically disqualified. It is strained to combine authorities who validate all future-oath violations with those who say a prohibition without lashes creates no disqualification even rabbinically. Rivash does not appear to rely on such a combination. The practical law therefore requires study. The underlying principle itself also raises difficulty. At the beginning of Bava Metzia, the Gemara asks whether one suspected concerning money is also suspected concerning an oath. It brings the rule that a bailee is made to swear that the deposit is not in his possession, and asks that if he withheld it he would violate "you shall not covet." If a prohibition without an act never disqualifies, what is the question? The object originally came into his hands permissibly, and he merely refuses to return it. One might answer that a deposited object remains legally in the owner's possession, so when the bailee transfers value or otherwise completes the withholding, the oppression is treated as an act. But that does not explain the Gemara's case of one who denies a deposit where evasion is not plausible. The property also came into his hands permissibly and the denial itself is verbal. Likewise, an interest-bearing lender becomes disqualified from the moment the interest obligation is imposed even if the underlying loan predated it and nothing was written. That too can be merely verbal. Ordinary speech is not considered an act, apart from special cases such as oath, substitution of a consecrated animal, and cursing with the Divine Name. It therefore appears that Rambam agrees that a rasha of monetary rapacity is more severe. Where Scripture expressly identifies the conduct as dishonest pursuit of money, even a prohibition without lashes can disqualify because it shows corrupt monetary character. Maharik, shoresh 126, likewise indicates that for greed-based misconduct lashes are not required. Accordingly, monetary dishonesty can disqualify even without a physical act, while an ordinary non-acting prohibition may not. This remains worthy of study.
Or: "I was compelled." Shach, citing Maharshal, explains this as one who was compelled to swear falsely. But if a person swore to pay by a certain date and the date passed without payment, Maharshal says he is not thereby disqualified, because perhaps he had no money then. This is difficult in light of Mechaber below, siman 98, seif 30. If one swore to pay his fellow by a fixed date and the time arrives while he cannot pay, we do not arrange exemptions for him. He must even sell his clothing and possessions and endure deprivation in order to fulfill his oath. If at the deadline we see his movable property and clothing still in his possession, how can we say he was compelled? I explain in siman 98 that many authorities, including principles cited by Rivash, disagree with Mechaber and hold that he need not sell his essential clothing. According to them, Maharshal's case is understandable. But according to Mechaber's ruling there, the present rule is difficult. It is strained to say that although he was legally obligated to sell, he considered the matter permitted and therefore is not disqualified. He knew he had sworn and should have asked a competent halachic authority. Had he done so, he would have been instructed according to Torah. If he refused, the fault is his. The matter therefore requires study. See what I wrote in siman 98.
