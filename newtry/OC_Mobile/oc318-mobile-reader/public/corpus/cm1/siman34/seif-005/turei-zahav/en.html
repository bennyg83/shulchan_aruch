(Seif 16: pigeon racers.) The Tur disputes the Rambam. To clarify their disagreement, I will briefly present the sugya. The Mishnah disqualifies one who gambles with dice. Rami bar Hama explains that the wager is an asmachta and therefore does not effect acquisition, whereas Rav Sheshet says it is not an asmachta; rather, the disqualification is because such people do not engage in the productive settlement of the world. The practical difference is whether the gambler has another occupation. The Mishnah also disqualifies pigeon racers. One Amora explains this as a contest in which one says, “If your pigeon reaches its destination before mine,” while another explains ara, training a pigeon to lure other pigeons, which entails theft. The Gemara asks why each Amora rejects the other explanation. According to the first, ara is prohibited only because of the ways of peace; according to the second, a pigeon race is simply gambling with dice. The Rambam writes that pigeon racers “in a settled area” are disqualified. By limiting the case to a settled area, he evidently follows the view that the issue is ara. Yet in chapter 6 of Hilchot Gezeilah he writes that gambling with a Jew entails theft, while gambling with a non-Jew does not entail theft, though it remains prohibited as idle occupation. The Maggid Mishneh objects that Rami bar Hama’s view, which treated the wager as theft, was rejected. The Kesef Mishneh struggles greatly to reconcile this in chapter 10 of Hilchot Edut. There is also an apparent contradiction in the Tur’s presentation of the Rambam: here he says the gambler is disqualified only if he has no other occupation, while in siman 370 he cites the Rambam as disqualifying him because of theft. In my opinion, the Rambam can be explained consistently. He rules like Rav Sheshet, not Rami bar Hama, and therefore regards gambling only as avak gezeilah, a lesser form of theft, while pigeon luring is rabbinic theft. There are thus three categories: Torah theft, rabbinic theft, and avak gezeilah, namely gambling. Rav Sheshet’s statement that gamblers do not engage in productive life means that although gambling is only avak gezeilah and not full theft, because it is not an asmachta, one who has no other occupation is presumed to live from this tainted income. If he does not support himself from it, avak gezeilah alone is insufficient to disqualify him. This is Rav Sheshet’s essential answer according to the Rambam. By contrast, rabbinic theft such as ara disqualifies even if the person does not live from it. Thus the Gemara’s statement in Shabbat concerning staking a larger coin against a smaller one still establishes at least avak gezeilah according to all views. Tosafot likewise explain that the statement that ara is prohibited only because of the ways of peace applies only according to the opinion that pigeon racing is the Mishnah’s case; according to the opinion that ara is the case, it is full rabbinic theft. Accordingly, when the Rambam discusses the prohibition of rabbinic theft in Hilchot Gezeilah, he does not mention the absence of another occupation, because the act is prohibited regardless. That condition is required only for disqualification from testimony due to avak gezeilah. The Tur understood the Rambam’s discussion there as also addressing evidentiary disqualification, and therefore wrote in siman 370 that the Rambam disqualifies the gambler because of theft even if he has another occupation. On my reading, however, the Rambam there speaks only of prohibition: gambling with a Jew is prohibited even if the gambler has another occupation, but he is disqualified from testimony only if he lacks another source of livelihood. Gambling with a non-Jew entails prohibition but no disqualification according to the Rambam. This yields two leniencies and one stringency in the Rambam. First, one who inherited sufficient funds and gambles is valid, because he does not live from avak gezeilah. Second, one who gambles with non-Jews is not disqualified even if he profits from it, though the activity is prohibited as idleness. The stringency is that even if he has another occupation, he is disqualified if that occupation does not support him fully, because he still lives in part from avak gezeilah. The Tur, however, understands Rav Sheshet’s concern as lack of familiarity with honest commerce and the true value of money. Thus the Tur disqualifies even one who gambles with non-Jews, but validates one who has another occupation even if it does not fully support him, since he understands commercial life and how difficult money is to earn. The Tur also holds that ordinary pigeon racing and dice play contain no theft at all, and the disqualification depends entirely on disengagement from honest worldly occupation. This reconciles the Tur’s statements in siman 370 and here, and also resolves the Kesef Mishneh’s objection to the Rambam. The Sema’s explanation of the two leniencies and one stringency may have intended this entire approach.
