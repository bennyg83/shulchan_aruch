Or compulsion. Shach distinguishes a case where witnesses were present when the oath was violated and the person claims the oath itself was made under compulsion, from a case where they merely know he failed to perform, such as not paying by the stated date, but did not observe his circumstances. In the first case his claim is weak because the witnesses did not see the alleged compulsion and he should have explained it then. In the second case genuine compulsion may have existed elsewhere, and the question is harder. Maharshal allows a plea of forgetfulness in some testimony-oath cases. Rashba and Baal HaTerumot do not contradict this where the testimony was old enough that genuine forgetting is plausible. A person who swears falsely about a loan itself cannot excuse himself by saying he forgot, because when making a substantive factual oath he is obligated to check carefully. But one who truthfully swears that he presently does not remember testimony may indeed have forgotten. Likewise, a person who swore to pay while wealthy and later became poor through an unforeseen calamity can be exempt if the poverty was an unexpected circumstance not contemplated at the time of the oath. In such a case he need not sell the shirt from his back merely because ordinarily one who remains in essentially the same circumstances must exert himself greatly to fulfill the oath.
Carrion or a tereifah. Sema notes that one who merely causes another to violate through 'placing a stumbling block before the blind' may lack lashes. Even so, such conduct can still create rabbinic witness disqualification. The absence of lashes does not automatically preserve fitness where the Sages disqualified the offender.
