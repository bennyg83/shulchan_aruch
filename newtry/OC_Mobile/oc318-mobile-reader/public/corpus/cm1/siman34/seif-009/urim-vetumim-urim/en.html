In Tumim I explain that according to the view in siman 43:8, witnesses may say they erred in the date even regarding a matter in which people do not ordinarily err, and even where their handwriting is independently verified. Therefore they should not be disqualified merely because perhaps they made an error in the dating, even if they had said, “We wrote it on its proper date.”
If there are witnesses, etc. Sema asks why those witnesses are not themselves the discrediting witnesses. He answers that they did not read the document and did not know what date or place it stated. Taz answers that the case can involve a document with a kinyan, where the date may lawfully be written as the date of the kinyan even though the document itself was written later. In Tumim I write that neither answer is necessary: this is ordinary contradiction, not hazamah, because the later testimony does not attack the witnesses' personal presence at the event.

That they testify before beit din that this is their handwriting, etc. See Tumim. Rambam specifically states this case rather than simply one where their signatures are independently authenticated. One might have thought that if the signatures are independently verified and the witnesses now say, “We wrote it on its proper date,” they are not believed to invalidate the document, whereas if authentication depends on them they have a migo to say the signatures are forged. I ultimately reject this explanation. If so, how could we disqualify them retroactively and thereby invalidate all documents they signed from the date the document was seen in the creditor's possession until now, including documents whose signatures are independently authenticated? Perhaps they are deliberately trying to invalidate those other documents, where they have no migo. It is strained to say they are believed to disqualify themselves only as to documents lacking independent authentication; no source states such a rule. I therefore conclude that Rambam follows the view of Baal HaMaor that hazamah does not operate on documentary testimony when the handwriting is independently authenticated. Only where authentication rests on the witnesses themselves do they effectively testify orally to the maneh in the document, making hazamah applicable. See Tumim, where I defend Baal HaMaor with numerous Talmudic supports.

Sema cites Rashba concerning one who hired witnesses to testify for him and says they are not disqualified until they testify. His abbreviated wording is misleading. Rashba's actual point is this: one who hires witnesses and they testify, but it is not established that their testimony was false, does not thereby disqualify them merely because they took payment. The rule that a paid witness's testimony is void does not itself make him a wicked witness. They become disqualified only if they took payment to testify falsely and in fact testified falsely.
