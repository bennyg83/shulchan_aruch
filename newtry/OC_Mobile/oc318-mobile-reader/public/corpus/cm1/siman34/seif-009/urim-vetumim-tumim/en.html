We wrote it and postdated it, etc. Sema implies that this applies to a loan document containing an after-acquired-property clause, where postdating does not invalidate the document. But in a deed of sale, or in a loan document lacking such a clause, the witnesses cannot explain the defect by saying the document was postdated. This appears to depend on the dispute between Baal HaMaor and Ramban. Baal HaMaor, at the beginning of Rosh Hashanah, holds that witnesses may say they erred in the regnal year even where their handwriting is independently verified and even concerning an error witnesses do not ordinarily make. They can say, in effect, "we signed concerning the substantive matter, not concerning that detail." Ramban disagrees and allows such explanation only where the handwriting is not independently established, or where the error is one people commonly make. According to Baal HaMaor, therefore, even where postdating would not be a plausible explanation, the witnesses are not necessarily disqualified. They can say they erred concerning the regnal year or similar date detail. This also explains Baal HaMaor's broader view that formal hazamah does not operate in written documents. Since the witnesses can say the date was not part of the matter upon which they intended to testify, and the document was never orally interrogated in beit din concerning that date, the temporal statement cannot create the ordinary hazamah framework. Rif and Rambam follow Ramban: the witnesses cannot simply say they erred concerning an uncommon date detail. Accordingly, hazamah can operate where the written date is part of the testimony. I discuss Baal HaMaor's position further below, but this framework gives it substantial halachic coherence.
If there are witnesses who saw them sign, etc. Sema explains that these later witnesses are not hazamah witnesses because they merely say they saw the signatories signing and did not pay attention to the date or place written in the document. Taz answers that the case is a document of acquisition: even if the document bears a Nisan date and they saw it signed in Iyar, perhaps the acquisition itself occurred in Nisan and the document properly records the acquisition date. That is strained because Rambam and Mechaber state the rule generally, without limiting it to a document containing a kinyan. I do not understand the original difficulty. Hazamah applies only when the later witnesses testify about the bodies of the first witnesses: "You were with us elsewhere." If a document says it was written in Nisan and later witnesses say it was actually signed in Iyar, that is not hazamah of the signatories themselves. It is ordinary contradiction concerning the document. We have two against two: the document's witnesses say it was written in Nisan, while the later witnesses say it was written in Iyar. Neither set becomes personally disqualified; each set remains capable of testifying separately according to Rav Huna. Therefore the law requires the later witnesses to say to the signatories themselves, "You were with us in Nisan." Once the contradiction addresses the bodies of the witnesses, it becomes Torah hazamah, in which the later witnesses are believed and the original witnesses are disqualified. This is straightforward. For the same reason the Yerushalmi, discussing an antedated document, labors to identify how the falsity becomes known. It cannot simply say later witnesses testify that the document was signed on another date, because that would create only two against two, not definitive disqualification.
They are disqualified retroactively, etc. A difficulty remains: why are they disqualified retroactively from the date written in the document, in Nisan? Perhaps the document was actually postdated or written later, and their later statement in beit din that it was written on time is the only falsehood. If so, they certainly become disqualified from the day they lie in beit din, but why regard them as wicked already from Nisan? I later found this question in Taz in siman 43, though his answer is difficult to understand. Perhaps he means that if the document had really been lawfully postdated and the witnesses were still valid, why would they now falsely insist that it was written on time? Their lie suggests they are covering up an original defect. That is too slight an inference to disqualify them retroactively. Perhaps they lied now for another reason, or even wished to invalidate the document. The better explanation is that a document is presumed to have been written on its stated date. Raavan writes at length that we do not ordinarily assume a document was postdated. We invoke strained explanations such as postdating only to preserve the witnesses' presumption of fitness. But once the witnesses come to beit din, say "we wrote it on time," and are refuted, they are in any event disqualified from that point forward for lying in court. The only remaining question is whether their disqualification begins now or from Nisan. Since their presumption of fitness has already been lost in any event, there is no longer reason to use the strained assumption that the document was postdated. We return to the ordinary presumption that documents are written on their stated date. Therefore we treat them as having acted falsely already in Nisan and disqualify them retroactively. This has practical consequences. For example, if there is a receipt dated the twenty-ninth of Adar, it may defeat the document if the document was actually written later, but not if it was truly written in Nisan. Their false statement about the timing can therefore materially affect rights. Still, the underlying rule creates another difficulty. Suppose witnesses later testify that they saw the document being written in Iyar even though it bears a Nisan date. The original witnesses are then disqualified only from Iyar, because we say the false antedating occurred at that later time and preserve their earlier presumption of fitness. Why not disqualify them still earlier once it is established that by Iyar they had certainly become false witnesses? Mechaber in Yoreh Deah siman 81 rules like Rashba that if an animal is found tereifah and the wound is clearly at least three days old, products made during the preceding period can be prohibited retroactively. Once the prior presumption is shown to have failed at some earlier point and we cannot say the defect arose only now, the chazakah does not necessarily preserve the earlier period. Rashba explains this at length in the first chapter of Chullin from the laws of stains. The same principle should apply here. If the witnesses are certainly disqualified from Iyar and we cannot say their defect arose at the moment of discovery, why should we preserve all prior testimony merely from their former chazakah? At minimum, once that chazakah is impaired, there should be sufficient doubt to prevent extraction of money, especially where the defendant's possession supports him. Peri Chadash likewise compares analogous questions concerning a slaughterer who forgot the laws of shechitah. Perhaps Rif and Rambam do not follow Rashba in that broader retroactive-chazakah rule, but rather Rosh, who in the animal case preserves earlier products despite evidence the tereifah existed for three days. If so, their ruling here is consistent: the witnesses are disqualified only from the first time their false conduct is known. But Mechaber in Yoreh Deah adopts Rashba's position, so his ruling here remains difficult. It is strained to answer that hazamah is a Torah novelty and we extend disqualification only from the point of proven falsehood. That may be true, but the source for limiting all retrospective doubt in this manner is not clear. The matter therefore requires study.
From the day it became known, etc. Ran cites Baal HaMaor, and Baal HaMaor himself writes explicitly, that the law of hazamah does not apply to testimony contained in a document. Bach explains that Baal HaMaor means only that the witnesses do not receive the punishment of hazamah, whether monetary, capital, or otherwise. The witnesses can nevertheless be disqualified and the document invalidated. Shach agrees, even though Ran did not understand Baal HaMaor that way, and Shach argues that otherwise Baal HaMaor would contradict the Gemara at the beginning of Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot. On that reading, Rif might also agree with Baal HaMaor. I do not understand this. Baal HaMaor writes that judges who authenticate a document may be related to the document's witnesses because the reason judges must normally be unrelated to witnesses is that the judges may later need to receive hazamah against them. In a document, he says, there is no hazamah. If Bach and Shach were correct that hazamah still disqualifies the witnesses and voids the document, the judges would still have to receive testimony that their relatives are disqualified from testimony and oaths. Why is that less problematic than receiving testimony that imposes a monetary punishment upon a relative? It is hardly a lesser judicial involvement to brand one's relative a false witness and disqualify him. Thus Ran correctly understood Baal HaMaor: there is no formal hazamah in written testimony at all. Later testimony can contradict and invalidate the document, but it does not make the original signatories zomemim or personally disqualified through hazamah. Bach's proof from hazamah given outside the original witnesses' presence also fails. We rule that such testimony is not hazamah but can function as contradiction. The original witnesses remain personally qualified, because one contradictory set can testify separately from the other. Only the original testimony is neutralized. Tur in siman 38 likewise treats the result as contradiction, not personal disqualification. Accordingly, Baal HaMaor's position is coherent: document witnesses are not subject to hazamah, but the document can be defeated through contradiction. Shach challenges Baal HaMaor from the Gemara at the beginning of Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot. The Gemara asks why a document dated in the first year of the Sabbatical cycle can be valid if civil law requires interrogation and examination. One could say the document was written later, but then, according to Shach's understanding, it would be testimony incapable of hazamah. If Baal HaMaor holds that no document testimony is subject to hazamah anyway, what is the Gemara's question? This question assumes the explanation of Torat Chaim and Raavan that the Gemara's problem is precisely testimony incapable of hazamah. But Baal HaMaor can answer even on their approach. As I explained above, Baal HaMaor holds that witnesses can say they erred in the regnal year or other date detail even where the mistake is not common. They can say, "We signed concerning the substance of the transaction, not concerning that date." That is possible because, under our final halachah, civil cases do not require formal interrogation and examination. The date is not an essential component of the testimony. But at the Gemara's initial stage, if one assumes civil cases do require full interrogation, the date becomes an essential component of the testimony. The witnesses could no longer say, "We did not sign concerning the date," because without the date their testimony would be legally incomplete. They would have been required to scrutinize that detail carefully. Thus Baal HaMaor's rule that a document lacks hazamah belongs to the final civil-law framework, not to the hypothetical stage in which every civil testimony requires the full interrogations. There is, however, a stronger explanation of Baal HaMaor. He asks in Yevamot how a document is ever valid at all, since written testimony ordinarily falls under "from their mouths and not from their writing." He answers that the written document is not treated simply as the witnesses' written oral testimony. Rather, the borrower or obligor authorizes the witnesses to write his obligation, and they function as his agents. The instrument is therefore treated as though the obligor himself wrote the document. If we later come to say the witnesses were zomemim, we necessarily say they were never authorized by the obligor and did not act as his agents. But once their agency falls away, the writing is merely their own written statement and returns to the category of testimony from writing, which is not oral judicial testimony. Thus there is no formal hazamah against them. The later witnesses can invalidate the document by showing that it was not created through the obligor's authorization. But how can the signatories be personally punished or disqualified as zomemim when, once their supposed agency is disproved, their writing is not legally treated as witness testimony at all? This appears to me the clearest basis of Baal HaMaor. On this view, the Gemara at the beginning of Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot actually supports him. Rashi there does not explain the question as Raavan and Torat Chaim do. According to Rashi, the question is not technically that the document is incapable of hazamah. Rather, if civil cases do not require interrogation and examination, we can presume witnesses ordinarily speak truthfully, and where possible we interpret their words to avoid contradiction or hazamah. Therefore we can say the document was written later. But if Torah requires interrogation and examination even in civil law, this shows we do not simply presume the witnesses truthful. The purpose of examination is to clarify whether they are lying. In that case, when another set challenges the date, why should we strain to say the document was postdated rather than suspect falsification? This question applies equally according to Baal HaMaor. Even if formal hazamah never applies to a document, the requirement of interrogation would still show that civil witnesses are not presumed truthful enough to justify strained reconciliation. The Gemara answers that the rule was changed by rabbinic enactment to avoid closing the door before borrowers. After the enactment, we do not insist on full interrogation and we preserve ordinary civil documents through reasonable interpretations. Rashi's explanation is also more compelling from the Gemara's formulation. Why does the Gemara challenge specifically from a document dated in the first year of the Sabbatical cycle rather than from the ordinary Mishnah that postdated loan documents are valid? If the problem were testimony incapable of hazamah, ordinary postdated documents would be an even stronger question. The more common postdating is, the easier it is for witnesses to answer any attempted hazamah by saying, "The document was written later," and therefore the less capable the testimony is of hazamah. But according to Rashi's explanation, the first-year Sabbatical document is a stronger question precisely because such extreme postdating is uncommon. It is harder to preserve the document by saying it was written much later. Therefore, if civil law truly requires searching examination, why assume this unlikely explanation rather than falsity? This strongly favors Rashi over the Raavan-Torat Chaim interpretation and removes Shach's objection to Baal HaMaor. Still, one might ask why the Gemara did not challenge the initial assumption from ordinary postdated documents on Raavan's technical ground, even if that was not its main question. If civil cases require testimony capable of hazamah, every postdated document appears problematic. The answer may itself be Baal HaMaor's principle: written documents do not belong to the ordinary hazamah framework at all. Therefore the Gemara could not ask from formal incapacity for hazamah and instead had to ask Rashi's broader question about why we favor a strained interpretation if Torah otherwise requires close scrutiny. This gives Baal HaMaor substantial support. Rif and Rambam can still disagree. Even according to Rashi's reading of the Gemara, they may hold that written testimony can enter the hazamah framework in appropriate circumstances, especially where the witnesses themselves authenticate and orally adopt the document. There is another way to understand the Gemara according to Rif and Rambam. The Torah may indeed require interrogation and examination in civil law, but Chazal removed that requirement to avoid closing the door before borrowers. Once they abolished the ordinary interrogation requirement, it would be inconsistent to preserve one fragment of it by invalidating every document whose date cannot be made subject to hazamah. That would effectively restore interrogation through the back door. Thus, after the enactment, civil documents are accepted even where their dates are not independently capable of the full capital-law hazamah structure. The issue is not whether written testimony can ever be hazamah testimony, but whether all civil documents must carry the complete seven-interrogation framework. Baal HaMaor has further strong support. Ketubot 18b initially entertains that even where the witnesses' handwriting is independently verified, they may be believed to say, "We signed under threat to our lives." If so, every document would appear incapable of hazamah: whenever later witnesses attempted to refute the signatories, the signatories could answer that they had been compelled under threat. How then could any Torah document ever satisfy a requirement of testimony capable of hazamah? One could answer that the initial suggestion there is later rejected. But the very fact the Gemara entertained it shows that the document was not automatically considered void merely because such a defense might be available. More striking is the end of Yevamot, where R. Tarfon and R. Akiva dispute whether testimony permitting a woman to remarry requires interrogation and examination. Many authorities, including Masat Binyamin and Maharshal, hold that testimony of kiddushin prohibiting a woman to the world does require interrogation and examination, and several sources cited by Beit Yosef in siman 30 take a strict view of personal-status testimony. Yet the Gemara in Yevamot asks why Chazal did not institute a date in a kiddushin document and gives other reasons. The general system assumes kiddushin documents were often written without a date. If personal-status testimony requires interrogation and a fixed time so it can be subjected to hazamah, how can an undated kiddushin document be valid? Even if one does not follow Masat Binyamin and Maharshal regarding kiddushin witnesses, the Gemara's broad question, "Why did they not institute a date in kiddushin?" presumes there was no ancient universal requirement of a date. It is hard to say R. Tarfon and R. Akiva would disagree about whether every kiddushin instrument itself must contain a date, yet the Gemara presents the lack of date as an established fact. The same problem exists with gittin. If by Torah law every testimony in personal-status matters requires interrogation, an undated get should lack the temporal information needed for hazamah. Yet our law is that an undated get, although rabbinically defective, can be valid after the fact. One might say R. Akiva's enactment for the welfare of deserted women suspended the interrogation requirement in personal-status testimony. But in gittin Chazal themselves required a date because of concerns such as protecting a relative from punishment and the right to fruits. Once they required a date, why would the Torah-level requirement not revive? And if the get lacked the date, why would it remain valid after the fact? It is difficult to say Chazal uprooted Torah kiddushin retroactively in every undated-get case through the principle that anyone who betroths does so subject to rabbinic authority. If so, a husband who wished to protect his wife from consequences could simply give an undated get now and claim he delivered it earlier, with Chazal's retroactive uprooting doing the rest. Tosafot discuss why Chazal did not fear such forms of evasion, and the whole structure becomes strained. One could distinguish according to R. Elazar, who holds the witnesses of delivery create the divorce and the signatures are only evidentiary. The delivery witnesses know the time even if the get does not record it. But this is difficult according to Rif's position that signed witnesses can also create the operative testimony even according to R. Elazar. Baal HaMaor's view resolves these problems neatly. A legal document is not ordinary oral testimony transcribed onto paper. It has its own Torah status as the obligor's written instrument created through the witnesses' agency. Formal hazamah and its interrogation requirements therefore do not apply to the paper in the ordinary way. Where oral witnesses later testify to establish or authenticate the instrument, their oral testimony can of course require the relevant procedural rules. This distinction may also illuminate Rambam's language in the present law. Rambam writes that the witnesses are not disqualified until they come to beit din, identify the handwriting as theirs, and say, "We wrote it on its date." Why does Rambam need to say they testify in beit din that this is their handwriting? If their handwriting is already independently known, one could seemingly ask them only whether the document was written on time. Nor is it enough to say their authentication is needed because otherwise they would not be believed to invalidate the document. The law under discussion concerns their personal disqualification, not merely validity of the document. The precision suggests that Rambam too recognizes a difference between the written instrument itself and oral adoption of it. When their handwriting is not independently verified and the witnesses themselves come to authenticate it, our law is that they testify concerning the maneh in the document, not merely concerning handwriting. Rambam explains below that they must remember the underlying oral event: the loan occurred on that day and in that place. At that point their testimony becomes oral testimony before beit din. It can enter the ordinary framework of hazamah. If they then say, "We wrote it on its date," and are refuted, they can become personally disqualified. Once their chazakah of fitness is broken, the falsehood can be traced back to the date of the document in the manner discussed above. But if their handwriting is independently established, the document stands without any present oral testimony from them. Their written act itself may not be subject to formal hazamah, much like Baal HaMaor's theory. This reading also resolves the problem of undated gittin and kiddushin documents. Where the instrument is independently established, its written status does not require the full oral hazamah framework. Where the signatories themselves must come and give oral testimony to establish it, then, according to the authority who requires interrogation in that category, the court can interrogate them orally. Thus Rambam can disagree with Baal HaMaor only partially. Baal HaMaor may say written testimony itself never carries hazamah. Rambam may agree that the mere writing is not hazamah testimony, but once the signatories orally authenticate and adopt the substance of the document before beit din, their present oral testimony is subject to hazamah. This also explains why Rambam writes not merely that they "say the document is ours," but that they say "this is our handwriting and we wrote it on its date." The latter statement supplies the oral time assertion necessary for the hazamah process. Tosafot in Ketubot 19b provide some support. They ask from the rule that witnesses can be believed to say a document is a document of trust, even where the document is otherwise authenticated. Maharsha asks why any issue of hazamah should arise if the witnesses can simply deny that the handwriting is theirs despite the borrower's admission. If Tosafot follow Baal HaMaor's conceptual approach, the difficulty falls away. The rule that hazamah must be in the witnesses' presence and the related rules of contradiction belong primarily to oral testimony. Documents, though not themselves subject to hazamah, were not given an entirely separate law of contradiction; Chazal generally apply the civil evidentiary framework consistently. Thus the document can be contradicted or invalidated without personally transforming the signatories into zomemim. I do not mean that every detail of Tosafot must follow Baal HaMaor, but the passage fits his structure better than Shach's reading. It would also be difficult to say that because Torah law does not require document authentication, every signed document is automatically subject to Torah hazamah and the witnesses become personally disqualified even without oral adoption. I have not found the early authorities stating such a sweeping rule. Rambam's own language, requiring them to come and identify their handwriting, points the other way. Accordingly, the most coherent reading is: Baal HaMaor holds that formal hazamah does not apply to written document testimony. A document can be contradicted and invalidated, but its witnesses are not punished or disqualified as zomemim merely through written hazamah. His reasoning can be grounded both in the special agency status of a document and in the witnesses' ability to say the date was not part of the substance upon which they signed where civil interrogation is unnecessary. The Gemara at the beginning of Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot does not refute him. According to Rashi, its question concerns the presumption of witness truthfulness and the reason to reinterpret a suspicious date, not technical incapacity for hazamah. Indeed, the Gemara's choice of a first-year Sabbatical document and its failure to ask from ordinary postdated documents support this reading. Undated gittin and kiddushin documents likewise provide strong support that written instruments do not require the full oral interrogation and hazamah framework. Rambam and Rif can still hold that once the signatories themselves come before beit din, authenticate the handwriting, remember the underlying transaction, and state that the document was written on time, their oral testimony is subject to hazamah. This explains Rambam's otherwise unnecessary wording. If, however, the handwriting is independently verified and no oral testimony of the signatories is needed, the written instrument itself does not necessarily create personal hazamah against them. This distinction, in my view, is correct and precise in Rambam's words.
