Those who inform to coercive authorities. See Panim Me'irot, part 2, siman 90, by my grandfather, who discusses a deed of kinyan signed by two witnesses. Testimony was given that one of the witnesses was an informer to coercive authorities, and the local decisor invalidated the deed. Panim Me'irot writes that the decisor did not act properly. Since no public proclamation of the witness's disqualification had been made and he had not been publicly punished for his offense, his testimony remained valid. An informer's monetary liability is only under the law of indirect causation, and the Shach in siman 386 rules that such liability is a rabbinic penalty. Bava Kamma 62a states, according to the view that one does not adjudicate liability for indirect causation, that one likewise does not adjudicate damages for informing. The rule that informers to coercive authorities are cast down and not raised is itself rabbinic, enacted as a protective measure. Accordingly, since an informer is disqualified only rabbinically, a deed should not be invalidated unless his disqualification was publicly proclaimed. This is especially so in generations where wrongdoing has become widespread, and sometimes a person who saves himself through non-Jewish authorities is called an informer by his fellow even though he is not truly one. Beit din must investigate such matters, and until the person has been formally disqualified by beit din the deed should not be invalidated. Beit Meir, Even HaEzer 17:3, cites this briefly and objects. Even if according to the Shach the informer is exempt by Torah law from payment enforceable by human courts, he is certainly liable in the heavenly court and has violated the Torah prohibition "You shall not go about as a talebearer." The statement that indirect causation is only a rabbinic penalty means only that the monetary obligation is rabbinic because he is not a direct tortfeasor. The underlying prohibition of informing is Torah law. This differs from forms of theft prohibited only rabbinically, where Torah law itself permits the conduct. The Shulchan Aruch's language that informers are worse than idolaters also indicates a Torah-level disqualification. In my opinion, however, Panim Me'irot can be defended. Certainly he knew that informing to coercive authorities is a Torah prohibition and that only the monetary payment is rabbinic. His point is that for a sin that is not one of monetary rapacity, even if Torah prohibited, a person is disqualified by Torah law only when the offense is punishable by lashes. If it has no lashes, the disqualification is rabbinic, as explained above in seif 2. A person wicked through monetary rapacity, by contrast, is disqualified by Torah law. Panim Me'irot therefore reasoned that because the informer is monetarily liable only rabbinically he is not classified as wicked through rapacity. Although he violated a Torah prohibition, that offense carries no lashes, and his disqualification is therefore rabbinic and requires proclamation. He must accordingly disagree with Maharit and Knesset HaGedolah cited above in seif 2, seif katan 6, much as Kenesset Yechezkel cited there also appears to disagree. Sha'ar Mishpat discusses Panim Me'irot at length and concludes that while the analysis itself is sound, his practical ruling to extract money on the strength of the Shach's position that indirect causation is only rabbinic is not correct, since most authorities hold that liability for such damage is Torah law. According to them no proclamation is needed, and one cannot extract money contrary to their view. In my opinion Panim Me'irot relied also on his second consideration, namely the circumstances of later generations. See Rama responsa, siman 13, citing a great authority who writes that in these generations one should not disqualify people merely because of alleged informing, since sometimes the misconduct of local leaders itself necessitates such recourse. I cited this in Pitchei Teshuva, Even HaEzer, siman 42, seif katan 18. That source seems to imply that absent this special consideration, an informer is disqualified even without proclamation. This requires study.
