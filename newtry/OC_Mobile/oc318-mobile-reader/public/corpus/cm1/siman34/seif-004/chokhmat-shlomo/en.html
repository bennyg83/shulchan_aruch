Seif 8. The Shach, seif katan 5, writes that Rivash’s view is primary and that the halachah follows a mediating opinion. I already wrote above in the laws of judges that this is difficult from Shabbat and the end of the first chapter of Pesachim. The rule that the halachah follows a mediating view applies only where the disputing authorities themselves articulated the relevant two positions in a way that allows the third opinion to function as a genuine compromise between their stated views. Where later poskim simply expressed differing conclusions, one cannot automatically declare that the halachah follows the middle position. Even if the Shach independently found that view more persuasive in this case, his broad statement that “everywhere the halachah follows the mediator” requires explanation. I addressed his words in my work on Even HaEzer, fifth edition siman 61, and in another responsum. There, in the gloss. “If witnesses come,” etc., “he is not believed to say, ‘I acted inadvertently.’” See my work on Orach Chaim, laws of Pesach, siman 455, in a responsum to Stanislav, where I concluded that this rule may be specific to an oath. When one swore falsely, his presumption of fitness is directly weakened by the objectively false oath, and he is immediately treated as wicked unless he can establish an excuse. For other transgressions, however, there is room to say that he remains believed to claim inadvertence. Even with an oath, if there was a mitzvah or legitimate reason to swear, he may be believed to say that his false statement resulted from inadvertence. See there. I believe there is further proof concerning Rashba’s intent from Gittin, in the discussion of one who tells another, “Your pure foods became impure.” Rabbi Yochanan says he is not believed, while Rav Asi says, “What can I do? The Torah believed him.” The Gemara asks where the Torah believed such a person. One might have expected an immediate challenge from the baraita that priests who render offerings piggul are exempt if they acted inadvertently, while the piggul remains effective. If the owners did not hear the priest’s words, his own statement appears to be believed. If they did hear, how can he later claim inadvertence if Rashba holds that one cannot say “I sinned inadvertently” once the prohibited act is established? The better conclusion is that Rashba’s rule is not universal. In ordinary transgressions a person may sometimes be believed to say that he acted inadvertently, especially where his own bodily presumption of fitness supports him. This presumption can be stronger than an ordinary monetary presumption. I have discussed the details elsewhere, including in additions to my work Shenot Chaim.
Seif 8. “A zomem witness.” The source is Bava Kamma 72. I have a substantial question. Why does the Gemara not state a practical difference between the reasons concerning the retroactive disqualification of zomem witnesses in matters of prohibition? Suppose between their first testimony and the later hazamah they signed gittin or kiddushin, or testified in a capital matter. If Rava’s reason is that hazamah is a scriptural novelty, then until the hazamah they retain their prior fitness even for matters of prohibition. If the reason is only loss to purchasers, that consideration is monetary and can be implemented through the court’s power over property, but it should not permit an otherwise married woman or validate a forbidden relationship by uprooting Torah law through an active act. Tosafot’s answer that the Sages did not distinguish within their enactment readily explains different monetary cases, but not matters of prohibition. It seems necessary to say that even Abaye concedes that hazamah is a Torah novelty. By Torah law, the zomem witnesses are therefore not automatically disqualified retroactively. The situation resembles two groups contradicting one another, where Torah law can preserve a prior presumption of fitness. Abaye’s retroactive disqualification is rabbinic because the Sages do not ordinarily rely on such a presumption in the face of two against two. Rava either holds that the presumption of personal fitness is stronger than ordinary presumptions even rabbinically, or that because the Torah imposed an especially severe consequence on zomem witnesses, the Sages did not add retroactive disqualification. According to the alternate explanation based on loss to purchasers, all agree that Torah law does not disqualify them retroactively. Rabbinically there would have been reason to do so, but the Sages left the Torah status in place to protect purchasers. Once they established that rule, they did not divide the witness’s legal status and therefore continued to treat him as fit for matters of prohibition as well, lest differing statuses create confusion and undermine the monetary enactment. The Gemara’s suggested practical difference involving two witnesses who testify about one witness concerns a situation where the later attack is not the special Torah institution of hazamah. There, Torah law itself can disqualify, and any monetary leniency due to purchasers does not automatically validate him for prohibitory matters. This appears the best resolution given the difficulty.
