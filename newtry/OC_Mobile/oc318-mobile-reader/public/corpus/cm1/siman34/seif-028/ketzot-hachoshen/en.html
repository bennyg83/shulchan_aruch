Two testified. Many ask why this differs from two sets of witnesses who contradict one another, where each set retains its presumption of fitness. Bach answers that in the ordinary contradiction no witness directly testifies that the others are disqualified. We merely infer that one set must be false. Therefore each set remains in its prior presumption. Here, by contrast, two witnesses directly testify that this particular man is disqualified, and two others directly contradict them. This produces the ordinary rabbinic uncertainty of two against two, which can displace even a presumption of fitness. Chacham Tzvi answers that with two contradictory groups there are two favorable elements: each group had a prior presumption of fitness, and we know with certainty that one group is truthful. Here there is only the individual's prior presumption. A third answer, cited in the glosses to Taz, is that we do not initially accept testimony of two against two merely to invalidate an entire group unless there is actual hazamah, while testimony directed against one particular individual is properly receivable and then remains in unresolved conflict when two others support him. Tosafot in Bava Kamma concerning plotting witnesses helps explain the matter. The Gemara distinguishes where two plot against one witness. Rabbeinu Tam emends the text because if only one witness is plotted against, the other original witness remains and effectively supports him. The case is best understood as follows. The plotting witnesses come to disqualify one man. The second original witness, who says he was with him, supports his fitness. We then have a direct two against two conflict concerning that one witness, leaving him doubtfully disqualified. If the plotting witnesses attack both members of the original pair as a pair, the structure differs. The ordinary rule of two conflicting sets operates. The deeper principle appears to be that when one individual is directly established as disqualified by two and restored by two, the conflict is genuine two against two and the rabbinic uncertainty remains. But when two groups merely contradict each other's account, trying to declare both groups doubtfully disqualified would defeat itself. If each group becomes only doubtfully fit, a doubtfully disqualified group cannot itself uproot the definite prior fitness of the other group. One cannot use doubtful witnesses to create the very doubt by which the other witnesses become doubtful. We are therefore compelled to retain both groups as qualified, as Rav Huna holds. This also explains Rav Chisda's opposing rule, "Why should I deal with false witnesses?" His concern is not the general stringency of two against two, but the mixture of true and false testimony among the groups. Tosafot in Ketubot explicitly says that even Rav Chisda, who will not use two contradictory groups, agrees that if one such group later tries to disqualify another person, its testimony cannot do so, because the group itself is already doubtful. Thus the difference between a direct dispute about one person's fitness and two groups contradicting each other is structurally necessary, not arbitrary.
