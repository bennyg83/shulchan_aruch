He is doubtfully disqualified. Most authorities, including Tosafot in many places and in Ketubot 23b, conclude that where two witnesses disqualify a person and two contradict them, the Torah law is to set two against two and retain the person in his prior presumption of fitness. Chazal nevertheless imposed doubt because two witnesses have challenged him. I therefore have a question where two witnesses say he is disqualified only rabbinically, such as a rabbinic robber, and two contradict them. Tosafot rule that where two say a kohen is the son of a divorced woman and two contradict them, he may eat rabbinic terumah because the underlying uncertainty of two against two is itself only rabbinic. In a rabbinic matter we do not compound rabbinic uncertainty upon rabbinic uncertainty. The same should apply here. If the alleged witness disqualification is itself only rabbinic and the two-against-two doubt is likewise rabbinic, he should remain qualified. This also resolves a difficulty in Tosafot concerning three judges who sat to authenticate a document and an objection was raised against one of them. The Gemara asks whether the objection concerns robbery and whether the result is two against two. Tosafot say that if two say he robbed and two say he did not, he remains qualified. Maharshal and Maharsha ask that according to Rosh, two against two still creates rabbinic doubt. The answer is that document authentication is itself rabbinic, since Torah law does not require authentication of a signed document, which is treated as testimony already examined in beit din. Thus this is doubly rabbinic: the doubt is rabbinic and the evidentiary proceeding is rabbinic. Tosafot therefore validate him for document authentication. Rosh may hold that once a person is rabbinically disqualified for ordinary Torah testimony, we do not distinguish among kinds of testimony and should also exclude him from authentication. Tosafot need not accept that extension. This supports the broader rule that where the substantive witness disqualification is itself rabbinic, two against two should leave him in his presumption of fitness. The same explains the sugya in Ketubot concerning whether a kohen can be elevated by one witness. Tosafot say that because two against two is only rabbinic doubt and terumah in that context is rabbinic, the kohen can continue eating. One might ask: Rabbah and R. Yosef in Yevamot are understood by many authorities to hold that two against two creates Torah-level uncertainty. How would they explain that Mishnah? The answer is that they need not establish the case as two witnesses saying he is a son of a divorced woman, which is a Torah disqualification. They can establish it where the two say he is a son of a chalutzah, a rabbinic disqualification. Then even if their two-against-two uncertainty is Torah in the abstract, the substantive issue and the terumah are both rabbinic and the result remains permissive. The Gemara's formulation involving a divorced woman follows the final halachic approach that two against two is treated as rabbinic uncertainty. Thus the principle of compounded rabbinic doubt is clear. Accordingly, where the alleged witness defect is only rabbinic and two witnesses affirm it while two deny it, he should remain in his prior presumption of fitness. A related question arises where major halachic authorities disagree whether a certain act disqualifies a witness. That dispute resembles two against two. One might think to preserve the person's chazakah of fitness. Panim Me'irot, responsum 108, distinguishes: with actual conflicting witnesses, if we preserve one person's chazakah we necessarily declare two witnesses liars and thereby destroy their chazakah. With a dispute among poskim, no person's personal chazakah is harmed by ruling leniently. I do not find this distinction sufficient. Tosafot and Ran rule that where two witnesses say a woman became betrothed and two say she did not, we do not simply preserve her prior unmarried status, even where the dispute is whether the kiddushin thrown to her landed closer to her or closer to him. Ran explicitly says both sets may have made an honest mistake. Neither set must be personally disqualified. Nevertheless the woman does not remain wholly in her prior chazakah. Thus the issue is not that preserving one chazakah necessarily destroys another person's fitness. Tosafot compare presumption of unmarried status and presumption of witness fitness directly. The same framework governs both. Rosh likewise writes concerning a disputed betrothal document that because the matter is a dispute among authorities we act stringently. He does not say to set the authorities against one another and preserve the woman's prior unmarried status. Accordingly, a genuine dispute among major authorities can be treated like a substantial halachic doubt rather than resolved automatically by chazakah. If anything, such a dispute can be more stringent because it reflects uncertainty in our knowledge of the law itself. This is clear and, in my view, compelling.
