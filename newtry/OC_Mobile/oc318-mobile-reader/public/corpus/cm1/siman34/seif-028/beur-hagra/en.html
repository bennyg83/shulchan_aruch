(Anthology) Two, etc. The Rif rules this way and bases it on the Gemara's conclusion, 'Rather, Rav Nachman said: establish,' etc. The Rosh likewise rules so and brings proof from King Yannai at the end of chapter 3 of Kiddushin. See Tosafot, Bava Batra 32a, Ketubot 26b, and Kiddushin there. Tosafot in Ketubot 22a, however, do not follow this approach and prefer Rabbeinu Chananel. Rashba explains that Tosafot distinguish between prohibitions, where we rule stringently, and monetary law, where stringency for one party is leniency for the other, so the Torah status quo remains. The Rosh and Ran distinguish the case of two conflicting groups of witnesses: there, when each group appears separately, we judge only that group; here, both testimonies must be judged together. Others explain that two against two creates a Torah-level uncertainty. The statement in Yevamot 31a that the matter is placed on its prior status and becomes only a rabbinic uncertainty applies where no present act removes it from that status. This resolves Tosafot's question in Ketubot 26b. Where the woman must now be removed from her husband, however, her prior status cannot resolve the matter. All the more so in monetary law, a presumption cannot remove money from its present possessor. This also appears to be the Rif's, Rambam's, and Shulchan Aruch's view, though it still requires explanation why the witness appears disqualified even for retaining money. Ramban in Milchamot holds that two against two is always a Torah-level uncertainty. He explains the textual variants in Yevamot and the passage concerning a person whose sanity was doubtful accordingly. Even according to Tosafot's text, the ultimate distinction is that in a rabbinic matter one may follow a presumption leniently, while without any presumption the matter remains a Torah uncertainty. Ramban similarly explains that if the presumption itself had Torah force, it should operate even against two and two, and therefore concludes that the woman is not restored to her prior status. Nevertheless, even Ramban must accept Tosafot's distinction that in rabbinic law we may follow a presumption leniently, as the passages in Ketubot and Bava Batra show.
(Anthology) But if they came, etc. This follows Rashi and the Rif. Proof is brought from the statement, 'set two against two,' from the doubtful divorces and betrothals in Yevamot, and from the incident of King Yannai in Kiddushin. Ramban in Milchamot writes that all the more so in monetary law we do not follow a presumption to remove money, just as in Bava Batra we do not follow either majority or presumption against the possessor. Baal HaMaor explains like Rashi in Kiddushin, but Ramban raises Tosafot's objections against him and adds that the witnesses testify to the priest's disqualification from birth. Tosafot's statement that two against two is a rabbinic uncertainty reflects the conclusion in Yevamot.
Or silence. As a General:
Two witnesses. This follows the incident of Bar Chama, Sanhedrin 27a. Although each witness testified to a separate act, the same rule applies to two distinct transgressions, just as separate testimony concerning years and concerning pregnancy combines at the end of siman 30.
(Anthology) Two witnesses, etc. As stated there: one said, 'He stole before me,' and the other said likewise. The same applies to two transgressions, because we rule like Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korcha.
