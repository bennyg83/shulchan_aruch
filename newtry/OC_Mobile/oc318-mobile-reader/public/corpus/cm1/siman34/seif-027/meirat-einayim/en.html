They combine to testify against him to disqualify him. The wording 'to testify' is precise. The case is that after the litigation has been concluded, the person to whom he swore and the witness come anew and testify before beit din that he swore falsely. Since his case was already completed, the litigant is no longer considered interested. The Rosh writes this in Responsa, klal 11. This resolves the apparent contradiction between the Tur here and what he writes in siman 87, seif 44, and between the Mechaber's rulings here and there at the end of seif 29; see the Perishah.
And some disagree. Meaning, they hold that the witness and the litigant do not combine. What follows, 'and even if they were, etc.,' also follows this dissenting view, and the entire passage is Ramban's position there.
After he stood in judgment and swore, etc. The reason is that once the Torah believed him through his oath against each of them, their testimony regarding that same matter is treated as though it had not existed. This differs from a case before he swore, where both testify that they know he is a robber. Even though one does not testify concerning the same robbery as the other, they combine. The cited source concludes that even the two persons who were robbed may testify against him, because we divide their statements. One must say they testify only for the purpose of disqualifying him from future testimony, as explained above regarding a lender at interest in seif 26.
