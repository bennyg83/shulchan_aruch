Or he was mistaken and thought, etc. The same principle is stated shortly at the beginning of seif 24. This does not contradict Rama's ruling in seif 5 that he is not believed to say, "I acted inadvertently," nor the rule concerning the oath of testimony that he is not believed to say, "I forgot." There, the prohibition of the oath is known to everyone, and he merely excuses himself by saying he forgot that he had sworn. A person is expected to keep in mind what he himself accepted through an oath. Likewise, one who originally saw testimony is expected to remember it for the purpose of testifying. Here, however, he says, "I thought there was no prohibition in this act." Why should he have been on guard regarding something he did not know was forbidden? Similarly, one who ties or unties on Shabbat and says he did not know that action was a melachah, or did not know that it was Shabbat, may be believed in the relevant sense. Since the circumstance came upon him from outside rather than through an obligation he personally accepted, it is less naturally kept in mind.
