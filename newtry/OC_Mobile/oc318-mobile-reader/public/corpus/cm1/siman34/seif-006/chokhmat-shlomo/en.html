Seif 23. In the gloss: “Even one disqualified by Torah law,” etc. Note: Ketzot HaChoshen asks why we do not reason as with a slaughtering knife: the knife is found nicked before us, and together with the animal’s prior prohibition this creates a combined basis to invalidate the slaughter. So too, where a witness is now seen to have acted wickedly, perhaps the present evidence of wickedness should combine with the possessor’s monetary presumption and disqualify the witness from extracting money. According to what I wrote in my novellae to Yoreh Deah siman 2, there is a major distinction. The reason a witness’s presumption of fitness can operate even to extract money is that the doubt about his personal fitness does not arise specifically within a monetary dispute. Witnesses can testify either to extract money or to maintain possession, and they also testify in nonmonetary areas. When uncertainty arises whether a person is a valid witness, the issue is first decided as a question about the witness himself, in a setting not inherently opposed by another person’s monetary presumption. If we preserve his presumption of fitness there, he remains a valid witness for all purposes. His personal status and the later monetary consequence are separate legal questions. A slaughtering knife is different. Whether the knife was intact matters only because of the slaughter. Every inquiry into its prior condition directly confronts the animal’s presumption of prohibition. It is therefore reasonable to combine the fact that the knife is now nicked with the animal’s preexisting prohibited status. With a witness, the doubt about his fitness can arise even where no opposing monetary presumption exists. In that independent inquiry we preserve his fitness, and once he is established as fit he remains fit even when his testimony later operates against a possessor’s monetary presumption. Peri Megadim also discusses the knife as a presumption subject to change, since a knife naturally can become damaged. A person is not ordinarily presumed to become wicked, so the analogy is further weakened. See also my work on Even HaEzer siman 17, first edition, where I addressed Ketzot’s question. The Mechaber also speaks here of one disqualified rabbinically. Sema and Shach explain that for the very testimony connected to the offense, no public declaration of disqualification is needed. In a responsum on Even HaEzer, sixth edition siman 169, I explained that this follows from the principle that an act performed in violation of a rabbinic prohibition may sometimes be legally ineffective. From this I derived a new point: where the person violated an oath or cherem, in an area where all agree that if he acted the act remains legally effective, even the testimony connected with that violation may require prior public declaration before he is disqualified. I also wrote that if judgment was not completed on the strength of his testimony alone but only together with other witnesses, perhaps that case is comparable to a separate later testimony and also requires public declaration. In rabbinic disqualification we should hear the lenient side. See the responsum there in detail.
