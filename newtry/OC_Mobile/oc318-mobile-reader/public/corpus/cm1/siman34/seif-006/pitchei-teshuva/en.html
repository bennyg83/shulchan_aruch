Disqualified rabbinically. See Birkei Yosef, letter 26, who cites Maharit, Even HaEzer, siman 43, that a matter which does not cause Torah-level disqualification because it carries no lashes nevertheless causes rabbinic disqualification. Although rabbinically disqualified witnesses ordinarily require a public declaration, perhaps that requirement applies only to rabbinic transgressions, which ordinary people may not recognize as rendering a person wicked. Where the act is a Torah prohibition, everyone knows it is forbidden, and there is no greater declaration than that. Knesset HaGedolah agrees in a responsum printed in Edut BeYaakov, siman 17, and writes that although his teacher Maharit stated the matter tentatively, to him it is clear. Ritva on Bava Metzia 72, however, implies in one explanation that even for Torah prohibitions and Torah disqualifications, where the matter is not publicly known, declaration is required. See Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchot Malveh 4:6, who discusses Maharit's words at length. Birkei Yosef earlier, letter 6, states simply that one who violates a Torah prohibition carrying no lashes and is therefore disqualified only rabbinically does not require declaration, as later authorities have written. See our discussion above, seif 1, seif katan 2. See Responsa Knesset Yechezkel, siman 39, concerning one who maintained grafted trees planted by his father despite warning. He rules that the man's testimony remains valid. The halachah does not follow Rabbi Akiva at the beginning of Moed Katan, who holds that maintaining such grafts is itself punishable by lashes, but rather the first Tanna, as the Rambam rules in Hilchot Kilayim chapter 2 and Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah, siman 295, seif 2. Thus he committed a transgression carrying no lashes and is disqualified only rabbinically, according to the Rama. Such disqualification requires declaration. Since, according to the facts presented, no declaration was made and he was not publicly punished or expelled from the synagogue, his testimony remains valid. Knesset Yechezkel then considers whether, because the person who summoned him as a witness knew that he maintained kilayim, he should be disqualified even without declaration. The Rambam writes that a rabbinically disqualified witness requires declaration so that testimony given before the declaration is accepted, lest people's rights be lost when they did not know that the witness was disqualified. This could imply that where the litigant knew of the disqualification, the testimony should fail. Responsa Maharam Trani similarly discusses a case where he and his beit din had disqualified a witness for a rabbinic transgression, and another beit din, knowing of that ruling, nevertheless accepted him because there had been no public declaration. Maharam Trani ruled that since the second beit din knew the first beit din had disqualified him, no further declaration was needed, relying on the Rambam's language. Knesset Yechezkel distinguishes that case, because an actual beit din ruling of disqualification is itself equivalent to a declaration. It is surprising that he did not cite Maharit, for according to Maharit a Torah prohibition would disqualify even without declaration. See also Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 3, siman 168, concerning Reuven, who came before a beit din to disqualify Shimon from testimony because Reuven and another witness testified that Shimon had stolen from Yehudah. Shimon claimed that Reuven himself induced him to commit the theft and produced witnesses to that effect. Shevut Yaakov writes that there is no basis to regard Reuven as a valid witness in this matter. Although there is no agency for a transgression and Shimon remains liable in human courts, one who induces another to sin is liable before Heaven even through speech alone, violates “You shall not place a stumbling block before the blind,” and the punishment for one who incites another is severe, not only regarding idolatry but other sins as well, as explained in Chavot Yair, siman 166. One who violates a prohibition carrying no lashes is at least rabbinically disqualified. Even though rabbinic disqualification ordinarily requires declaration, for the very testimony through which the witness became disqualified, no declaration is required, as Nimukei Yosef, cited by the Sema below in seif 23 and Baer Heitev there, explains. Alternatively, Shevut Yaakov may agree with Maharit and Knesset HaGedolah that such Torah violations require no declaration. Perhaps Reuven had repented, but if he repented sufficiently to fulfill his obligation toward Heaven he would have had to return the stolen money to its owner. He would then plainly be interested in testifying that Shimon stole, so that Shimon would repay the theft and reimburse Reuven for what Reuven had paid. Shimon therefore cannot be disqualified through Reuven's testimony until Reuven has properly repented.
