One who shares with a thief. Urim VeTumim asks why a person who knowingly holds another's stolen property is not like one who denies a deposit and therefore disqualified as a person of monetary greed. Ketzot HaChoshen answers that taking from a thief does not make the second holder a thief of the original owner unless he consumes or otherwise newly changes the property, because the first thief already removed it from the owner's domain. I agree only in part. If the second person changes the object in a way that legally transfers ownership, uses it without permission, consumes it, or otherwise adds a new act of dispossession, he certainly commits a fresh act of theft. A change that creates acquisition is itself a new removal from the owner's ownership, and unauthorized use is a new act of wrongful possession. If he merely receives a share of the already stolen item without adding any new act of use, change, or destruction, there may be no additional theft acquisition beyond the first theft. Still, so long as he knowingly retains another's property because of monetary desire and refuses to restore it, Urim VeTumim's approach is primary for practical law: unless he admits and wishes to return it, his conduct is sufficiently characterized by dishonest gain to disqualify him.
