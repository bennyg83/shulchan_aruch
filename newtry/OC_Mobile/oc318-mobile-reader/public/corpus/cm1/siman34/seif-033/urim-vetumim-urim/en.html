Where they do not know him. The requirement that one who violated an oath, a butcher, or a plotting witness go to a place where he is unknown is not because repentance would otherwise be impossible in a place where people know his past. Such a person could demonstrate sincere repentance through other meaningful conduct. Rather, because he sinned specifically in oath-taking, testimony, or butchery, the Sages required him to repair the very area in which he failed under conditions where social pressure would not be responsible for the improvement. Since they imposed this stricter form of repentance upon the butcher regarding his trade, they extended it to his restoration for testimony and oaths as well. By contrast, gamblers and other disqualified persons are not restored through the exact area of their prior misconduct, and therefore their repentance was not made as strict. Sema.
Specifically those who violated oaths. Hagahot Ashri records Or Zarua's doubt whether one suspected of a false monetary oath requires so demanding a form of repentance, because he must in any event return the money gained through the false oath. It seems that where beit din already compels repayment because witnesses established the false oath, forced restitution cannot itself prove repentance. But if he was already disqualified by one false oath and then voluntarily admits that an earlier oath also was false, when beit din had no knowledge of that earlier wrongdoing, and he returns the money, such conduct is a strong sign of repentance. If two witnesses established only the false oath sufficient to disqualify him but not to extract the money, and he voluntarily returns the money, perhaps this is exactly the case about which Or Zarua remained uncertain.
