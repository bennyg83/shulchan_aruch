And his circumstances are pressing, etc. This law is learned from the rule that where property is sold to a woman or orphans, bar metzra does not apply because it is difficult for them to trouble themselves to find another purchase. Therefore Rama's gloss belongs more properly before seif 7, where the Mechaber says that bar metzra does not apply to a woman or minor. Terumat HaDeshen gives another reason in the name of the Or Zarua. Since bar metzra rests entirely upon doing what is good and upright, it is not good and upright to let one person buy out of abundance while another person must wander from place to place for lack of a dwelling. He further writes that because the authorities dispute this point, the law favors the purchaser, who is regarded as the possessor because his purchase is valid by Torah law. He adds that even the Hagahot Maimoniyot, who disagree with the Or Zarua and say the custom is not to follow him, may disagree only where the purchaser already has a house or field and wishes to acquire another merely because his circumstances are tight and he seeks income from it, as in the case of Runya according to Rashi. In such cases it is difficult today to assess accurately how pressed his circumstances are. But where the purchaser has no house at all, perhaps even they agree that bar metzra does not apply. He further concludes that if it is known that the purchaser could buy a house elsewhere, even if that alternative is smaller and cramped and he seeks this more spacious house because it better meets his needs, bar metzra does apply. Mahari Weil adds another distinction between a purchaser who is a resident of the city and one who is not. Each authority brings support for his view. See Darkei Moshe. This explains Rama's wording that the exemption applies specifically where he cannot find another property.
