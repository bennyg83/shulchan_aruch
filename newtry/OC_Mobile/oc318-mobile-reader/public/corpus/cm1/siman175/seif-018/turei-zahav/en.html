Seif 40: They did not penalize his son. The Beit Yosef was uncertain whether this applies only where the purchaser did not undertake liability, or even where he did. The Sema rules like the latter view. This is surprising, for the Rosh explains that the Sages did not wait until the damage became clear and then adjudicate payment. Instead, they penalized the purchaser by requiring him to execute a lien against his property effective immediately. This implies that the adjoining owner may collect even from purchasers from the date of the undertaking, and certainly from heirs. Once the assets are encumbered from that day, damage occurring after the purchaser's death should also be collected from the estate. Rabbeinu generally follows his father, the Rosh, and then cites the Gaon that the son was not penalized. The Gaon must therefore speak where no undertaking was made, while holding that payment itself does not require an undertaking. One cannot ask why the Rosh did not answer that excommunication was imposed so that the son would also be liable. His answer is stronger: even subsequent purchasers become encumbered from the time of the undertaking. Although in the law of informing to non-Jews the son is not penalized after his father, even where the father already stood trial, here a written undertaking creates a stronger personal obligation. Yet siman 385 rules that in cases of rendering food impure or forbidden the son is not penalized even when the damage occurred during the father's lifetime. Here the likelihood of damage is less certain, since a non-Jew may live beside a Jew without causing harm. We must therefore conclude that here too the father undertook to pay, yet the son is exempt because the property was encumbered only for damage occurring during the father's lifetime.
