Rishmei She'eilah, siman 55, explains that the Shach's disagreement with the Sema concerns combining Rabbeinu Tam's broad view that bar metzra does not apply to houses. It does not necessarily mean that the Shach rejects combining the Raavad's special view regarding synagogue seats. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 95, discusses one who sold two synagogue seats together, one in the men's section and one in the women's section. The adjoining owner was adjacent only to the men's seat and sought to remove the purchaser. Chatam Sofer rejects a simple kim li defense based on Rabbeinu Tam or Raavad. He also explains that the Rivash's rule regarding two seats separated by a substantial wall does not assist the purchaser here, because the Rivash dealt with a real partition, more than nine handbreadths high and a handbreadth thick, making combination impossible. Where only a board separates adjacent seats, the adjoining owner may remove it to widen his place, and even if he does not remove it, there is value in acquiring neighboring seats so that family members may sit together. The purchaser nevertheless has a different argument: he bought two seats together while the adjoining owner borders only one. According to Sema and Taz, a purchaser who buys a combined package may not be displaced from only one component. Chatam Sofer adds that if local custom treats each men's seat and its corresponding women's seat as a paired unit, sold and acquired together, the two are like one large field. An adjoining owner bordering one side may then acquire the entire pair if he offers to take both. If the two seats are not customarily linked, however, he cannot displace the purchaser even from the men's seat merely because he borders it. Chatam Sofer follows Sema and Taz on this point.
