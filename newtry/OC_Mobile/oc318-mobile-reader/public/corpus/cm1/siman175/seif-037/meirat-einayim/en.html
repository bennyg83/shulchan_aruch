From the person from whom he had purchased it. Meaning, Reuven purchased this field from Shimon and later resold it to Shimon. Since it originally belonged to Shimon, the Sages did not wish to remove it from him, because there is also an element of what is good and upright in allowing property to remain with its former owner. The same applies even to the son of the former owner if he already purchased it first. This is specifically where he preceded the neighboring owner and bought. If both come simultaneously to buy, the neighboring owner has priority. Another person cannot rely upon this rule at all, and even a former owner who buys from a later purchaser rather than directly from the person who originally acquired from him does not receive this preference. Rama states these details in his gloss. See Darkei Moshe and the Beit Yosef for dissenting opinions.
The neighboring owner cannot displace him. He may say: I drove a lion away from your boundary. Nimukei Yosef writes in the chapter HaMekabel that one who buys a collapsing wall is nevertheless subject to bar metzra. He cannot say, "I drove a lion away, because had I not bought it the wall would have fallen upon your house and damaged you." This is not comparable to buying from a gentile, where the purchaser truly removes a dangerous neighboring owner. See Darkei Moshe.
