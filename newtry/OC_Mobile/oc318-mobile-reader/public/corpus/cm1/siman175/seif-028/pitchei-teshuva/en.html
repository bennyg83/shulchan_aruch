The tenant cannot remove it from his possession. See the Sema, who writes that according to what he established above, namely that a lease and a mortgage are governed alike, the Rama's ruling below, that even if the adjacent owner purchased first the mortgagee can remove the property from him, should apply here as well, and the tenant should be able to remove it from the purchaser. Netivot HaMishpat cites this. Shaar Mishpat notes, however, that Maharshal, responsum 43, states explicitly that although a mortgagee may remove the property from an adjacent purchaser, a tenant may not. Even if the landlord sold the property to another, the tenant cannot remove it from the purchaser. Maharshal ruled this in practice; the Shach cites him briefly, and Bach, responsum 69, rules likewise. The Bach adds that this is the Rama's own view, for he distinguishes in this respect between a lease and a mortgage. Since the matter is doubtful, property is not removed from the purchaser, as explained above in seif 45. Shevut Yaakov, III, 165, sets out the following practical rules. If the landlord has already sold to another, the tenant cannot remove the purchaser, because the purchaser may say, "I hold like the authorities who deny any bar metzra right to a tenant." If, before the sale, the purchaser and tenant both come to buy, the tenant has priority. If the tenant and an ordinary adjacent owner come together, the adjacent owner has priority because his right is certain. If the tenant purchased first, however, the adjacent owner cannot remove him, for the tenant may say, "I hold like the authorities who recognize a tenant's bar metzra right." The adjacent owner may nevertheless require the tenant to swear that the tenancy was not arranged as a subterfuge to defeat his right. If the landlord merely wishes, after the first term has expired, to rent to another tenant, the first tenant cannot compel him to renew the lease. Since the land belongs to the owner, he may prefer another tenant who will care for it better, and a person may deal with his own property as he wishes, unless the original agreement expressly granted the first tenant a right of priority for an additional period. A mortgagee has a stronger position than a partner and cannot be removed by him, but if mortgagee and partner come simultaneously, the partner has priority because he owns an actual share in the land. Likewise, where husband and wife own a house and the husband dies, the wife has priority to take the house in payment of all or part of her ketubah debt, because she is considered among the original owners. Shevut Yaakov concludes that these principles apply both to leases and to mortgages.
