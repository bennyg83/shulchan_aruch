There is an authority who says that an adjoining owner, etc. The Tur writes this in the name of the Ramah. The Ramah's intention appears to be that the adjoining owner cannot authorize another person to act, even where the land will belong to the adjoining owner, as the Sema explains. This is also indicated by the concluding phrase, 'But to stand in his place for the purpose of preparing the money,' implying that authorization is effective only to prepare the money, but not otherwise. This is unlike the Bach, who explains that the restriction applies only if the land is to belong to the agent, while authorization is valid if it will belong to the adjoining owner. He argues that the language of authorization implies this and follows the language of the Gemara. On the contrary, the language of authorization in the Gemara there implies that the matter is to belong to the one granting authority. One should not object from the Tur above, siman 122, in the name of the Ramah, that a person may grant authorization even without transferring ownership of the underlying claim. The answer is that the Ramah holds that authorization is itself a rabbinic enactment that treats the agent as the principal, as the authorities explain, while the right of an adjoining owner is also a rabbinic enactment. We do not create one enactment upon another. See the Sema. The Bach answers differently, that here the purchaser bought only one-sixth of the field or a similar fractional portion; see there.
