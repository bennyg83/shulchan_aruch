And attached to the ground. In cattle sheds there are troughs or channels used to water the animals, some passing from room to room or through an opening in a wall so that water poured in one area drains outside. One rented part of a cattle shed and installed his own channel there. He later sold the channel to another person, and the landlord claimed a neighboring-owner right to purchase it. It appears that even if the channel is tightly secured to the wall with wood, nails, and pegs, the purchaser's sale should not be cancelled. The Sema writes that according to some views pegs are not treated as a permanent attachment, and where the matter is uncertain we do not undo a completed sale merely because of doubt in an equitable enactment. The case may also resemble a removable opening or fixture attached to a door, which is not always treated as part of the realty for sale purposes even though the door itself is attached to the house. A fixture that can be transferred to any other door is less integrated into the real property. The pegs fastening this channel also seem less permanent than the pegs discussed by the Gemara. Similar guidance may be drawn from the law of a watchman's hut in a vineyard and from distinctions between items inserted into soil or walls. By contrast, a reed fence whose entire continued existence depends on being fixed into the ground would reasonably be considered attached. The exact boundary requires study.
