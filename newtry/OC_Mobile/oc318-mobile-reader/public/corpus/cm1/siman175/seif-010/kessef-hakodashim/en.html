The neighboring owner was in another country. One sold his share in land held jointly with the heirs of his brother. One heir lived about six parasangs away and another was outside the country. The sale was not made before the communal officer and attendant in the customary manner and was not publicly announced. It nevertheless appears to be precisely the case discussed in seif 49 of Tur, siman 175. Beit Yosef there cites the responsa of Rashba that even without a formal announcement the sale may count as public. It is difficult to say that in our times publicity depends more strictly upon announcement than it did then, for the responsum itself indicates that announcements were customary and yet, despite the absence of one, the sale was treated as public. Even today, where the parties know there will be no challenge or creditor, sales are sometimes completed without an announcement. Likewise, failure to perform the kinyan sudar before the communal officer and attendant should not matter, because ordinary witnesses are sometimes used and the formal communal recording is done only afterward. Here the witnesses testified that the sale was openly publicized and concealed in no way. The rule that one should initially notify the neighboring owner's relatives before beit din is therefore only an initial requirement. At a minimum, where the relatives knew and remained silent there is no basis for a later challenge. The facts also showed that the relatives had ample time to inquire and object, yet the heirs themselves, who knew whether they possessed writings giving them more than a half share, did not claim preemption for about two years. The heir abroad later gave his own share as a gift to his sisters after the sale. In such a case even the view that protects an absent neighboring owner should agree that the right is lost once the absent owner himself had notice and allowed substantial time to pass without asserting it. The sisters, receiving by gift only after the prior sale, acquire no stronger right of preemption than their donor had. This is comparable to the rule in seif 15. Although elsewhere I wrote that with respect to 'buy or I will buy' a purchaser may sometimes acquire a right that his seller could not personally assert, preemption and ordinary division are different from that special equitable remedy. The purchaser further claimed that he informed the heir more than a year earlier, though the heir denied it. Were the sale not otherwise public, it would require study whether private notice alone suffices, since the heir could say he did not believe the report. Still, the implication of the Tur in the name of the Rosh is that actual notice is enough to cause the right to lapse. There was also a further reason to defeat preemption: the seller expressly stated that he would not have sold at that price to anyone else, because he loved this purchaser, and he provided him room and board for a period before and after the sale. That is comparable to a case in which property worth one hundred is sold for two hundred. Even if the objective value was not established, where the seller sincerely says, without apparent stratagem, that this purchaser's special relationship made the transaction worth more to him, his statement is significant. In every unresolved point the purchaser is in possession, as discussed in Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 84. Here the value of the hospitality itself need not be appraised because the seller also stated the independent factor of affection. Indeed, if at the moment of sale the seller could say that he wished specifically to eat at this purchaser's table during that period, the right of preemption never arose. The newly printed Tur contains an erroneous reading in the cited Rashba response, 'to whoever offers more.' The correct sense is 'according to the view that grants the longer period,' meaning the dispute whether the neighboring owner receives only enough time to go home and bring money or a full day. Even the longer view gives no more than about two days. 'Whoever gives more' has no relevance to the law of preemption, and the correct reading is also found in Maharam Alshich. The sister later sold her share to another sister. A married woman may possess a right of preemption, since there is no concern that no one will trouble himself to act for her. The fact that her husband is temporarily away does not change that. Yet here the seller's children held rights under the final marriage conditions against this land for unpaid support promised by their deceased father, approximately equal to the present purchase price. A purchase from heirs is not ordinarily subject to preemption, even when they have a guardian. A co-owner may have a stronger position, as implied by the Shach, but the document also obligated clothing for the widow and expressly gave her credibility. She says that obligation was not paid. She also says she had the customary additional ketubah from her deceased husband, so those personal rights belonged to her and only the support obligation belonged to the heirs. Even though the ketubah itself is not before us and she mentioned it only later, she is credible, because collection under the marriage conditions depends on her own credibility and she could have said that those conditions had already been paid. Thus she has an effective migo with respect to what belonged personally to her, while only the support claim remained for the heirs.
