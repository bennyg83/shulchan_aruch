In every case where there is loss to the seller. See Responsa Mayim Chayim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 9. A rav had ruled that a seller who wished to defeat bar metzra could insert into the sale deed a penalty payable to the purchaser if the sale were canceled, and then demand that the adjoining owner reimburse that penalty, on the theory that bar metzra was not enacted where the seller would suffer loss. Mayim Chayim rejects this. Where the penalty is deliberately engineered to evade the adjoining owner's right, Rama in seif 28 already provides for measures against such stratagems. Even absent fraud, however, the penalty does not defeat bar metzra because the seller need not suffer it. The purchaser is treated as the adjoining owner's agent for purposes of the acquisition, and the deed may therefore ultimately be written in the adjoining owner's name. The seller is not the one canceling the sale; rather, the law causes the purchaser to yield to the adjoining owner. The seller therefore does not breach his commitment and owes no contractual penalty. Mayim Chayim supports this from the Shach's case of a seller who swore to sell his land to a particular purchaser. The Shach implies that if the seller himself does not retract, the adjoining owner may still remove the purchaser. Even an oath does not create a loss to the seller, because the adjoining owner takes through the purchaser's legal position and the seller has not violated his oath. The same reasoning applies to a contractual penalty.
