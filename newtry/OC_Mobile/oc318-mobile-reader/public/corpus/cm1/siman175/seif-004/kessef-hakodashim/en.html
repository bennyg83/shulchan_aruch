A loss to the seller. If the seller obligated himself to pay a fixed penalty should the sale or lease be cancelled, that may defeat the neighboring owner's right, because forcing cancellation would cause him a real loss. This is so only if the penalty was structured in a manner that avoids the defect of asmachta. If money was deposited with a third party, there is room to argue, based on siman 56 and the responsum of the Rosh cited there, that the stakeholder must act according to the terms of the deposit, and that because the money is already in third-party hands the party may claim that asmachta does not apply. Yet this requires study. Perhaps that is true only before the matter comes before beit din. Once the stakeholder comes before beit din prior to payment, he may not disburse the funds without judicial authorization, and the case is judged according to the status in which the property came before the court. Since the Mordechai writes that the stakeholder's hand is like the depositor's hand, perhaps the depositor is treated as retaining possession by virtue of beit din, similar to an attachment, or perhaps the money remains indefinitely with the stakeholder; alternatively, the presumption of the original owner may control. This requires study. Even where preemption is displaced by a penalty clause, the purchaser may still be required to take an oath or accept a ban to establish that there was no collusion. If the right was evaded through a stratagem but not through actual collusion, it requires study whether, after the fact, the lease remains displaced. When the Shulchan Aruch says that there must be no stratagem, perhaps that rule differs before and after the fact. If, before the penalty was deposited, there had already been an effective acquisition of the land or lease, such as a kinyan sudar for a sale or a handshake or other situmta effective for a real-estate lease, then once the acquisition was complete the purchaser effectively acquired on behalf of the neighboring owner. A later penalty arrangement cannot undo the existing right based on 'you shall do what is upright and good.' Where the kinyan is not itself an act in the body of the property, the parties must still have been engaged in the matter when it was made. If the acquisition was already complete before the penalty and neither party could retract, the right is not displaced. But if it was made clear that the sale or lease was conditioned on the penalty arrangement, the transaction may take effect on that basis, for the earlier kinyan then existed only under that condition.
