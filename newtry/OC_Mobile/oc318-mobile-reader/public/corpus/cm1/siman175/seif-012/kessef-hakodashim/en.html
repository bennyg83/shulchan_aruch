One who sells to a non-Jew, and he is placed under a ban, through the end of seif 41. I was once asked about a person who wished to sell his house, located on a street among Jewish homes, to a non-Jew. He could not find a Jew there willing to pay its fair value and urgently needed the money. I initially answered that the sale was permitted, but instructed him not to act without the agreement of the neighboring Jewish owner, because the matter involved parties from another town and I did not wish initially to enter the dispute. Later both sides asked me to state my view. Even though the basic law is plainly permissive where one cannot sell to a Jew at the same price, as stated in siman 175, the case is even stronger in our type of houses. These houses do not open into one another, nor into a common alley or courtyard. Each opens separately onto the public street, each has clearly defined boundaries, and each has its own courtyard. The concern addressed by the law of selling adjoining land to a non-Jew therefore does not arise in the ordinary way. The Talmudic concern principally relates to fields that directly adjoin one another and where boundary encroachment, damage, and interference can readily occur. The reason we apply neighboring-owner law to houses is that adjoining houses can sometimes be combined and one's property expanded. But where each house is structurally and legally distinct, no comparable boundary intrusion follows merely from the identity of the owner. Nor does the remote possibility of fire create a claim. I have previously rejected an attempt to prevent construction near another house merely because close buildings might increase fire risk. Such construction occurs every day and is not treated as actionable damage. The harm is neither certain nor immediate, and is not even ordinary indirect causation, but a very remote contingency. Even after a fire, clearly defined foundations and legal boundaries remain. Under current governmental law there is likewise no plausible concern that ownership lines would thereby be altered. The Sema and the authorities cited by the Shach who discuss removing harm in the case of houses were speaking of houses opening to a common alley or otherwise creating concrete interference, visibility, encroachment, or similar harm. None of that was present here. Nor was there a legal objection based upon the sale of drinks in the two houses. Merchants, Jewish or otherwise, are generally free to call potential customers before they enter another merchant's premises. Once a customer is actually inside another's house, neither Jew nor non-Jew may properly remove him by force, but this does not create a special neighboring-owner claim. Likewise, concern that the non-Jew might lower prices does not establish actionable harm. Even among Jews each merchant ordinarily sets his own prices. I recall that Responsa Panim Meirot discusses this at length in connection with the Mishnah concerning a shopkeeper distributing nuts or roasted grain to children, where the Sages permit competition because the other merchant may respond in kind. I do not now recall his conclusion, but accepted practice is plainly permissive. There was also an outhouse situated between the two houses, whose walls were the courtyard walls. One could worry that the non-Jew might refuse to have his courtyard wall function as the wall of that structure, though in strict law he would acquire no greater right than the prior owner possessed. Even this possible harm was slight, because a small separate wall could be built at minimal expense. The seller later died. According to the conclusions of the Sema and Taz, the heirs and successors are not penalized for such matters even where the seller had expressly undertaken liability for resulting harm, and certainly not here where he made no such undertaking. In practice, with the help of Hashem, no harm arose from the ruling or from the eventual sale.
