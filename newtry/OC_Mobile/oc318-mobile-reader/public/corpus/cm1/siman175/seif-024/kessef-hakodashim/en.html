One who rented a house and then another acquired rights in it. Where a house was transferred under a conditional arrangement and the occupant remained there, the legal characterization depends upon whether he held an actual lease for a defined term or merely a right to remain until payment or notice. If the first purchaser could leave whenever he wished, or at least at the fixed payment dates, then after that point the continuation is ordinarily treated as an unspecified tenancy. Under siman 312, an unspecified tenancy may require thirty days' notice, but those thirty days do not necessarily have all the legal qualities of a fixed lease. Consequently, even if neighboring-owner law applies to a true lease, it is doubtful whether the mere notice period creates such a right. Where the parties expressly or implicitly continued the first rental arrangement, however, the continued occupancy follows its terms, including adjustment to current rental value. If they remained together longer than a month without a new agreement, the arrangement may become an ordinary month-to-month or indefinite tenancy with the standard notice requirement. Still, because those notice days differ from a true lease for other legal purposes, there is substantial room to say that they do not create a right of preemption. In any event, the existing purchaser is in possession and cannot be displaced on the basis of a doubtful extension of a rabbinic equitable enactment. If the first tenant sublets the premises, the subtenant actually residing there is certainly the present adjoining occupant. Whether the first tenant also retains preemption because his own possessory right will later return depends on whether the law looks only at the moment of sale or also at the eventual equitable benefit. The latter seems plausible because the entire institution is based on 'what is upright and good.' It may even follow that the original landlord has a future neighboring interest. Yet the order among these competing rights requires further study. If a person lives there rent-free under a loan for use for a fixed period, the same temporary-sale logic can apply, since the owner has merely waived rent. But if no term was set and the owner may remove him every day, the occupant does not acquire neighboring-owner status merely from long residence. If he claims that a year was granted to him, the circumstances may support his assertion, especially when such gratuitous occupation would not ordinarily be left wholly undefined. With a child living in a parent's property the inference may be weaker. If the arrangement is truly indefinite, perhaps the ordinary requirement of notice itself supplies some temporary stability, but that question is uncertain. Where the claimant lacks funds and another person offers money only on the express condition that the acquired property ultimately belong to that financier, the claimant's preemption is even more doubtful. He is not presently purchasing with his own funds, and the condition may make the financier the true acquirer. Since stratagems do not create neighboring-owner rights and the current purchaser has a completed sale, the claimant should not be able to defeat it merely by enjoying temporary fruits until the financing condition is resolved. The Shach's conclusion that an existing purchaser prevails where the very applicability of preemption is doubtful supports this. This is unlike a poor purchaser whose creditors may later collect, for that is a separate future contingency rather than a defect in the current purchase. According to the Taz, if a tenant is certainly a neighboring owner with respect to the house itself, a tenant of one partner's share may have preemption over the other partner's share. This may depend on the opening dispute in tractate Nedarim regarding partners' use, and according to the Ran's explanation that each partner's present use is treated as use of the whole, the tenant's right touches the subject of the sale itself. This requires study.
