There, in the gloss: According to the beit din's appraisal of the land. This ruling is very surprising, apart from the Sema's question as to why the land is appraised when the Rivash says that the debt itself is appraised at its market value. The reason is that the creditor should suffer no loss. Appraising only the land may cause him loss. This is obvious if the debtor owns other land but compromised with the creditor, who accepted this parcel for the debt because he particularly desired it and therefore waived part of his claim. The creditor could no longer recover the balance, since he settled. Even if this was the debtor's only land, a purchaser of the debt instrument would have paid at least the value of the land plus something more, because the debtor might later become wealthy or other assets might be found. This is included in the Rivash's statement that the debt is appraised at what it was worth, namely somewhat more than the land alone. The Sema asks that the seller will suffer loss by being discharged from the entire debt. I do not understand the question. The transaction between the seller and creditor remains valid, and the seller is discharged. The adjoining owner who seeks to remove the creditor must pay what the creditor gave, meaning the amount for which the debt could have been sold in the market, as though that sum had been paid in cash. This is explicit in the Rivash, who writes that the purchaser may be removed under the law of adjoining ownership for the amount the beit din appraises the debt to have been worth. The rule that one who buys for two hundred what is worth one hundred receives two hundred applies only where cash was actually paid. When land was acquired in exchange for a debt, it is like an exchange of land for movable property, whose value is appraised.
