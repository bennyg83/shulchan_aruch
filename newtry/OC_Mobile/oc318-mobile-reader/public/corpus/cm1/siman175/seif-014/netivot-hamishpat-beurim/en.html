And he removed the purchaser. See Shach, seif katan 15. The purchaser can sometimes say, "Do not remove me yet, because perhaps the husband or master will not consent." At first glance this means that the purchaser wishes to wait until the husband arrives. But if he does not wish to wait, perhaps he should be removed immediately, as in seif 37 regarding relatives. That is difficult. The purchaser could say, "Why should I accept this uncertainty? If the land later decreases in value, the husband may return it, but if it rises he may confirm the sale, while I will no longer wish to buy." Such an arrangement is also unfair to the seller, who would have to wait indefinitely. It therefore appears that relatives are different. They have their own money and can buy the property even if the neighboring owner ultimately does not consent. Removing the purchaser causes no disadvantage to the seller, because the relatives are definite buyers. We therefore presume that the neighboring owner will agree, since the transaction benefits him. A wife or slave, however, lacks independent funds and can buy only conditionally upon the husband's or master's later approval. If approval is withheld the seller must return the money. That is not fair or equitable to the seller, so the purchaser cannot be removed at all merely on that speculative basis.
