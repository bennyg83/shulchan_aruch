Rambam holds that whenever the holder of a time-limited right cannot be told during that period to dismantle or remove his property, he is treated as a partner and therefore as an adjoining owner. The Rosh and Tur hold that one who has no perpetual right in the property does not have the status of a partner. Yet regarding an ordinary lease the positions seem reversed: Rambam does not say that a lease for a day is considered a sale for purposes of neighboring-owner law, while the Rosh does. I answered, without lengthy review, that the distinction between a lease and a temporary proprietary acquisition is well known, as indicated at the end of siman 212:4. Even the Rosh concedes that an ordinary tenant cannot oust an adjoining owner or other purchaser who has already acquired. The dispute here appears to be that Rambam holds that a present, time-limited acquisition may, by virtue of its current reality, displace a later permanent acquisition, whereas the Rosh holds that a temporary acquisition cannot displace permanent ownership. Even if ownership of fruits can sometimes be considered ownership of the body, the owner of the underlying permanent title is not thereby displaced. When the Sema at the end of the siman says that according to the Rosh, a lease for a day is considered a sale and the adjoining owner may displace the tenant so that he himself may rent, the meaning is that if a lease were not classified as a kind of sale, the entire enactment of 'you shall do what is upright and good' would not apply to leasing. Since neighboring-owner law is a rabbinic equitable enactment framed in terms of sale, doubt concerning its scope is interpreted narrowly. But according to the Rosh, who classifies temporary leasing as sale for this purpose, leasing falls within the enactment. Elsewhere I wrote that the principle that a lease for a day is a sale also clarifies the customary mechanism of certain temporary sales. This is especially helpful for the ruling I heard that if a house sold under such a temporary arrangement burns, the original owner repays the principal. Rambam's view may fit this even better. Although the Shach in siman 313 distinguishes among applications of the rule that leasing is a sale, a true temporary sale is stronger. The Tzemach Tzedek's ruling concerning a customary temporary sale, that the property may be compelled to be redeemed according to the accepted practice, is likewise easier to understand if a temporary sale cannot confer greater power than the permanent title held by the original owner. I have also written elsewhere that this type of arrangement between two Jews does not involve prohibited subterfuge in a Torah matter, because there is a presumption that a person does not abandon what is permitted and choose what is prohibited. Both Jewish parties genuinely intend a temporary sale. This differs from sales to a non-Jew where the non-Jew's conduct may reveal that he has no real intention to acquire, as discussed regarding chametz. The same concerns of subterfuge can arise in sales for firstborn-animal issues or castration, although those cases contain other mitigating factors, including that Torah law does not recognize agency for a non-Jew. In a pressing situation there may even be room to distinguish a sale of animals to a non-Jew who ordinarily buys animals, because his intent to acquire is not visibly absent. Nevertheless, with chametz there remains a special concern about contrivance in a Torah prohibition, as discussed by the Bechor Shor. Between two Jews, however, where both fully intend the temporary transfer in order to avoid prohibition, the arrangement is not merely a sham. I was asked further about the distinction I drew between ordinary neighboring ownership and a right in the very property itself. Here the holder of trees has a right in the actual location, yet according to the Rosh no right of preemption results. I answered quickly that the place occupied by the trees may be secondary to the field as a whole, and legal classification can follow the principal object. This may work for trees in a field, but in a house the matter is less clear. Still, according to the Rosh it seems that a temporary lease cannot defeat a permanent sale. This requires further study.
