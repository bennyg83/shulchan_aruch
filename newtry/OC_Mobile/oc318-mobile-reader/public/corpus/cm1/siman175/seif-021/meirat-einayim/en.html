The neighboring owner's claim is not against the purchaser at all. The Mechaber added the word "at all," which the Tur does not have, because he follows what he wrote in the Beit Yosef. In his understanding, the Rambam holds that where the seller has property from which to repay the purchaser, the neighboring owner gives the purchase money directly to the purchaser, and the purchaser then returns against the seller. The seller cannot say to the neighboring owner, "You are not my litigant," because the neighboring owner is effectively taking over the transaction. The Ramban disagrees and holds that even where the seller has assets and the purchaser will suffer no loss, the purchaser need not trouble himself to sue the seller. According to this framework, Rama's conclusion, "and so appears primary," follows because we rule like the Rosh that witnesses who saw the transaction may write a deed in the seller's name directly to the neighboring owner. If so, the seller plainly cannot say to the neighboring owner, "You are not my litigant." Even if no new deed was actually written, the neighboring owner remains the seller's litigant because it could have been written to him. Examine this carefully.
