From Terumat HaDeshen, siman 340, he infers that the purchaser defeats the adjoining owner only where it is not certain that the purchaser can obtain another suitable house free of an adjoining-owner claim. Thus Rama's phrase that he "cannot find another house" need not mean absolute impossibility; even a genuine doubt whether another available house exists may suffice, at least according to the first opinion and perhaps according to all. He further argues that the rule that the adjoining owner prevails where the purchaser can certainly obtain another house, even if that house is small and cramped, may apply specifically where the purchaser already owns a house that is merely too small. In that setting, another modest house may combine with his existing one to provide adequate residence. But where the purchaser owns no house at all, perhaps all authorities agree that bar metzra does not displace him even if he could certainly buy another house that is too small for his needs. On this basis he rules in a case where Reuven bought a synagogue seat from Shimon for money and Levi, who owned the adjoining seat, asserted bar metzra. Reuven had no synagogue seat at all, and it appeared unlikely that he could find another seat of comparable quality. The purchaser prevails. Every synagogue seat is itself adjacent to someone, and at best Reuven might later find a place whose adjoining owner waives his right. That uncertainty is enough to protect the purchaser, especially where he presently owns no seat. There is also room to combine the Raavad's view that bar metzra may not apply to synagogue seats at all. He considers whether Reuven had yet acquired the seat because he paid only money and in places where deeds are normally written, land is not fully acquired by money until the deed is written. If so, perhaps Levi should be treated as competing initially for the property rather than seeking to remove an established purchaser. He answers that even where money alone does not complete the rabbinic form of acquisition, the transaction has Torah-level force and withdrawal may entail the sanction of mi shepara according to the cited authorities. Once Reuven has made such an acquisition, the adjoining owner no longer precedes him. In addition, local custom regarding synagogue seats may treat entry in the communal table as merely evidentiary rather than as an indispensable deed of acquisition. He therefore rules for Reuven.
