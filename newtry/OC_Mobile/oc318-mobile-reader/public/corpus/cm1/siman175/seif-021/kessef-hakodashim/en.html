In all types of land. I am uncertain whether neighboring-owner law applies to the lease of village mills. Yesterday, because I was distracted, I initially erred and said that it does. The questioners have already left, but with Hashem's help I sent word that they should disregard what they heard, for I had not issued a final ruling. I am now uncertain even where two mills stand immediately adjacent to one another. Since the neighboring-owner principle in houses is based upon the possibility of expanding one's boundary, and a mill does not readily serve that purpose, perhaps the rule should not apply. On balance, however, because we have accepted that neighboring-owner law applies to houses, there may be no distinction among kinds of buildings. Even though the income of a mill is not essentially dependent on the building, and some mills are movable, a mill housed in a building may still fall within the rule. There is also an independent possibility that neighboring status arises from the mill business itself. Grain is sometimes brought from one mill to another, and if so physical intervening buildings might not break the commercial adjacency between them. One could even suggest that mills in two neighboring villages, so long as no other mill intervenes, might be treated as adjacent because customers commonly bring grain from one village to the other. The existence of a non-Jewish mill between them may not constitute the 'lion at the boundary' discussed in seif 39, because competition for customers is not itself the sort of destructive interference that creates that rule. Nevertheless, my inclination is that at a substantial distance no right of preemption exists. Since extending neighboring-owner law to buildings is itself an expansion, we should not add further extensions without clear basis.
