Hagahot Ashri records a case in which the steward of Bar Ziza deposited a litra of gold and both Bar Ziza and the steward died, leaving Bar Ziza adult and minor children. Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yosei directed half to the adult children and half to remain until the minors matured. After his death, Rabbi Chiya ruled that the remaining half should go to the steward's children, while what the adult Bar Ziza children had already received would not be taken from them because they had received it through a judicial ruling. Shach treats the question as a genuine dispute and says that if the minors seize, we do not remove it. I find this difficult in light of the rule that a partner may divide for himself where the joint debt is doubtful or endangered. Here, by the very fact that beit din was uncertain to whom the money belonged, there can hardly be a more impaired claim. Why could the adult heirs not say, "I am saving my own share"? The answer is that the first judicial ruling was an error in legal judgment, not an unresolved legal doubt. In such a case seizure does not ordinarily help against the settled law. The judgment became final only to the extent actually carried out, namely the half delivered to the adults. Once that half had been given, the remaining dispute concerned only the other half. A partner who faces an impaired half claim cannot take the entire remaining fund as "his share," because he would thereby take more than the amount corresponding to his own uncertain interest. If, however, the original issue were a genuine unresolved halachic doubt and one heir seized only the amount of his own claimed share, the others could not necessarily seize it back. A true doubt makes the joint claim itself impaired, and the partner can then say, "I save my own."
