If the adjoining owner desires it. See Shaar Mishpat below, seif 42. After defending the Shach's rule concerning one who moves his residence, he asks why a purchaser who buys land worth one hundred for two hundred may still be displaced by the adjoining owner if the latter pays the full two hundred. Why is that not like a sale in circumstances in which compelling the seller to wait for the adjoining owner may cause the unusual purchaser to withdraw, thereby harming the seller? He answers that although land is not subject to the ordinary restitution rules of onaah, it is still forbidden initially to deceive another in price, and according to some authorities the prohibition is a full Torah prohibition even though the excess is not recoverable. The sages did not suspend bar metzra in order to protect a seller's opportunity to commit such a wrong. If, however, the purchaser knows the true value and voluntarily pays two hundred because the land is especially dear to him, the adjoining owner cannot remove him even by offering the same amount, because such a buyer is unusual and forcing delay would materially endanger the seller's transaction. Likewise, if a purchaser buys land without warranty, bar metzra should not apply even if the adjoining owner also offers to buy without warranty. A buyer willing to take land without warranty is uncommon, as reflected in the presumption that omission of warranty is generally a scribal error. Requiring the seller to notify the adjoining owner may therefore cause the unusual purchaser to withdraw, which is a hardship the bar metzra enactment was not intended to impose. Shaar Mishpat cites a responsum of Rashba in support and treats this as the principal rule.
