Seif 9: If the adjoining owner desires. Rabbeinu explains that one might have thought the sale is void because the purchaser, who acts as the adjoining owner's agent, distorted his mandate. The ruling teaches that this is not considered a distortion. He derives this from the Gemara's case in which land worth one hundred was bought for two hundred. The scholars initially thought the adjoining owner could say, “I sent you to improve matters, not to damage them,” but Nehardea said that there is no law of overreaching for land. The Rosh explains that the purchaser wished to return the land and void the sale. This is difficult, for according to the scholars' initial assumption that overreaching applies, the purchaser himself could rescind because he was overcharged. Were it not for Rabbeinu and the Rosh, I would explain that the scholars thought the purchaser must reimburse the adjoining owner for the loss, as one who damages the field is liable below in seif 6. Their claim was: you were sent to improve matters, yet you caused damage and must pay. The Rambam's wording supports this, for he says that the adjoining owner cannot remove the purchaser until he pays two hundred. On this approach, the scholars already knew that overreaching does not apply to land. They thought, however, that the purchaser is treated as the seller's litigant and must bear the extra one hundred personally, while the adjoining owner takes the land for one hundred. Rav Nachman therefore taught that even here there is no overreaching, and the adjoining owner may take the land only for two hundred. According to Rabbeinu and the Rosh, the matter requires study. Since there is no overreaching for land, why would they think the sale void? Every purchaser buys conditionally: if the adjoining owner wishes, he takes it, and otherwise the purchaser keeps it. Since here the adjoining owner will not take it at that price, the purchaser never became his agent and must pay the full two hundred.
