The law of a neighboring owner. The general reason for the law of bar metzra in this siman is the Torah's command, "You shall do what is good and upright." From it we learn that people should conduct themselves toward one another beyond the strict letter of the law. Thus, although a purchaser acted first and legally bought a field, since he can generally buy elsewhere while the owners of the adjoining fields gain a particular benefit by joining the purchased field to their own, the Sages enacted that an adjoining owner may displace the purchaser. They are called neighboring owners because their properties stand upon the boundary of the field sold. The Tur and Mechaber begin with one of several brothers or partners who sold his share. It is obvious that where two brothers or partners own one field and one sells his share to a third party, the remaining partner or brother may displace the buyer, for upon division his portion would adjoin the sold share. The novelty is that even where there are three or four partners or brothers and one sells his share, any one of the remaining partners may displace the buyer. The purchaser cannot say, "Perhaps when you divide, your portion will fall far from the share I bought and you will not actually be a neighbor." So long as they have not divided, each partner is regarded as having a portion throughout the entire field and therefore has the status of a neighbor everywhere. More than that, even if the outside purchaser himself owns an adjoining field, the partner may displace him, because a partner has a stronger position than an ordinary neighboring owner. So long as the partnership has not been divided, each partner has a share in the whole, and the selling partner is considered as having sold part of the other partner's own interest. It is surprising that the Mechaber introduces this law as "some say," because I found no authority who disputes it.
