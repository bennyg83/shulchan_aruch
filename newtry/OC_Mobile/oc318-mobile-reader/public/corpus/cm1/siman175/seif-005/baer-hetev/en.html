To the purchaser. The general reason for the law of adjoining landowners in this siman is derived from the verse, 'You shall do what is good and upright.' From this we learn that the Torah desires people to act toward one another beyond the strict letter of the law. Thus, although a purchaser already bought a field, since he can buy elsewhere while owners of neighboring fields derive particular benefit from acquiring it so their fields are contiguous, the Sages enacted that one of those neighboring owners may remove the purchaser. They are called metzranim because their fields border the sold field. The Tur and Mechaber therefore begin the law with one of the brothers. Thus far the Sema. See Responsa R. Aharon Sasson, siman 89; Responsa Mahari ben Lev, part 3, siman 41 and part 4, folio 63; Responsa Raanach, simanim 82, 106, 114, 119, and 125; and Responsa R. Moshe Alshich, siman 148.
Removes him. Even if the neighboring partner already purchased the field, he may remove the first purchaser. This excludes Rashi's view, cited by the Rosh, that only if both arrive together to buy does the partner have priority, but once the other has purchased he cannot remove him. The Rosh does not rule that way. This resolves the Sema's question why the Mechaber states this law in the name of 'some say' when no dissenting view appears. So it seems to me. A case came before us where one person swore to sell his land to another, and then an adjoining landowner came seeking to displace the buyer. The seller argued that since the adjoining owner wanted it, he would not sell at all, for his oath was only to the other person. We ruled that since no kinyan had yet been made, the seller could retract and retain the land. See Responsa Maharashdam, siman 299. Shach. Whether a family member may displace a purchaser, see Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 2, siman 161.
