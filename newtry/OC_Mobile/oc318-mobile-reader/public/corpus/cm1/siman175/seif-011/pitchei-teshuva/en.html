The seller reconsidered. See Be'er HaGolah, and see Chemdat Shlomo, siman 8. Even if the adjoining owner initially said that he did not want the property and the seller therefore sold to another, if the seller later remains willing to sell to the adjoining owner, the adjoining owner may still displace the purchaser. The rule that the adjoining owner loses priority after the seller has had to trouble himself to obtain money or another buyer is a protection for the seller. Where the seller expressly waives that protection and is content to deal with the adjoining owner, the purchaser cannot invoke it for himself. The same applies even though, unlike cases where the difficulty arose after the sale, here one might argue that the adjoining owner's right had already disappeared before the purchaser bought. The law concerning a seller who sells all his properties to one purchaser shows otherwise. That exception is also for the seller's benefit, yet according to the cited authorities, if the seller is willing to deal separately with an adjoining owner, the adjoining owner may remove the purchaser from the relevant parcel. Thus even a purchaser who originally acquired without a then-enforceable bar metzra defense can later be displaced when the reason for the exception belonged solely to the seller and the seller waives it. Chemdat Shlomo applied this in practice to a lease of a house and shop. He adds that since bar metzra rests on the duty to do what is right and good, and the second tenant did not act with intent to encroach on the first, fairness and peace require reimbursing the second tenant for expenses he reasonably incurred. Pitchei Teshuva notes that this may in fact be strict law because the second is treated as the first tenant's agent for relevant purposes, unless Chemdat Shlomo meant expenses exceeding the amount legally recoverable.
