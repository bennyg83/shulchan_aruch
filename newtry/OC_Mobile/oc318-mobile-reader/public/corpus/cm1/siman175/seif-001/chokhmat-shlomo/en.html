Seif 31: If the seller consulted the adjoining owner, etc. Note: A case came before me in which a person had rented a shop. Another prospective tenant offered the owner a higher rent. The owner informed the existing tenant of the offer and gave him the opportunity to match it. The existing tenant declined and told him to rent it to the other person. The owner then contracted with the new tenant. No cash deposit was paid, only collateral was given and a formal handshake undertaking was made. The former tenant then changed his mind and asserted the right of an adjoining holder, offering the same rent. Because whether the law of bar metzra applies to rentals is disputed, and the owner was indifferent which tenant remained, there is reason to preserve the existing tenant's position under the principle that property should remain where it already stands. The difficulty was that the owner had already consulted him and he had declined, which ordinarily forfeits the right of bar metzra. Nevertheless, I ruled that he retains priority here. The new tenant gave no money, only collateral, and we rule that where there is no principal obligation, the collateral itself creates none. Thus there was no acquisition by money, only possible situmta. Even if situmta can generate the curse of “He Who exacted punishment” when one retracts, the Bach cited by the Shach states that the commercial custom of situmta applies to movables, not land. At minimum, the new tenant had not yet acquired the lease. Before the buyer acquires, the adjoining claimant may retract his prior waiver and assert his priority. The reason he cannot do so after acquisition is that the purchaser cannot be forced retroactively to become his agent, but before acquisition that concern does not arise. Even if one assumes that situmta in land can create only the obligation of “He Who exacted punishment,” that sanction does not apply here, because the owner is not retracting voluntarily. The halachic law of bar metzra itself requires that the adjoining claimant be preferred. One who changes course because Torah law commands “You shall do what is right and good” is not subject to that sanction. I therefore ruled that the former tenant has priority. I have also written elsewhere that an adjoining claimant who cannot purchase alone may take a partner, and the original purchaser cannot insist that he himself become that partner, because the adjoining claimant may say that the proposed partner is easier for him to deal with. I further ruled, together with distinguished elder rabbis in my youth, that even with a tavern or similar business a partner who personally uses the jointly held premises may say, “I used my own share.” If, however, he rents the premises to an outsider and receives rent, that rental income belongs to both partners, because it is profit produced directly by the jointly owned property. By contrast, profit from a business conducted there is not fruit of the land itself. It is profit from one's labor and commerce, and the premises merely provide the location. In that case the partner may say that he used his own share. If before renting he expressly states that he rents only his own share and that the rent is to belong to him alone, perhaps even rental income would be his; this requires study. In any event, where he personally occupies the property, no amount is deducted from his share for such use.
