With delay there is no such rule, because the seller is not particular. If the seller says that the adjoining owner and the purchaser are equally acceptable to him, the law of a neighboring owner applies even if he already rented or sold to the other party after the neighboring owner said that he too wished to buy at that price. Perhaps, however, if the seller says that at the time of sale the purchaser was easier for him to deal with and the adjoining owner was difficult to collect from, and only now does he prefer the adjoining owner, once the right of preemption was displaced for a substantive reason it does not revive. If afterward the adjoining owner gives collateral or produces a reliable guarantor, one may say that the right was never displaced in a case where such security was contemplated from the outset. But if at the time of sale he was unable to obtain a guarantor or collateral, he is no better than one whose money was not immediately available without delay. If he could have obtained such security but the seller never explained that this was his concern, the adjoining owner's right remains strong. It also appears that no binding acceptance is created merely by asking, 'Perhaps the two of us are equally acceptable to you.' Even where the seller asked another party for a higher price in order to raise the price and then sold or rented to him because he was embarrassed to reject the person who had raised the bid, there is, by reason and common practice, no binding acceptance that defeats the neighboring owner, for the seller remained free to choose. If a neighboring owner was rejected because the seller said, 'This purchaser is more convenient for me,' and beit din issued a final ruling in the form of 'go and give,' he cannot later retract and say that the adjoining owner is equally convenient. If the ruling was only 'you are obligated to give,' he may retract, and the right of preemption then applies retroactively once the landlord is appeased, even after considerable time. Likewise, where the tenant promised the adjoining owner a partnership and later retracted, the adjoining owner's right may revive even after a substantial period, so long as the adjoining owner had relied upon the promised partnership. The same applies where another defined benefit was promised and then withdrawn. In leases of an estate or revenue concession, many considerations may justify a seller saying, 'This person is preferable to me,' because of possible concerns involving the local ruler and governmental authority. If, however, both parties are equally acceptable to him, the adjoining owner's right remains.
