A:
With houses. It appears to me that if the houses are not actually adjacent, but there is a small public walkway between them, the law of the adjoining owner does not apply. This is also implied by the Beit Yosef according to the Raavad's reason that the law applies to houses because it is customary to connect them by doorways, which is not applicable here. Taz.
In places. The Tur writes: Regarding seats in a synagogue, the Raavad writes that strictly by law the right of the adjoining owner applies because they are like land, although this is not intuitively compelling. The Sema writes that he copied all of this in order to combine these considerations. This follows the Rashba, who considered joining Rabbeinu Tam's opinion, as cited by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe. The Shach writes that the Rashba's words are not proof, because he did not state that the widespread custom was contrary to Rabbeinu Tam, and in his time there were not yet so many authorities who disagreed with Rabbeinu Tam. Therefore, the primary ruling appears to be that of Terumat HaDeshen, siman 338, a later authority cited by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe: Rabbeinu Tam's opinion should not be combined, since all the leading authorities rejected it entirely and the accepted custom does not follow it. The Rashba's words also imply that he combined Rabbeinu Tam's view only for theoretical discussion, not as practical halachah. Shach.
The matter. There are additional distinctions concerning the law of the adjoining owner with respect to synagogue seats, recorded by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, which the Mechaber and Rama omitted. See there. Sema.

It appears. This follows what the Darkei Moshe writes, that the Beit Yosef's words do not necessarily refute Maharik. In my humble opinion, however, the Beit Yosef is correct. Maharik derived his view only from an implication in the Rambam, while in truth no such implication is evident. We therefore rule that the law of the adjoining owner does not apply to movable property at all. Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 144, writes that if a person wishes to sell his promissory note to another for a discount before maturity, the debtor can compel him to sell it to the debtor for that amount. He brings a proof from here, but it is no proof. His other proofs there are likewise inconclusive. Reason itself indicates that the debtor cannot compel him, because the creditor can say: I wish to benefit that other person, not you. See Responsa Maharam Alshakar, siman 193, and Responsa Ibn Lev, book 4, page 63. Shach.
