In all land in the world, etc. The Tur cites Rabbi Yehudah Barcelona: the law of bar metzra applies to all real property, such as a bathhouse, olive press, dovecotes, towers, fields, vineyards, irrigated fields, cisterns, pits, caves, and the like, and to anything attached to land. The general rule is that anything in which chazakah applies is subject to bar metzra, except slaves, who can be subject to chazakah but not to bar metzra. See the Perishah.
But movable property and slaves are not subject to bar metzra. Meaning, if one owns movable property or a slave jointly with another and the partner wishes to sell his share to a third party, the co-owner cannot remove the purchaser. Chazal enacted bar metzra specifically for land, which is a permanent acquisition, under the principle 'you shall do what is upright and good,' not for these items. However, if the co-owner has a genuine objection to the particular purchaser, Rama concludes at the end of the gloss that if beit din finds substance in his claim they may listen to him and remove the purchaser.
The law of bar metzra applies to houses. This excludes Rabbeinu Tam's position that the enactment applies only to fields, because fields are readily available elsewhere, whereas houses are not as readily found. He also reasons that a wealthy person may need many fields and the purchaser has preempted him, whereas a wealthy person already having a fine residence who seeks to remove a purchaser merely to rent the additional house has less equitable claim. Furthermore, a rock or row of palms separating fields ends bar metzra, while houses too are separated by walls. The Rosh comments that these arguments are reasonable, but the practice is not so. The Tur cites this and then Rabbi Yehudah Barcelona's general rule that anything subject to chazakah is subject to bar metzra. The reason is that the dividing wall between houses can be opened, allowing movement from one to the other.
The same applies to seats in the synagogue, etc. The Tur writes that Raavad says strictly speaking bar metzra applies to synagogue seats because they are like land, since attached property is treated as land, although the result may seem counterintuitive. Still, if a bench is crowded and one member wishes to sell his place, he must sell it to the other members of that bench. Beit Yosef explains Raavad's observation that the rule may seem counterintuitive because one person ordinarily needs only one synagogue seat. I have quoted Rabbeinu Tam and Raavad at length to teach that in other cases, if the judge sees a strong equitable reason not to remove the purchaser, these considerations can be joined to what appears upright and good in the particular case. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe record additional distinctions regarding synagogue places that the Mechaber and Rama omit. Rivash writes that if a dividing wall between two seats makes it impossible to join them, there is no bar metzra because their use is unequal, one being higher than the other. See also Responsa Rashba, siman 1022.
