The same applies to seats in the synagogue. See the Sema, seif katan 99, who cites the Tur and the Raavad that, as a matter of law, the right of the adjoining owner applies to synagogue seats, although this may appear counterintuitive. The Sema copied the views of Rabbeinu Tam and the Raavad in order to teach that, in another case where the dayan perceives a fair and proper reason not to remove the purchaser, those views may be combined. This follows the Rashba, who considered combining Rabbeinu Tam's position, as cited by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe. In my view, however, no proof may be drawn from the Rashba. He did not write that the settled custom rejected Rabbeinu Tam, nor were there in his time so many authorities who disagreed. The principal ruling is therefore that of the Terumat HaDeshen, siman 338, the later authority cited by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, that Rabbeinu Tam's position should not be combined, since all the leading authorities entirely rejected it and the universal custom does not follow it. The Rashba likewise appears to have mentioned Rabbeinu Tam only for dialectical discussion, not as a practical ruling.

Maharik derived his position only from an implication in the Rambam, but the Rambam implies nothing of the kind. We therefore rule that the law of the adjoining owner does not apply at all to movable property. Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 144, writes that when a creditor wishes before maturity to sell his promissory note to another at a discount, the debtor may compel him to sell it to the debtor for that same amount. He adduces proof from here, but it is no proof. His other proofs likewise fail, as I have written in my work. Reason itself indicates that the debtor cannot compel him, for the creditor may say, 'I wish to benefit that person and not you.'
