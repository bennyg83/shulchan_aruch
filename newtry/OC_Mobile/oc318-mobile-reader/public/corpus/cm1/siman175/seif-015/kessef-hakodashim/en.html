Peace. May peace and blessing come to my beloved son-in-law, dear to me as my own soul, the outstanding rabbi, sharp and expert, renowned in Torah, our teacher Rabbi Avraham David, may his light shine forever. Your honored letter reached me. You asked that I inform you of the facts of the matter about which I wrote last year to the distinguished philanthropist Rabbi Mordechai of Lantshin, telling him not to sell his house here to a certain known non-Jew. The substance of what I wrote was that the sale would create a stumbling block and serious obstacle for the two Jewish houses sanctified for Heaven, namely the synagogue and beit midrash here, because of several activities forbidden by Torah law that the non-Jew, acting by force, would perform. In addition, the non-Jew raised his offer because he intended to profit by forcing the owners of the small rear dwellings there to sell him those units for half their value, since they would be unable to bear living beside him because of the unlawful harms he would cause. He also intended to annul their rights in the local common structure. In short, it was evident that the non-Jew's plan was to drive those owners from their holdings and dispossess them. Beyond all this, it was clearly known that the non-Jew was a tanner by trade and that the smell from spreading hides during processing is intolerable even at some distance. Under strict law neighbors may prevent a sale to such a person; see the beginning of siman 156. It was also certain that he would spread the hides in the open area between the synagogue and that house, because no other nearby area was available. That was the place where the community gathered for Kiddush Levanah and for wedding canopies, and there was serious reason to fear that prayer in the synagogue might become forbidden while those hides were being processed. All these circumstances were seen personally by the emissaries of Rabbi Mordechai, Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yosef, when they were here. They said that certainly Rabbi Mordechai would never sell to that non-Jew even if avoidance of the sale were merely beyond the letter of the law, all the more so after hearing from me that strict law prohibited it. That was last year's case. Anyone can therefore see that there is no basis to criticize your ruling in the present matter, which is entirely different. Those who asserted that when Shulchan Aruch, Choshen Mishpat 175, says that if we see that the non-Jew intends to destroy a Jew's property, the rule applies even where the non-Jew would merely exercise rights that Torah law itself permits, and who criticized you for rejecting that reading, have misunderstood even the plain language. The Shulchan Aruch first says that the Jewish seller is placed under a ban until he accepts liability according to Jewish law, and if the non-Jew then coerces the neighbor in a manner not authorized by Jewish law, the seller must pay. It then limits this where the seller could have sold to a Jew for the same price, and finally says that if we see that the non-Jew intends to destroy the neighbor's property, everything depends upon the judges' evaluation. Plainly, that final clause restores the original liability for harm the non-Jew inflicts contrary to Jewish law. I cannot understand how anyone could interpret it as referring to conduct that Jewish law itself permits. The Rosh similarly uses the expression that the non-Jew intends to drive the Jew from his inheritance. Many arguments from reason and the authorities refute the distorted interpretation, and I see no need to lengthen the matter. You initially answered the seller when he asked only the legal question of permitted and forbidden, and afterward, when the opposing party complained by letter, you cautioned the seller to avoid causing harm as much as possible. No complaint can therefore fairly be directed against you except by someone searching for an excuse to criticize. I am surprised that you trouble yourself to answer such foolish objections, especially since in my view they originate with the familiar confused individual. I have no desire to dwell upon his learning or conduct. Because I am heavily occupied, I have written this hurriedly on reused paper. Peace from the Master of peace. From your father-in-law, who seeks your welfare with enduring affection, the humble Tzvi Hirsch, rabbi of the holy community of Buczacz.
