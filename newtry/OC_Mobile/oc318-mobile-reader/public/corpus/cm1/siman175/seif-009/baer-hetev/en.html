And he takes. Regarding what is stated in seif 54, that if the purchaser says he paid two hundred for property worth one hundred he is not believed, see the Beit Yosef, who distinguishes between the cases, and his distinction is sound. The Emunat Omen, page 92a, writes that here the case is one in which the purchaser expressly accepted liability for two hundred and the adjoining owner claims that they colluded. Shach.
With certainty. At first glance, even in the first case, where there are no witnesses, it would seem that the adjoining owner must claim with certainty that there was collusion; otherwise the Sages would not have instituted an oath merely because of a doubt. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch state this here to teach that although witnesses testify that the purchaser paid two hundred, and one might have thought that the adjoining owner could not impose an oath even after removing him, they teach otherwise. This is not conclusive, however. It may be that specifically where the purchaser has witnesses, he first receives the entire sum without an oath, and only afterward may the adjoining owner require him to take a heset oath. A heset oath was instituted only for a definite claim, as explained in siman 75 and elsewhere, and therefore they specify a certain claim here. In the first case, however, where there are no witnesses and the purchaser seeks to collect double the apparent value, contrary to ordinary valuation and purchasing practice, perhaps even without a definite claim the Sages required him to swear before extracting so much from the adjoining owner. This resembles the oath with a sacred object instituted for partners, sharecroppers, and the like even without a definite claim, because they may rationalize taking for themselves. The same concern may apply to this purchaser, since he purchased first and wishes to retain the field. So writes the Sema. The Taz likewise writes that the Maggid Mishneh was uncertain whether an oath is imposed even on a doubtful claim because there are grounds for suspicion, and this appears primary, since the Rama already ruled in siman 75, seif 3, that wherever there are grounds for suspicion an oath may be imposed. The Shach writes, however, that the Beit Yosef and Maggid Mishneh agree that the oath is imposed only on a definite claim, and this appears correct.
Appraisal. See the Sema, who leaves this ruling requiring study. The Shach also argues at length against the Rama, who derived it from the Rivash, because that is not the Rivash's intent. He concludes that if the creditor comes to collect the land and his note of indebtedness remains in force, the adjoining owner cannot remove him until he pays the amount of the creditor's debt, as the Rivash writes. This is the primary ruling. The Taz likewise writes that this ruling is exceedingly difficult; see there.
