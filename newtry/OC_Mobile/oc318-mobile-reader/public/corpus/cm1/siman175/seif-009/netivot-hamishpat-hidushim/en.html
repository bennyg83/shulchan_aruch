He purchased it for two hundred although it is worth only one hundred. "One hundred" is not literal, but means only slightly more than one hundred, because according to those authorities who hold that land is subject to ona'ah up to one half, a discrepancy of a full half allows rescission of the sale. According to beit din's appraisal of the land. Although he bought it for two hundred and it is worth only one hundred, he must pay two hundred. That case differs because the creditor actually paid cash, whereas here the property is worth only one hundred. There is a difficulty, however. The Rivash discusses orphans whose father left only this parcel and whose debt is not worth more than the land. That differs from a living borrower, where the creditor says that he accepts the land in satisfaction of the entire debt and the seller borrower loses if the adjacent owner takes it only at market value. This resembles the Mechaber's ruling in siman 114 that if the creditor says, "I will accept the land for my entire debt," we do not listen to the purchaser. One can answer that the case is where the creditor did not initially state that he accepts the land for the entire debt. The Shach rules that so long as the promissory note remains in the creditor's possession, the adjacent owner certainly has no greater right than if he himself were the purchaser, from whom the creditor could collect and say, "To me it is worth my entire debt," according to many authorities, as explained in siman 115. But if the creditor already accepted the field and tore up the note, then when the adjacent owner later comes the creditor can no longer say, "It is worth the whole debt to me," because he no longer holds the note. This resembles exchanging land for movables. However, the Shach's statement that if the promissory note is worth less than the land the adjacent owner need pay only the value of the debt is difficult in my eyes. Since the borrower's land is worth more, the reason the debt itself is valued lower must be the borrower's difficulty or inability to pay. Once the creditor has already collected and exerted himself, it is as though the debt is worth more, because at the moment of collection it is worth the full appraised value of the land.
