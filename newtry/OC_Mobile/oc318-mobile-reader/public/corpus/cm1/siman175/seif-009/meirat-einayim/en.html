He bought it for two hundred although it is worth only one hundred, etc. The Tur concludes: we do not say that the adjoining owner can tell him, 'You were my agent, and I sent you to improve matters for me, not to harm me,' so that the sale should be void, as with an agent who erred, as explained below in siman 182. This is not considered distortion, because a person sometimes purchases land for far more than its market value because he desires it. Therefore, if the adjoining owner wants the property he must pay the full two hundred. See the Perishah, where I explained this. I also wrote there that the phrase 'he bought it for two hundred although it is worth only one hundred' is the Gemara's wording. According to Tosafos, Rosh, and Tur, who hold that a disparity of double voids even a land sale, as the Tur writes in their name at the end of siman 66 and in siman 227, the Gemara's 'two hundred' cannot be literal and must mean somewhat less than two hundred. So it is printed in accurate Tur editions. The Mechaber follows Rambam and Rif, who hold that land is not subject to ona'ah even beyond double, and therefore uses the literal wording. Rama there cites the dissenting view. See what I wrote in Sema there explaining the Mechaber and Rama.
And he knows with certainty, etc. At first glance, even in the opening case, where there are no witnesses, the adjoining owner must be claiming with certainty that there was collusion. Otherwise the Sages would not have instituted an oath on a doubtful claim. The Tur and Mechaber may specify here that he knows with certainty to teach that even where witnesses are present testifying that the purchaser actually paid two hundred, one might have thought the adjoining owner could not require an oath even after removing the purchaser. The ruling teaches otherwise. However, this is not conclusive. One can say that specifically where the purchaser has witnesses, he first takes the entire amount without an oath, and only afterward may the adjoining owner sue him and impose a hesset oath. It has already been explained in siman 75 and elsewhere that hesset was instituted only on a definite claim. Therefore they specify that here the adjoining owner asserts certainty. In the opening clause, by contrast, where there are no witnesses, the law may operate differently.
According to the appraisal of beit din. It is common for a creditor to accept movable property, and all the more so land, at more than its market value in satisfaction of a debt. This is unlike the preceding case of land worth one hundred purchased for two hundred, where cash was actually paid and we therefore apply the rule that land is not subject to ona'ah, as Rivash writes in siman 483. However, I find this difficult, because Rivash appears to have said this only in the case before him, where the borrower had already died and left no property other than this land, so the creditor had no other place from which to collect. Where the borrower is still alive, it benefits him as seller for the creditor to retain land collected in satisfaction of the debt, because this discharges the entire debt. The Tur and Mechaber already explain in siman 114 that if the creditor says, 'I will take the land for my entire debt without an appraisal,' while the purchaser demands appraisal, we do not listen to the purchaser for the reason just stated. One can answer with some difficulty that here the creditor initially collected the land through a beit din appraisal and did not say he wished to take it for the entire debt. He therefore receives only according to that appraisal. The matter requires study.
