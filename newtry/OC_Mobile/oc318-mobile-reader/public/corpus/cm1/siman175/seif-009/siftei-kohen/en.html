Agudat Ezov, page 92a, writes that here the purchaser expressly accepted liability through a formal act of acquisition, and claims that the parties colluded against him.
And he knows with certainty, etc. See Sema, seif katan 15, through his statement that this is not conclusive. The Beit Yosef and Maggid Mishneh agree that he may impose the oath only when asserting a definite claim, and this appears correct.
He collected it for his debt, etc., according to the beit din appraisal of the land. In my humble opinion, the words of the Rama and Sema here are incorrect, for what relevance does the appraisal of the land have? The Rivash rules explicitly that even where a purchaser already bought land and possesses it, and a creditor comes to seize it, if the land is worth less than the full debt the purchaser cannot remove the creditor by paying only the appraised value. The creditor may say, 'Either pay my entire debt or give me the land, which is worth the full debt to me. So long as you have not paid the entire debt, it is mine.' He may say this even where it produces no benefit for the debtor, such as where the debtor died leaving no property. It follows that here one cannot say that the adjoining owner need pay only the appraised value. An adjoining owner has no greater right than a purchaser who already owns and possesses the land, from whom the creditor may seize unless the purchaser pays the entire debt. Even if the adjoining owner removes the purchaser under the law of adjoining ownership and takes possession, the creditor may later seize the land from him unless he pays the full debt. Indeed, the Rivash's case itself involved a purchaser who was an adjoining owner, and he nevertheless ruled that the creditor could seize the land and that the purchaser could not offer merely its appraised value. The creditor could demand either payment of the entire debt or the land. The Rivash's concluding statement, cited by the Beit Yosef, does not mean what the Rama and Sema understood. The Rivash writes that in that different matter the purchaser may remove the creditors under the law of adjoining ownership by paying the amount that beit din assesses the debt to have been worth. Although property worth one hundred that was sold for two hundred ordinarily requires payment of two hundred because land is not subject to overreaching, that applies where the land was bought with actual money. Those who bought it in exchange for their debts are like one who exchanged land for movable property, concerning which the Rambam rules that the movable property is appraised. Plainly, the end of the responsum addresses a different case: the creditor had already purchased the land from the debtor in exchange for the debt. The adjoining owner may then remove him by paying what the debt was actually worth. The value of the land is irrelevant, because every adjoining owner pays what the buyer gave for the land. Here the creditor gave his promissory note, so the adjoining owner must reimburse the value of that note, whether less or more than the land. The creditor can no longer say, 'Pay the full face amount of my debt or give me the land,' because the debtor now owes him nothing. The debt was discharged when he acquired the land in exchange for it. Had the land later been in another purchaser's possession, he could not have seized it, since no debt remained. The adjoining owner stands as a purchaser and need only reimburse what the creditor actually gave, namely the debt instrument, whose true market value must be assessed. Thus, when the Rivash mentions a debt of two hundred against land worth one hundred, he means that the creditor cannot insist that because the face value was two hundred he should be treated as having paid two hundred. That would be so only had he paid two hundred in cash. When he paid with a debt instrument, the instrument itself is appraised, just as movable property exchanged for land is appraised. This is, in my view, the clear intention of the Rivash and the correct law, unlike the Rama and Sema. Were it not for the Rama's words in Darkei Moshe and the Sema, one could perhaps have forced a different reading, namely that the Rama relied not on the Rivash but on the Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch above, siman 109, beginning of seif 1, who hold that a creditor cannot demand the full debt or the land, and that an heir or purchaser may remove him by paying the land's value. On that reading the gloss here would concern a case in which the debt instrument remains outstanding. The Sema, however, clearly understands the Rama to derive the ruling from the Rivash, and the Darkei Moshe says so expressly. That derivation is mistaken, for the Rivash's intention is as I have explained. Since I concluded above, siman 109, seif 1, that the primary law follows the Raavad, Ramban, and Baal HaTerumot, and not the Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch, here too, when the creditor comes to seize while his debt remains outstanding, the adjoining owner cannot remove him until he pays the entire debt. This is the Rivash's ruling and the primary halacha.
