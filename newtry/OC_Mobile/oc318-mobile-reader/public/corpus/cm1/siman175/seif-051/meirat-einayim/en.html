Rather, whenever he wishes he can say to him, 'Uproot your trees,' etc. This is Rambam's wording. It implies that if the tree owner or building owner has a fixed period during which his tree or building may remain, so that during that period the landowner cannot say, 'Uproot your trees,' then for that period he too is considered like a partner and can remove another person who comes to purchase the land. Beit Yosef writes this in Rambam's name. Rosh and Tur, however, do not say so. In their view, unless the tree owner or building owner has a permanent right in the land, such that even if those trees dry up he may plant others in their place, and similarly with the building, he is not called a partner. In the Derishah I wrote that this also appears to be Rif's view. Therefore the Tur states only this position. It is surprising that neither the Mechaber nor Rama mentions it at all.
The house and the upper story, etc., until: they are not called partners. See Beit Yosef, who cites Responsa Rosh, siman 57, ruling 3, that if the house owner sells the lower house to the owner of the upper story, an adjoining owner living beside the house cannot remove the upper-story owner from the house he purchased, because he is considered like a partner. Beit Yosef writes that it appears to him that even if the adjoining owner purchased the house first, the upper-story owner can remove him according to the opinion that a partner removes an adjoining owner, though the wording of the responsum does not prove this. This explains Rama's statement here that if the adjoining owner bought first, the upper-story owner cannot remove him. I do not understand Beit Yosef and Rama in this. The responsum explicitly says the upper-story owner is considered like a partner. Although the actual case was where the upper-story owner bought first, the Rosh appears merely to have answered the facts before him. By calling him a partner he indicates that the same should apply if the adjoining owner bought first, allowing the upper-story owner to remove him. One cannot object from Beit Yosef and Rama's earlier ruling that an owner of a building with a permanent right in the land is stronger than an adjoining owner and can remove him. They may distinguish an upper-story owner. Although he has a permanent right for the upper story to stand upon the lower structure, he does not own a partnership interest in the lower house itself; the house and upper story are like adjacent properties resting one over another, giving each merely a neighbor's right against the other. By contrast, a house or tree fixed directly in the land creates a more direct partnership between the structure owner and landowner. It is possible, however, that Beit Yosef and Rama understand the Rosh's conclusion that the upper-story owner is 'like a partner' not as describing his status in the purchased house itself, but as part of the Rosh's response to a potential objection from the rule of land owned by one person and a house by another, where the house owner is not called an adjoining owner. The Rosh explains there that Rif limited that rule to one whose building right ends when the present structure falls. If he has a right to rebuild, he is like a partner. Beit Yosef and Rama may understand that language as applying specifically to land and a building directly upon it, not to an upper-story owner. The upper-story owner is at least treated as an adjoining owner, so if he buys first the side neighbor cannot remove him, which was the actual case. Darkei Moshe also raises a difficulty with Beit Yosef's statement that an owner of a building could remove an adjoining owner who bought first, from the case of a house and upper story where the upper-story owner cannot remove the adjoining owner, and concludes that perhaps there is a distinction. That distinction may be that a house fixed directly on the land has a stronger status than an upper story. Nevertheless, Rama's unqualified attribution to the Rosh that an upper-story owner cannot remove an adjoining owner is difficult, because that is not stated expressly in the responsum, and its plain wording may indicate the opposite. This requires study.
