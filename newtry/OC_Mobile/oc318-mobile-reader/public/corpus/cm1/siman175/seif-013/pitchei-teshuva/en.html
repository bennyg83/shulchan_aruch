If he sold them to a son. See Baer Heitev, who leaves in doubt a case where a person sells land to his son. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 11, writes that where no son wishes to purchase, a daughter should likewise be treated as a child for this purpose, because it is advantageous for her to retain her ancestral estate. In the laws of redemption by relatives we do not find a distinction between male and female relatives. At the very least the matter is doubtful, and throughout this siman a doubt is resolved in favor of the purchaser who already holds the property. Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia 108 quotes a responsum of the Rif that articulates the same principle and extends it even to grandchildren. Yaakov owned a large residence and gave one house within it to his son Shimon. Shimon died and the house passed to his daughter, who married Chanoch. She later died, leaving the house to Chanoch as her husband, while Yaakov was still alive. Yaakov then gave the remainder of the residence to grandchildren descended from his other sons, by a gift effective immediately and with warranty. Chanoch claimed the right of bar metzra to the rest of the residence and offered to pay the donees its value. The Rif rejected the claim sharply. The very foundation of bar metzra is the duty to do what is right and good. It is plainly more right and good to leave ancestral property in the hands of the descendants who inherit their fathers' estate than to remove it from them and give it to an outsider. A contrary claim reverses the natural order of equity. The Shach apparently did not have Shitah Mekubetzet before him and therefore remained in doubt. Pitchei Teshuva adds that perhaps the Shach's text itself contains a scribal error and should have referred to selling to a grandson, which would also resolve Baer Heitev's objection.
