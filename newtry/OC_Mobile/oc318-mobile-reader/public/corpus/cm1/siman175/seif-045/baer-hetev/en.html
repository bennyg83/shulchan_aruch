A sharecropper. The Beit Yosef writes that it appears from the Baal HaIttur, and Tosafot and the Nimukei Yosef likewise rule, that one who is a sharecropper in a field and purchases an adjoining field cannot be removed by the owner of the field he works, even though the land itself belongs to that owner. This is not comparable to seif 58, where a borrower may prevent a lender, because here the case concerns an hereditary sharecropper who cannot be dismissed and is therefore like a partner. Ibid.
Proof. Meaning that if he does not bring proof, the purchaser remains in the field and, according to the Sema, need not even take a heset oath, since he could have advanced this claim on the basis of doubt, and the Tur does not mention an oath here. The Shach writes that the Sema's words imply that even if the purchaser makes a definite claim he need not swear, because he could have claimed uncertainty. This is difficult. What difference does it make that he could have made a doubtful claim? Since he now claims certainty, he should take a heset oath. Moreover, where he claims that the seller sold because of taxes, he could likewise have said perhaps the sale was because of taxes, yet when he claims certainty he must swear. The correct explanation is that the Tur and Shulchan Aruch relied on what they state immediately afterward, that if the adjoining owner brings no proof the purchaser must swear. The same applies here when the purchaser makes a definite claim. There is no distinction between this rule and the rule concerning a sale because of taxes. See the Shach at length.
