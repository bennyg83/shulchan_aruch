The adjoining owner must therefore bring proof. This is simple, but the Sema, seif katan 81, did not understand it this way. This too was written in my youth.
You are a sharecropper on this field. The Beit Yosef writes in his novellae, paragraph 33, that it appears from the Ittur, and Tosafot and the Nimukei Yosef likewise rule, that if a person is a sharecropper in one field and purchases an adjoining field, the owner of the field in which he is a sharecropper cannot remove him, even though the body of that field belongs to the owner. This is not comparable to what is stated in seif 58, that a borrower may prevent the lender, because there the case concerns a hereditary sharecropper. Since the owner cannot remove him, he is treated like a partner.
The adjoining owner must bring proof. If he does not bring proof, the purchaser remains in possession of the field. The Sema writes that the purchaser need not even take a rabbinic oath, since he could have raised the claim as a matter of uncertainty. His words imply that even if the purchaser now asserts the claim with certainty, he need not swear, because he could have pleaded it uncertainly, and because neither the Tur nor the other authorities mention an oath here. This is astonishing. What difference does it make that he could have pleaded uncertainty? Once he asserts the claim with certainty, he must take a rabbinic oath. Moreover, where he claims that the seller sold because of a tax burden, he could likewise have pleaded uncertainty, as the Sema himself writes in seif katan 79, yet when he pleads with certainty he must swear, as is explicit in the Tur, Rambam, and Mechaber. The truth is that the Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and Rambam rely on the ruling stated immediately afterward, that if the adjoining owner brings no proof, the purchaser must swear. The same applies here when he pleads with certainty. There is no distinction between this case and a claim that the seller sold because of a tax burden. With a definite claim he swears; with an uncertain claim he does not. More requires analysis: if he says, 'Perhaps he sold to me because of a tax burden,' must the adjoining owner also bring proof? It appears that once the claimant proves that he is an adjoining owner, a mere possibility cannot displace his certain right. The Rambam's phrase, 'even where the matter is uncertain,' refers to the examples that follow, such as a claim that perhaps the claimant is only a sharecropper, where the very basis of the claimant's adjoining right is uncertain. This differs from a claim that perhaps the seller sold because of a tax, where the adjoining status itself is certain and the purchaser raises an external possibility. Otherwise the law of adjoining owners would be nullified, for every purchaser could claim that perhaps the seller sold in order to redeem nearby property, buy a better field, pay the king's dues, obtain food, or for any of many similar reasons, and the adjoining owner would have to disprove each possibility. Such claims therefore help only when asserted with certainty. The Sema's words may perhaps be forced into this interpretation. I later found another note explaining that the Sema distinguishes between a challenge to the claimant's very status as adjoining owner and a case where that status is certain but the purchaser claims that the sale was for tax purposes. Yet this distinction still requires analysis, and the omission of an oath by the Rambam and Tur may simply be because they relied on their earlier ruling regarding the tax claim.
