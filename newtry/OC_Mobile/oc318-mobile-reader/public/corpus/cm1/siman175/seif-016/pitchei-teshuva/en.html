A superior field. See Baer Heitev and what I wrote elsewhere regarding a field inferior for one person but superior for another. The Shach infers that one who uproots his residence and moves to another city may be treated like a seller acting under hardship. Noda BiYehudah, siman 24, strongly questions this. The exception in bar metzra is not merely that the seller dislikes his present property; rather, the classic case is selling an inferior field in order to acquire a superior one, where delay may cause the desired superior field to be sold to someone else. Merely moving away does not itself establish such urgency. Noda BiYehudah therefore limits the Shach's reasoning to cases in which the move is itself time-sensitive and dependent on the sale. For example, the destination may admit only a fixed number of residents and an opening may be taken by someone else, or the seller may need immediately to acquire a licensed commercial right, or to accept a rabbinic post before another candidate does. If the seller cannot make that move unless he first sells his present house, delay for bar metzra could cause real loss. If, however, he is leaving in any event and simply wishes to sell the house whenever a purchaser appears, the sale does not delay the move and the usual law of bar metzra applies. Shaar Mishpat defends the Shach. He cites the Rosh's comparison of one who sells all his properties to one purchaser with the case of selling distant land to redeem nearby land. The broader principle is that the sages did not enact the adjoining owner's preference where doing so would harm the seller. Even an inferior field ordinarily has many buyers at fair value, so selling it alone does not harm the seller. But a person uprooting his residence may have difficulty finding an immediate buyer; if the existing purchaser withdraws while the seller waits for the adjoining owner, the seller may be forced to remain or return and await another buyer. In such circumstances the enactment does not apply. Shaar Mishpat therefore makes the controlling question practical: whether losing or delaying this purchaser will actually obstruct the seller's relocation or force him to return and wait for another sale. Everything depends on the circumstances.
