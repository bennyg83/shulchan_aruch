The craftsmen. See Baer Heitev, through the statement that, in the Shach's view, even when the work is performed in the owner's house the craftsman is a paid custodian, because the opportunity to earn his wages is itself his compensation. See Machaneh Ephraim, Laws of Custodians, siman 71. He initially inclined to the Shach's view: since we rule that a renter has the status of a paid custodian because the object is in his possession for his benefit, the same should apply to a craftsman even when he performs the work in the owner's house. His benefit is that the owner gives him the work and permits him to earn wages from it. On this reasoning, even a day laborer should be a paid custodian. He then notes, however, that Rashi explains the Mishna's phrase "all craftsmen" as referring to contractors who accept articles to perform the work in their own homes. This is difficult if a craftsman working in the owner's premises would have the same status. Machaneh Ephraim therefore concludes that a craftsman becomes a paid custodian only when the article is in his own premises. Since it is there for his benefit, he resolves to guard it from theft and loss, for if he does not guard it no one else will. When he works in the owner's premises and the article is not under his own control, this rationale does not apply. Rashi's statement that a craftsman resembles a renter because the benefit of his wages is in his possession likewise implies that if he works in the owner's premises he is not a paid custodian. His presence there is not for his own benefit in this respect, because the object would be in the owner's domain even without him. Indeed, Machaneh Ephraim writes that in such a case the craftsman is not merely exempt from the obligations of a paid custodian; he is not even an unpaid custodian. As Bach writes, the owners have not withdrawn their own guarding, since the craftsman has no authority to take the article home to repair it there. Ordinary custodianship arises when the owners remove the object from their own domain and place it in the custodian's domain, as stated in the Mekhilta. This accords with the Sema. Machaneh Ephraim continues that, at first glance, once we rule that a renter is a paid custodian there should be no need to base a craftsman's liability on the fact that he retains the article as security for his wages. Even if that rationale is absent, such as where his wages were paid in advance, he should still be a paid custodian because he benefits from the article's being with him while he earns money through his work. Yet Raavan writes in a responsum that when the craftsman's wages were paid in advance he is not a paid custodian, and Mordechai writes similarly. This indicates that, in their view, the craftsman's liability rests solely on his retaining the article for his wages. The She'iltot of Rav Achai Gaon and Nimukei Yosef likewise indicate this. Whenever that reason does not apply, the craftsman is exempt from the law of a paid custodian. A renter is different because he uses the very body of the animal and needs it to remain in his possession in order to enjoy that use. A craftsman does not use the body of the article. Nor can the article be said to remain with him for his benefit, because he gains no more from its being in his premises than he would if it remained in the owner's premises and he performed the work there. The Gemara's comparison of a craftsman to a renter is therefore a fortiori: if a craftsman is liable because he earns money from the article, how could Rabbi Meir exempt a renter, for whom there is an even stronger basis for liability? See there. Responsa Galya Masekhta, beginning of siman 13, discusses a wagon driver who received his hire before departing and states simply that a craftsman who received full payment in advance is nevertheless a paid custodian. The principal rationale for a craftsman, as for a renter, is the benefit that the owner passed over others and hired him. Apparently the author did not see the aforementioned words of Machaneh Ephraim. Even according to Machaneh Ephraim, however, it appears that one should distinguish between an ordinary craftsman and a wagon driver. An ordinary craftsman who was paid in advance is not a paid custodian because, as explained, the object cannot be said to remain with him for his benefit, since he could perform the work in the owner's house. A wagon driver, by contrast, earns his livelihood by carrying merchandise on his own wagon from place to place. The goods are therefore in his possession for his benefit just as with a renter. Accordingly, he is a paid custodian even if his hire was paid in advance. This appears straightforward. I later saw Responsa Be'er Yitzchak, siman 6, which discusses this issue at length. He rejects Machaneh Ephraim's distinction between a craftsman and a renter and concludes that a wagon driver is certainly liable according to all opinions as a paid custodian even when paid in advance, as is evident from Bava Metzia 58 concerning townspeople who sent their shekalim, and from Rashi there, s.v. "their wages." Regarding an actual craftsman, if his wages were paid after he had already taken possession of the article, it is likewise clear that he remains liable as a paid custodian. If, however, he received his wages before taking possession of the article, Be'er Yitzchak leaves the matter in doubt. See there. See also Responsa Chut HaMeshulash at the end of Tashbetz, second column, siman 30. The questioner considered whether the rule that a craftsman is a paid custodian applies when an object is given to him merely so that he can examine its form and make another object like it. Perhaps the original object should then be treated as a mere deposit, making him only an unpaid custodian. The responsum rejects this. Had the owner not shown the article to the craftsman, he would not have known how to make another like it. The wages he receives for producing the new article are therefore earned through the original article that was given to him. That article is consequently equivalent to an article upon which he is directly paid to work, and in both cases he is a paid custodian. This continues until the craftsman informs the owner that he has already fixed the form of the article in his mind and no longer needs it. If the owner thereafter leaves it with him, from that point he is only an unpaid custodian, in accordance with the law governing one who has completed the work. See there.
