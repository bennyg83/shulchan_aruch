All craftsmen are paid custodians. The Sema writes that in the Derisha he proved that this applies specifically to a contractor, but not to a day laborer. I do not know his source, and I have found no authority who draws that distinction. The conclusion of the Gemara in Bava Metzia 80b also indicates that there is no distinction. The Tur's reference to contract work is not precise, or it was stated because of the continuation concerning whether a craftsman acquires the enhancement of the article, as the Bach explains. The Bach also writes that a day laborer is a paid custodian, though he appears to limit this to one who receives the work into his own home. In my view, even when he performs the work in the owner's premises he is a paid custodian, because the benefit he receives from being given profitable work constitutes his compensation. Tosafot's reference to a craftsman working in his own home follows the opinion that a renter has the status of a gratuitous custodian. According to the accepted ruling that a renter is a paid custodian, Tosafot themselves indicate that even one who works in the owner's home is a paid custodian because receiving the work from which he earns wages is itself a benefit.
Take what is yours and bring the money. See Tosafot, Bava Metzia 49a, who ask from this case, where he is a gratuitous custodian, and give a strained answer. In my view there is no difficulty. Here he said, “Take what is yours and bring the money.” Since the customer still owes him money, he remains a gratuitous custodian. In the other case nothing is owed to him. I later found that Rabbeinu Yerucham, Netiv 30, part 1, writes this in the name of the Ramah.
