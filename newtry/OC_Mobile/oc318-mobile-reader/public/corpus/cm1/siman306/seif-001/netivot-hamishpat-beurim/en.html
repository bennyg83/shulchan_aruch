All craftsmen. See Shach, seif katan 1, who writes that this applies even where the work is performed in the owner's house. Although custodians ordinarily require meshichah, it appears that with a renter or hirer the beginning of use itself effects the relevant acquisition, both regarding the right to retract and regarding liability for unavoidable accidents, as I wrote concerning borrowing in siman 340. The beginning of use is comparable to receipt of money. Here too, because the owner can no longer retract, that benefit is treated as consideration with respect to the renter.
"Take what is yours." See Shach, seif katan 2, through his statement that the cases there differ because he owes them nothing. His meaning is as follows. A craftsman remains liable because we interpret his statement "take what is yours" not as an intention to terminate his guardianship, but merely as notice that he no longer wishes to retain the object as security for payment. If the owner wishes, he may take his property and the craftsman will wait for payment. If the owner voluntarily leaves it with him, he will still guard it, and he therefore becomes an unpaid custodian. In the Bava Metzia case, by contrast, once the party informed the other that he was retracting, the other could simply take his own money whenever he wished. Why then did he additionally say, "Take your money"? That statement must have been intended to terminate his guardianship. Baal HaTerumos states this expressly in sha'ar 50. Nevertheless, the craftsman is no longer a paid custodian, for he resembles a paid custodian whose term has ended. Once he completed the work and informed the owner, he is no longer obligated to retain the object. See Sema, seif katan 4, who states that when the craftsman says, "Bring the money and take what is yours," we infer that the owner may not take the item until payment is brought, and he says this is also evident from Nimukei Yosef. This is surprising, for Nimukei Yosef proves no such thing, and the Perishah states the opposite. The Sema's own reasoning also implies that if the craftsman expressly says that he will no longer guard the item, he should be exempt even before payment is brought. In my view he is liable even in that case, because one who holds another's property as security for a debt is a paid custodian by virtue of the benefit of possessing security for his money, as is evident from the chapter HaSocher es HaPoalim regarding one who seized property to collect damages.
