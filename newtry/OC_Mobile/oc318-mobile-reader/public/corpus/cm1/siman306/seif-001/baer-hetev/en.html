The craftsmen. The Tur writes: All craftsmen to whom an article is given for repair on a contract basis have the status of paid custodians and are liable for theft and loss. Their wage consists in the profit they make from being given the work to repair and collect payment for it. The Sema writes that this appears to refer specifically to a craftsman working as an independent contractor, who is a paid custodian, unlike a day laborer. Although at first glance the same rationale might seem to apply to a day laborer, one can distinguish as follows: the payment is not for guarding but for the labor of repairing and working on the item. The craftsman is deemed a paid custodian only because of the benefit that this work was entrusted to him rather than another. That rationale fits a contractor who keeps the item until he completes the work and receives payment, but not a day laborer, whom the owner may employ today and replace tomorrow, since the work was given to him only temporarily as circumstances happened to require. A further distinction is that an independent contractor ordinarily works in his own home, so guarding the article there against thieves naturally falls upon him, whereas a day laborer ordinarily works in the owner's premises, where guarding is not his responsibility. The Shach writes: I do not know the source for this distinction. Indeed, the conclusion of the Gemara in HaOmanin, Bava Metzia 80b, implies that there is no distinction. The Tur's use of "contract work" is not precise, or else he used it because of the later rule that a craftsman acquires an interest in the improvement of the article, as the Bach writes. Yet the Bach seems to limit liability to a day laborer who is given the work to take home. In my humble opinion, even one who performs the work in the owner's premises is a paid custodian, because this is the benefit he earns by receiving the work. Although Tosafot there say that a craftsman works in his own home, that is according to the opinion that a renter has the status of an unpaid custodian. According to our ruling that a renter has the status of a paid custodian, Tosafot clearly imply that even one who performs the work in the owner's premises is a paid custodian for the reason stated. Shach.
And bring. See Tosafot, chapter HaZahav, Bava Metzia 49a, s.v. "rather," who ask from this case, where he has the status of an unpaid custodian, and their answer is difficult. In my humble opinion there is no difficulty. Here the craftsman told him, "Take what is yours and bring the money." Since the owner still owes him money, he remains an unpaid custodian. There, however, the owner owes him nothing. I later found that Rabbeinu Yerucham, netiv 30, part 1, writes in the name of the Ramah exactly as I have explained. See there and consider carefully. Shach.
Exempt. Meaning, he is not even an unpaid custodian, because he expressly disclosed that he no longer wishes to guard the item at all. But if he first said, "Bring the money," and then said, "I will no longer guard it," that case is not discussed here. Even there we say that he meant he would cease guarding only after the money was brought, as is clear from Nimukei Yosef. Sema.
