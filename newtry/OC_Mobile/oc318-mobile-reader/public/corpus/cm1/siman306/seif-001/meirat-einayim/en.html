All artisans are paid bailees. The Tur writes: all artisans to whom an item is given to repair under a contract arrangement have the law of a paid bailee with liability for theft or loss. Their wage is itself the compensation that gives them that status, since they earn by being given the item to repair for payment. In the Derishah I proved that this applies specifically to a contractor, whereas a day laborer does not have the law of a paid bailee. Although at first glance the same rationale seems applicable to a day laborer, there is a distinction. The wage is not paid for guarding the object but for the work done on it. The additional benefit that the owner chose this artisan rather than another can be treated as compensation for guarding only in the case of a contractor, who ordinarily keeps the work in his possession until completion and then receives his payment. A day laborer, by contrast, may work for this person today and another tomorrow; one cannot say in the same sense that the owner conferred the benefit of entrusting the item specifically to him, because he was hired merely because he happened to be available. One can also distinguish that a contractor ordinarily performs the work in his own premises, so guarding the owner's utensils from theft in his premises is relevant, whereas a day laborer ordinarily works in the owner's premises and is not independently charged with guarding them. See further what I wrote in the Derishah and Perishah.
"Take yours and bring the money," or he said, "I have finished it, etc., and bring, etc." This teaches that even in the first formulation he becomes only an unpaid bailee, and in the case where he says, "I have finished it," it teaches that even so he does not remain a paid bailee. This is straightforward.
"Bring the money and take, etc." Meaning, by phrasing it this way he reveals that he wishes to retain the utensil in his possession until he first receives his wage.
And he no longer guards it, etc. Meaning, he is not even an unpaid bailee, because he expressly revealed that he no longer wishes to undertake any guarding at all. If, however, he first said, "Bring the money and take yours," and then added, "and I no longer guard it," that case is not discussed here. Even there, we say that his statement, "Take yours and I no longer guard it," does not take effect until after the money is brought. This is also evident from Nimukei Yosef.
