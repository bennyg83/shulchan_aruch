What it ought to have appreciated. The Jerusalem Talmud discusses wool and dyes valued at five each, with ten in labor, where the owner instructed the dyer to dye red and he dyed black. Had he dyed red the finished product would have been worth twenty five, but in black it is worth only twenty. The artisan cannot make the owner bear the loss caused by deviation. Tosafot and Rosh explain that the amount the object ought to have appreciated according to the owner's instruction is included when calculating the owner's capital. Even where the artisan supplied his own dye and incurred real expense, the owner need pay only the amount by which actual appreciation exceeds the protected value the owner should have received. The Shach limits the artisan's loss. The dyer does not have to lose his actual out-of-pocket materials or ordinary labor wage merely because the intended red dye would have produced greater appreciation. What he loses is any specially enhanced wage promised above the ordinary rate, to the extent necessary to cover the lost expected appreciation. Thus actual expense and ordinary labor remain part of the protected outlay.
The owner of the utensil said. Maharshal reads the case as one where there is no appreciation at all and the finished article is worth less than the original material. The Shach rejects this, because the rule follows the preceding discussion of a case where there is appreciation. In my view the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch mean that even where there is actual improvement, the owner cannot say, "Since you deviated from my instructions, keep your improvement and give me the cash value of my original material." The rule requiring the owner to pay the lesser of the artisan's expense or the appreciation is a protective enactment for both parties. It is not left solely to the owner's choice. This is not contradicted by the general law that a tortfeasor can sometimes pay with property rather than cash. The issue here is not whether the artisan must pay damages in money. It is whether the owner can refuse the legal arrangement altogether and force the artisan to take the finished object. Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah states expressly that the owner cannot do so. The special rule of expense versus improvement controls even though the artisan acted contrary to instructions. Raavad disputes this and treats the enactment as designed primarily for the owner's benefit. Under his view, the owner can sometimes say, "Take your wood and stones." But Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch follow the reciprocal enactment and therefore bind the owner to its formula.
