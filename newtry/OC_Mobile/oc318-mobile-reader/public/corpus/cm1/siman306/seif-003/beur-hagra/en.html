The owner said, etc. This follows from the discussion there 99b to 100a: "I hold like Rabbi Meir, as we learned concerning one who gives wool to dye, etc." If even according to Rabbi Yehudah the owner of the utensil or wool could make such a claim, the Mishnah could follow Rabbi Yehudah as well.
And likewise, etc. This follows from the statement there 102a, "a dispute and afterward an anonymous Mishnah, etc.," for the Mishnah states, "whoever deviates, etc." If according to Rabbi Yehudah his position is inferior in some circumstance merely because he receives the lesser amount, in what respect does that differ more from Rabbi Meir? If the expenses exceed the improvement, even according to Rabbi Meir, who pays the value of the wool, the artisan receives only the improvement. If the improvement exceeds the expenses, then under Rabbi Meir, if the owner wishes he can return the value of the wool. Rather, according to Rabbi Meir even the owner of the wool can say, "give me the value of my wool," whereas according to Rabbi Yehudah he cannot. What, then, is meant by "his hand is on the lower side"? It is strained to say that the Mishnah speaks in two separate directions.
For an artisan does not, etc. Unlike Rabbi Meir, who holds that a change effects acquisition. This is the point of the statement there 95a: "come and hear, one who gives wool to dye, etc.; and if you would think, etc." From here there is support for the Rif in seif 2 that the conclusion is that even Rabbi Meir holds an artisan does not acquire through the improvement of the utensil, unlike the Rosh in chapter 2 of Kiddushin, siman 11, who writes, "this does not appear to be a proof, etc.," meaning that the earlier Tanna remains and we rule like Rabbi Meir. This cannot be said, for then how could the Gemara prove that Rabbi Meir holds that a change effects acquisition? Rabbi Yehudah, although he generally holds that a change effects acquisition, gives the owner of the wool the improvement because he accepts the rule "whoever deviates, etc." This is what is stated there 102a, "a dispute, etc.," for Rabbi Meir does not accept that rule. Although there 102b the Gemara says that according to Rabbi Yehudah a change does not effect acquisition, this is because of the enactment embodied in "whoever deviates." Meaning, in such a case the Sages completely enacted that the change should not effect acquisition. This resolves Tosafos's question in Avodah Zarah 6a, at the end of s.v. "Rabbi Meir," "and if you ask, etc." Their answer requires study, because if the dispute were simply whether a change effects acquisition, what would the Gemara mean by "a dispute and afterward, etc."? According to what I wrote, it is understood. Yet it remains difficult why the Gemara there says they disagree whether a change effects acquisition. It should have said that Rabbi Yehudah holds "whoever deviates, etc." The source for saying Rabbi Yehudah holds that change does not effect acquisition is that he entirely disagrees with Rabbi Meir, so that even if the owner of the wool says, "give me the value of my wool," we do not listen, even where the improvement exceeds the expense. This is why the Gemara says they dispute concerning whether change effects acquisition, deriving it from Rabbi Yehudah's language, "if the expense, etc." He did not say, "if the expense exceeds the improvement, he gives him the value of his wool." Hence Rabbi Yehudah holds that the artisan does not acquire the wool, and neither party may demand to take the wool contrary to the stated rule; Rabbi Meir holds the opposite.
