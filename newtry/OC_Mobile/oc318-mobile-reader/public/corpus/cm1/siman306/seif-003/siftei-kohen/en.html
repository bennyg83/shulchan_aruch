If the enhancement exceeds the expense. The Sema attempts to prove that the craftsman must also receive his contractual wage, but his proof from one who improves another's field without permission is not decisive, since even there a person who performed the labor himself receives ordinary laborer's wages. The principal ruling is that of the Beit Yosef, following Rashi and the Rambam's Commentary to the Mishna. The Darkei Moshe argues that Tosafot and the Rosh rejected Rashi because of the Yerushalmi. Yet the Yerushalmi rejects Rashi only according to Ri's interpretation, and Tosafot themselves object that its language does not fit Ri. Rabbi Elchanan explained it differently. Maharshal, citing the rulings of Mahariḥ, likewise concludes that the Mishna should be understood in its plain sense, like Rashi, the Rif, the Rambam, the Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Raah, and the Bartenura. The Yerushalmi can itself be explained consistently with Rashi. Its question is whether the craftsman's contractual wage counts as an “expense.” It answers that the Mishna speaks specifically of actual expense, not the full agreed wage. Thus, if the owner supplied wool worth five maneh and dyes worth five maneh, and promised ten maneh for dyeing it red, but the craftsman dyed it black, the owner may say: had you dyed it red it would have been worth twenty-five, whereas now it is worth only twenty; you lost your own compensation, not my principal. Likewise, if an agent was paid to buy wheat in Tiberias, where the measure was larger, but bought in Tzippori, where the measure was smaller, the loss is deducted from his agreed compensation. The Yerushalmi does not say that the craftsman's actual expenditures are reduced by the enhancement that might have been produced. Rashi therefore explains that Rabbi Yehuda penalizes the craftsman who deviated: he may not enjoy the enhancement and may not collect his full contracted wage. The Rambam and Mechaber, who use the Mishna's language, mean the same. The reason the Mishna speaks of expense rather than the agreed wage is that the craftsman never receives the full contractual wage after changing the instructions. Nevertheless, he must receive the value of dyes, fuel, and ordinary daily labor, because these are included in “expense.” Rashi expressly states that he receives ordinary day-labor wages, though not the higher contract price. This also answers the comparison to an unauthorized improver of another's property, who likewise receives expenses and ordinary labor value. Where it is known how much the article would have been enhanced had the instructions been followed, the loss from the deviation is deducted from the contractual wage. If the actual enhancement equals what the intended work would have produced, the craftsman receives the full agreed wage, even if it exceeds his out-of-pocket expense. If the actual enhancement is less, the deficiency is deducted from the agreed wage. If the expense exceeds the actual enhancement, he receives only the amount of the enhancement. The practical rule is therefore as follows. One who was given wool to dye red and dyed it black receives, where the enhancement exceeds the expense, the cost of materials and ordinary day-labor wages, but not automatically the full contract price. The owner does not deduct from those true expenses the enhancement that might have been produced. He may deduct that lost enhancement from the agreed contractual wage. If the actual enhancement equals the expected enhancement, the full contractual wage is paid. The same applies to an agent sent to buy wheat in Tiberias who bought in Tzippori: the difference in value is deducted from the agreed agency fee, but not from the ordinary compensation due for his labor, provided sufficient benefit remains.
If the owner of the article says, etc., we do not listen to him. Maharshal limits this to a case in which there is no enhancement and the article is not worth its former value. This is not correct. The ruling follows the preceding law, which expressly concerns a case containing enhancement. Moreover, the conclusion that a craftsman does not acquire the enhancement applies to the entire discussion. If the article were worth less than before, there would be no reason not to listen to the owner, for the craftsman damaged it directly. Rather, the case is as stated: the article contains enhancement, but the owner does not wish to trouble himself to sell it and demands the original value of his wool in cash. We do not listen to him. The Rambam's ruling is primary, not the Raavad's dissent. The Gemara explains Rabbi Yehuda's view on the basis that a change does not transfer ownership to the craftsman. The wool therefore remains the owner's. The Sages lowered the craftsman's recovery for the owner's benefit because he deviated from instructions, but there is no basis for compelling the craftsman to purchase the owner's wool for cash. Maharshal's assertion that, by strict law, the craftsman would acquire through the change as a robber does is contrary to the plain Gemara and to Tosafot. A robber intends to acquire through his act; a craftsman who deviates inadvertently does not. Accordingly, the Rambam's ruling is necessary. If the owner could compel the craftsman to pay cash for the wool, the law would effectively follow Rabbi Meir, who treats the change as transferring ownership, whereas the accepted ruling follows Rabbi Yehuda.
