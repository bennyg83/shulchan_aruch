If the improvement exceeds the expense, etc. The Mechaber copied the Rambam, who explains the Mishnah here in the same manner as one who descends into another's field without permission, whose hand is on the lower side. Thus, if the owner gave the dyer wool worth ten dinars and the dyer spent ten dinars on dyes and other expenses, then if the dyed wool is worth more than twenty, the improvement exceeds the expense and the dyer receives his ten dinars of expense. If the wool is worth only eighteen, the dyer receives only the eight dinars of improvement. Rashi explains similarly. The Rosh writes that this would at first appear to be the correct explanation, but the Yerushalmi interprets "improvement exceeding expense" and "expense exceeding improvement" differently, and Tosafot likewise cites that interpretation. According to the Yerushalmi, if the owner gave the dyer wool and dye materials worth ten dinars and fixed his wage at ten, and had he dyed it as instructed the finished wool would have been worth twenty five, the dyer receives nothing unless the actual finished value exceeds fifteen. The five dinars that the article should have increased beyond the capital are first reserved for the owner. Therefore, if the finished wool is worth twenty six, the improvement beyond that benchmark is eleven and the dyer receives the ten dinars originally fixed as his wage. If it is worth twenty four, he receives only nine, the actual remaining improvement. If it is worth fifteen or less, he receives nothing. One may ask why this differs from one who descends into another's field without permission. The answer is that an artisan is different. Since the owner agreed to give him whatever amount the article was expected to increase by through the assigned work, it is treated as though that increase had already been acquired by the owner, and an artisan who negligently deviates from the instructions must first make good that full expected increase. Tosafot gives another answer as well. In the Derishah I proved from their discussion that according to this approach, even if the dyer himself paid the expenses, he does not receive reimbursement until the owner first receives the amount of the expected improvement. Otherwise there would be no difficulty from the law of one who descends into another's field, because there the improver at least receives his expenses, while under this approach the dyer can receive nothing until the owner's expected gain is first preserved. Tosafot writes that this explanation cannot easily fit the Mishnah's case where he dyed it poorly, because how could poorly dyed wool ever appreciate more than properly dyed wool? I answered in the Derishah that this can occur if the market price of dye rose in the interim. The Mechaber abbreviated and copied only the Rambam. His intent is certainly the ordinary explanation used in unauthorized improvement of another's property and elsewhere. Otherwise he should have explained the unusual Yerushalmi calculation. This also follows from his grouping poor dyeing together with changing black to red and applying one rule to both. Moreover, he later writes that if the owner says, "Give me the value of my wool," without mentioning the cost of dyes, which accords with Rashi and the Rambam, where the artisan himself incurs the dyeing expenses. The Beit Yosef also writes that the Rambam follows Rashi. Rama nevertheless adds, "and what it ought to have improved," which reflects the Yerushalmi as understood by the Rosh and Tur. Darkei Moshe states explicitly that Rama considers their explanation primary. It is difficult, however, that he did not write this as "some say," since there is a practical difference. According to the Rambam and Rashi, if the artisan spent his own money he receives the lesser of his expense or the actual improvement even if the owner is left with less than the expected gain. According to the Rosh and Tur, the owner first receives the amount the article should have improved, and only the remainder can go to the artisan. This requires study.
We do not listen to him. The Sages made an enactment for both the owner and the artisan, and neither side may unilaterally retract from it.
