If the improvement exceeds the expense. Shach rejects Sema and says the owner does not deduct the five gold coins he would have earned from the artisan's dye-material expenses, but only from the artisan's labor compensation. In my view Sema is correct. Bava Metzia 74 discusses one who gives money to an agent to buy goods at a favorable market and the agent negligently fails to buy. Rav Ashi exempts because the purchase was not fully within the agent's control. Shitah Mekubetzet asks why any liability should exist, since merely preventing another's profit is usually exempt. It answers that the owner relied on him, and by that reliance the agent became obligated like a guarantor. Had the owner not relied on him, he could have used someone else. Raavad similarly compares this to a tenant who leaves a field fallow after undertaking, "If I leave it fallow, I will pay the best expected yield." Even according to final halachah, if one gives wine to a carrier for a market day and the carrier fails to bring it, he can be liable for the lost market profit. Rashba explains that Chazal imposed such liability wherever performance was within the worker's power. Therefore, in hired labor, where the worker backs out from a monetary matter that will otherwise be irretrievably lost, he can owe the entire lost profit that he could have produced. This applies particularly to an irreversible loss. If the worker withdraws while replacement workers remain available, the employer's remedy is to hire others against him up to the ordinary limits of siman 333. Now apply this to one instructed to dye red who instead dyes black. The worker can be liable for the profit the owner would have earned from red dye, because the work was within his control, the owner relied on him, and the opportunity is now irretrievably lost on this wool. At first glance this should mean the worker must pay the five gold coins from his own pocket even if the black dye added no value. In siman 326, if one was instructed to sow wheat and instead sowed sesame and the field yielded less, he pays what the field should have produced. The Gemara and poskim, however, imply that the owner's five-gold loss is taken only from whatever improvement the black dye created. Two explanations are possible. One is that even here the worker would technically owe from his own funds, but the sources did not discuss that uncommon extension. A better distinction is that in the market-day case and the field case there was a fixed time. Once that time passed, the opportunity itself was gone. Here no precise completion time was fixed. Had the worker done nothing, the owner could still take other wool later and dye it red to earn the profit. Therefore the worker does not necessarily owe the whole lost profit from his own pocket. Nevertheless, when calculating what the worker may recover from the black-dyed wool, the owner first deducts the profit he lost by being unable to dye this wool red. An ordinary unauthorized improver is entitled at least to expenses up to the value of the improvement because the owner actually benefited. Here the black dye is not a benefit to the owner to the full amount. On the contrary, it prevented the red-dye profit. Only whatever value of the black-dyed wool exceeds the owner's lost five-gold profit counts as true improvement. Therefore the worker can recover only that excess. The same deduction applies even against the cost of the black dye materials. Those materials were expended without the owner's consent. An unauthorized improver receives expenses only to the extent of benefit, and the first five gold of apparent "improvement" is not benefit at all but merely replacement of a profit the owner lost. This is exactly Sema's conclusion. Tosafot's question from an ugly dye-job can also be answered similarly. Rashi explains "ugly" as dye-residue of little value. The materials cost almost nothing compared with high-quality dye. Since using cheap remnants saves expense, there can sometimes be profit in a low-grade result despite lower sale value. Rosh therefore did not regard Tosafot's objection as decisive.
One does not heed him. See Shach, seif katan 6, through his words that there is no reason to require the owner to take money out of his pocket for the artisan. In my view, Raavad means only that the owner cannot be compelled to pay all the money demanded. The owner may say: why should I pay you the improvement in cash? I will become your partner in the wool to the extent of the improvement you added. He can then invoke gud o agud: either I buy your share by paying you its value in money, or you pay me the value of my wool and buy my share. Rambam may also agree to this. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that the owner may say, "Take your dye." In my view that is incorrect. One can say "take your improvement" only where the improver can actually remove it for himself, such as one who planted trees and can uproot and replant them elsewhere. But where the improvement cannot be removed from the object, the owner must pay for the benefit. Otherwise one who dyes another's wool without permission would always escape liability by saying "take your dye," and the Gemara's discussions of a monkey that dyed wool, plowing another's field, and digging pits would make no sense. Therefore the rule is as stated above.
