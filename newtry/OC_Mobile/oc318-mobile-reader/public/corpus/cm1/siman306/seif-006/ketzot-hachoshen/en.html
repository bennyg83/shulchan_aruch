One who shows a coin to a money changer. Mordechai writes that the expert is liable only where the coin is intrinsically defective but he says it is good. If the coin merely lost its stamped form and the expert says it is valid, he is exempt, because one who rubs away another's coin design is likewise exempt from direct damages.
The omission of Mordechai's limitation by the major decisors suggests they disagree. Even a defect of form can create liability for the mistaken appraisal. The Gemara speaks of a newly minted coin whose validity the expert failed to recognize. Rashi explains that an old form was invalidated and a new form introduced, and the experts were not yet familiar with it. This shows that errors concerning form can fall within the law of showing a coin to an expert. Mordechai's proof from rubbing away the form is also not decisive. One who physically erases the form does not damage the metal itself, and the coin can be restamped. The cost of restoring the form is analogous to medical expense for property and is not ordinary direct damage. But the expert who advises the creditor to accept such a coin causes a different loss. The creditor had a valid claim for a fully negotiable coin against his debtor. Relying on the expert, he accepts the defective coin and releases the debtor, or the debtor leaves the country. The expert's advice therefore destroys the creditor's existing claim to a proper coin. Once the expert becomes liable for that loss, he cannot discharge his own liability by tendering a coin whose form is invalid. This is conceptually different from the person who merely rubbed away a stamp. The matter nevertheless requires study.
