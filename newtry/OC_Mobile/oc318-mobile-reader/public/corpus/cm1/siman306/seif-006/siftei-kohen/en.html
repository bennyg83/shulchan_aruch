If he examined it for payment, he must compensate. Maharshal rules like the Rashba that a fully expert money changer, such as Dankhu and Issur, is exempt even when paid. Mahari ibn Lev likewise says that, because the authorities dispute the point, money cannot be extracted. Their proof is not decisive. Rav Pappa's answer may mean that the baraita exempting an expert concerns Dankhu and Issur, while the baraita imposing liability includes an ordinary expert even without pay and a superior expert when paid. He did not spell this out because the questioner already assumed a distinction between paid and unpaid advice and erred only in applying it to an ordinary expert.
Nevertheless, the first view is primary. Maharshal follows the Rosh, but in my view the Rif and those who agree with him are correct. The Gemara's words, “See, I rely upon you,” are not superfluous, and Reish Lakish's statement to Rabbi Elazar was likewise deliberate. The Rosh's proof from Rabbi Hiyya is answered by the Rif's qualification that where the circumstances themselves clearly show reliance, an express statement is unnecessary. The incident of Rabbi Hiyya was such a case. The Baal HaMaor and Milchamot Hashem also indicate that this is the Rif's view and appear to accept it. The Rosh argues that one normally shows a coin to a money changer in order to know whether to accept it and release the giver. This is not conclusive. Rabbi Ephraim explains that liability without an express declaration exists only where the recipient cannot avoid accepting the coin if the expert approves it. In an ordinary sale, collateral transaction, or other case in which he remains free to decline, the expert may say that he did not know the inquirer intended to rely upon him conclusively. The Mordechai supports this distinction from the law of one who purchases from a robber. Thus the expert is liable only if the inquirer says, “I rely upon you,” or if the circumstances unmistakably establish that reliance, such as payment of a debt or a transaction that the inquirer is legally bound to complete once the coin is approved. Rabbeinu Yerucham rules plainly that a layman is liable only when told, “I rely upon you,” or equivalent words. The Rosh's responsum, the abbreviated Tosafot, and the Yerushalmi likewise support this. The Yerushalmi's phrase describing one who “holds by” the expert means one who relies upon him. Even in situations where the inquirer could otherwise withdraw, an express statement of reliance creates liability. The same rule applies where one asks whether a prospective borrower or customer is financially sound. If another says that he is wealthy and the creditor extends credit, the adviser is exempt unless told expressly, or unless the circumstances clearly show, that the creditor is relying upon him. If the adviser says expressly, “Do not rely upon me,” he is plainly exempt. Shiltei HaGibborim writes that once the recipient accepts the coin and releases the giver in reliance upon the expert, his claim lies only against the expert. This is not correct. The giver was not expressly forgiven, and any such waiver would be a clear mistake. A bad coin, such as copper represented as silver, is a defect for which the transaction may be rescinded even after a long time. Tosafot, the Mordechai, Maharshal, and the Rosh's responsum all indicate that the original giver remains liable to replace it when he is available and subject to collection. The expert's liability is relevant where the giver has gone overseas, is a violent non-Jew, or otherwise cannot be made to pay. A deficient coin that was retained beyond the period for showing it to a money changer is governed by the separate law of onaah.
