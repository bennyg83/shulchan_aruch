Where he certainly rendered it a neveilah. Ramban explains why a professional slaughterer who invalidates the slaughter can be liable even though some invalidations are not visible. If the slaughter itself was performed improperly, the act of slaughter is the damaging act. The animal was not halachically slaughtered at all, but was effectively beheaded. The distinction between a living animal and an improperly slaughtered carcass is visible enough to constitute direct damage. This differs from piggul or another later prohibition attaching to an otherwise valid slaughter. There the slaughter itself was proper and removed the animal from the status of neveilah. A separate prohibition afterward made the meat forbidden. That damage is not visibly embedded in the slaughter itself and can therefore be treated as nonapparent damage.
Although our practice is to prohibit it. Terumat HaDeshen rules that a paid professional slaughterer who violates an accepted extra stringency taught in the laws of slaughter can be liable, because he knew the meat would be prohibited by communal practice and should have guarded against that defect. I would distinguish types of stringency. If the error was a minimal pause that is prohibited only as an extra stringency, the underlying Torah slaughter can still be valid and remove the animal from neveilah status. The resulting loss is therefore nonapparent damage. If committed unintentionally, the artisan should be exempt under the ordinary rule for accidental nonapparent damage. But if the extra stringency concerns an act such as improper cutting location, where the underlying event itself resembles a visibly defective slaughter, the damage is apparent. Then even if the prohibition rests only on customary stringency, the professional can be liable because he was obligated to avoid the recognized defect. The Shach elsewhere holds that even a custodian can be exempt for accidental nonapparent damage. This distinction therefore has practical force.
