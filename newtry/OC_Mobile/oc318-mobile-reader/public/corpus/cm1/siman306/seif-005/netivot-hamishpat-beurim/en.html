At the vertebra of the neck. See Sema, seif katan 15, who writes that cutting the vertebra is an unavoidable accident. At first glance this is surprising. Why is that relevant? Even if it were negligence, the slaughterer should be exempt because he can say that perhaps the animal was in fact kosher, just as in a case where he delayed after cutting only a minority of the required signs. One cannot answer that the Sema means that even the opinion cited by Rama that disagrees in that case concedes here because the event was unavoidable. If so, the slaughterer would still seemingly be liable under the rule of an uncertain claim combined with inability to swear, since he does not know whether the event was truly unavoidable, like the ruling in siman 340, seif 4, in the Rama regarding an animal that may have died because of the work. Ketzos HaChoshen writes that where the slaughterer delayed after cutting a minority of the signs he is exempt because the damage is not externally recognizable. This does not appear correct to me. If Torah law itself invalidated such a slaughter because the Torah insisted on this requirement, the slaughter would certainly be comparable to cutting off the animal's head with a sword. The same applies when the invalidity is rabbinic, because rabbinic enactments are modeled upon Torah law, and even a stringency of the Geonim is treated like a rabbinic prohibition. Moreover, it resembles a judge who mistakenly prohibits what is permitted and must pay so that judges will examine the matter carefully. The same rationale applies to a slaughterer.
Although some disagree. See Shach, seif katan 10, who concludes that the slaughterer is liable. Regarding an inspector who tears adhesions in order to permit the animal and thereby causes it to become prohibited, it appears that he is exempt even according to the Shach, because this is damage that is not externally recognizable. With slaughter, when the slaughterer failed to slaughter properly, the slaughter itself is recognizable damage. Inspection occurs only after the animal has already been slaughtered, and the resulting invalidity is not externally recognizable. According to the opinion that penalties are not derived from one another in the discussion of one who renders terumah impure, mixes terumah into ordinary produce, or pours wine for idolatry in Gittin, even intentional invisible damage is exempt unless specifically penalized. Even according to the opinion that allows such derivation, liability for intentional invisible damage exists only so that anyone should not go and damage another's property with impunity, as explained there. Here, when the inspector tore the adhesions, he intended to permit the animal, not to damage it.
