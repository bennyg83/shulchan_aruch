An artisan does not acquire the improvement. Rosh was asked about a person who gave a tablet to an artisan to decorate, and after decorating it but before being paid the artisan prohibited it to the owner by vow. Rosh ruled that since we hold an artisan does not acquire the improvement in the object, he has no power to prohibit it. Rosh adds that even according to the view that an artisan acquires the improvement, here there is only decorative appearance, and the Gemara leaves unresolved whether visible dye itself has substantive value. Ketzot HaChoshen explains that if a monkey dyed Reuven's wool with Shimon's dye, Shimon cannot say, "My dye is in your possession." The artisan is no stronger. He may have a claim only to the added value of the object, not to the physical object itself. Since visual appearance alone can be treated as though nothing substantial remains, he cannot prohibit it. In my view Ketzot's explanation is difficult. If an artisan acquires the improvement, he acquires a proportional interest in the improved object even where the improvement has no separate tangible body. If a garment was worth one hundred and after softening is worth one hundred fifty, the artisan can own one third of the value in the body of the garment. The softening itself is not a separate object, yet the Gemara still speaks of artisan-acquisition. If so, why should he not be able to prohibit his proportional share like any partner? Further, if he damages the item, why should he owe the owner the value of the original material plus improvement if the improvement is merely appearance with no property content? The proper explanation follows Rashba in Bava Kamma 98. Even according to the opinion that an artisan acquires the improvement for certain purposes, so long as he has not damaged the object the improvement is fundamentally the owner's. The artisan improved it for the owner. Artisan-acquisition means only that if he later damages it, we do not say he had already fully transferred the added value to the owner in a manner that would automatically make him liable for destroying the owner's improvement; and it also gives him a lien-like proprietary right corresponding to his wage. Accordingly, so long as the owner pays him, the artisan cannot prohibit the finished object to the owner. The improvement is the owner's. Rosh's real difficulty was different. The dye substances themselves belonged to the artisan. If an artisan supplied actual material, how did those materials leave his ownership? The doctrine that the artisan does not acquire the improvement concerns value added to the owner's object, but an artisan's own tangible addition is not ordinarily transferred without acquisition. Rosh answers that dye absorbed as mere appearance is treated as lacking separate substance. Even if one scrapes it away, nothing significant remains, and the artisan may not damage the owner's object merely to retrieve it. Thus the dye is legally treated as having disappeared into the owner's object, and no separate property remains for the artisan to prohibit. Rosh's text appears to have an omission. It should be understood: even according to the opinion that an artisan acquires the improvement, he cannot prohibit this item here because there is only a decorative appearance. That follows Rashba's model. Ketzot asks from a wife who vows, "What I produce for you is forbidden," which can prohibit her future handiwork even though the husband ordinarily acquires her work. There is no difficulty. The reason the artisan normally does not retain ownership is that he improves with intent to transfer the benefit to the owner. If the wife has already prohibited herself from conferring that benefit, she cannot then make the transfer in the ordinary way. Ketzot also cites Pnei Yehoshua's question about a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor writing a get according to the view that an artisan acquires the improvement: such persons cannot validly transfer ownership. If the ink belongs to the husband, there is no problem. The artisan merely supplies labor, like dyeing with the owner's dye, and everyone agrees the artisan receives only labor compensation. If the ink belongs to a minor, one might ask how the ink itself is transferred. But if the minor has reached the age of commercial transactions, his rabbinic sale is valid and hefker beit din gives the buyer title. If he has not reached that age, one should indeed not buy the paper or ink from him for the get. He may write only on the husband's paper using the husband's ink, in which case he is providing labor alone.
In the improvement of the utensil. As explained above in the name of Rashba, when the item remains intact the improvement belongs to the owner because the artisan improved it with the owner's benefit in mind. Artisan-acquisition matters when the artisan damages the object, because he did not intend to transfer the improvement in a way that would make himself fully liable for destroying that added value, and it also gives him a lien-like acquisition corresponding to his wage. That lien cannot be full ownership of a fraction of the object. If it were, then when the object suffered an unavoidable accident the owner should not owe the artisan wages for the portion that belonged to the artisan. Yet an artisan can still be entitled to wages in such circumstances. Ran likewise says an artisan is weaker than a pledge-holder, who according to Rashi can have stronger accident liability. Therefore the artisan's "acquisition" for his wage is a security interest, like a pledge taken after the loan, not full title. Shach writes that after the owner pays the wage, the artisan has no remaining interest in the improvement. The reason is that money acquires here. The rabbinic concern that money should not acquire lest the goods burn in the seller's attic does not apply: even if the artisan had a proprietary interest, an unavoidable loss of the object would not ordinarily require return of the wage, and an artisan is already a paid custodian for theft and loss. Thus once the owner pays, the wage payment fully acquires and removes the artisan's security interest. An artisan who worked for free can likewise be exempt for damaging the improvement according to the view that an artisan acquires it. Rashba's reason is even stronger there: one who works gratuitously certainly did not intend to transfer the improvement so as to expose himself to damage liability before delivering the object. The printed text of Shach, seif katan 3, contains errors. The sense is that Maharshal's conclusion from the Sages' view is not decisive because under Rava's explanation the dispute can concern betrothal with a loan plus a perutah; and that once the artisan has actually received his wages, no authority exempts him from the corresponding liability. The same principle can apply to land. If one hires an artisan to build or improve a house and the artisan later damages it, there is room to say he acquires the improvement in the land for the same limited purposes. Sema in siman 378 says one who builds on another's land cannot simply say, "I will take my building," because he built intending it for the owner. That is the same underlying logic. The Gemara in Bava Kamma 20, where one who benefited from another's house may have to pay because the owner suffered blackening from the fire, does not disprove this. The blackening may not reduce the appraisal enough to matter commercially, yet it is still some actual loss. Since the improver will be paid for his improvement, that slight physical loss remains the owner's loss. Therefore it is reasonable that artisan-acquisition can apply to improvement in land as well.
