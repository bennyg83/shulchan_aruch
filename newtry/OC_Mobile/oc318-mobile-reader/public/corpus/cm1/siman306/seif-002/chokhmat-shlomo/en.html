Seif 3. One gave wool to a dyer, etc. Note: See Shach, seif katan 6, who asks: if the altered item is no longer worth its original value, why do we not listen to the owner? The artisan diminished it with his own hands. Rabbi Elisha Aharon, av beit din of Leshnov, asked me that we rule that damaged property is appraised for the tortfeasor, so why should the fact that he damaged it directly change the rule? I answered that he raised a good point, and Gaon Tzvi already asked this question on the Gemara itself. In my view the distinction is clear. A paid custodian who causes loss without directly damaging the object may be treated like an ordinary tortfeasor for purposes of appraisal. But if the custodian directly damages the entrusted object, he resembles one who makes unauthorized use of a deposit, and unauthorized use gives him the status of a robber. We do not appraise damaged property in favor of a thief or robber in the same manner. This differs from an ordinary tortfeasor who never became a custodian. Where the same person is both custodian and direct damager, the case is more severe. A parallel distinction appears in siman 380 concerning one who says, 'Tear my garment,' where liability differs depending on whether the garment first came into his hands for safekeeping. Here too, and especially where he intentionally worked on the object in a manner different from the owner's instructions for his own profit, his act is actual unauthorized use and he becomes a robber. The Shach's language is therefore understandable. Even though a robber who lacks cash may generally satisfy liability with movables, the Shach may mean that if he has money, why should the owner be forced to accept this altered article? The Shulchan Aruch's language sounds absolute. Moreover, the general rule that movables are all considered 'best quality' applies where the creditor never indicated beforehand that he rejected a particular item. Here the owner gave wool to be dyed red and the artisan dyed it black, so the owner had already made clear, innocently and before any dispute, that he did not want black. That makes this case worse than ordinary payment with movables, and the artisan cannot force the altered item upon him. I made a similar point in my earlier Chiddushim to siman 100 regarding repayment with a particular commodity. The suggestion that where the article is worth less the owner may fear that it will not sell at the appraised value is not persuasive. Beit din appraises according to the present place and time, so that it can be sold immediately. The same concern could be raised against every judicial appraisal. This is what I answered, and I expanded the discussion further in my third edition on Choshen Mishpat. There, seif 5, gloss. See my Chiddushim to Bava Kamma 99b, where I brought proof for the dissenting view and explained, on the basis of Rashba, why one can be held liable even in a case of doubt. See also Ketzot HaChoshen, who cites a responsum of the Rosh that even according to the opinion that an artisan acquires the improvement in an article, mere visible coloring does not create such acquisition. Ketzot questions this from Bava Kamma 99. In my work on Stam, siman 49, I resolved this, with God's help.
