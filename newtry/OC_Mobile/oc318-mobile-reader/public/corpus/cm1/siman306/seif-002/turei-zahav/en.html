Although our custom is to prohibit it, etc. This was written to reject the position of Terumat HaDeshen, responsum 186, who states that whenever our established custom is to prohibit something, even merely because of doubt, the slaughterer should have been cautious and is therefore liable. According to him, the exemption stated in the first chapter of Bava Kamma applies only where the slaughterer could not have anticipated that the animal would be declared nonkosher. The Ramah disagrees, and this is also evident from the Tur. For if the knife was found defective, the animal is certainly nonkosher according to our practice, yet the slaughterer is exempt. We must therefore say that whenever the disqualification arises from doubt, the rule is that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant.
