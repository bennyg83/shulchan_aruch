For an artisan does not acquire the improvement of the utensil. Rosh was asked about an artisan who painted a gaming board and, before being paid, tried to prohibit the board upon its owner. Rosh answered that because we rule an artisan does not acquire the improvement in the utensil, he owns no part of the body and cannot prohibit it. He adds that even according to the view an artisan acquires improvement, mere coloring can lack substantive existence. The Gemara leaves unresolved whether visible dye itself constitutes a monetary improvement on wool. If a monkey dyed Reuven's wool using Shimon's dye, Reuven may not owe Shimon for the appearance alone. Therefore the painter's visible design may not create a proprietary thing to which his prohibition can attach. This is not contradicted by the Gemara that an artisan can acquire improvement through dyeing. That rule applies where the improvement itself has legal substance. The unresolved issue is whether the visual appearance of the dye remains a separate monetary substance. If it does not, the artisan cannot use it to impose a prohibition on the owner's object, even though according to the theoretical view of artisan acquisition he could scrape away his own physical dye. Rosh's discussion also sheds light on vows concerning a wife's handiwork. Tosafot and Ran treat "that which I make" as capable of becoming a substantive object once produced. That is easier under the view that an artisan acquires the improvement he creates. Where the act produces no substantive improvement, such as merely giving straw to an animal, the vow concerning future handiwork has no bodily object to attach to. I discussed this more fully in Even HaEzer siman 81.
The artisan acquires. The Gemara asks from the prohibition of delaying wages. If an artisan acquires the improvement in the object, why does the owner's nonpayment count as withholding wages? The answer distinguishes employment for the result from payment per act of beating or softening, where the artisan is paid for labor whether or not an improvement ultimately results. This distinction can produce both effects at once. If the act actually creates an improvement, the artisan may acquire that improvement according to the view that an artisan acquires. Yet because his wage was fixed for each act regardless of result, his compensation is also ordinary labor wages, and the owner can violate the prohibition of delayed payment. A further complication is that if the artisan's proprietary interest in the improvement functions like a pledge securing his wage, perhaps holding that interest already counts as payment and there should be no delayed-wage prohibition. One can answer according to the view that on the first act no improvement yet exists. The delayed-wage prohibition can attach to that initial labor even if later acts create improvement that secures part of the claim. This also explains the Talmudic presumptions after the wage period. The employer says one sela was fixed, the artisan says two. The first sela, already admitted or paid, can be the portion tied to the initial labor for which delayed-wage rules apply, while the disputed additional amount is supported by the artisan's proprietary improvement. The exact structure depends on the competing views in Tosafot and Ran.
In the improvement of the utensil. Maharash Halevi investigated whether the artisan acquires only the improvement or a share in the body of the utensil itself. He concluded that the artisan acquires the body together with the improvement. Terumat HaDeshen, however, expressly says that even according to the view of artisan acquisition, the artisan acquires only the improvement, not the body. The better understanding is that even the improvement itself does not become the artisan's property in the full sense of an ordinary purchaser. The body remains the owner's. The artisan receives a limited proprietary interest in the improvement, comparable to a creditor's interest in collateral. He can use it for kiddushin and is protected regarding damage to the improvement, but the owner can discharge him by paying the wage, even against his will. This resolves the otherwise severe difficulty: if the artisan fully owned all improvement, how could the owner reacquire it merely by paying the agreed wage, especially where the improvement is worth more than the wage and did not exist when the employment contract was made? The original contract could not have purchased future property. Rather, the artisan's interest is a security-like proprietary right that exists only until the wage is paid. Ran and Ramban compare it to a creditor's collateral. The artisan may have stronger rights than a creditor in some respects, but he is not a full purchaser. Accordingly, once the wage is paid, even forcibly, the artisan's proprietary interest ends automatically and the entire improvement belongs to the owner. If the artisan worked for free, he never acquired such a security interest at all because there is no wage to secure. This also explains why an artisan may remain only a paid custodian rather than becoming liable as a purchaser for unavoidable accidents. His proprietary interest is partial, not complete. Regarding theft or loss after payment, the artisan can remain a paid custodian of the owner's object because his paid-custodian status derives from the benefit already received through the work until he tells the owner to take it. Yet there is room to distinguish the improvement itself, which before payment was never fully the owner's guarded property. This question remains for further study.
