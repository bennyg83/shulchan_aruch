Some say that this applies specifically to an oath concerning the past. Shaar HaMishpat cites Knesset HaGedolah and several authorities who hold that since the matter is a doubt in law, money cannot be extracted on the basis of the doubt, and the defendant is exempt without an oath. Shaar HaMishpat argues the opposite. The doubt is not whether a Torah oath is owed, for that obligation is certain; the doubt is only whether this person may be administered the oath, since if he is disqualified one may not make him swear even at the claimant's request. Consequently, because he certainly owes a Torah oath but cannot take it, he should pay. This is especially clear according to those who apply the rule 'one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay' even where the matter was not something he should have known. Even according to Tosafot, the Rosh, and Rashba, who limit that rule to matters the person should have known, a forward-looking oath is different, because he was at fault in violating his oath. Although that violation may not definitively disqualify him because his inclination overcame him, his own misconduct created the doubt that now prevents the oath. A different case is where two witnesses testify that he committed a disqualifying offense and two others contradict them. There we genuinely do not know whether he committed any offense at all, and whether inability to swear requires payment depends upon the aforementioned dispute. See there at length.
