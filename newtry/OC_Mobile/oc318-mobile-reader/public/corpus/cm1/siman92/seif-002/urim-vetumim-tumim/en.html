One is an oath of testimony. The Sema writes that this must mean the event occurred very recently, so that the witness cannot plausibly say he forgot the testimony. Otherwise, how could he be disqualified? Perhaps he truly forgot and therefore swore truthfully. Even if he later admits what occurred, a person does not become suspect through his own admission. The Shach writes that from a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 34, letter 7, it appears that with respect to being classified as suspect regarding an oath we do not say that perhaps he forgot. He proves this from the case of one who becomes suspect through a false oath of testimony. Why not say he forgot? From this the Shach rules that if one later says, concerning an oath he violated, “I forgot,” he is not believed. If so, since the Shulchan Aruch above, siman 34:5, follows the Rashba that one is not believed to say he acted inadvertently, the Ran’s ruling here appears difficult. Yet this does not compel us to reject the Ran. Even if the Ran’s ruling is correct, the Rashba’s rule can still stand. The distinction is already implicit in the Ran. The authorities ask regarding an oath of testimony that perhaps the witnesses forgot, but they do not raise the same possibility regarding an oath of deposit, namely that the custodian forgot that he still held another’s deposit and thought it was his own. Likewise Tosafot in Shevuot 37, s.v. he acted intentionally, say that the Gemara does not ask about intentional violation of an oath of testimony after warning because the witnesses could say they forgot. Yet the Gemara does ask about intentional violation of an oath of deposit. Why not say that he forgot the deposit and thought it was his? The necessary distinction is that testimony concerning another person is not something one is constantly obligated to keep in mind. It is therefore plausible that he forgot. A deposit in one’s possession, however, is something one is responsible to remember. He must know what property of another remains in his hands, and ordinary forgetfulness is not a plausible defense. If this is true of a deposit, then certainly one who explicitly swore to pay his fellow by a set time is responsible to remember the oath and not let the date pass. His later claim, “I forgot,” is no better than forgetting a deposit. Consequently, Baal HaTerumot at the end of gate 42, which also speaks of an oath of deposit, gives no proof regarding an oath of testimony. As to the Ran’s own explanation that the baraita speaking of one who is suspect through an oath of testimony can concern witnesses who are relatives of the creditor and debtor but unrelated to the testifying witnesses: they cannot themselves testify because of their kinship to the litigants, but they can disqualify the witnesses by testifying that those witnesses violated an oath of testimony. The Ran answers the possible claim of forgetfulness by saying that when the matter is still immediately present, at the time of the event, we do not say that they forgot, just as the Gemara in Ketubot speaks of warning at the time of the act. This requires study, because Tosafot in Shevuot seem to disagree. They write that one cannot readily find a case of warning and lashes for an oath of testimony because the witnesses can say they forgot, even though one could seemingly warn them immediately after the event as the Ran proposes. This implies that Tosafot fear forgetfulness even very soon, perhaps because the witnesses did not pay close attention to the testimony. According to the Rashba, one could answer that with respect to actual lashes or capital consequences we must be concerned for the possibility of forgetfulness, but to classify a person as suspect for future testimony and oaths we do not preserve his eligibility merely by a general possibility that he forgot. Ordinarily we assume a person did not forget. To punish him with lashes, however, a doubt remains. Still, the Ran appears directly inconsistent with Tosafot. In Keritot 12b the Gemara says that Rabbi Meir agrees with the Sages where two witnesses say, “You knew testimony for so-and-so,” and the person says, “I did not know.” Rashi explains that he had sworn an oath of testimony and is exempt from a korban because he could say that he did not intend to serve as a witness. Birkat HaZevach cites a question in the name of Rabbi Leib Zunz, who was my maternal ancestor through the pious and righteous Rebbetzin Schindel, of blessed memory. According to Tosafot, the Gemara could have said more simply that the witness has a migo to say, “I forgot,” and there would be no korban for the oath. Birkat HaZevach suggests that the witnesses could testify that he admitted before them that he had not forgotten. But if so, they could just as well testify that he admitted that he intended to testify and deliberately denied the testimony. Why is one admission stronger than the other? Further, if two witnesses say, “You ate forbidden fat,” and he admitted before them that he ate it inadvertently, the dispute between Rabbi Meir and the Sages would likewise disappear. This appears to support the Ran, who does not generally fear forgetfulness, and creates a difficulty for Tosafot. Conversely, the Ran faces Rashi and Tosafot’s reason why the Gemara does not ask about intentional violation of an oath of testimony. According to the Ran, their answers based on lack of warning or possible forgetfulness are not available. One possible reconciliation is that the Ran holds that the Gemara gives us a meaningful boundary between remembering and forgetting only while the event is literally ongoing, namely within toch kedei dibbur. Once that interval has passed, the “time of the act” has ended and the witness may claim forgetfulness. Thus, without warning, if immediately upon seeing the event and still within toch kedei dibbur he says, “I swear that I do not know testimony for you against so-and-so,” he incurs the oath liability and can also be rendered suspect through witnesses, because forgetfulness is implausible within that immediate interval. But to impose lashes, a prior warning is required, and warning itself contains more than the few words that fit within toch kedei dibbur. By the time the oath is uttered, the strict interval of immediacy has passed, and the witness can say he forgot in the meantime. Tosafot’s statement regarding lashes is then correct, while the Keritot case can still concern an oath uttered immediately upon seeing the event, where forgetfulness is not plausible. Yet it remains difficult to say that after so brief an interval a witness may claim to have forgotten. There is also a difficulty in Rashi. He explains that the Gemara does not ask about intentional violation of an oath of testimony because there can be no certain warning: who knows whether the witnesses actually know testimony? If other witnesses are present they can testify, and then the oath is no longer an oath of testimony, as Maharsha explains. But perhaps there are witnesses related to the creditor and debtor yet unrelated to the testifying witnesses, as the Ran says, and they could give the warning. If Maharshal intended this, Maharsha’s objection to him would be unwarranted. In any event, the question remains against Rashi. Maharsha also asks that according to Rashi the same problem of doubtful warning should exist for an oath of deposit, because by hypothesis no valid witnesses are presently available and perhaps they have gone overseas. Rashi may hold that an oath of deposit can be imposed outside beit din. Even if witnesses stood there when the oath was taken and warned him, perhaps they will die before reaching beit din. That resembles witnesses currently overseas, where one who denies a debt despite existing witnesses is still liable. An oath of testimony, by contrast, must be in beit din. The witnesses who would give the warning would then themselves be present in beit din, eliminating the oath of testimony. Perhaps this is what Maharsha intended by his brief answer.
In truth, in my novellae I offered another answer to this last difficulty. We rule that one who swears falsely concerning a deposit and later admits must add the one-fifth surcharge, whereas if witnesses prove the falsehood he does not pay that surcharge. Suppose the witnesses were initially overseas and the defendant falsely denied the deposit under oath. Later the witnesses arrived, and he was asked to swear again that the deposit had never been in his possession. Even though the witnesses are now present, this second oath is not merely about an abstract fact. If he admits that the deposit had been in his possession, he thereby admits that the first oath was false and becomes liable for the one-fifth surcharge, which is monetary. If he continues to deny it, he avoids that additional payment. The present witnesses can therefore warn him, “Do not swear falsely, for we know the facts,” even though they are now before beit din. The Gemara can still infer that one who denies a loan despite witnesses overseas is liable, because if such a denial were exempt, the original first oath concerning the deposit would not have been an oath of deposit at all and no one-fifth surcharge could ever arise; it would merely be an oath of expression. This construction does not apply to an oath of testimony, where there is no one-fifth surcharge and no analogous second oath. Maharsha’s difficulty can thus be answered, though it is difficult to attribute such an elaborate case to Rashi when he did not say it. A more plausible approach is that Rashi and Tosafot intended the same basic point. The main reason lashes cannot be imposed for an oath of testimony is the possibility of forgetfulness, as Tosafot say. The Ran asks that one could warn at the time of the event. But if there are no other witnesses, who will later establish the violation? The witness’s own admission cannot create lashes. If there are other valid witnesses standing there, then there is no oath of testimony because they can supply the testimony. One must therefore say that the validating witnesses had already left. But then it is no longer literally the time of the event. They could not have flown away instantly; some time must have passed for them to leave the city, and during that interval the prospective witnesses can say they forgot. If one says that other witnesses were present but their existence was unknown to the parties, then how did they give a warning? In the Mishnah’s case of disqualification it is possible that their existence was unknown, but for actual lashes they must give a warning. There remains a possible case where the warning witnesses are relatives of the creditor and debtor but unrelated to the witnesses. They saw the event and could warn immediately. Yet if one member of the witnessing group is related or disqualified, the entire testimony may be invalidated. See Tosafot in Shevuot 35 and what I wrote above in siman 36. One cannot simply say that the relatives came only to observe and therefore do not invalidate, because, as explained above in siman 36 from the Shach, beit din does not interrogate the witnesses before the testimony is received to determine whether each came merely to observe or to testify. Until testimony is received, the related witness’s statement is not enough. The warning therefore remains doubtful: perhaps he will later say that he came to testify, which would retroactively invalidate the group. Each warning witness also does not know what the other intends. For the warned witness himself, who must accept the warning in a manner exposing himself to lashes, the uncertainty is even clearer. Perhaps the related observers will later say, “We came to testify,” and retroactively there will have been no valid oath of testimony. This makes the warning doubtful. One might still ask about a case where all were unrelated at the time of the event but became related before the oath. Then the original testimony remains valid because they were unrelated when they saw the event, while the newly related observers can no longer testify but can still warn the others. Yet becoming related generally occurs through marriage, which cannot happen instantaneously. Again some time must have passed, and the witnesses can claim forgetfulness. This supports the framework above. It also resolves Maharsha’s difficulty from an oath of deposit. As the Ran writes in his novellae, and as I cited at the end of siman 34, witnesses related to the creditor cannot testify so as to cause the debtor lashes for swearing falsely against that creditor, because that creates an interest: the debtor may pay the creditor to avoid punishment. In a deposit case, however, there can have been valid witnesses when the deposit was made, along with related observers who did not intend to testify. The valid witnesses then left overseas. When the custodian later denied the deposit, the related observers could warn him not to swear falsely. Even if it is uncertain whether they might later say they had originally intended to testify and thereby invalidate the original witnessing, the basic false oath denying the deposit would still exist regardless. Unlike an oath of testimony, whose very legal character disappears if the testimony is invalid, an oath denying a deposit remains a false oath. The Gemara’s inference that one who denies a loan despite witnesses overseas is liable also remains sound. Without any valid witnesses at all, no one could later impose lashes, since witnesses related to the creditor cannot do so, although they can provide a warning because they know with certainty that the oath is false. This is consistent. Where forgetfulness is not plausible in the deposit case, one may also posit that the witnesses were originally unrelated and later became related, so their original witnessing remains valid and the warning is certain. The Keritot difficulty is likewise resolved. One can posit observers related to the creditor but unrelated to the witnesses who were present at the same time. One might object that joining relatives would invalidate the testimony, but that is relevant according to Rabbi, whereas according to Rabbi Yosi the monetary testimony remains valid. The Gemara’s statement that Rabbi Meir agrees with the Sages cannot be explained merely by saying, “Perhaps they forgot,” because that might work according to Rabbi but not according to Rabbi Yosi. See Sanhedrin 9 and Maharsha, who understand Tosafot’s question from the principle that the victim himself would save the defendant by being an interested or hostile witness and note that Rabbi Yehudah presumably does not disagree with Rabbi Yosi. Rabbi Meir’s view is itself identified there with Rabbi Yosi under Ravina’s explanation, where the disqualification rule does not apply to monetary matters. Thus the forgetfulness answer alone cannot explain Keritot. There is another, shorter route to justify Rashi. Rashi in Sanhedrin 23 reads the dispute whether a party who claims to have two sets of witnesses must clarify and produce both sets. If he produces only one set, he cannot extract money. The Amoraim dispute this in Bava Batra, chapter Get Pashut. If so, how does the Gemara infer that one who denies a loan despite existing witnesses is liable simply because witnesses later contradict him and say he swore falsely? Perhaps the claimant asserted that he had two sets of witnesses but produced only one, and Rabbah holds like the view that one must clarify. The claimant then cannot recover money through the single set. Still, those witnesses may be fully valid to establish that the defendant swore falsely, because the claimant’s own overstatement that he had two sets prevents monetary recovery but does not render the existing set invalid. This creates a serious difficulty unless we invoke the Ran’s principle that witnesses whose testimony would pressure the defendant into paying cannot be used to punish him, because that indirectly advances the creditor’s monetary claim. If the claimant, by saying he had two sets, made one set insufficient for collection, using that one set to impose lashes would pressure the defendant to pay and thus circumvent the rule requiring clarification. Therefore they cannot create the punishment. And with this, the Rambam’s formulation and the surrounding cases can be understood consistently. This sort of analysis shows how carefully the cases must be framed. The upshot is that ordinary claims of forgetfulness concerning one’s own monetary obligations or deposits are not accepted as they are regarding another’s testimony, and the Rashba’s ruling can coexist with the Ran’s discussion.
But concerning an oath about future conduct, etc. According to Rashi’s explanation and the Rosh’s textual version cited above at the beginning of siman 28 in Urim, if a person adjures a witness, “If you know testimony, come and testify,” and the witness remains silent without verbally denying knowledge but simply fails to testify, his silence may count as denial. In such a case, at the moment of adjuration no falsehood left his mouth. He could still have testified truthfully. Only afterward, by failing to testify, did he violate the oath. This is exactly like one who swears, “I will not eat,” and then eats almost immediately. We nevertheless say that he is not rendered suspect merely because, at the moment the oath left his mouth, it was not false. The same should therefore apply to an oath of testimony in this form. If so, there remains a difficulty with the Mishnah’s omission of an oath of expression on the ground that its status is not uniform. An oath of testimony is likewise not uniform, because in the case just described the person may not become suspect. One must answer that the Mishnah lists only cases in which a korban for the oath can arise. In the type of oath of testimony just described, where the liability itself is doubtful, no korban would be brought, and the Mishnah is not discussing it. This requires study. There is also a difficulty with the Rivash, responsum 311, cited above in siman 34. He rules that one who swears to eat and then does not eat is not suspect because the violation is a prohibition without an act, for which lashes are not administered. But an oath of testimony likewise carries no lashes, as explained by the Gemara cited above. Why then does one who violates it become suspect? In the case of an oath of deposit, where the Rambam likewise rules that there are no lashes, there is no difficulty, because as I wrote above in siman 34, one who acts out of desire for money is a wicked person of theft, and actual lashes are not required. But what can be said concerning an oath of testimony? One cannot answer that the only reason there are no lashes is the possibility that he forgot, whereas if the falsehood were clear he would receive lashes. That answer applies only at an earlier stage of the discussion. The final halachah is that even an oath of deposit carries no lashes because the Torah assigned a korban to it. The same should be true of an oath of testimony. Perhaps the answer is that an intentional oath of testimony at least creates korban liability in the relevant framework, and the korban stands in place of lashes. This is strained. Alternatively, because withholding testimony causes another person monetary loss, and an oath of testimony exists only where the testimony concerns a monetary claim, the violator is also within the category of a wicked person of theft, who disqualifies himself through monetary wrongdoing even without lashes. This requires study.
