An old doubtful loan. The Tumim asks from the law that the same persons may serve as agents and witnesses in kiddushin, and similarly below in siman 121, where agents are believed to say that they delivered the money to the creditor because they could have said that they returned it to the debtor. He asks: what migo is this? We do not suspect them of outright theft, but only say that perhaps they have an old doubtful claim. It is therefore possible that they have an old doubtful claim against the creditor and not against the debtor, so that they are compelled to say that they delivered it to the creditor. In my opinion this is not difficult at all, because there the credibility is necessarily not based on ordinary migo. If it were an ordinary migo, several difficulties would arise. First, the language there implies that if they were appointed as agents to lend money to a debtor, they too are believed by this reasoning, because the Mishnah states generally that the same applies in monetary matters. Yet ordinary migo cannot extract money. Second, what superior claim is afforded by saying that they returned it to the debtor rather than that they gave it to the creditor? A migo ordinarily rests on the availability of a more advantageous claim. Third, the Gemara concludes that nowadays they cannot testify because they are witnesses who require an oath. If their credibility rested on migo, why would that matter? Fourth, if ordinary migo were the basis, even one agent, or a relative or otherwise disqualified witness, should be believed by virtue of the same migo. Rather, the Gemara's reasoning is that they are believed as witnesses. The concern is merely that perhaps they are giving false testimony for their own benefit because they want to retain and steal the money. To this the Gemara answers that they have an alternative, meaning not that migo itself confers credibility, but that they are not considered interested witnesses. Had they wanted to steal the money, they could have done so without giving false testimony, by saying that they returned it to the debtor, thereby avoiding the prohibition of false testimony. Certainly, if they testified that on a certain day they delivered the money to the creditor and were then discredited by hazamah, they would incur lashes for false testimony or payment under the law of “as he conspired to do,” since their credibility is that of witnesses, and many poskim require testimony in monetary matters to be susceptible to hazamah. Thus, if they wanted the money for themselves, they would not give false testimony but would simply say they returned it to the debtor. This also answers the Tumim's question that perhaps they have an old doubtful loan against the creditor but not against the debtor. The Gemara in Bava Metzia 6 says that people refrain from a doubtful oath more readily than from doubtful monetary wrongdoing because money can be restored, whereas an oath cannot. Shitah Mekubetzet there writes that in the case of interest, where returning the money does not repair the prohibition itself, that distinction no longer applies. Here too, even if the agent has a definite monetary claim against the creditor, the debtor is not thereby discharged, as explained below in siman 128 seif 3. Certainly, if the agent has only a doubtful claim and seizes the money for that doubt, the debtor is not discharged and must immediately pay other money. Therefore, when the witnesses testify that they gave the money to the creditor and thereby discharge the debtor, if their testimony is false they violate the prohibition against false testimony, and that prohibition is not repaired by restitution. It is therefore analogous to a doubtful oath. We say that even if they have an old doubtful monetary claim against the creditor, they would not give false testimony. They would instead say, “We returned it to the debtor,” and retain the money until their doubt became clarified. People refrain from false testimony more than from doubtful monetary seizure. Since they nevertheless testify so as to discharge the debtor, their testimony is accepted as true. This also answers the Tumim's difficulty in his rules of migo, siman 138, against Maharit, who rules that we do not say a migo from one person to another. Tumim asks from siman 121, where the agent becomes a witness because he could have claimed that he returned the money to the debtor, and similarly from the incident involving Avimi in Ketubot, discussed above in siman 58. According to what I have explained, there is no comparison. Those cases do not derive from the doctrine of migo. We see that this reasoning applies even where ordinary migo does not, such as a migo to extract money and other situations. Their credibility stems from testimony, and the alternative claim merely removes the concern that they are lying to steal the money, because they could have stolen it without false testimony. By contrast, when an ordinary litigant, whether claimant or defendant, offers a migo that shifts the monetary loss from one person to another, such a migo is not accepted. Tumim also asks from the incident of the gardeners in Gittin, below in siman 126, where a person is believed to say, “I erred,” because he could have said, “I returned it.” I do not understand the difficulty. It appears that Tumim understood “migo from one person to another” to mean merely that under the present claim he contradicts one person and under the alternative claim he would contradict another. That is not the definition. It means that under the present claim he takes money from one person, while under the alternative claim he would take from another. Such a migo is not accepted. In the case of the gardeners, the transfer was made by ma'amad sheloshtan and the recipient acquired it. Whether the transferor says, “I erred,” or says, “I returned it,” the monetary loss falls upon the same person. It is therefore a migo concerning one person. See Tumim, who cites the Ran that where a person previously waived all claims against the other party, we no longer suspect an old doubtful loan. Tumim concludes that if a person has been released from all other claims and nevertheless holds collateral or is otherwise one who swears and collects, care must be taken because perhaps no oath is required. In my opinion this is incorrect. One may still say that the claimant himself is uncertain about the very claim asserted by the defendant. For example, where the defendant says, “I paid you,” and the claimant denies it, perhaps the claimant has forgotten and is uncertain, but states his claim as definite until he remembers. In Bava Metzia that possibility could not be used, because the oath was enacted so that people would not simply seize property from one another. In the case of two holding a garment, the oath was not enacted merely because a person might make a mistaken assumption, but because of concern for deliberate seizure, and therefore the Gemara had to invoke the possibility of an old doubtful loan. Here the same limitation does not apply. Moreover, the Ran's basic reasoning is difficult. What does it matter that the claimant waived all other claims? If he knew that at the time of the waiver he had forgotten one debt, the waiver would surely be mistaken and therefore ineffective. He may be unable to prove in beit din that the waiver was mistaken, but if he himself knows the truth he may seize and claim in beit din that the property is his when he has a migo. Perhaps the Ran holds that a mistaken waiver is nevertheless effective. According to our ruling that a mistaken waiver is not effective, his reasoning does not apply. Therefore, no special caution of the kind suggested by Tumim is necessary.
