But without witnesses he is not suspect, etc. At first this seems obvious, since a person does not become suspect through his own admission, even if he admits circumstances where there is no possibility of an old debt. The Sema seeks to explain that the Tur and Shulchan Aruch intend only to reject the Gemara’s possible inference that one suspected regarding money is necessarily suspected regarding an oath. I do not understand that explanation. The Rosh writes that there is no practical difference between the Gemara’s two resolutions, as I shall explain in the following seif katan. If that is the point, the authorities should simply have stated that one does not say, “Since he is suspect regarding money, he is suspect regarding an oath.” It therefore seems that the case is as follows. An object is in Shimon’s possession, and Shimon says that he inherited it from his ancestors. He thereby admits that he did not purchase it from Reuven. Reuven establishes through witnesses, by clear visual recognition and distinctive identifying marks, that the object is his. They also saw it recently in Reuven’s possession, in circumstances making it impossible that Shimon’s ancestors acquired it from Reuven. Shimon must therefore return the object. Nevertheless, he does not thereby become suspect regarding an oath, because it remains possible that he took it in satisfaction of an old debt that Reuven owed him. In such a case we may speak of an actual old loan, not merely a speculative possibility, because we judge his seizure in a favorable way. The authorities already ask in Shitah Mekubetzet why the same reasoning is not used regarding one who denies a deposit. Many answers are given there. One is that if Shimon really had a claim against Reuven, Reuven would not have deposited property with him. Another is that because Shimon brazenly denies a deposit that was entrusted to him before witnesses, we do not assume an old debt. See the end of siman 34, where I concluded that this latter explanation is the primary one in understanding the Shulchan Aruch. Similarly, where one denies a loan under oath, we do not defend him by saying that perhaps he has an old debt against the claimant. If that were his position, he should have stated his claim explicitly rather than swearing in general terms and thereby uttering a false oath. This is the point.
An old loan. In Bava Metzia the Gemara repeatedly asks: should we not say that one who is suspect regarding money is also suspect regarding an oath? One answer is that we do not make that inference at all. Abaye instead answers that perhaps the person has an old loan against the claimant and therefore withholds the money, while people refrain from a doubtful oath even when they do not refrain from holding doubtful money, because money can later be returned. The Rif cites Abaye’s answer. The Rosh writes that there is no practical difference between the two answers; they disagree only about the underlying rationale. The Derishah asks that Nimukei Yosef and the Ran identify a significant practical difference. If before the oath the claimant fully waives all old claims against the defendant, then according to Abaye there should be no oath, because the explanation based on an old debt has disappeared and the question returns: if he is suspect regarding money, why entrust him with an oath? According to the first answer, however, he still swears, because monetary suspicion simply does not imply oath suspicion. How then can the Rosh say there is no practical difference? The answer is that the Gemara did not need either of these resolutions for most of the cases, because those could be explained by saying that the defendant is merely temporarily withholding payment and intends to pay later. The special answers are needed mainly for three cases: two people holding one garment, where the Sages imposed an oath so that everyone should not seize another’s garment; Rabbi Chiya’s teaching concerning a shopkeeper on his ledger, where both shopkeeper and workers swear and collect from the employer; and the guardian who must take the three oaths. Regarding an ordinary hesset oath, Tosafot also say in one explanation that temporary withholding can apply. With that in mind, why does Abaye need to posit a separate old debt? In the shopkeeper case the shopkeeper may simply be uncertain whether he actually paid the workers and, because people do not refrain from doubtful money, he seeks reimbursement from the employer. The workers may likewise be uncertain whether they received payment and therefore seek their wages. In the garment case the Gemara itself says there need not be a definite liar, because perhaps both lifted it together; each may be uncertain and think he lifted it first, so each holds the garment from doubt but will not swear on a doubt. The need for an old-loan rationale arises particularly from the guardian’s oaths, such as the oath that he did not use the entrusted item for himself or that it is no longer in his possession. There, if the deposit was entrusted before witnesses or by document, he cannot plausibly be uncertain whose item it was. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 3b therefore explain that the Gemara’s suspicion question concerning the three guardian oaths focuses specifically on the oath that he did not use the deposit. Abaye’s answer is that the guardian perhaps believes the depositor owes him money and therefore treats the deposit as effectively his own, making use of it seem permitted. This is straightforward. Once so understood, Nimukei Yosef’s proposed case of waiver does not create a practical difference. In the shopkeeper and garment cases no old loan is needed at all. The parties may be uncertain about the very transaction at issue, and the claimant’s waiver of unrelated old claims does nothing. In the guardian case, where the old debt is used to explain his prior use of the deposit, the timing of the waiver does not help either. If the claimant waived all claims before the deposit was ever entrusted, perhaps a new doubtful claim arose afterward and before the guardian used the object. We do not know when the supposed old debt arose; it could have arisen immediately before the accident or loss. If the claimant waives only now, when the parties come before beit din, that does not alter the past. Earlier, when the guardian used the deposit, he may have thought he had an old debt and therefore did not regard his conduct as forbidden. His later waiver does not retroactively render him suspect. Thus the Rosh is correct that there is no practical difference.
Now, this also allows another answer, according to Nimukei Yosef and those who follow him, to a question I raised in the preceding siman. Tosafot explain that the Gemara challenges Rabbi Chiya’s teaching that both shopkeeper and workers swear, because Rabbi at first thought only the workers should swear. I asked: even if only the workers swear, why not ask that they are suspect regarding money and therefore suspect regarding an oath? The same question could be raised from every category of one who swears and collects. The answer may be that the Gemara’s statement that people withdraw from a doubtful oath because an oath cannot be reversed, unlike doubtful possession of money, is needed to explain even where the oath’s wording may not correspond literally to reality. Why not answer more simply that in the garment case the person would certainly swear falsely if he says, “I lifted it first,” when in truth he did not, and that in the shopkeeper case he would swear, “I paid the workers,” when he did not? The explanation must cover a more general class, including a defendant who is sued for a maneh and responds, “I have an offsetting claim against you.” In such a case he may swear according to what he genuinely believes, so the distinction is that he will not swear on a mere doubt. Once the Gemara already understood that the shopkeeper or workers might be uncertain about the underlying payment itself, ordinary cases of workers who swear and collect were not difficult. They may seek money because of uncertainty and withdraw when required to swear. Rabbi Chiya’s special formulation that both swear in each other’s presence creates a different question. The Gemara explains that the workers swear in front of the shopkeeper so that he will be embarrassed if they lie, and vice versa. Why should this matter if one who is willing to take money improperly is equally willing to swear falsely? If the parties merely thought the other might be mistaken, the presence requirement would add little. Abaye’s old-loan explanation provides the answer. The workers know with certainty whether they received the specific wage payment. They do not think the shopkeeper simply forgot a payment that occurred moments earlier. Rather, they may think the shopkeeper is withholding because of some older claim he believes he has. Therefore they are not embarrassed by his ordinary monetary claim. But if he must swear in their presence, “I actually gave this payment into your hands,” the oath’s factual statement is plainly false even if he has some unrelated old claim, and he may then be ashamed and withdraw. The same works in reverse. The Gemara’s statement that people withdraw from a doubtful oath is still needed for ordinary hesset cases, but the face-to-face requirement is especially explained by the old-loan concept. This resolves the earlier question. This understanding of Nimukei Yosef also illuminates another issue in the rules of migo. How did Rabbah know that a migo based on an audacious denial does not exempt from an oath? One might say that the Torah obligated a partial admitter even though he could have denied everything because the alternative is audacious, but perhaps the real reason is simply Abaye’s old-loan theory. If the claimant waives all old claims, that explanation disappears. According to Nimukei Yosef, however, this itself proves that a migo based on an audacious claim does not exempt from an oath. This is Rashi’s meaning in explaining that in the partial-admission case the defendant must admit some amount and cannot avoid the oath merely through a migo of total denial. Tosafot ask that we already rule that monetary suspicion does not imply oath suspicion. According to what I have written, Rashi can hold that Rabbah agrees with Abaye that the initial suspicion question is softened by the possibility of an old loan. When all prior claims are waived, the old-loan concern disappears, yet the Torah still requires the partial admitter to swear. The reason must be that the migo of total denial is too audacious to exempt him from an oath. Finally, the old-loan theory creates a large conceptual difficulty in other rules. In Kiddushin, and below in siman 121, where an agent claims that he delivered money to the creditor and the creditor denies receipt, the agent can sometimes be believed by a migo that he could have denied receiving the money from the debtor. Is this not a migo from one person to another? If the reason the agent is withholding is that he has an old or doubtful claim against the creditor, that does not provide any reason for him to lie to the debtor, against whom he has no such claim. How can the alternative denial against the debtor support his credibility against the creditor? One can attempt various local answers in Kiddushin, but the question recurs in the broader rules of migo. It therefore seems clear that “perhaps he has an old loan” is not a strong factual presumption. If so, why would he not simply present that claim to beit din, as the Razah and others note? The better framework is that the proposition “one suspected regarding money is suspected regarding an oath” is not an absolute rule. Some people who wrongfully withhold money are still deeply reluctant to swear falsely, as daily experience shows, while others whose desire overcomes them in monetary matters may also be unreliable regarding oaths. This is why a robber is disqualified from swearing: beit din must fear that he may be among those for whom even an oath is not sufficiently severe. Abaye does not replace that with certainty that an old loan exists. He adds another possible justification: perhaps the person has an old claim. We therefore have two separate uncertainties favoring the possibility that the oath will still restrain him. Perhaps oaths are more severe to him even though money is not; and even if not, perhaps his monetary conduct arose from an old claim that he regards as legitimate. With these combined considerations we require him to swear. In oath law, even a relatively weak reason is enough to impose the oath, much as a migo involving an audacious claim does not exempt from an oath. If his claim is true, what harm is there in requiring him to swear? In monetary extraction, by contrast, where even a weak migo can be effective, the remote possibility of an old loan is not enough to defeat a migo. This distinction resolves the conceptual difficulties. It is an important general principle.
