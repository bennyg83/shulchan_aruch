The concern that perhaps he has an old loan against the claimant indicates that this authority accepts the principle that one suspected concerning money may be suspected concerning an oath unless there is a basis to explain his conduct through some old debt. According to the view that we do not infer oath suspicion from monetary suspicion, this reason would not be needed. Ran notes a practical difference. If the parties previously executed a general release of all claims through a specified date, then there is no room to suspect an old loan from before that date. If a new claim arises a day or two later in a setting where the defendant would otherwise swear and collect, such as on a document, collateral, or a known object, the judge must consider whether any plausible old debt could have arisen in that short interval. The length of time sufficient to create concern cannot be fixed mechanically and depends on judicial assessment. In a very short period there may be no basis for the old debt suspicion. This is a common practical issue and judges should be attentive to it. Shitah Mekubetzet cites Raah that there is another difference in a claim of fixed prohibited interest. If a claimant says, "You took one hundred from me as fixed interest," and the defendant denies it, according to one approach he can swear. According to Abaye, however, once there is suspicion that he took prohibited interest he should not be made to swear, because unlike robbery, repayment of interest does not fully repair the prohibition. Beit Yosef later cites students of Rashba that ordinary people do not understand the distinction that repayment of interest does not fully repair the prohibition, and therefore the practical law treats the case more like robbery and permits the oath. This explains the Shulchan Aruch's rules requiring an oath regarding interest claims.
