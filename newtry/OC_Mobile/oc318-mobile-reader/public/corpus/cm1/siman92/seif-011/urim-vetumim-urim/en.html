A general cherem is proclaimed. Sema questions why the cherem should not be directed at him personally, since by strict law he should even have been required to take hesset. Taz writes that this is indeed the law, and the Mechaber merely employed the customary expression. See there.
And then he is exempt even from accepting a cherem. Sema understands that he is even exempt from accepting a cherem against one who imposes a needless oath, for otherwise he could simply accept that cherem and be exempt. According to what I wrote above in siman 87, however, there is a distinction between the two forms of cherem. In this cherem he must accept upon himself that he knows with certainty that he owes nothing to his fellow. A cherem concerning a needless oath means only that it is not clear to him that the law favors his fellow, and thus his fellow is forcing him to swear needlessly. There is therefore no proof for Sema, and this distinction is correct. Sema also writes that if the claimant says, 'I do not wish to swear, rather you should accept a cherem,' we listen to him. He therefore explains that Rama and Mordechai, who write that the defendant can say this, mean that if the claimant wishes to swear, the defendant need not accept a cherem concerning a needless oath. This is strained, as Taz also writes. It therefore appears simpler that one might have thought that because a disqualified defendant has no oath obligation, he cannot shift the oath to the claimant. Ordinarily, where the defendant has a rabbinic oath, we rule that he may shift it. The ruling teaches that even here he may shift it.
