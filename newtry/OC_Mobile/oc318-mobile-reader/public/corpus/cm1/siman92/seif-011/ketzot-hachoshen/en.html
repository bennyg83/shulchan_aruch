Regarding a person suspected concerning an oath, the rule of hesset follows the Gemara's principle that we do not create one rabbinic enactment upon another. Tosafot says that the rule, "Since he cannot swear, he must pay," does not apply there. Later authorities debated whether Tosafot meant that this payment rule itself is only rabbinic. That cannot be correct. As explained in siman 75, the rule is derived from the Torah verse concerning the oath of Hashem between the two parties and not between heirs. Why, then, is it not applied to a rabbinic oath? In the ordinary cases there are two possibilities. If the defendant is suspected, disqualification from swearing is itself rabbinic, so making him pay would indeed be an enactment upon an enactment. If he says, "I do not know," however, hesset itself permits an oath of uncertainty. Unlike a Torah oath, where "I do not know" cannot satisfy the required definite oath and therefore creates payment, a rabbinic hesset oath can be taken on the uncertainty itself. The classic piece of metal case likewise cannot ordinarily arise through a merely rabbinic oath on land, slaves, or documents, because one witness does not impose the Torah oath there. Therefore the Gemara had no need to list "unable to swear and therefore pay" as a separate difference between Torah and rabbinic oaths. Wherever a genuinely comparable inability does arise in a rabbinic oath, the payment rule can apply. This explains Rama in siman 58 concerning a claim that money was applied to another debt with one supporting witness, and also the law in siman 59 where a creditor holding a document says he does not know whether it was paid. A suspected creditor can sometimes collect because we do not make one enactment upon another, but a claimant who cannot give the required substantive oath because he says, "I do not know," can lose under the ordinary rule of inability to swear.
