Just. It’s a bit difficult why Petroho chose only and did not impose an explicit boycott on him because if he wasn’t a suspect, he died as “a week” but we don’t allow him to swear because of a week that he would receive at least an explicit boycott. A:
To say. This teaches that although in a rabbinic oath we do not automatically reverse the oath when the defendant is suspect, meaning that the beit din does not force the reversal upon the defendant so that the claimant swears and collects, the defendant himself may reverse it onto the claimant. One might ask why he would do this, since without an oath he is exempt. The Rama therefore writes that by reversing the oath he becomes exempt from accepting a ban, and for that reason he may choose to reverse it. It also seems clear that the claimant may respond, 'I do not wish to swear and collect; instead, accept an unspecified ban upon yourself.' A suspect defendant cannot have greater power regarding the ban than an ordinary defendant, because one who ordinarily swears and collects may make this choice, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 82, seif 9. The suspect defendant does have this advantage: he may say to the claimant, 'If you wish, swear and collect, and thereby exempt me from every ban,' including the ban ordinarily accepted by one who causes another to swear, as stated in siman 87, seif 22. He can say, 'If I accept the ban, I will not pay you, because the Sages imposed upon me only acceptance of the ban.' An ordinary defendant, by contrast, is exempt only by a hesset oath, or by reversing that oath onto the claimant, in which case the claimant accepts the ban that he is not causing a needless oath. The ordinary defendant cannot say, 'If I accept the ban I will not pay,' because if he refuses the ban he himself must swear to be exempt; he cannot be exempt merely by accepting a ban when he is capable of swearing. See what I wrote in siman 82. Sema. The Taz disagrees. Here the suspect defendant seeks exemption from an explicit ban, and he may properly say, 'Either swear and collect, or release me from the explicit ban and impose only an unspecified ban.' In siman 82, however, the discussion concerns an unspecified ban, from which one cannot escape. The Sema's words are therefore very difficult. The Taz also challenges the Sema's statement that reversing an oath entails acceptance of a ban by the party who reverses it; see his discussion in siman 87, seif 22.
