The opposing party does not swear and collect. Teshuvot Avodat HaGershuni, siman 91, discusses a borrower known to be deceitful, against whom one witness testified that he had previously sworn falsely over a trivial sum. The question was whether the judge could reverse the oath onto the claimant or whether the defendant should merely take a heset oath and be exempt. The responsum first writes that neither the single witness nor the defendant's reputation alone removes his ordinary procedural rights. Nevertheless, where the judge has compelling factual indications that the defendant is lying, he may reverse the oath onto the claimant. Although the Geonim restricted reliance on personal confidence in a litigant in our times, compelling objective presumptions may still be used, as appears from responsa of the Rosh and later authorities. Thus, if such a compelling presumption exists, the judge may reverse the oath; if there is only one witness to an earlier false oath and a general reputation for deceit, he may not reverse the oath to extract money, though he may impose a more stringent form of oath according to the circumstances. Shevut Yaakov, part III, siman 152, similarly allowed an attachment where an apostate wished to sell his synagogue seat and a reliable merchant had a debt against him recorded in his ledger. Although ordinarily an oral claim does not justify an attachment and reversal does not generally apply to a heset oath, the court treated the merchant as entitled to swear and collect in those exceptional circumstances. He may say. Baer Heitev cites the Sema's view that even if the claimant wishes, he may say, 'I will not swear,' thereby forcing the suspect party to accept a general ban. Teshuvot Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat siman 71, questions this. If the claimant has the power to force the suspected defendant to accept the ban, what is added by the Ramah's statement that the defendant may choose this course? If the defendant proposes it and the claimant agrees, no ruling is needed. The natural reading of the Ramah is that the choice belongs to the defendant. Beit Ephraim also questions the Sema's analogy to siman 82, especially according to the Sema's own view that even one who swears and collects requires the opposing party first to accept a general ban. The matter therefore requires study. See also Urim VeTumim. As to whether reversing an oath entails a general ban upon the party who reverses it, see what I wrote above, siman 87, seif 22, seif katan 23.
