If the suspected person was a custodian, Ramah and Rabbi Yeshayah hold that because the custodian cannot take the required oath, he must pay. This proves what I wrote in siman 75, seif katan 9: even where the claimant does not make a certain factual claim, the rule of one obligated to swear but unable to swear can apply when the defendant himself says, "I do not know." This is contrary to the Taz and Pnei Yehoshua. Even the authorities who disagree with Ramah and Rabbi Yeshayah do so because the claimant cannot take the shifted oath. Where the claimant does not need any oath, for example because the defendant himself pleads uncertainty in a manner that creates inability to swear, payment follows.
The Shulchan Aruch says, "or he was negligent." Its wording suggests the depositor makes a certain claim, since it says he claims that the custodian misappropriated or was negligent. According to the Shulchan Aruch, the principal custodian's oath is that the object is not in his possession, as explained in siman 294. If the depositor says with certainty that the custodian consumed or disposed of it, the custodian is effectively admitting it is no longer in his possession. The principal oath would then appear unnecessary, and without a principal oath there should be no oath extension to misappropriation or negligence. Perhaps the phrase is written to accommodate the authorities who hold that the principal custodial oath is the oath of no negligence. According to Tosafot, Rosh, and Rama, the oath against negligence is independently principal, while the oath against misappropriation is by extension. Thus "or he was negligent" makes sense under that view. In any event, this passage proves that custodial oaths can arise even when the depositor makes a certain claim. The Shach, in defending his position in siman 72 that no custodial oath applies where the depositor claims certainty, tries to limit our seif to the opening case where the depositor is not certain that the custodian consumed the object. He says that although no Torah oath would then arise concerning negligence or misappropriation because the claimant's claim is certain, the custodian would still have to take the oath that he did not consume it, and the opposing party can then swear that there was misappropriation or negligence. This is difficult. Everyone agrees that the oath against misappropriation is only by oath extension. According to Maggid Mishneh and the Shulchan Aruch, even the oath against negligence is extended from the principal oath that the object is not in his possession. According to Tosafot and Rosh, negligence may be principal, but misappropriation certainly is not. If the claimant's certain assertion removes the principal oath, from where does the extended misappropriation oath come? Thus the better conclusion is that a custodian can indeed face the relevant custodial oath even against a certain claimant, depending upon which substantive oath is at issue. The Shach's attempt to harmonize his earlier position does not fully succeed.
