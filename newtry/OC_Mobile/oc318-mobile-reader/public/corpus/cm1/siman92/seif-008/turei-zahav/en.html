(Ibid.) “And Rabbi Yeshayah, etc., a Torah oath,” etc. This means that if the defendant were obligated only by a rabbinic oath, such as the oath between partners, and the claimant likewise could not swear in his stead, the disqualified defendant would certainly be exempt. By Torah law he is entirely exempt; the Sages merely required an oath, and in such a case the matter is left to Torah law. Here, however, the oath is of Torah origin, and we may properly apply the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. Tosafot’s question, cited by Sema, seif katan 13 or 21, may be raised against Rabbi Yeshayah: the two answers Tosafot give there do not apply here, for according to those concerns the bailee too should not pay. This can be answered somewhat by saying that Rabbi Yeshayah follows the answer that otherwise disqualified people would be unable to live. A disqualified bailee is different, because he can avoid accepting responsibility as a bailee. In ordinary commerce, however, it is impossible to avoid all transactions.
