If the suspect person was a bailee, etc. The Tur records that Rabbenu Yeshayah and the Ramah disagree and maintain that the depositor collects without an oath. The Bach writes that according to one answer of Tosafot in Bava Metzia, where Tosafot asks why, when the defendant is suspect, the opposing party should swear, since this should be a case of one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear and therefore pays, the answer is that the oath of the opposing party was instituted for the benefit of the suspect defendant. That consideration does not apply to a bailee, since no one compelled him to accept the bailment, and accidents are uncommon. We therefore return to the Torah law that one who cannot take his required oath must pay. On that basis the views of Rabbenu Yeshayah and the Ramah appear sound. At first glance this is persuasive. Yet if so, the Tur should have mentioned that this is also the view of his father, the Rosh, for in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in the Rosh distinguishes between a suspect person, whose opponent swears and takes, and an ordinary case of one who cannot swear and must pay, and explains the distinction as a provision for the livelihood of the suspect. According to the Bach, the Rosh therefore agrees with the Ramah and Rabbenu Yeshayah. One should likewise wonder why the Rama did not note that Rabbenu Yeshayah, the Ramah, the Rosh, and Tosafot in Shevuot, who give only this first answer, disagree with the ruling before us. It therefore seems necessary to understand Tosafot in Bava Metzia, s.v. "We do not make an enactment upon an enactment," where they write that the principle of one who cannot swear and therefore pays does not apply here, as they explained. Earlier they had given an answer that this principle does not apply to a suspect defendant because he cries out, "I am prepared to swear." Besides the question what the first answer would then mean, there is a further difficulty. Regarding a hesset oath, even one who explicitly says, "I do not know," is not subject to the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. Rav Nachman himself rules that when one claims, "You have a maneh of mine," and the other says, "I do not know," the defendant is exempt. What then do Tosafot mean by "as we explained"? Rather, in both answers Tosafot hold that Torah law does not require a suspect defendant to pay merely because he is disqualified from taking the oath. He himself says that he knows the truth and is ready to swear, but the court refuses to believe his oath. This is not intrinsically a case of one who cannot swear. This is especially so according to Tosafot's view that where the defendant could not have been expected to know, the rule of one who cannot swear and therefore pays does not apply. Here too, from his perspective, he says that he does know; it is the court that refuses to accept his oath. His prior misconduct is merely the reason for the rabbinic penalty and is not itself the Torah law governing the present monetary claim. Tosafot therefore understand clearly that Torah law does not place a suspect defendant in the category of one who cannot swear. Rather, the Sages penalized him and treated him in that category. Tosafot's question is that once the Sages imposed that status, why did they not go further and allow the opponent to collect without an oath, as in a genuine Torah case of one who cannot swear? One answer is that they did not do so because of the need to preserve the suspect person's livelihood. The other answer is that because he insists that he is prepared to swear, the Sages deliberately distinguished him from an actual person who cannot swear and required the opposing party to swear before collecting. There is a proof to this reading. With respect to a hesset oath the Gemara says that the opposing party does not swear and collect because hesset itself is a rabbinic enactment and we do not make one enactment upon another. If Torah law itself required the suspect defendant to pay without any oath by the opposing party, and the Sages merely instituted the opponent's oath for the benefit of the suspect, the Sages never enacted anything to worsen the suspect person's position. Their entire enactment was for his benefit. How then could the Gemara describe extension of that rule to hesset as an enactment upon an enactment? In a hesset case, where Torah law has no principle of one who cannot swear and therefore pays, there is no basis at all for the opponent to collect merely by swearing unless the Sages created such a right. The expression "an enactment upon an enactment" therefore proves that the very treatment of a suspect defendant as one who cannot swear is itself a rabbinic penalty. The Sages imposed that penalty where there was an underlying oath obligation, but did not impose an additional derivative enactment in a hesset case. This is precisely Tosafot's intent when they write that the rule of one who cannot swear does not naturally apply here, as explained, because the defendant is demanding to swear. This reading is clear.
And so forth. This is also evident from Tosafot in Shevuot 47, who adopt the first answer that the opponent does not collect without an oath because of the enactment for the suspect. In s.v. "If you say," Tosafot ask according to the opinion that a certain claim prevails over an uncertain claim how the verse "the oath of Hashem shall be between them" can be applied. Why not answer that the verse concerns a suspect defendant, upon whom an oath has fallen and who consequently would have to pay? It cannot concern heirs, because heirs who say "we do not know" are exempt, as the Shach writes at the end of siman 75 that a prudent heir may simply plead lack of knowledge. If heirs would be exempt by pleading ignorance, their being suspect cannot make them worse than if they had honestly said they did not know. This demonstrates that Torah law does not require a suspect person to pay without the opposing party's oath. The rule is rabbinic. One should not infer otherwise from Tosafot's answer there that according to the view that a certain claim prevails over an uncertain one, the verse can be established according to Rabbi Tarfon. Tosafot in Bava Metzia ask, according to the opinion that we do not apply the rule of one who cannot swear and therefore pays, why every person who admits half a claim does not have a migo that he could have said regarding the other half, "I do not know." They answer that a person does not willingly plead ignorance. That explanation is needed according to the view that a certain claim does not automatically prevail over an uncertain one. According to the contrary view, if he did plead ignorance he would in any event owe the money, so no migo arises. It is therefore possible to read the verse in the straightforward case of one who says, "I have fifty and do not have fifty," and distinguish heirs because their uncertain plea exempts them. Yet there remain further considerations, including the rule that a migo to avoid an oath is recognized by Tosafot and the possibility that the reluctance to plead ignorance is itself necessary. Regarding bailees the question is also different, because their oaths inherently arise from uncertainty and it may be possible to require an oath that they do not know, as the Ran writes. In any event, the principal point remains that Tosafot understand the suspect defendant's payment rule as rabbinic rather than an application of the Torah rule of inability to swear. There is also a different way to resolve the Rif's proof from the verse "the oath of Hashem shall be between them, and not between the heirs." The Gemara first considers a case where the son claims that his father was owed a maneh and the defendant's father had admitted fifty and denied fifty. The Rif derives from the Gemara's formulation that the halachah follows Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov. This proof is not as simple as it seems, because the Gemara may merely be showing that even if one attempted to establish the verse according to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, the same objection would remain. The stronger proof is that the verse cannot simply refer to the death of the creditor, so that the debtor's partial admission to the creditor's heirs would not create an oath because they are like one returning a lost object according to the Sages. To exclude that reading, one must assume the halachah follows Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov. There is, however, room to say that the Gemara's challenge to Rav and Shmuel is specifically because Rav appears to follow Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov elsewhere. According to other Amoraim, the verse could perhaps be read according to the Sages. This also explains the Raavad's distinction that even the Sages obligate an oath when the heir himself stood by and knew the facts, whereas their exemption concerns one who says only, "My father told me." The Rif's proof can still stand because otherwise the verse could have been applied to the case of a deceased creditor whose heirs know only by report. This approach also resolves another difficulty in Shevuot 32 concerning Abaye's statement that all agree regarding one who is suspect with respect to an oath. The Gemara asks who the suspect person is. If it is the borrower, who says that he will actually swear? One might have thought Abaye follows Rav Pappa, who holds that even a dice player is included in the laws of the oath of testimony, since by Torah law his testimony could create monetary liability even though rabbinically it is ineffective. If a suspect borrower by Torah law would automatically pay because he cannot swear, then testimony against him would likewise be testimony capable of creating monetary liability on the Torah level. The Gemara's question would then be difficult. According to what has been explained, however, a suspect defendant is not subject by Torah law to the rule that inability to swear requires payment. Rabbinically, too, the claimant must swear. Thus the witness's testimony does not directly constitute monetary testimony either by Torah law or by rabbinic law, and the Gemara's question is well founded. The same analysis explains Tosafot's discussion at the end of their comments s.v. "Because." Tosafot ask why Rav and Shmuel cannot themselves accept the rule that one who cannot swear must pay, and then interpret the verse differently. If their rule that an oath returns to Sinai were independently known to exclude that principle, the question would indeed be unnecessary. But even if they accept the Torah rule in ordinary cases, they could still hold that a suspect defendant is outside its Torah scope and that the Sages merely penalized him. When both litigants are suspect there is no reason to penalize one more than the other, and the law returns to its Torah baseline, expressed as the oath returning to Sinai. Thus their position is compatible with the general rule while still producing their result in the special suspect case.
Now we must understand the Ramah and Rabbenu Yeshayah, who rule that in the case of a paid bailee the claimant collects without an oath because this is one who cannot swear and therefore must pay. It would seem all the more so that if the claim is supported by one witness, the claimant should collect without an oath. That case is stronger than a bailee, because there the claimant may himself be uncertain, whereas testimony of one witness supports a definite claim. Why should it differ from two suspect litigants, where payment is imposed without an oath? The Shach writes that even with one witness the claimant does not collect, but this follows the Rambam's approach that even a paid bailee does not pay without the claimant's oath. Under that approach we say only what the Sages expressly instituted. This is also forced by the law stated above in siman 90 concerning the employee or household member of one who was robbed, where even if that person testifies and the robber is suspect, the robbed party does not collect without the required oath. According to the Rambam this is unquestionable. According to the Ramah and Rabbenu Yeshayah, however, the matter requires consideration. There is no clear reason to distinguish. It is strained to say that the bailee caused his own loss by voluntarily accepting custody, whereas in the case of one witness the Sages were reluctant to extract money on the basis of a single witness. In the case of two suspect litigants the claimant himself also caused his problem by becoming suspect, yet money may still be extracted. The more likely conclusion is therefore that according to these authorities the claimant supported by one witness likewise collects. One difficulty is the Gemara's question, "All agree with one witness regarding one who is suspect as to an oath: who says that he will swear?" Why not establish the case simply where the claimant presents one witness against a suspect defendant, in which event the defendant cannot swear? I have already addressed this above in siman 87. More particularly, according to what was explained above in siman 75, it is possible that the Ramah follows the Tosafot answer that Abaye holds the rule of one who cannot swear and therefore pays does not apply generally, while nevertheless accepting the law of the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba and the division rule for two suspect parties. According to an opinion that does not apply the payment rule, a claim supported only by one witness would indeed not permit collection, just as under the Rambam's ruling, and the Gemara's question is therefore sound. Once we say that this law depends upon whether the rule of one who cannot swear and therefore pays is operative, the language of Tosafot in Ketubot 88 can also be read comfortably. Tosafot discuss the practical difference according to Rabbi Eliezer, whose ruling is accepted, and who holds that the oath returns to the person obligated to take it, while one who cannot swear pays. Their discussion indicates that the result in a claim against a suspect person supported by one witness turns on that very principle. This supports the distinction drawn here.
