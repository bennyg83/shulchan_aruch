Is not. The general rule is that whenever the matter can be explained in a way that avoids branding a person as disqualified from an oath, we do not establish him as suspect. Therefore, regarding a loan, which is given for expenditure, even after the due date we say that his denial may merely be an attempt to gain time. Regarding a deposit, although it is not given for expenditure, if witnesses did not see the deposited object in his possession when it was demanded, we say perhaps he does not know where he placed it or it rolled from its place, and he fears being required to take the Torah oath of bailees. He therefore says, "You never deposited it with me," which exempts him from a Torah oath until he finds it and returns it. Likewise, if he does not deny the deposit but says that he is retaining it for a debt owed him, we listen to him up to its value, provided there are no witnesses that it came to him specifically as a deposit and no witnesses presently saw it in his possession. Sema.
In his possession. Sometimes a person becomes disqualified from an oath even without witnesses who saw the property presently in his possession. For example, Reuven gave Shimon money to invest for Reuven's benefit, and afterward Shimon denied receiving anything, but it became known that he had indeed received it. Here the explanation that he was merely gaining time does not apply. If that were his intent, he should have said, "I spent the money in the investment for your benefit," since that is why it was entrusted to him. The Rosh writes this in a responsum, cited in Darkei Moshe, which notes that the reasoning is straightforward. Sema.
