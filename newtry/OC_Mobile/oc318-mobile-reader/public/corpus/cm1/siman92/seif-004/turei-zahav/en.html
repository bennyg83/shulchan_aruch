(Seif 7) “The opposing party swears and collects.” Sema questions Rama for citing the final answer, but there is no difficulty. If we knew with certainty that the claimant knew the defendant was disqualified from swearing, there would be no remedy allowing the disqualified person to exempt himself entirely. Conversely, one who certainly did not know of the disqualification should by law collect without an oath, because the disqualified defendant is obligated to swear but cannot swear. In practice, however, it is impossible to know whether the claimant knew. Who can testify that he never heard from anyone that the defendant was disqualified? Thus Rama’s words, “he does not know that he is disqualified,” mean that we do not know that he knows, while “but otherwise” means that we know clearly that he did know. The enactment is therefore understandable. The Sages were concerned that a person might know of the disqualification and pretend ignorance, leaving disqualified people unable to live under constant manufactured claims. According to the cited opinion, they therefore required every claimant in such a case to swear before collecting.
