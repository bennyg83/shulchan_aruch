Where one claims a loan from another, Shaarei Shevuot says that even if the defendant swore falsely concerning a loan, he does not thereby become a suspected swearer. The proof is that the Mishnah listing suspected parties mentions false oaths concerning testimony and deposits but does not mention an oath concerning a loan. Mordechai and Rivah take the same position. Responsa Maharam HaLevi discusses this at length and challenges Rif, who rules that if a defendant took hesset and afterward admitted part, he becomes suspected, pays the admitted part, and the opposing party swears and collects the rest. At first one might say the distinction is that a borrower who denies payment may only be delaying until he can pay. But once he actually swears, that explanation should no longer suffice. One is never permitted to swear falsely merely to delay payment. Indeed, in the deposit case the Gemara says that even where the object was no longer physically in his hand and temporary delay was plausible, once he swore falsely he became disqualified. Beit Yosef gives the correct explanation of Rivah. In an ordinary loan, people commonly phrase the oath as "I owe you nothing." Perhaps the borrower truly has some separate old claim against the lender and is therefore not lying according to his own understanding. In a deposit, by contrast, the usual oath is whether the object was ever deposited with him. If witnesses establish that it was, they establish directly that his oath was false. A difficulty remains from a custodian who denies the deposit while witnesses saw it in his possession. Tosafot says we do not suspect an old loan once witnesses establish the act of seizure, because an uncertain old debt cannot defeat certain wrongful possession. Why then in an ordinary loan, when witnesses or a document establish the debt, should we still suspect an old counterclaim and refrain from disqualifying him after he swore? The answer is that monetary liability and oath disqualification are different. If a borrower says, "I repaid," and a valid document remains outstanding, the court rejects his payment claim and makes him pay because of the strength of the document. Yet he is not automatically disqualified from future oaths, because the witnesses did not prove that he swore falsely. Perhaps he truly believed the creditor owed him an equal amount from some other matter. Siman 87 expressly rules that one who swore he owed nothing and afterward a valid document is produced must pay but does not become suspected if the testimony does not establish that he never paid. Likewise, a custodian who says, "I owe him nothing because he owes me from another matter," may still have to return the deposit because he has no right to keep another's property without proof. He can therefore be treated as a wrongdoer for monetary and testimonial purposes. But if he swore simply that he owes nothing, the court still cannot know with certainty that the oath was false. The possibility of an old counterclaim prevents oath disqualification even though it does not prevent monetary collection. This reconciles Rif and Rivah. Rif's case where the defendant first took hesset and later admitted part can make him suspected when the content of the earlier oath is thereby shown to have been certainly false. Rivah's ordinary loan rule applies where the wording leaves room that another valid claim may have offset the debt in the defendant's own mind. The decisive point for oath disqualification is not whether the court believes the monetary defense, but whether it knows with certainty that the oath itself was false.
