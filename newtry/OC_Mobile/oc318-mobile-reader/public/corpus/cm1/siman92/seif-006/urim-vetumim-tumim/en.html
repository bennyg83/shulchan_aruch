But if he says, etc. The Mechaber created this distinction because Responsa Rosh says that a person who asks for such a communal ban deserves rebuke, while Responsa Rashba appears to permit it. The Mechaber distinguishes between one who has only an uncertain suspicion and one who makes a definite claim. This is strained, because any litigant can simply tell beit din that he is certain and thereby obtain the announcement. The Bach tries another distinction. He says that the witnesses themselves must come to beit din and say, "We will not testify until a ban is publicly announced in the synagogue." This is even more difficult. Nothing of the sort appears in the responsum. If particular witnesses are known, beit din can simply order those witnesses directly to testify. Why would a general synagogue announcement be needed? The clearer distinction is between an announcement made by beit din and one made privately by the litigant. If a party asks beit din to direct its officer to announce in the name of beit din that anyone who knows the other person is disqualified should come forward, beit din should not do so. Such an announcement makes it appear that beit din itself suspects the person of being disqualified, and there is hardly a greater public humiliation. But the litigant himself may stand in the synagogue and say under ban, "Anyone who knows that so and so is disqualified should come and testify." Litigants commonly accuse one another of wrongdoing, theft, and similar matters. The private party's words do not carry the dignity or implication of a judicial finding. This is exactly the difference in the responsa. In the Rosh's case, Shimon asked that "they should announce a ban in the synagogue," meaning that beit din should make the proclamation. The Rosh says, Heaven forbid, and that such a request deserves rebuke. Rashba writes that if witnesses do not wish to testify, "the claimant may administer an oath in the synagogue to anyone who knows testimony." He places the act in the claimant's hands, not beit din's. Under this distinction the two responsa agree, and the practical rule is clear.
That it is within "if he does not tell." This wording might imply that a witness who withholds testimony that another person is disqualified violates the Torah prohibition of withholding testimony. That is not generally correct. Such testimony ordinarily only creates an indirect monetary consequence. If the witness establishes that one litigant is suspected of false oaths, the result may be that the opposing party becomes entitled to swear and collect. But who says that opposing party would actually swear? The Gemara in Shevuat HaEdut explicitly treats this type of testimony as merely causing money rather than constituting direct monetary testimony, and under our ruling an indirect cause is not treated as money for the offering of testimony. Rashba therefore chose his language carefully. He says that one who withholds such testimony is liable in the heavenly court, not that he violates the verse "if he does not tell." Bava Kamma similarly asks concerning one witness who withholds testimony: is liability in Heaven not already written in the verse? Tosafos explains that the verse's direct sanction depends on the categories carrying the testimony offering, while one witness may create only indirect monetary consequence and therefore is not literally included. He is nevertheless liable in Heaven. The same applies here. The people addressed by the claimant's synagogue oath are generally not subject to the formal offering of testimony for withholding this information, because the testimony is only indirectly monetary. That is why Rashba says "liable in Heaven" and does not say they violate the formal oath of testimony. The Rama's wording should therefore be understood only as comparing the moral obligation to ordinary testimony, not as creating the full halachic category in every case. There can, however, be circumstances where the testimony becomes directly monetary, such as where both parties are otherwise disqualified and proving the second disqualification changes who collects without an oath. In such a case the formal rules may apply.
