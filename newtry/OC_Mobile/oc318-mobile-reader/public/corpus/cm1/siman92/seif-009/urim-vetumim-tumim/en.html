He takes a hesset oath, etc. According to this ruling, where the defendant has only a hesset oath, we must say that when the Mishnah originally instituted the law of a suspect litigant, before hesset existed, the Sages necessarily ruled that a suspect person holding a document loses the document and the opposing party need not take any oath at all. Otherwise that oath would have had to be a Mishnah level rabbinic oath, since hesset had not yet been instituted. Evidently, when one seeks to collect the Sages required the opposing party to swear, but they did not create such an oath merely to cause loss. It follows clearly that where both are suspect, the holder of the document does not collect. No Mishnah oath was ever imposed on the defendant in this situation. At most there is later a hesset obligation analogous to an oral claim. If he is suspect, why should the document holder lose merely because of a hesset oath? We certainly do not create one enactment upon another in hesset. The Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer siman 96 likewise rules that even when both parties are suspect, the holder of the document loses. It is true that the Rosh in chapter HaKotev, as reflected in the Tur's summary and as the Maharsha explains Tosafot, agrees that when both are suspect the defendant must pay because he cannot swear to exempt himself. I discussed above in siman 82 a distinction between a ketubah, which is rabbinic, and an ordinary debt document, which has Torah force. Nevertheless, this point requires analysis. It appears clear that the Rosh understands the oath by which the opposing party swears and is exempt from a document held by a suspect claimant as a Mishnah oath. At the end of Kol HaNishba'in the Rosh is very uncertain whether a suspect person may collect a document from heirs, asking whether that case is included in Rav and Shmuel's rule or falls under the principle, "Do not extend the enactment." He adduces proof from Rav's refusal to collect a ketubah for a widow. Yet if in an ordinary document held by a suspect person the opposing party simply swears and defeats the document, as the Rosh cites in the name of the Rif, then one would expect all the more that collection from heirs would fail. The uncertainty must therefore concern a case in which the opposing side cannot swear. The Rosh holds that where two living litigants are both suspect the document is not automatically lost, so he asks whether heirs who cannot take the relevant oath are treated like two suspect parties and permit collection, or whether the stricter law concerning heirs causes the document to be lost. This shows that the ordinary oath against a suspect document holder is a Mishnah oath, for in the era of Rav and Shmuel hesset did not yet exist. Accordingly, the Tur's formulation describing it as hesset requires study, as the Bach also writes. As a matter of practical law it seems clear that a Mishnah oath is required, especially since other authorities hold that the opposing party may defeat the document without any oath at all; there is no basis to reduce the requirement merely to hesset.
And he is exempt from the document. The Shach challenges this view from the rule that when one who must swear before collecting a document dies, the Sages said, "Do not extend it," so the heirs do not necessarily lose. Why should a suspect creditor be worse than a deceased one? The Rif requires explanation here. We cannot simply rely on the Ran's distinction that the suspect person's own misconduct caused his inability to swear, whereas death was unavoidable, because the Rif does not appear to accept that reasoning as decisive. If he did, he would not have rejected the Ran's view that where both litigants are suspect they divide the money merely from the halachah that one who cannot swear pays. Rabbi Nachman could accept that general rule while imposing a special penalty of division on two suspect parties. Rabbenu Yerucham indeed advances such a distinction. The Rif's rejection implies another basis is needed. It seems that the Rif was driven by Tosafot's question in Shevuot. The Gemara treats Rabbi Abba, who holds that one who cannot swear pays, as inconsistent with Rav and Shmuel's doctrine that a person cannot transmit to his heirs a monetary claim dependent upon an oath. Tosafot ask why. Perhaps Rabbi Abba agrees that inability to swear causes payment and, correspondingly, inability to take the collecting oath causes loss. Rav and Shmuel would then certainly say so even for a rabbinic oath. Tosafot answer with some difficulty. The Rif can be understood more simply. The Sages modeled a Mishnah oath upon a Torah oath. Just as one who cannot take a Torah oath must pay, one who cannot take the Mishnah oath required for collection loses. But they did not make the derivative rule stronger than its Torah model. Regarding a Torah oath, the verse "the oath of Hashem shall be between them" excludes heirs from the rule that inability to swear results in payment. Whether because the heirs could not have been expected to know, as Tosafot explain, or because of a specific Scriptural decree, heirs are outside that rule. The same exclusion therefore applies to a Mishnah oath imposed on one who seeks to collect. If the creditor himself could have sworn but died, his heirs do not lose merely because they cannot swear, for the subsidiary rabbinic rule cannot exceed the Torah rule on which it was modeled. This explains the Gemara. Rabbi Abba derives the payment rule from the verse and therefore necessarily recognizes that heirs are expressly excluded. Once the Torah excluded heirs from payment because they cannot swear, there is likewise no basis to make them lose a claim because they cannot take a collecting oath. Thus Rabbi Abba does not accept Rav and Shmuel's broad rule. Rav and Shmuel, by contrast, are asked how they interpret the verse. If they read it as Rabbi Abba does, it would reveal an exemption for heirs and undermine their rule. The Gemara answers that they interpret the verse according to Rabbi Shimon and do not derive Rabbi Abba's rule from it. Their general premise can therefore be that when the Sages require an oath for collection, failure to swear causes loss, with no Scriptural distinction between the original litigant and heirs. The sugya becomes straightforward. Accordingly, once the halachah follows Rabbi Abba and we derive from the verse that heirs are excluded, Rav and Shmuel's rule is not accepted in full. The Sages nevertheless retained its limited application where the heirs themselves inherit a claim already impaired by the required oath, as the authorities explain, because the ruling had issued from Rav and Shmuel and was not uprooted entirely. But this special protection of heirs does not apply to a living suspect creditor. In his own case, just as inability to take a Torah oath causes payment, inability to take the collecting oath causes loss. The party in possession has the stronger position. The Rif's ruling that a suspect person loses his document is therefore fully consistent. It is possible Tosafot did not adopt this simple explanation because they understood the statement that the law of Rav and Shmuel is retained for heirs as a substantive equitable rule rather than merely a surviving exception. If even a Torah level collecting oath would not cause heirs to lose, it would seem improper to deprive another person of money merely for the benefit of heirs. Tosafot therefore distinguish instead between Torah and rabbinic oaths, explaining that Rabbi Abba would agree that inability to take a Torah oath can cause loss, whereas a merely rabbinic oath ordinarily cannot. The oath imposed on one collecting from heirs was made especially stringent, in some respects like a Torah oath, and therefore the Sages found a basis to apply Rav and Shmuel's rule there. This also resolves the Maharsha's question why Tosafot needed their final distinction between Torah and rabbinic oaths rather than simply say that whenever the oath cannot be taken, the defendant pays. Without the distinction, the special treatment of heirs would remain difficult. The same reasoning explains Rabbi Nachman's rule that when both are suspect they divide. The Gemara infers from this that Rabbi Nachman does not accept Rav and Shmuel's rule. One might have answered that Rabbi Nachman rejects Rabbi Abba and simply penalizes the suspect defendant by requiring half payment. But a rabbinic penalty of this sort must still be modeled on the Torah rule. If under Torah law inability to swear never produced payment, there would be no coherent basis for the penalty. Thus Rabbi Nachman's division assumes the essential rule of Rabbi Abba, while modifying its application when both parties are themselves disqualified. The result is that the law of a suspect creditor who holds a document differs fundamentally from heirs. The heirs' inability to swear is involuntary and is expressly protected by the verse's model. A living suspect creditor caused his own inability through misconduct and has no such exclusion. He therefore loses when the required collecting oath cannot be taken. This gives the Rif's ruling a clear foundation and leaves the sugya well ordered.
And some say. The Shach rules in accordance with this latter opinion and cites many authorities who agree. I have already explained the Rif's position, and it appears that Tosafot and the Rosh in Ketubot support it as well. Who, then, can conclusively decide to extract money from the party in possession? There is an additional consideration. Most authorities who follow this latter opinion also rule against Rabbi Nachman, who says that when both litigants are suspect they divide. Yet the Rama rules that they divide, the Shach agrees, and the clearest explanation of that ruling is the Ran's. The Ran maintains that Rabbi Nachman can accept Rav and Shmuel's principle, because when both parties are suspect the obstruction comes from both sides, whereas in Rav and Shmuel's case the obstacle is not caused by the defendant. Regarding one who must swear before collecting and dies, the Sages said not to extend the rule because he was not at fault. A suspect claimant, however, caused the inability himself. Why then should he not lose? It is true that the Ran gives another rationale: when both are suspect, the claimant is also penalized because the entire rule allowing him to collect by oath was created only because the defendant was suspect. But one cannot rely on that reason alone to extract money, since the Ran himself offers the first distinction that turns on fault. If so, where a suspect creditor seeks to collect a document and the opposing party is ready to swear, the entire obstacle lies with the creditor, and it is reasonable that he should lose the whole claim. Rabbenu Chananel likewise rules like Rabbi Nachman, and Tosafot in Bava Batra explain the reason as a penalty imposed on the suspect borrower, which is balanced when both are suspect. According to that rationale too, a suspect holder of a document should be penalized and lose, just as in the case of two suspect parties. The Mordechai gives Rabbenu Tam's reason for division: one party would otherwise lose and the other would pay, so the amount is divided. Here, where the suspect claimant would lose while the defendant can swear and be exempt, there is likewise reason to impose the loss on the party whose own misconduct created the problem. Rabbenu Tam necessarily distinguishes a suspect claimant from an ordinary person who is merely unable to swear, since Rabbi Nachman's rule itself differs from Rav and Shmuel for reasons of penalty. It follows that the same penalty should apply to a suspect document holder. The Shach's conclusion therefore requires study. Once we rule like Rabbi Nachman that two suspect parties divide, the first opinion, that the suspect creditor loses the document, is more persuasive.
