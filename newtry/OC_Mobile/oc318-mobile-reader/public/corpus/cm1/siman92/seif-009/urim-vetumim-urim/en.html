And so on, and so on, that there is no such thing as that which has not yet been condemned to me, that I swear that it is not so wild, and that I am writing this as a result of many differences:
He takes a hesset oath, etc. In Tumim I wrote that Rosh actually appears to require an oath while holding a sacred object, and this seems primary. I also explained a practical difference where both parties are suspected. According to the hesset view the document holder loses, but according to the sacred-object view, if both are suspected the document holder may collect, as I wrote above in siman 82. Likewise, if one witness says the document was paid and the document holder does not know personally but relies on the witness, then according to authorities above in siman 84 who treat this as a Mishnah oath, the same distinction matters. Under the hesset view the document holder loses; under the stronger-oath view he collects. Consider this carefully.
And some say, etc. Shach argues at length for this second opinion. His proof is not decisive. See Tumim, where I defended Rif at length. See also what I wrote there and in siman 82 that Tosafot appear to follow Rif.
Because an authenticated document, etc. Regarding the oath of a wounded person, robbery victim, and shopkeeper against workers, Sema says the opposing party takes hesset and is exempt. This is difficult. For a shopkeeper there is plainly no hesset, because the employer does not know whether payment occurred. Shach also writes this. Regarding the robbery victim and wounded person, hesset is likewise inappropriate because they themselves are suspected, as explained above in siman 90. See siman 90, where in a case of partial admission or witnesses to part, the claimant may collect without an oath because both parties are treated as suspected and payment follows. If the shopkeeper himself is suspected, Shach argues at length that he collects without an oath, following his view that by Torah law the shopkeeper already has a claim and the oath is only rabbinic. I wrote above that if the workers contradict him, by Torah law he would have no right to collect at all, and therefore a suspected shopkeeper should lose. As for suspected workers, Rabbi Zerachiah HaLevi, Ramban, and the Shach imply that according to all views they collect without an oath. It appears that even Rif, the first opinion, agrees, because there the employer says with certainty, 'I paid you,' whereas here his claim is uncertain. See above, siman 89, regarding a suspected hired worker.
