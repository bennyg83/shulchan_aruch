This appears to follow the view, cited at the beginning of siman 89 in the name of the Ramban and Ran, that in the case of a hired worker the oath was initially imposed upon the employer, but the Sages removed it from the employer and placed it upon the worker. Therefore, if the worker is disqualified, the employer must take the Mishnah oath resembling a Torah oath. However, as explained there, Baal HaMaor and many other authorities disagree and hold that even regarding a hired worker the employer takes only a shevuat heset. See there.
And says. This phrase cannot refer to one who impairs his document by admitting partial payment. It was explained in siman 84 that if a lender impairs his document and the document contains no ne'emanut clause, beit din itself requires him to swear even if the borrower does not demand an oath. If the document contains ne'emanut, the lender is believed without an oath even if the borrower demands one. Rather, the phrase refers to one who produces a promissory note against another without a ne'emanut clause and has not impaired the note. In that case, unless the borrower says, "Swear to me that it has not been paid," beit din does not require the lender to swear, as explained there and at the beginning of siman 82. The Tur states this explicitly, and the Mechaber alludes to it with the words, "and all similar cases." Sema.
Collects. The Shach writes that Responsa Maharashach, part 2, siman 102, rules that the party in possession may say, "I rely upon the first opinion," and the Bach rules similarly. The Shach says that several aspects of their reasoning are incorrect and concludes that the latter opinion is the primary halachic ruling. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch themselves rule in siman 82, seif 8, that this view is more reasonable. See there for his lengthy discussion.
