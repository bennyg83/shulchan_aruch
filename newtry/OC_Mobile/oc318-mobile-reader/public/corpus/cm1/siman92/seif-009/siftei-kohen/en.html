Some say that since the document is authenticated. Maharshal rules that the possessor may say, “I rely upon the first opinion,” and the Bach rules similarly. Their reasoning is not correct in several respects. The later opinion is primary. The Tur and Mechaber themselves above in siman 82:8 say that this is more reasonable. The Levush and Sema there agree. Baal HaMaor, Ramban in Milchamot, Ritva, Baal HaTerumot, and other authorities likewise support the Raavad and Ramah that an authenticated document is not nullified merely because its holder cannot take the required rabbinic oath. The Gemara's language supports this. Concerning a Torah oath imposed upon a disqualified person, the oath is transferred to the opposing party. Concerning a rabbinic oath, however, the Gemara says that one enactment is not added to another. This refers not merely to hesset but to all rabbinic oaths. The transfer itself is an enactment. Where the claimant's oath is only rabbinic, the Sages did not enact a second transfer. The matter therefore returns to Torah law. Accordingly, one who ordinarily swears and collects by virtue of a document, but is disqualified from swearing, collects without an oath. The same applies where the debtor demands an oath that the document was not paid, where one witness testifies it was paid, where collection is sought in the debtor's absence, or from encumbered property. The end of tractate Shevuot also proves that an authenticated document is not invalidated merely because its holder cannot swear. Even where a lender died before the borrower, the Gemara limits the rule that heirs cannot inherit an oath and refuses to extend it beyond its established cases. There is no reason to treat disqualification more harshly than death. The Sema adds that in cases of a robbed person, an injured person, or a shopkeeper on his ledger, the defendant takes a hesset oath and is exempt. His inclusion of the shopkeeper is mistaken, because the employer does not know what happened and the claimant knows that he does not know. No hesset oath applies to such an uncertain response. More fundamentally, the Sema assumes that a shopkeeper on his ledger would have no right to collect at Torah law even with an oath. Baal HaMaor states the opposite: everyone who swears and collects by virtue of a document, and likewise a shopkeeper on his ledger and similar claimants, would at Torah law collect without an oath. The Sages imposed the oath. If such a claimant is disqualified, he therefore collects without it, except in the special case of collection from orphans governed by the rule of Rav and Shmuel. Ramban objects that a shopkeeper's case is merely “You owe me one hundred” and “I do not know,” where the defendant is exempt. Baal HaMaor's position is nevertheless correct. In the ordinary uncertain-debt case, exemption rests on leaving money with its possessor. Here money will necessarily be extracted from the employer, and there is also a presumption that an agent performs his agency. Since the employer did not instruct the shopkeeper to pay only before witnesses, he trusted him to pay without witnesses. The Gemara's incident concerning Avimi, together with the Rif, Rosh, Tosafot, Ran, Maggid Mishneh, Ramban's responsum, and many other authorities, shows that an agent is not legally negligent for paying without witnesses unless specifically instructed otherwise. Responsa Rabbi Yosef ibn Plat, Baal HaTerumot, Rabbi Yosef Migash, Maimonides, and the Mechaber likewise support this. Therefore the shopkeeper's underlying right resembles that of other claimants who collect by rabbinic oath, and if he is disqualified he collects without that oath.
