(Seif 8.) Choshen Mishpat siman 92, seif 8. If a person who is disqualified from taking an oath was a custodian and claims that the deposit was lost or stolen, while the owner cannot swear and collect because he does not claim with certainty that the custodian consumed or misappropriated it, the custodian is exempt. The Sema, seif katan 20, identifies this as the Rambam's view, and the Maggid Mishneh and Mechaber rule accordingly, unlike Rabbi Yehudah and Ramah, who hold that the defendant is one who is obligated to swear but cannot do so and must therefore pay, as cited by the Tur. The Maggid Mishneh explains the Rambam's reasoning: if the custodian were liable, every person disqualified from oath would effectively be liable for every deposit unless the unavoidable loss occurred before witnesses. The Sages never imposed such an extraordinary stringency on one disqualified from oath. Rabbi Yehudah and Ramah necessarily reject the argument that this would make life impossible for every person disqualified from oath, because no one is compelled to accept deposits. When such a person accepts a deposit, he can be understood as accepting the monetary consequence if an unavoidable loss occurs without witnesses. At first glance I thought their dispute may depend on the two answers of Tosafot in Bava Metzia 5. Tosafot ask why, where the defendant is disqualified from oath, the opposing party swears and collects rather than treating the defendant as one obligated to swear who cannot swear and therefore must pay. One answer is that the Sages enacted the claimant's oath for the defendant's benefit, because otherwise no person disqualified from oath could survive financially; strictly by law the defendant would pay without the claimant's oath. The second answer is that by strict law the disqualified defendant would be exempt entirely, because he says, "I am prepared to swear," and it is the court that prevents him from taking the oath. The Sages enacted the shifted oath for the claimant's benefit. According to the second answer, there is no doubt that a paid custodian who is disqualified from oath is exempt where the claimant cannot swear, because by strict law the defendant is exempt. According to the first answer, the dispute between the Rambam and the opposing authorities depends on the policy consideration described above. The Shulchan Aruch therefore rules like the Rambam, whose reasoning works according to both answers. After writing this, I saw that the Urim VeTumim cites the Bach as making this dispute depend on the same two answers. Nevertheless, it appears to me that there is a strong proof for Rabbi Yehudah and Ramah. In Bava Metzia 5, in the incident involving the shepherd, the Gemara concludes that if Rabbi Chiya's rule applies, the depositor swears and collects. Once the depositor swears that he entrusted the item to the shepherd, the shepherd is immediately liable, because we do not entertain the possibility that the deposit was lost through an unavoidable accident. By denying that he ever received it, he effectively admitted that no accident occurred. In siman 72, seif 17, the Shach indeed writes that in such a case the claimant can swear generally that the defendant has property of his in hand. The difficulty is why the custodian is not believed through a migo that he could have claimed an unavoidable loss. If he had claimed an unavoidable loss, according to the Rambam he would be exempt because the opposing party could not swear. This seems to prove Rabbi Yehudah and Ramah. One cannot answer that the depositor also has a migo that he could have claimed an ordinary loan, because that would be a migo to extract. Moreover, if loan and deposit are considered different types of claim, such a migo would not apply at all. The proof can perhaps be rejected, however, according to the authorities who hold that a migo cannot be drawn from a defense that would require a Torah oath to one that would require only a shevuat heset. Here, before witnesses appeared, the shepherd's denial of receipt required only a shevuat heset, whereas a claim of unavoidable loss would have required a Torah oath. It would also be a retroactive migo. According to Rabbeinu Tam, however, who holds that a claim of unavoidable loss does not require a Torah oath, the issue remains.
