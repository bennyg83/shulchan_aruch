Concerning one suspected of an oath, Bava Kamma asks whether one person can become liable for two added fifths or two double payments through repeated false custodial claims and oaths. Tosafot notes that once his first false oath is exposed, he is rabbinically disqualified from future oaths. They answer that this does not prevent the second Torah oath from having legal force for double payment, because the disqualification of a suspected swearer is rabbinic. On the Torah level the second oath is still an oath. This seems difficult from the opening of Bava Metzia. There the Gemara proves that we do not say, "Since he is suspected concerning money, he is suspected concerning oaths," because otherwise how could the Torah impose a partial admission oath? If even a person already established as suspected concerning oaths remains biblically valid to swear, what proof is there from the Torah's imposition of an oath? The answer is that Tosafot means that one who previously committed a false oath is not permanently disqualified from another oath by Torah law. Perhaps he has repented in the meantime. The Torah expressly disqualifies a wicked person from testimony until repentance is established, but no parallel verse says that one who once sinned can never again take an oath. By contrast, the Bava Metzia argument concerns suspicion arising from the present dispute itself. If one says that a person who is now willing to steal money is also willing now to swear falsely, then administering the present oath would be purposeless and even on the Torah level would be an oath in vain. That is why the Torah's requirement of the oath disproves the equation of present monetary suspicion with present oath suspicion. This also explains Rashi's wording regarding the custodian's three oaths. The Gemara asks why we administer the oath that the item is not in his possession if, by hypothesis, perhaps it is in his possession and he is denying it. That is present monetary suspicion tied directly to the very oath now being administered. The same challenge does not arise from the oath that he did not previously misuse the object, because even if such misuse made him a robber in the past, past wrongdoing alone does not biblically invalidate his present oath. Rabbinically he may be disqualified, but where the usual shifted oath remedy cannot operate, the Sages may still require him to swear rather than let the sinner profit. This fits Shitah Mekubetzet's explanation that the disqualification of one suspected concerning money is rabbinic. The Sages penalized a suspected person by shifting the oath where that remedy is available. But where both sides are equally suspect or no shifted oath is possible, they did not simply exempt him and allow wrongdoing to profit. A remaining difficulty is Tosafot in the opening of Bava Metzia, which appears to cite Rabbi Yehudah HaChasid that a robber is disqualified from an oath by Torah law. If even one actually suspected of an oath remains biblically capable of another oath because he may have repented, then all the more one suspected only because of robbery should not be biblically disqualified. This requires further study.
Mordechai in Perek Shevuat HaDayanim cites Raavyah and Maimonides that if a claimant tells a suspected defendant, "Swear to me," the court does not administer the oath because of desecration of Hashem's Name. Rabbi Baruch and others agree. Rashba dissents and brings proof from the shopkeeper's ledger case, where the halachah follows the first tanna against Ben Nanas even though the structure can create a certainly vain oath. Maggid Mishneh likewise writes that a suspected person does not swear even hesset, and that this appears true even if the claimant wishes him to swear. He says the matter is clear from Perek Shevuat HaDayanim, though the precise passage is not obvious. The Gemara there distinguishes Torah and rabbinic oaths by whether an opposing suspected party can shift the oath, but it does not expressly discuss whether the claimant may insist that the suspected person himself swear. There is, however, a strong proof from Perek Shevuat HaEdut. The Gemara discusses the case where one witness obligates a person whose opponent is suspected, and asks, "Who says he will swear?" It ultimately establishes a case where both are suspected and applies the rule that the oath returns to the one originally obligated. If the defendant, who must now pay, could say to the claimant, "Swear to me before I pay," then the same question would return: who says the claimant will swear? The fact that the Gemara does not say this implies that once the claimant is himself suspected, he cannot be made to swear even if the defendant requests it. According to the explanation in Shitah Mekubetzet that the reason a suspected defendant does not swear is a rabbinic penalty designed to shift the oath to the other side, the difficulty is sharper. In the case where both are suspected, why may the defendant not tell the claimant, "Swear and take"? Shitah Mekubetzet states that rationale only for suspicion concerning money, but Tosafot's broader approach makes even a suspected swearer rabbinically disqualified. The conclusion must therefore be that a suspected person is not administered the oath because of desecration of Hashem's Name, even at the request of the opposing party.
