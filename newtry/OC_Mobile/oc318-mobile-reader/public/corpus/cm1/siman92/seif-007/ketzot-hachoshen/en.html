One should not transact with a suspected person. This follows Raavad's discussion at the beginning of Bava Metzia. Another opinion, represented by a responsum of the Rosh, rejects the idea that the innocent party forfeited his rights merely by choosing to deal with someone suspected. Rather, the suspected person caused the problem for himself. Tumim distinguishes between a deposit and a loan. In a deposit, the owner knowingly entrusted property to a person who, because of his suspicious status, could later claim unavoidable accident without being able to take the required oath. The depositor therefore accepted that risk and brought the loss upon himself. In a loan, says Tumim, there is no comparable unavoidable accident defense. This does not fully eliminate the dispute. Even in a loan, a suspected borrower can often evade a partial admission oath by denying everything, claiming payment, or arranging his response so that no Torah oath arises. In monetary law we recognize migo even from a bolder plea. Rashba states that one who intends to take money wrongfully can also be bold enough to deny completely. The borrower might also preempt the claim with an admission framed so as to avoid partial admission, or use "here it is" and similar devices. Raavad's mention of unavoidable accident is therefore only an example. The broader principle is that one who knowingly transacts with a suspected person accepts the risk that the oath machinery may become unavailable. Because the suspected person can often shape his plea to avoid the oath, the same rationale applies to loans as well as deposits. The disagreement with the Rosh therefore remains even in loan cases.
