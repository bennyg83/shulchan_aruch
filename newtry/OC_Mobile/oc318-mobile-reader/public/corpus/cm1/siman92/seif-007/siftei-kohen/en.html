One who became obligated to take a Torah oath. This is the view of the Rif, Maimonides, Ramban, and Ritva in the first chapter of Kiddushin in the discussion of an oath imposed by extension. The phrase includes both one who admits part of a claim and one who is contradicted by a single witness. The Rosh states this explicitly at the beginning of responsum rule 11: one who denies the entire claim, is contradicted by one witness, and is disqualified from taking an oath, causes the opposing party to swear and collect. The witness cannot now exempt him as a supporting witness, because once that witness created the obligation to swear, he cannot subsequently remove it, as explained above in siman 87:11, seif katan 27. Some say that they divide. The Tur cites this in the name of Rav Hai Gaon, Rabbenu Tam, the Rosh, and the Mordechai. This appears primary from the Gemara, for Rav Nachman actually ruled that the parties divide, while elsewhere Rav Nachman accepts Rabbi Abba's rule that one who admits fifty and says of the other fifty, “I do not know,” must pay because he cannot swear. Ramban's explanation that Rav Nachman stated the ruling only according to Rabbi Meir is strained, since it would make the anonymous Mishna and the Gemara's discussion follow a rejected view. The Ran also argues at length against Ramban and rules like Rabbenu Tam. The Maggid Mishneh records that several later authorities rule this way, as do the Semag, Responsa of the Geonim, Raavan, Baal HaMaor in the name of Rabbi Moshe bar Yosef, and Baal HaIttur. The Ran's extended explanation resolves the objections raised by Tosafot in the chapter HaShoel. One textual difficulty is answered by the version in Halachot Gedolot, which reads that Rav Nachman stated the matter according to Rabbi Ami, not Rabbi Meir. This refers to Rabbi Ami's distinction between whether judgment had already been rendered. That reading is more satisfactory and also appears in Baal HaMaor's citation of Rabbi Moshe bar Yosef. See also Piskei Rekanati, siman 249. Cited cross-references in the source include: 71, 164, 240, 270.
But otherwise not. This means that the claimant knew the defendant was disqualified from swearing. The Levush says so, and Rabbenu Yerucham writes likewise in the name of the Raavad, deriving it from the opening passage of Bava Metzia concerning the shepherd. The Raavad understands the Gemara's question, “But he is a shepherd,” to mean: why impose an oath on the remainder when the claimant knew the shepherd's disqualification and therefore caused his own loss? The Bach rejects the Raavad because of Tosafot, the Rosh, and other authorities. In my opinion they all agree with the Raavad. Their concern is only that otherwise anyone could transact with a disqualified person and later claim ignorance, leaving no practical life for one whose disqualification is not notorious. But where it is clearly established that the claimant knew of the disqualification, he is treated as having accepted that the disqualified party would be exempt without swearing. This also answers the Sema's objection to the Levush. Hagahot Maimoniyot proves that these positions are compatible, since it cites the Raavad and immediately afterward cites Tosafot without indicating disagreement. Rama's statement that “some disagree,” based on a responsum of the Rosh, is therefore not conclusive. The Rosh may discuss a case where it was not established that the claimant knew of the disqualification. Where such knowledge is certain, as in the case of the shepherd, all may agree with the Raavad. This is the primary view and gives the correct reading of the Gemara. The Rif also appears to have understood the passage this way. Piskei Rekanati, siman 269, likewise states the Raavad's rule without qualification. Cited cross-references in the source include: 4, 260.
