He is suspected of the oath. At the time of the High Court, I will not be suspicious, and if the Buddha was not suspected, and then he swore to be suspicious, or to be ashamed of his guilt, and then he made an answer to the end of the SS:
He swears and collects. See Baer Heitev, citing the Shach that there is no distinction between a defendant who partially admits and one who is contradicted by a single witness. The Shach adds that this is explicit in a responsum of the Rosh, klal 11. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 27, responds to a scholar who asked why the Shach needed the responsum of the Rosh when the basic law appears expressly in Shevuot 32b. He explains that the Shach did not cite the Rosh merely for the result, which is explicit in the Gemara, but to establish the rationale. The rationale suggested by Maharam differs from that of the Shach and yields practical distinctions. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 178, offers another resolution of the question.
