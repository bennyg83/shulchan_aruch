And some say they divide. The Shach, seif katan 9, cites a host of authorities who rule this way, and the Rama decided accordingly. Who can come after his decision? Yet the Shach analyzes the Ran and understands him to hold this position, namely that we rule like Rav Nachman that when both litigants are suspect they divide, while at the same time the law follows Rav and Shmuel that a person cannot transmit to his heirs a claim whose collection depends on an oath he himself could not take. There, because the creditor cannot swear, it is proper that he lose. When both litigants are suspect, however, the defendant’s inability to swear is itself the result of his own misconduct, so it is also proper that he lose half. The Rama must adopt some such distinction, because here he rules that they divide, while below in siman 108 he follows the Mechaber and Rav and Shmuel that a person cannot transmit such an oath-bound claim to his heirs, causing the creditor’s side to lose the entire claim, unlike authorities who award half even there. See Mordechai, chapter Chezkat HaBatim. The Gemara discusses Rabbi Ami’s distinction: the rule of Rav and Shmuel applies only where the creditor had already come to judgment, but not where he died before doing so. Rav Nachman objects, “Does beit din create the oath? From the moment the debtor died, the creditor became obligated to swear.” Rav Nachman therefore says, “If Rav and Shmuel’s rule is valid, it is valid in all cases; if it is not valid, it is not valid at all.” The Gemara then asks whether Rav Nachman was uncertain about Rav and Shmuel, since elsewhere he rules that in the case of two suspect litigants they divide. It answers that he spoke only according to the reasoning of the other view and did not personally accept it. The Ran explains the Gemara’s challenge to mean: why should Rav Nachman be uncertain? He should certainly accept Rav and Shmuel, contrary to Rashi’s interpretation that he should certainly reject them. If where both sides are at fault they divide, then where the defendant is not at fault and only the claimant cannot take the required oath, the claimant should surely lose everything. This is the Ran’s explanation. The Shach asks how the Gemara’s answer, “He said it according to Rabbi Meir’s view,” resolves the issue. He therefore suggests that the Ran’s text may have read, “He said it according to Rabbi Ami’s view and did not agree with him.” On that reading Rav Nachman’s point would be: Rabbi Ami’s very attempt to distinguish whether the creditor had already stood in judgment shows that Rabbi Ami is uncertain about Rav and Shmuel, but there is no coherent distinction. If their rule is valid, it applies in either case; if invalid, in neither. This is strained, because Rav Nachman’s opening challenge already made exactly that point: “Does beit din create the oath?” There is no room for the proposed distinction. A simpler reading of our text is possible. Rabbi Meir holds that when both litigants are suspect, the oath returns to the party originally obligated, who must pay because he cannot swear. This is so even though the claimant too had been assigned a rabbinic oath. Rabbi Meir must therefore hold that inability to take a rabbinic oath does not by itself cause the claimant to lose. Accordingly, in a case where the debtor died during the creditor’s lifetime and the creditor’s oath is only rabbinic, one can genuinely doubt whether Rabbi Meir rejects Rav and Shmuel because a rabbinic oath does not cause forfeiture, or whether he agrees that the special oath imposed on heirs and estates was strengthened by the Sages to resemble a Torah oath, as a special protection for debtor’s heirs. That is a real uncertainty. Rabbi Yosi, by contrast, holds that when both litigants are suspect they divide. This shows that even inability to take a rabbinic oath can cause some forfeiture. Thus where the creditor cannot take the heirs’ oath, Rav and Shmuel’s result follows naturally. The fact that in the suspect-litigation case he loses only half while against heirs he loses all is explained by the Ran: in the former case the defendant also caused the problem through his own misconduct, so he is penalized as well; in the heirs case the defendant side is blameless. Thus the Gemara can say that Rav Nachman’s uncertainty was only within Rabbi Meir’s framework. Rav Nachman himself follows Rabbi Yosi, where no such uncertainty exists. This resolves the text. Yet a deeper difficulty remains. If the Ran truly ties the rule “they divide” to Rav and Shmuel’s rule concerning inherited oath claims, why did Rav Nachman later say, “Do not extend Rav and Shmuel beyond their case,” and refuse to apply their principle to a creditor who had impaired his own document and then died? If Rav and Shmuel’s rule is conceptually correct because the claimant who cannot take a required oath must lose, why should an impaired note be different? In both situations the claimant cannot take a rabbinic oath, while the defendant is not at fault. The Ran distinguishes a suspect claimant because his own wrongdoing caused his inability to swear, and his entire right arises only because the defendant too is suspect. In the case of an impaired document the claimant is not blameworthy. But those same distinctions seem equally relevant where a debtor died before the creditor. Why, then, did the Gemara assume that Rav Nachman’s ruling to divide among two suspects necessarily means he accepts Rav and Shmuel? One could distinguish the cases just as the Ran distinguishes an impaired note. This requires study. It would be strained to say that when the Ran says the impaired claimant is not at fault, he also means that the debtor in that case is at fault for having paid without witnesses, whereas in an inherited-claim case we assume that the debtor really did pay before witnesses but after his death the heirs cannot identify those witnesses. Then the latter would more closely resemble the case of two suspect parties. The Ran does not mention this rationale, and the Gemara suggests Rav and Shmuel stated a broader principle while later authorities merely say not to extend it. Another difficulty is that if the Gemara equates the rule of division among two suspects with Rav and Shmuel, then because we rule below in siman 108 that a judge who ruled like Rabbi Elazar may be upheld, one might expect the same latitude here: a judge who orders the suspect defendant to pay the entire claim to the suspect claimant should likewise be upheld. If one answers that here it is less plausible to extract the entire amount from a suspect defendant, then the Gemara’s original equation between the cases becomes weak. My view is as follows. The phrase “He said it according to Rabbi Meir’s view, though he does not hold that way” itself requires explanation. Why would Rav Nachman spend effort clarifying the consequences of a view he rejects? The general method of the Gemara is that Amoraim debate according to positions that have practical standing. According to the Ran, the structure should be understood differently. Rabbi Yosi’s rule that they divide indeed implies that inability to take a rabbinic oath can cause forfeiture. This could extend both to inherited oath claims and to an impaired document. Yet there is also a rational distinction: in the case of two suspect litigants the claimant is personally blameworthy, whereas in the inherited-oath and impaired-document cases he may not be. Where there are plausible arguments on both sides, however, we do not extract money from the possessor merely on a doubtful extension. That itself is Rav and Shmuel’s practical rule: they too were aware that one might distinguish, but absent a compelling basis to do so, the claimant cannot extract from heirs when he cannot perform the required oath. This is especially persuasive if the accepted halachah is clearly like the view that divides when both are suspect, or even if the view that the oath returns to the defendant can coexist with Rav and Shmuel because the defendant’s oath obligation had already caused the underlying money to be considered due. But if Rabbi Meir holds that when the oath returns to the original defendant he pays, while at the same time inability to take a rabbinic oath never causes the claimant to lose, then Rav and Shmuel may not follow at all, because their case involves only a rabbinic oath. Rav Nachman may therefore have been uncertain not because he did not know whether division was correct, but because he did not know whether the halachah followed Rabbi Meir or Rabbi Yosi. This also answers Tosafot’s question on Shevuot 47, where Rav Nachman says he does not know whether the halachah follows Rabbi Yosi or Rabbi Meir, yet elsewhere he acted by dividing. He may still have divided because of the rule that the claimant cannot extract the disputed half from the possessor when the halachic issue itself is uncertain. Under this framework there are layered doubts regarding Rav and Shmuel: perhaps the halachah follows Rabbi Meir, in which case their rule may be false; and even if Rabbi Yosi governs, perhaps his rule is limited to a claimant whose own suspect status caused the problem. These cumulative doubts reinforce leaving the money with the heirs. Even if one does not formally extract on the basis of a double doubt in monetary law, we do not invent a broad dispute among authorities unnecessarily. Rabbi Meir himself does not cause a suspect claimant to lose even where both are suspect. It is therefore enough to say that Rabbi Yosi’s division is a penalty specific to the suspect claimant, not a general rule for every rabbinic oath. Rav and Shmuel can then still be correct on independent grounds. This explains the Gemara’s question and answer: Rav Nachman’s uncertainty about Rav and Shmuel arises because Rabbi Meir may reject their principle, while Rav Nachman himself, in practical conclusion, can still preserve the distinction and say, “Do not extend Rav and Shmuel.” Their express ruling remains in force, but beyond it one need not apply the same logic. The wording is somewhat compressed, but the structure is coherent.
However, Tosafot’s approach is cleaner. According to Tosafot, Rav Nachman’s own legal reasoning does not accept Rav and Shmuel. Since he rules that two suspect litigants divide, he does not say that a claimant automatically loses merely because the Sages imposed a rabbinic oath that he cannot take. Rather, the suspect claimant loses half as a penalty for his own disqualification. Rav Nachman nevertheless says, “Do not extend Rav and Shmuel,” because although he would not have originated their ruling, he would not annul a specific rule that had already issued from Rav and Shmuel, especially because one can say that the Sages found a special reason to protect orphans, as the Rosh writes. The Gemara therefore asks simply: why is Rav Nachman uncertain about Rav and Shmuel if his own rule of division shows that their principle is not his? It answers that the uncertainty is only within Rabbi Meir’s view. Rabbi Meir says the oath returns to its original place. It is then uncertain whether he means it returns to the party originally obligated, as Rabbi Elazar understands, in which case the defendant pays because he cannot swear and Rav and Shmuel would not be correct, or whether the oath returns to Sinai, meaning the claimant who cannot swear loses, which is exactly Rav and Shmuel. According to Rabbi Yosi, who divides, one can be certain that he accepts the rule that the original defendant is liable because he cannot swear, while the claimant loses the other half only as a penalty for his own suspect status. This Tosafot approach is more straightforward than the Ran’s and should be carefully noted.
For otherwise everyone, etc.; and some disagree. This is the position of the Rosh in a responsum. He writes explicitly that we do not say that people who knowingly dealt with a person already known to be suspect brought the loss upon themselves. On the contrary, the suspect person brought it upon himself. Knowing his own status, he should have taken care not to enter a transaction that might lead to an oath, where the opposing party would then swear and collect. The Bach and Sema write that Tosafot likewise follow the dissenting view. Tosafot explain that the opposing party was required to swear and collect rather than simply receive payment by the rule that one who cannot swear must pay, as an enactment for the suspect party, for otherwise anyone could deliberately transact with a suspect person and cause him loss. If the whole discussion involved only someone who did not know the other was suspect, what special enactment would be needed? The Shach’s suggestion that the claimant may say he did not know is strained. A person’s status as suspect is generally public. That is why for Torah-level disqualification no formal proclamation is needed, because the matter becomes known, while for rabbinic disqualification there is a proclamation so that his status becomes known publicly. The Shach’s explanation is therefore difficult. Especially untenable is his suggestion that even the Rosh’s responsum concerns a case where the claimant did not know at the time of the transaction that the other was suspect. The responsum’s question explicitly concerns someone publicly established as suspect and asks whether one who nevertheless transacted with him lost his protection. That necessarily assumes the status was known. Otherwise, in what sense did he bring the loss on himself? The Rosh himself answers that we do not say the claimant brought the loss on himself; rather, the suspect person did, which plainly means the claimant knew of his status. Yet it is also difficult to say that the Rosh disagrees with the Raavad without mentioning him, especially because the Raavad’s language has real support from the Gemara’s statement, “Let him be disqualified because he is a shepherd.” With due respect to the great rabbis who discussed this, it is clear that they did not reach the Raavad’s intent. The Raavad never spoke of an ordinary loan where the suspect person holds the money. In that case the Rosh is correct: the suspect person brought the problem on himself, and there is no concern that the claimant will exploit the rule, because the claimant must still swear before collecting. The very purpose of Tosafot’s enactment is that the opposing party does not collect without an oath. Once an oath is required, we do not suspect ordinary Jews of swearing falsely merely to exploit a suspect litigant. The Raavad speaks instead of a deposit entrusted to a person already known to be suspect. In that case, if the depositor knew his status, the suspect custodian can be exempt and the opposing party does not receive the special right to swear and collect. The depositor knowingly accepted the custodian’s credibility. Had the custodian wished, he could have claimed that the item was lost through unavoidable accident and been exempt without any opposing oath, as explained in seif 8. Thus the depositor who handed him the property knowingly accepted the risk of relying on a suspect person. Why should the Sages create a special protective enactment for someone who did not protect himself and effectively abandoned his property knowingly? The Gemara’s statement concerning a shepherd can likewise be understood this way: he is a shepherd publicly known to be suspect, and yet the owner entrusted him with property in circumstances where he could later claim unavoidable loss. The owner therefore cannot invoke the ordinary suspect-litigant enactment. This is exactly the language in which the Beit Yosef cites the Raavad: “when he knew that the person was suspect at the time he deposited with him, he himself caused his loss.” The words point directly to this distinction. There is therefore no dispute at all. I said this in my youth before several leading scholars of the generation, and they agreed. Later Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia was published, and I found precisely what I had hoped for. It cites the Raavad: “Let him be disqualified because he is a shepherd. From the outset, when he deposited with him, he deposited on the possibility of loss. Had he said, ‘They were stolen,’ he would not have sworn and the opposing party could not have sworn; therefore now we apply the migo and the depositor loses.” I blessed Hashem for guiding me on the correct path. The supposed dispute therefore disappears, and the Rosh does not disagree at all.
