One who became obligated to take an oath, etc. See Shach. Shevuot 32b implies that the suspected party must swear. This rejects Maharam's strained explanation that a supporting witness cannot exempt a suspected litigant because otherwise anyone could create liability through one witness. Once the oath obligation arose through the witness, that same supporting testimony cannot remove it. See citations 80 and 86.
And if both are suspected, etc. This follows the view that the oath returns to its original place and Rabbi Abba, whose ruling is accepted. Rava and Abaye follow him, and the mishnayot in Bava Metzia and the discussion in Bava Batra are explained accordingly. Rav and Shmuel are accepted only within their limited case, while Rav Nachman's ruling to divide is not followed. See citation 80.
And some say they divide. This follows Rav Nachman, who acted according to Rabbi Yose, and the law follows Rabbi Yose and Rav Nachman in monetary matters. Rabbi Abba is not a contradiction, because Rav Nachman also accepts his rule where a Torah oath rests on the defendant. Here the division is a penalty because the claimant too is unable to take the oath imposed upon him. The sources show three accepted rules: Rabbi Abba for a Torah oath, Rav and Shmuel in their limited rabbinic cases, and Rav Nachman where both parties are unable to swear. See the extended discussions in Tosafot, Rosh, Mordechai, Baal HaMaor, Ran, and the cited passages. See citations 4, 32, 35, 80, 86, and 146.
(Likkut) And some say that they divide, etc. See Tosafos there, s.v. "because," and Bava Metzia, daf 97b, s.v. "here too," and the Mordechai in Shevuos there. The conclusion of their words is that although we find three opinions in this matter, R' Abba, Rav and Shmuel, and Rav Nachman, we rule in accordance with all of them. The halacha follows R' Abba, as Rava there and Abaye in chapter Shevuas HaEdus, the sugya in chapter Chezkas HaBatim, and many other places follow him. The halacha also follows Rav and Shmuel, as stated there: "Come and uproot it," etc. And it follows Rav Nachman, who acted accordingly, since the halacha follows him in monetary law. The explanation is that all agree in principle with R' Abba, but R' Abba applies the rule specifically to a Torah-level oath. Therefore, according to R' Meir, the defendant is one who is obligated by Torah law to swear but cannot swear, whereas the claimant is not. Rav and Shmuel extend the principle even to rabbinic oaths, as in the case of orphans, and similarly in comparable cases. They therefore hold in the Mishnah that the claimant loses, because the oath was transferred to him and he cannot swear. R' Yose likewise agrees in principle, but holds that since neither party can swear, each loses half. This is why they said concerning Rav and Shmuel, "Let us not add to it," because the halacha follows R' Abba that the rule applies specifically to a Torah-level oath, except for orphans, toward whom the Sages were lenient on a slight pretext. In the Mishnah too the halacha follows R' Yose, because the claimant's oath results from the defendant's Torah-level obligation. This is what is meant by "our teachers in Eretz Yisrael," namely R' Abba, who explicitly holds that where a Torah oath cannot be sworn, payment is made. What remains difficult is the statement there, "Rav said: the view of R' Abba is reasonable," and on daf 48a, "apparently he is uncertain," etc., for according to the above there is no dispute. Tosafos there also raise difficulties; see there. End.
