Like a get. The law of a get on this point is explained in Even HaEzer simanim 120 and 141.
To three. Three are mentioned to teach the greater novelty. Even though three constitute a beit din, mere verbal instructions cannot be delegated through them. Sema.
Themselves. The Sema explains that if they themselves write, they have deviated from the giver's instruction, which was that they tell other witnesses to sign. See the Bach. This requires study. See what the Taz writes on this.
To two. The opening clause speaks of one who told three, while the concluding clause speaks of two, because when he says, 'Write and sign,' two suffice. In the opening clause, where he tells them to instruct others, three are mentioned to teach that even three who constitute a beit din cannot transmit mere verbal instructions. One might ask why the later phrase says, 'Likewise if he said to two,' when that follows all the more so from the first rule. The answer is that one might have thought that the first case fails only because he gave them no authority to write but only to tell others, making it mere words. Where he expressly commanded them to write and deliver, however, they become direct actors in the transaction, and one might think they can appoint substitutes. The ruling teaches otherwise. Thus far the Sema. See what the Taz writes on this.
