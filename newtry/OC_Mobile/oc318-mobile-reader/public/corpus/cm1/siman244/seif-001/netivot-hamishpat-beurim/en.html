With a deed of gift. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that according to the view that kiddushin are considered mere words because if the woman does not consent she is not betrothed, the same should apply to a gift. Even if the agent was given the gift itself, since it does not become a gift if the recipient refuses it, the matter remains mere words, and an agent cannot appoint another agent for a gift. See there. See what I wrote above in siman 182. The need for agency in a gift or sale applies specifically where the buyer or recipient appointed an agent to perform meshichah, because meshichah by another person who lacks the status of an agent is ineffective. We therefore need the derivation that an agent is like the principal. Likewise, if a seller tells someone, "go sell at your discretion," and the agent sells and sets the price, then without the law of agency the agent's pricing and consent would have no legal effect. So too in kiddushin and divorce, where a woman who takes her get from the ground is not divorced because transfer from his hand to hers is required, we need the law of agency so that the agent's hand is treated as the husband's hand and the agent's statement as the husband's statement. Kiddushin likewise requires the man's declaration, since if he gives the money and she makes the declaration she is not betrothed. Agency is therefore necessary. But where a donor sends a gift and tells someone to bring it to a particular person so that the recipient may take possession of it and it shall be his as a gift, then even if the carrier is incapable of halachic agency, once the gift reaches the recipient he acquires through his courtyard or by meshichah. Even in Even HaEzer, siman 141, seif 35, it is ruled that one may send a get with a gentile to deliver it to a Jew whom the husband appointed in writing as his agent, because the gentile merely performs the physical act of conveyance. The same applies to a gift. Since one who takes a gift from the ground acquires it, no declaration by the giver to the recipient is required. Even where there is merely a compelling presumption or disclosure of intent that he gives it as a gift, the recipient acquires, as the Shach writes in siman 358, seif katan 1. Therefore, even if he sends it through someone who is not legally an agent, the recipient certainly acquires, because the carrier merely performs a physical act, bringing the item to the recipient after the giver has indicated that the recipient should take it and own it. All the more so where a duly appointed agent transfers it onward through another person. Once it reaches the recipient's hand or courtyard, he acquires. It would be no different if the agent had projected it by some mechanical means into the recipient's courtyard, in which case the recipient would acquire. The same applies where a seller told an agent that anyone who gives a certain sum may acquire the item. Since the principal himself could tell any person who wishes to give that amount to perform meshichah and acquire, the information may certainly be conveyed through someone who is not legally an agent. The second person merely communicates the seller's statement to the buyer. Ketzot HaChoshen also asks concerning divorce, where a husband tells the woman to act as his delivery agent until she reaches a certain place, and upon arrival to appoint another delivery agent from whom she will receive the get. Since the first agent has the power to revoke the second, the woman can effectively cancel the second agency and decline to receive the get from him. It should therefore revert to being mere words, and how can she appoint a second agent at all? See there. There is no difficulty. First, the essence of this agency is one that could effect divorce even against her will, and therefore it is not considered mere words. Although the agency can be revoked, formal language of revocation is required; merely saying "I do not want it" does not revoke the agency. Moreover, it appears that only an agent who was empowered to effect the divorce can revoke a second agent, because the first remains an agent, since he can take the get from the second and deliver it himself. As he retains the power to effect the divorce, he bears the status of principal with respect to revoking the second agent. By contrast, where the first agent was appointed only to appoint another agent to effect the divorce, once he has made that appointment his entire agency ends and the second person becomes the husband's agent. The first can no longer revoke him. Further, the Ran's discussion of "say to others" indicates that the rule that mere words cannot be delegated applies specifically to an agent appointed to write, meaning that the agency itself lacks the capacity to be transmitted again to another person who will perform the writing. But where agents were appointed only to communicate to others that those others should become agents, the first agency is not being transmitted at all, and the rule that mere words cannot be delegated is inapplicable. See my Torat Gittin, siman 104, seif katan 4.
And they shall give it to a particular person. Ketzot HaChoshen raises a doubt according to the view that an agent for receipt cannot appoint another agent because this is mere words: perhaps such an agent also cannot acquire through a courtyard, since courtyard acquisition is derived from agency and an agent for receipt cannot appoint another agent. According to what I wrote above in siman 200, that a guarded courtyard of an adult is regarded by all as an extension of his hand, it certainly works. Even where the courtyard is unguarded and the owner is not standing beside it, so that according to the Rif and Rosh its acquisition is based upon agency, in a sale or gift where another person's intent confers the property, it also appears effective. In a gift, a formal agent for receipt is not effective at all, as stated in the Yerushalmi at the beginning of HaOmer, because in monetary matters the agent must be the agent of the owner of the money. Thus there is no issue of mere words. If one transfers property to another person for a recipient and it then reaches the recipient's courtyard, the recipient acquires, as explained in siman 243, seif 4. The same appears true in a sale. An agent can act for the buyer even though the concept of an agent for receipt does not apply to money. As explained in siman 188, the buyer's agency means that he gives the agent authority over his money to expend it for the purchase. It therefore is not mere words, since the agency attaches to money placed in the agent's hands, and according to all views such an agent may appoint another agent.
They cannot tell the scribe to write. A deed of gift does not require writing for its own specific purpose. Accordingly, if they were told, "give a deed of gift to a particular person," why should a deed written and signed for someone else not be valid? Indeed, even a deed of gift found discarded and then given to him should seemingly suffice. If the giver said "write," one might answer that he was particular about who should write, but this does not apply where he merely said "give." Moreover, in the case of "say to others" he certainly did not care, yet according to the first view it is invalid. The reason given is that mere words cannot be delegated, indicating that the defect is not the giver's particularity. What concern would a donor have as to who writes the deed? With a get there can be an issue of the husband's dignity because he bears responsibility for its writing, but not with a deed of gift. It therefore appears that writing a deed of gift also requires agency. Just as the verse concerning a get says "and he shall write" and we interpret this as referring to the husband, requiring agency, so too concerning a deed that effects a transfer, where Scripture says to write in a document and sign it, agency is required. Such a deed does not require writing for the specific recipient because the word expressing that requirement is not written for gifts as it is for divorce, but since the command to write appears in both contexts, agency is required in both. See my novellae to Gittin for cases that are invalid due to lack of specific intent rather than lack of agency. This also resolves Tosafot in Gittin 10, s.v. chaspa be'alma hu, who write that one cannot say the document is merely earthenware for this reason. Pnei Yehoshua questions their words. According to what I have explained, that agency is required for the writing of a deed effecting transfer, writing by a gentile is certainly invalid, and all of Tosafot's words are readily understood. See what I wrote in Torat Gittin.
And some validate where he says, "say to others." This implies that it is valid even if he told them to instruct others to write, sign, and deliver it. Tosafot in Gittin 67 ask that this should constitute testimony from one witness through another, and answer that the essential testimony was entrusted only to the latter witnesses; the first persons are merely the husband's agents instructing them to prepare the get. At first glance this is difficult. If two people come to two others and say that a certain person appointed them as agents to tell those others to write a promissory note or deed of sale stating that he borrowed money or sold a field to someone, or to testify orally that he borrowed or sold, could the latter possibly testify or write such testimony? Surely anything stated outside beit din is not treated as admissible testimony, and witnesses may testify only to what they themselves perceived. I explained at length in my novellae to Gittin 66 that the requirement of testimony before beit din applies specifically to a document of proof, and likewise to oral testimony where the testimony concerns a completed event, such as that a person already sold his field or already borrowed money. There, testimony relayed from one witness through another is invalid because the original witnesses did not testify before beit din. Here, however, two witnesses tell two others that they were appointed as agents to divorce, betroth, sell, or give a field to another. The second pair are not witnesses repeating other witnesses' testimony. Rather, they become legal actors based upon testimony, acquiring the authority to effect the transaction. The first witnesses are not testifying to a completed act, such as that this woman was already divorced. They testify that this man has empowered someone to divorce a certain woman or sell a certain field, and such testimony is effective even outside beit din. Likewise, the witnesses who sign the get do not testify to the completion of the divorce, or that a field has already been transferred as a gift, for they did not witness the delivery. Their essential testimony is only that the document was written at the direction of the owner. We then regard ourselves as witnesses to the transfer itself, as the authorities explain. Testimony that a document was written at the owner's direction likewise does not require a declaration before beit din, and such testimony may validly be conveyed in writing and through the testimony of others. I discussed this at length there.
