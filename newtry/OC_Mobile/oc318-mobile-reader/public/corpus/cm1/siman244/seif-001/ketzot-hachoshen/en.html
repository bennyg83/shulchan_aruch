Some validate when he says, Say to others. The rule that verbal matters cannot be delegated means that an agent charged only with words cannot normally appoint another agent to perform those words. Ramban distinguishes, however, between ordinary redelegation and a principal who expressly says, "Tell so and so." The latter can be understood as the principal himself appointing the named person through the intermediary. In divorce, even this can fail because writing and signing must be performed for the husband's sake and by those directly commanded under the relevant views. In a gift there is no comparable requirement of writing for the donor's sake, so an express instruction, "Tell so and so," can work. Rambam likewise validates an instruction naming the later agent, treating it as appointment in absence. But where the principal merely says, "Tell whomever you choose," the first agent is being asked to create a new agent from verbal authority, and the rule against transmitting words still applies. There is no reason to distinguish gift from divorce merely on the ground of for its sake. Ran makes this clear. Tur's broader wording in the name of Ramban therefore requires study, though in Even HaEzer Tur appears willing to validate even "say to others" in divorce.
With a deed of gift. A delivery agent for a gift cannot necessarily appoint another delivery agent merely because a divorce agent can. Mordechai, citing Rabbi Mordechai, says that an agent appointed to betroth a woman with a ring cannot redelegate, even though he physically holds the ring. Divorce differs because a woman can be divorced against her will. Once the get reaches the first agent, his hand stands fully in the husband's place and the get itself is the instrument that effects the divorce. Kiddushin depends on the woman's later consent, so the first agent's authority remains verbal in character. A gift resembles kiddushin because the recipient can refuse it. Therefore even if the first agent holds the gift or gift deed, his delivery authority can remain verbal and may not be redelegated. This also explains the episode where an agent was told to bring a get to a specifically named intermediary. When the intermediary was absent, the sages proposed that he appoint another agent, and Rav Safra objected that this first agent was never appointed for divorce itself. Since the named intermediary could refuse to receive the get, the first agent's task was merely verbal conveyance to him and could not be redelegated. There is a difficulty from the Mishnah of a woman who brings her own get as an initial delivery agent and is told to appoint a second delivery agent and then receive the get from him. Her first agency also depends on her willingness, yet the redelegation works. One can answer that once she transfers the get to the second agent, he possesses an actual get and can deliver it against her will. The second stage is therefore a completed act rather than mere words. This still encounters the practice of having the first agent swear not to revoke the second. If he retains power to revoke, perhaps the authority remains verbal. The better conclusion is that the opinion of Rabbi Mordechai must accept that an express "say to another" instruction works. That also resolves the woman's get case. Consequently, in a gift, if the donor expressly empowered the first agent to appoint another, the redelegation is effective. This also supports the broad powers written in contemporary divorce authorizations, even though forced divorce is no longer ordinarily available because of the ban of Rabbeinu Gershom.
And they shall give it to so and so. This is a delivery agency. A receiving agent in divorce ordinarily cannot appoint another receiving agent because the appointment is verbal authority. I was uncertain about a receiving agent appointed to pull an object purchased or gifted to the principal. If the object enters the agent's courtyard, can the courtyard acquire for the principal, given that a man's courtyard ordinarily operates through agency and a receiving agent cannot redelegate? Tur raises an analogous doubt in divorce, whether a get thrown into the receiving agent's courtyard is effective. The Jerusalem Talmud asks whether the agent's courtyard is like his hand. The issue is best understood not as a double derivation from hand, but as follows: if the courtyard functions only as the agent's subagent, verbal authority cannot be redelegated; if it is legally like the agent's own hand, it can receive. For monetary acquisition there is proof that an agent's courtyard can acquire. Bava Batra states that goods deposited with a third person can be acquired by the purchaser when the custodian accepts responsibility for the purchaser, through the custodian's courtyard. This shows that the custodian's acquisition authority can operate through his courtyard. The reason is that beneficial acquisition is not identical with ordinary agency. As explained in siman 243, it can operate as an independent institution. Therefore an adult who acquires for another can have his courtyard acquire for the beneficiary, and a receiving agent in a beneficial sale or gift can effectively use his courtyard. A three party arrangement likewise can operate through an agent for the recipient even though its mechanism itself rests on beneficial transfer. Mordechai offers a different theory: even a receiving agent can redelegate because the rule against transmitting words applies only where the entire task remains incomplete verbal work, such as an instruction to write a get. Once the agent is appointed for the final act of receipt, the act is substantive and can be redelegated. But the sugya of "write and give" shows that even delivery can remain verbal when no get was placed in the agents' hands at the time of appointment. The decisive distinction is whether the agent was entrusted with the actual instrument that effects the transaction. This is why a delivery agent who physically receives the get can appoint another, while one merely told to write and give cannot necessarily do so even after the writing, since the principal's original appointment was only words and could include authority to write another get until actual delivery. Tosafot Rid similarly distinguishes kiddushin by money: even if the first agent holds the money, the money is not itself the exclusive instrument because he could replace it with other money, so the essential agency remains verbal. A get, by contrast, is the specific object that effects divorce. The varying readings of the sugya concerning "write and give to my agent" follow this distinction. Rashi understands the named delivery agent as already appointed by the husband, so the writers merely transmit the completed get to an existing agent. Rambam's broader wording can be understood because, on his view, once the writers have produced a valid get and hand that specific get to the delivery agent, their task is completed and the instrument itself now carries the agency. The Mishnah requiring the original appointees to "write and give" remains different where their entire original appointment was merely verbal and continues until the get reaches the wife. This resolves the principal sugyot.
