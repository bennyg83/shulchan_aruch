(Seif 1: they said, “tell so-and-so,” and so forth.) To explain the Tur's words in this siman, we must first set out the central principles in chapter HaMekabel, 65. We rule like Rabbi Yose that if one tells three people, “write, sign, and deliver,” even if they are the supreme beit din, we do not say that he appointed them as judges who may instruct others to write. They are merely agents, and they themselves must write and sign. His reason is that he entrusted only words to them, and words cannot be delegated to another agent, unlike a tangible act. The Gemara further records a dispute where he told three, or told two, “tell a scribe to write and two witnesses to sign,” and they did so. Some invalidate this too because it is a delegation of words. The Maggid Mishneh writes this in chapter 2 of Hilchot Gerushin. The Ran, cited by the Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 120, invalidates it because a get must be written for her sake, and where the scribe does not hear directly from the husband it is not called his writing. In ordinary monetary law, however, there is no such defect, for this is merely appointing an agent in the agent's absence. It appears that this latter reason is the one accepted by the Tur in Even HaEzer, where he invalidates “tell others,” whereas here he writes that it is not a delegation of words. Necessarily, he invalidates a get for the Ran's reason. In the Gemara, one opinion validates “tell others” according to the strict law even under Rabbi Yose, though it should not be done in Israel because of a decree lest one hire witnesses to instruct a scribe falsely in the husband's name. There is no difference between saying, “tell a scribe to write and witnesses to sign,” and saying, “tell particular persons to write and sign.” These are the same. Therefore, in chapter 2 of Hilchot Gerushin the Rambam mentions only telling the scribe and does not separately mention telling the witnesses. Whoever treats one as an impermissible delegation of words treats the other the same. The Sema in seif katan 3 distinguishes between a get and a waiver for the reason explained in his work. I am astonished at his reasoning, for it appears that he did not examine the sugya there. The Gemara expressly says that we do not fear that the scribe will sign without the husband's command, because a witness signs only upon the husband's direct instruction. Even according to the view that “tell others” is valid, there is no concern that the husband told two people to instruct a scribe to write and witnesses to sign, and that from embarrassment they told the scribe himself to sign, because the rule that one should not do this in Israel makes the circumstance uncommon. More surprising still, in Even HaEzer siman 130 the Tur cites the Rambam that a get signed by the scribe and one witness is valid even initially. The Sema writes that the get is initially invalid when the scribe signs, and the Beit Yosef also says so there in the Rambam's view, but with respect, they did not examine the Rambam carefully. He writes in chapter 2 of Hilchot Gerushin: “If he said to two, write, sign, and give it to my wife, they write, sign, and give it to her,” which implies that the scribe may sign even initially. It therefore appears to me that the Sema's words are difficult. The reason “tell others” is invalid is that words cannot be delegated to an agent. The Ramban holds that in the case of a gift, “tell others” is valid, since even regarding a get there is a dispute whether it is valid. Although we are stringent regarding a get even according to the validating opinion, in monetary gifts one may rely upon the essential law leniently. This is the “some validate” opinion, and this appears entirely clear.
