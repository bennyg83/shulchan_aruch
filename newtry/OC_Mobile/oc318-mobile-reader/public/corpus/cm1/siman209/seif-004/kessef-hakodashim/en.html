Seif 5, gloss. He exerted himself and purchased with this in mind. The rule that when a seller exerted himself and bought the item he is presumed to have done so in order to uphold his credibility is stated from the seller's side, but it is not explicit whether the purchaser is correspondingly unable to withdraw. At first glance, principles of agency and zechiyah should apply, like siman 269 where one says 'acquire it for me,' and like the cases in siman 183 where the authorities derive that if one tells another to buy merchandise so that he may become a partner, the instruction has legal effect. Terumat HaDeshen and the Taz similarly discuss a seller who exerted himself and bought in siman 209. Yet siman 374 rules that when the buyer knew the item did not belong to the seller, the principle that the seller later exerted himself and acquired it does not apply. In simanim 183 and 269 too, the buyer knows that the merchandise has not yet been acquired. The distinction must be that in simanim 183 and 269 their words expressly concern a future purchase and therefore create agency, whereas one who purports to sell property he does not own speaks only of a present sale. Once the buyer knows the seller lacks title, the money is treated as a gift or deposit, and no agency for a future acquisition is implied. It is more reasonable to interpret the money in that way than to transform the parties' present sale into an undertaking concerning a future purchase. Accordingly, there is no direct proof from simanim 183 and 269 that the buyer must accept the goods merely because the seller later acquired them. Even if the buyer could have informed the seller not to purchase, and even if he knew the seller was seeking to acquire the property, their transaction still concerned only a present sale. The seller's inability to withdraw when he later purchases rests on the buyer's reasonable reliance, because the buyer did not know the seller lacked the property and reasonably assumed he had it. Even if the buyer privately suspected otherwise, he relied on the seller's representation that he could supply the item. Ordinary commercial language can also encompass merchandise presently available to be acquired and delivered, as in a sale at the market rate, which carries the sanction of mi shepara. Thus a seller who claims he had no goods when witnesses testify only that he used language of sale may still contend that the language referred only to present goods; but if the buyer says 'perhaps you had it,' the seller may nevertheless be required to swear, because there are grounds to assume he had it. This differs from a buyer, upon whom it is difficult to impose a binding obligation merely by the seller's later acquisition when no future agency was expressed. Yet if the seller cannot withdraw because he bought in order to preserve his credibility, his acquisition must contain some element of zechiyah for the buyer. If so, the case again resembles siman 183. This may depend on the dispute in siman 374 regarding a seller who, after acquiring the property, sells it to another. Those who hold that the second sale defeats the first may understand that the buyer is not bound to accept; the seller's acquisition is a zechiyah only if the first buyer later chooses the sale, and his later choice reveals retroactively that the acquisition was for him. But if the buyer rejects it, the seller's acquisition is retroactively not a zechiyah for him. Those who hold that the seller's later sale to another does not defeat the first may understand that his acquisition creates actual zechiyah for the first buyer, so that the buyer too cannot withdraw where he failed to revoke the seller's authority beforehand. This requires study.
