The cow or maidservant is pregnant. Pnei Yehoshua asks that consecration can take effect upon a fetus. Ketzot HaChoshen likewise asks from the law of an object given as a harlot's fee, which can apply to a fetus, and from Shabbat 138, where one person acquires a maidservant and another her fetus. These sources show that a fetus can be acquired. Ketzot notes that Bnei Yaakov and Machaneh Ephraim also prove from these passages that acquisition of a fetus is effective. At first glance this is genuinely difficult, because Kiddushin 62 indicates that even when the pregnancy is recognizable, the fetus is still regarded as something not yet in existence, and acquisition is effective only according to Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, who hold that one can transfer property not yet in existence. Yet the other sugyot treat a fetus as transferable without distinguishing whether the pregnancy is recognizable. It therefore appears to me that Kiddushin discusses one who sells the fetus only from the time it will be born, meaning that he sells a fully born offspring. If it dies in the womb, or the mother is slaughtered before birth, the buyer has no rights in the fetus, because what was sold was only a viable offspring upon birth. That certainly concerns something not yet in existence, with multiple uncertainties, since perhaps the mother will die or miscarry. But if one conveys or consecrates the fetus immediately, so that the fetus itself belongs to the recipient from now and, if the mother dies or is slaughtered, the flesh of the fetus belongs to him, the acquisition is certainly effective. A fetus is considered a limb of its mother, and conveying it is like conveying one of her limbs, comparable to selling the meat, spleen, or liver of an animal. According to the opinion cited by the Rama that a person can convey property by saying the acquisition should take effect when the thing comes into existence, the distinction is relevant where he says concerning a fetus, "from now and when it is born," which the Mordechai in chapter 13 treats as ineffective. But where he transfers the existing fetus itself immediately, everyone can agree that the acquisition works. A clear proof is Gittin 23. If a master says to a pregnant maidservant, "You remain a maidservant, but your fetus is free," she acquires freedom for the fetus because a fetus is considered a limb of its mother, as though he freed one of her limbs. If the fetus is a limb of the mother for emancipation, it is likewise a limb for acquisition and consecration. Consecration has a special rule: if one consecrates a limb such as the leg of an animal as an offering, sanctity may spread through the whole animal because that limb cannot be offered independently. If one consecrates the fetus, however, it can later be offered separately after birth, so the sanctity need not spread through the mother's body. Ketzot's proof from Tosafot and the Rosh at the beginning of Bekhorot, who say that conveying the fetus's ear is a transfer of something not yet in existence, does not refute this. There the owner could have conveyed an ear of the mother herself to a gentile and thereby exempted the future firstborn, but he did not wish to give the gentile any share in the mother's body. He wanted the gentile to have an interest only in the offspring. Had he conveyed an immediate interest in the fetus, which is treated as one of the mother's limbs, it would again be as though the gentile owned a share in the mother's body, allowing either party to compel division through gud o agud or slaughter. That is exactly what the owner wanted to avoid. He therefore sought to convey the ear only once the fetus would be born, and that future transfer is indeed of property not yet in existence.
If the buyer first seized it. It appears that the case must be one where there was an acquisition mechanism capable of taking effect later. For land, this can be acquisition by money, which can be structured to take effect after a period, as explained in siman 191. With movable property, the seller might say, "Draw it and acquire it after it comes into existence," and the buyer then actually draws it after it comes into existence. Alternatively, the seller could convey land by money together with the movables agav the land, expressly providing that even the land should not be acquired until the movables come into existence. At that moment, when the land acquisition takes effect, the movables can take effect agav it. If the land became the buyer's immediately while the movables were to be acquired only later, agav would not work, because once the land is already the buyer's, movables cannot later be acquired agav his own land, as explained in siman 250:17. Thus the case is one where a valid acquisition act existed but initially failed only because the subject matter was not yet in existence. If the buyer later seizes the property, his seizure can be effective. But if the attempted acquisition was by kinyan sudar, which would fail even if the property already existed where the transfer was postponed until later because the cloth has already returned to its owner, later seizure does not help. There the defect is not merely the absence of the subject matter.
Rather, he wrote him a deed and delivered the deed to him. See Taz, who disagrees with the Sema and explains that the deed was written at the time of the original sale. He proves this from the Ittur's case of a person who sold his mother's ketubah, where the deed existed from the time of sale, and the requirement is that the deed remain in the buyer's possession when the subject matter later comes into existence. Aside from the difficulty of the Rama's wording, which should then simply have said that he sold by deed, the Gemara appears to say the opposite. In Bava Metzia 17, concerning one who later purchases property back from the original owner, the Gemara asks, "By what did he acquire it? By this deed? This deed was mere earthenware," and compares it to "what I will inherit from my father is sold to you." According to the Taz, if a deed was present, even property that one will later inherit should be acquired so long as the seller did not retract before it came into existence. Tosafot in Yevamot 93, the Rashba, and the Ran likewise explain the declaration, "this field, when I buy it, shall be acquired to you," as a case where the transfer was made by deed and the deed still existed when the acquisition was supposed to take effect, yet the seller can nevertheless retract. Furthermore, the Ittur's own reason applies specifically to the sale of a debt instrument. The Ittur writes that the rule barring transfer of future property applies where the recipient does not hold the item and must sue the seller for it. If the recipient already holds the operative item, seizure can be effective. Thus, when a debt instrument is sold to another person, the seller transfers the actual note and also writes the required deed of assignment. The buyer no longer needs to sue the seller, but only the debtor, and this is considered possession. By contrast, if one sells a field that he will later buy, the field remains in the seller's hands after he purchases it. The buyer must still demand it from the seller. A sale deed in the buyer's possession does not amount to seizure of the field itself. This also appears to be the Rama's intent. He first writes that the same principle applies to other forms of property not yet in existence, and a debt is itself within that category. When he then says that seizure helps and adds that the same is true if "he wrote him a deed and delivered the deed," he abbreviates the Ittur. "He wrote him a deed" means the written assignment of the debt instrument, the formula conveying it and all its liens; "and delivered the deed" means he actually delivered the underlying promissory note being sold. That is truly equivalent to seizure because the buyer no longer needs to sue the seller. If one instead interprets the Sema as meaning that after the future property came into existence the seller gave the buyer a new debt instrument against himself, that ruling can be true, but it is not derived from the Ittur. It derives from Rivash, responsum 335, who writes that if, after the future property comes into existence, the seller converts the obligation into a new loan, the buyer acquires, evidently through a new note against the seller. The Rivash also writes that although a pledge given at the time of the original attempted sale is ineffective because there is no underlying debt to support the pledge, a pledge delivered after the property comes into existence can help as a form of seizure. However, the Baal HaTerumot, siman 64, cited here by Beit Yosef, requires seizure specifically of the very property sold and does not recognize a separate pledge. The matter therefore appears to be disputed.
That he should acquire from him the proceeds of the wine when he sells the wine. Responsum Mahari Alshekh, siman 7, rules that even if the owner conveys the wine for purposes of its future proceeds, the transfer is ineffective. He distinguishes this from conveying a palm tree for its fruit, because money realized upon sale does not grow physically from the wine as dates grow from a palm tree. In truth, this is a dispute among early authorities cited by the Shitah Mekubetzet. Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 23 of Hilchot Mekhirah, concludes that such a transfer is effective. Siman 213 likewise shows that one can convey an existing trapping apparatus for the fish it will catch, as Ketzot cites. This is the principal ruling. Independently, the Alshekh's particular case is difficult. A shechiv mera instructed that a fixed sum of his money be lent to a certain person. A loan is comparable to a gift for a period, and is in one sense stronger because the borrower returns other money. A healthy person can convey money for purposes of a loan through meshichah, and anything a healthy person can effect can be accomplished by the words of a shechiv mera, which are treated as though delivered. Ketzot challenges the Shitah's proof that a trap cannot be conveyed for future fish. If such a transfer were ineffective, why did the Sages need a special enactment that one who says, "what my trap catches today shall belong to you," acquires? One could simply convey the trap itself, and then the fish would belong to the recipient by virtue of his courtyard. The answer is that some traps have no enclosed interior and therefore do not acquire by the law of courtyard. They are protected only by the rabbinic rule of darchei shalom in favor of the person who exerted himself to set them up. In such a trap it is necessary to convey the trap itself for the future fish, and the Shitah's point is therefore sound.
He cannot forgive it. See Sema, seif katan 21, who writes that for this reason the creditor can remove that lien even from property the debtor has not yet acquired. His wording is somewhat difficult, because it seems to imply that all the more so he can release the lien from property already acquired. Tosafot in Ketubot 95, s.v. Vekhi Katav Lah, explain that a creditor cannot remove himself from a lien upon property already acquired except through a kinyan, because he can collect from it at any moment and it is comparable to a married woman's property, from which the husband cannot simply remove his rights. Only with respect to property not yet acquired does a bare removal of rights help, as explained by the Mordechai. It appears, however, that Tosafot require a kinyan only where the creditor uses the weak formula of "I have no claim or dealings concerning it." Since an existing lien is treated like a right already in his possession, such weak language is ineffective without a kinyan. A kinyan then helps even with that weak language because the kinyan is taken from him with respect to the substance of the property, as explained in Ketubot 83. But if he uses clear language, saying, "I hereby forgive you the lien," that should be effective. He can certainly forgive the entire debt by language of forgiveness, so there is no reason he cannot forgive the security interest alone. Likewise, he can certainly release a guarantor by language of forgiveness, and a person's property stands as guarantor for his debts. It also appears that forgiveness of the debt itself would not work if he merely said the weak formula "I have no claim against you" or "my hand is removed from you," unless a kinyan was made. Maharik, shoresh 89, supports this. The Taz questions the Sema and writes that even before the lien comes into being one cannot remove himself from it, bringing proof from a husband who writes such a waiver while the woman is still betrothed. His words are very difficult. Before betrothal the husband certainly cannot waive marital rights because he has no connection to them at all, and this is explicitly stated by Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 23 of Hilchot Ishut. Ketzot likewise questions him. The remainder of Ketzot's discussion is also difficult in my humble opinion. In seif katan 11 he says that Tosafot hold a waiver ineffective with respect to a Torah right because it is like stipulating against the Torah, and he argues that even an unconditional waiver is itself equivalent to a condition, like saying, "my son Ploni shall not inherit." Since we rule that a condition concerning a monetary Torah right can be effective, he concludes that a waiver of a Torah right should likewise work. But Tosafot in Ketubot, s.v. Kederav Kahana, explicitly ask why Rav Kahana is needed at all, since a stipulation concerning a monetary Torah right is effective, and answer that the case is one where the person speaks not by way of a condition. This demonstrates that even according to the view that a monetary condition against Torah law can be effective, a mere waiver does not remove a Torah right. Waiver works particularly with a right instituted for a person's benefit, as Tosafot explain there and in Gittin. According to the view that even a monetary condition against Torah law is void, all the more so an attempted waiver is ineffective. Even according to the Ran's distinction between inheritance from one's father and inheritance from one's wife, the result is the same. Inheritance from one's father is continuously expected and therefore treated as though already in his possession, so a waiver is ineffective. Inheritance from a wife while she is merely betrothed is not presently available to the husband and is treated as not yet in his possession, so a waiver before marriage can work. Yet the Ran agrees that according to the opinion that a monetary condition against Torah law is void, the husband cannot waive inheritance from his wife if the husband's inheritance is a Torah right. Ketubot 84 makes this explicit even though the dispute of Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel and the Sages concerns a waiver made while she is still betrothed. Ketzot also asks, according to the Ran, from Bava Batra 126, where the Gemara discusses one who says "my son Ploni shall not inherit" and answers that the son did not forgive. Ketzot argues that inheritance from one's father is treated as though already in his possession, so the son's consent should not help in any case. But the same question would apply to Tosafot, who also agree that a son cannot simply waive inheritance from his father. Ketzot overlooked Tosafot in Ketubot 56, s.v. Harei Zo Mekudeshet, who explain that the Gemara said "he did not forgive" merely for added force and not because forgiveness would necessarily be effective in every formulation. Ketzot further challenges the Rashba, who holds that even in monetary matters a condition against Torah law is effective only where the party says, "on condition that you forgive me," whereas "on condition that you have no claim upon me for food, clothing, and marital relations" is void. Ketzot says that a waiver resembles the latter weak formulation. In my humble opinion, an actual waiver is equivalent to real forgiveness. He also questions Maharit, responsum 45. Maharit discusses a woman who deposited a chest and stipulated with the custodian that he should not seize its movables for her husband's debt. Maharit rules that the custodian can retract from his undertaking because a later declaration can cancel an earlier declaration. Ketzot challenges this from the Ran's statement in chapter Elu Na'arot that a seduced woman can forgive her fine, comparing it to a husband removing himself from rights in property while the woman is still betrothed, and from the Ran in Ketubot 80 that a yavam can remove himself from rights in his brother's estate. From these he infers that a waiver cannot be retracted. This question is not persuasive. Maharit himself writes that a husband who waives his rights in his wife's property cannot retract, because at the moment he removes his rights the wife acquires the corresponding freedom in her own property. In the chest case, however, Maharit holds that the custodian can retract because his waiver was directed toward the wife, who had no present proprietary right in the husband's movables. The husband could sell or consume them, leaving her with nothing. One therefore cannot say that at the moment the custodian waived his right she acquired the assets, because they were not hers. By contrast, regarding the seduced woman's fine, the man immediately acquires the benefit of her forgiveness, and regarding the yavam, the brother's estate immediately becomes free of the yavam's claim. In those cases the waiver takes present effect and cannot be withdrawn, just as a husband cannot retract after removing himself from rights in his wife's property. Finally, Ketzot writes in seif katan 13 that a creditor cannot remove himself even from property the debtor will acquire later, comparing it to a husband who, while his wife is still betrothed, removes himself from property that will fall to her after marriage. In my humble opinion the cases are not comparable. In marriage the husband's hand is legally identified with hers, so property that later falls to her simultaneously falls under his marital rights. A creditor has no such immediate legal identity with property his debtor will later acquire.
