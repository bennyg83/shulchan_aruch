Even if the cow or maidservant was pregnant. Rambam rules that if a man says, "If your wife gives birth to a daughter, she shall be betrothed to me," the statement is ineffective, but if the wife is already pregnant and the pregnancy is recognizable, the daughter can be betrothed. This suggests that a recognizable fetus is treated as sufficiently in existence. There is support from the laws of offerings. The Gemara discusses the application of a prohibited payment to the fetus of a consecrated animal and cases where holiness attaches to the fetus in the womb. In several such cases one cannot explain the result by saying the mother was transferred for the fetus, because the mother's body was already consecrated. This indicates that the fetus itself can be treated as present for some legal effects. Rambam's separate ruling that one who transfers property to a fetus does not effect acquisition does not necessarily contradict this. The defect there may not be that the fetus is absolutely nonexistent, but that a fetus lacks legal capacity to acquire until birth. Ramban explicitly distinguishes the two questions. A fetus is partially in the world, yet presently lacks a hand for acquisition. That is why Rav Nachman can say that if the donor says, "When you are born, acquire," the acquisition can work, while a transfer to a person not conceived at all cannot. This also explains the laws of a pregnant slave and her fetus and the discussion of a firstborn animal. Where the fetus is recognizable, it can exist sufficiently as the object of a transaction even though it cannot itself be the recipient of property. The case of sale of a fetus before the firstborn sanctity attaches raises an additional timing question. If the sale is stated to take effect only at birth, sanctity of the firstborn and the sale may arrive together, and the Torah's sanctity should prevail. The practical solution is to make the gentile's interest effective before the emergence of the majority, such as upon the first part of the fetus emerging. Under the view that firstborn sanctity takes effect only prospectively upon emergence of the majority, that earlier transfer prevents full sanctity. Terumat HaDeshen also discusses whether money or meshichah is needed in selling a fetal interest to a gentile. Even if the fetus itself cannot be directly pulled, the mother can be pulled and may serve as the means of acquiring the fetus. Yet one can argue that since the fetus is the true subject of sale and has no independent meshichah, we look to the primary subject and money can be effective. The issue remains dependent upon the larger disputes concerning gentile acquisition and the status of the fetus.
If the fruits have set, they are considered to have come into existence. Rama's ruling follows Rashi, who describes the sale of future palm fruits as a sale made before the fruits have set. Bnei Yaakov objected from the dispute concerning a fetus and from the law of produce designated as future terumah, where even a recognizable beginning may still be treated as future property. Rashi can be defended by distinguishing between the object that will ultimately be acquired and the degree to which it already exists. Once fruit has set on the tree, the body of the fruit is physically present. Further growth is only development of an existing object. This differs from produce that is only grass or from a fetus whose acquisition as a legal recipient is still incomplete. More broadly, the Talmudic comparison between recognizable pregnancy and recognizable produce does not establish that every stage has identical legal consequences. It asks whether the emerging object has reached a level at which a future act can be related to it. Rashi can therefore hold that set fruit is already a present object for sale even if other sugyot treat an earlier developmental stage as insufficient for certain consecrations or transfers. The practical ruling is thus that once the fruits themselves have formed on the tree, the sale is no longer necessarily a sale of something wholly nonexistent.
Even after the cow gives birth, a fetus that remains inside a slaughtered mother should be treated like an object placed in a container. Tosafot in Niddah writes that where the mother has died, the fetus is no longer dependent on her life and is regarded as separately present. Accordingly, if one sold the fetus and the mother was afterward slaughtered, there is strong reason to say the fetus is then a present object even before it is physically removed. This also helps explain the inheritance discussions where a fetus can inherit from a mother who died before it. The precise rule depends on which death occurred first and on the differing views in Tosafot, but the conceptual point remains that once the mother's life no longer sustains the fetus, the fetus can be treated as independently present.
Maharash Alshikh ruled that saying, "These coins for their profits," is ineffective. A palm remains in existence when its fruits later emerge, so one can transfer the palm for its fruits. Money, by contrast, is commonly spent and replaced in the course of business. When the profit arises, the original coins may no longer exist. Therefore, he argued, there is no enduring body from which the future profit grows. I do not agree. The concept of transferring an existing body for future yield is not limited to natural growth physically emerging from the body. The Gemara asks whether one can sell a slave for the future fine payable if the slave is gored. The Sages' side of the question rests on the fact that "the ox is here and the slave is here," even though the future fine does not grow physically from the slave's body. If the slave can serve as the existing legal base for a future monetary consequence, money can likewise serve as the existing legal base for future commercial profits. Moreover, the Gemara's doubt concerning the slave is only because it is uncertain whether the event producing the fine will ever occur. Profits from invested money can be more expected than such a fine. Thus "money for its profits" can function like "a palm for its fruits," provided the wording truly transfers a present proprietary interest in the underlying money for purposes of the future gain. Maharash Alshikh also argued from Rav Nachman's rule that if the purchaser seizes future fruits after they emerge, beit din does not remove them. He suggested this may rest on uncertainty whether the seller transferred the place of growth. But the better explanation is that the seller knowingly entered a sale and, after the fruits emerged, voluntarily allowed the buyer to retain them. That later waiver can be effective because the seller prefers to keep his word. The same principle can apply to profits produced from money. Even though the gain is not physically generated from the coins, the existing capital can be the legal source to which the future profit right is attached. Therefore I conclude that "money for its profits" can be effective.
And he seized the fruits. Rosh, citing Rabbeinu Tam, explains that in the case of a loan secured by future fruits the borrower's failure to object is a mistaken waiver, because he never intended a sale. By contrast, one who actually sold future palm fruits intended a sale. Although he may legally retract when the fruits come into existence, he morally should not retract if he wishes to maintain his reliability. Therefore, if the purchaser seizes and eats the fruits with the seller's knowledge, beit din does not remove them. Rosh adds that the seller's knowledge is necessary. If the purchaser seized without the seller knowing, there was no waiver. Pri Chadash questioned this because even if the seller did not know at the moment of seizure, we know that had he known he would probably have allowed it in order to preserve his reliability. That is insufficient. A waiver without awareness resembles unknowing despair. Tosafot says that even where one knows that the owner is the sort of person who would not object, taking without the owner's present awareness remains problematic because the later consent was not present at the time. So too here, without the seller's knowledge there is no present waiver. Therefore Rosh's distinction is sound.
Rather, he wrote him a document. Baal HaIttur writes that where a person sold a future right and gave the buyer a written document, the document can place the buyer in a stronger position analogous to seizure, even though it was written before the future property came into existence. This directly contradicts Sema's suggestion that the document must be delivered only after the property has come into existence. Baal HaTerumot distinguishes a pledge. If the seller merely gives collateral to secure an obligation concerning future property, the pledge does not help because there is no existing debt to which it can attach. "There is no maneh here and no pledge here." Bnei Yaakov therefore asked why a document should be stronger than a pledge. The distinction is that the document is not merely security for a nonexistent debt. It embodies the sale claim itself and can give the purchaser a recognized hold in the transaction. Baal HaIttur analogizes it to the rule that if the purchaser has already seized and eaten the fruits, they are not removed. The document functions as a continuing legal hold that can support the later waiver or reliance once the property emerges. This does not mean every pledge must also work. A pledge, without an underlying personal debt, is only security and cannot create the principal obligation. A sale document can itself embody the transaction and therefore can stand in the place of possession more readily than collateral can.
That he should acquire when it comes into existence. Mordechai records an opinion that the rule, "A person cannot transfer something not yet in existence," applies even where the transfer is phrased, "When it comes into existence, acquire it." If the acquisition act is performed now, before the property exists, it does not remain suspended indefinitely until the property later appears. There is proof from the law of an after dated debt instrument. The Gemara asks whether future acquired property can be subjected to a lien. If saying, "When the property comes into existence, it shall become subject," always worked, the question would largely disappear once the debt instrument was dated for the future. Yet the Gemara treats the future acquired property problem as real. This indicates that merely postponing the effective date does not cure the defect of future property.
So too one cannot waive something not yet in existence. Ran proves that future rights cannot be waived from the law of a receipt for an unknown future debt. If one could waive a future debt, the creditor could simply write, "Any debt instrument of such and such amount that may later arise is waived." The law would not need the special rules governing undated receipts. Bnei Yaakov objected that Ran himself explains elsewhere that the phrase "from now" is needed in delayed acquisitions primarily in case the document is lost, not necessarily to prevent retraction. If so, how does Ran know that a future waiver cannot be made irrevocable by saying "from now"? The two points are linked. If "from now" allows the original document to remain effective even if destroyed before the later date, that means the acquisition has already begun in a legally completed way from the present. Such a completed present acquisition cannot ordinarily be retracted. Conversely, if the transfer remained wholly future and retractable, destruction of the present instrument should prevent later acquisition. Thus Ran can properly infer that a true present "from now" acquisition defeats retraction only where a present object capable of acquisition exists. It cannot create a waiver of a right that does not yet exist.
