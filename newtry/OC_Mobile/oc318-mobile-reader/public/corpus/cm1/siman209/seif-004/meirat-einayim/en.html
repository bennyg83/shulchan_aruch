A person cannot transfer something that has not yet come into existence, etc. This applies to transfer of ownership. A person can obligate himself with respect to something not yet in existence, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 60; see there.
Whether in the gift of a dying person. Meaning, do not say that because the Sages were concerned that a dying person's mind not become distraught, they granted his words greater effect, just as they enacted for this reason that his words are treated as though written and delivered. See below siman 250.
"Whatever this field produces is sold to you," etc. It is possible that because the produce of an entire field can amount to a large sum, the text uses the language of sale, whereas for the produce of one tree it uses the language of gift.
"Give what she will bear," etc. This refers to the words of a dying person who tells his sons, "Give..."
Nevertheless, regarding a tree, etc. The reason is that fruit that has already formed is physically present and then grows naturally, so it is regarded as something already in existence. A fetus in the womb of an animal or maidservant, by contrast, is not treated as a presently existing independent object.
Even after she gives birth, etc. According to the opinion that a person can transfer something not yet in existence, this works only if he does not retract before the object comes into existence. From this we infer that according to the halacha, which follows the view that one cannot transfer something not yet in existence, even after it comes into existence the transferor can still retract. See the Perishah.
If the purchaser preceded him and seized it. Meaning, before the transferor retracted, as I proved in the Derishah.
We do not remove it from him. This does not result from unresolved doubt; rather, this is the substantive law. In the Derishah I quoted the Gemara's wording that proves this. See there.
Rather, he wrote him a document, etc. It is regarded as though the recipient is already in possession. It appears that the case is one where, after the property came into existence, the document was delivered to him. The document's delivery is then effective as though the property itself came into his possession. Hagahot Maimoniyot explicitly writes this. See also the beginning of siman 87 in the Tur and Rama, where a document is regarded as "here it is."
The tree for its fruit, etc. Since the tree itself exists, the acquisition can attach to the tree with respect to the fruit it will later produce.
He acquires immediately. Meaning, even before the fruit emerges.
If he transfers it so that it will be acquired when it comes into existence, he acquires, etc. Some disagree. A similar dispute is cited by the Tur, Mechaber, and Rama in siman 210 regarding a sale to an unborn person; see there.
He can create a lien upon it, and some disagree. See the beginning of siman 60 and the beginning of siman 112. There I wrote that the dispute concerning a lien exists only if he seeks to retract before the property comes into existence. Once it comes into existence, all agree that he cannot retract from the lien. For this reason a clause of d'akni is effective without dispute after the debtor has acquired the property and it has come into his possession. See there and examine this carefully.
It is comparable to "you and a donkey acquire." See siman 203, seif 10, where Rama cites three views from Mordechai concerning one who transferred money and movable property together by kinyan sudar. See what I wrote there and the sound reason I gave for why here he rules simply according to the final opinion.
As will be explained below at the end of siman 210. This is the correct text.
He must fulfill his oath, as explained above, etc. See there, where I cited Rivash's wording and explained the distinction.
Likewise, a person cannot waive something that has not yet come into existence. The case there concerns Reuven, who knew that Shimon intended to give him a gift and said, "I hereby waive against Shimon everything he will give me." Shimon afterward gave him a gift through a valid kinyan. Ran ruled that the prior waiver was ineffective. See there. Rama's ruling below in seif 8, that a person can remove himself and his rights from something not yet in existence, is different. There the case concerns someone who already has a lien upon another person's assets. Since the obligor is already subject to him, he can remove that lien even from property that the obligor has not yet acquired. The underlying personal obligation already exists. This is clear. See what I write further there.
