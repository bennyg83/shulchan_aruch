If the seller exerted himself. Maayan Ganim records a case in which a person sublet to another a vaulted premises that he himself held under a three-year lease. The sublease stated that if the original landlord would agree to rent the premises to him beyond those three years, he in turn would be obligated to rent it to the subtenant for the same additional period. In the end, the first tenant did renew his lease for a further term, and the subtenant claimed entitlement to remain for that additional term by virtue of the written agreement, which had been made with a formal kinyan. The first tenant wished to retract after the original three years. Maayan Ganim writes that, in his view, the first tenant may retract because at the time of the agreement the future lease beyond the first three years was not yet in his possession. This is not comparable to the Rama's rule here that if the seller later exerted himself and acquired the property, the buyer acquires it. That rule applies where a seller simply sold something not presently in his possession, such as thirty kor of wheat, and later acquired it, in which case we infer that he bought it for the original buyer in order to keep his word. Here, however, the tenant was under no obligation to renew his own lease at all, so the rationale of keeping faith with the subtenant does not apply. He notes that Responsa Har HaCarmel, Even HaEzer, discusses a similar case and disputes this conclusion, and the matter requires study there. Netivot HaMishpat, citing the Taz, adds that the rule that later effort by the seller benefits the buyer applies specifically where the seller knew at the time of sale that the property was not his while the buyer did not. If the buyer also knew that it was not in the seller's possession, the rationale of keeping faith does not apply unless the seller clearly promised to procure it. If the seller himself did not know that it was not his, that rationale likewise does not apply. Netivot further writes that the presumption that the seller procured the property in order to keep faith applies only to a specific identified object. With grain and similar merchandise in which the seller routinely trades, one cannot infer that his later acquisition was made specifically for this buyer.
