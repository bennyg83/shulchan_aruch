He accepts the curse of "Who exacted payment." Sema limits this to an item readily available in the market. If the goods are not ordinarily available, he says there is no "Who exacted payment." The Shach disagrees and says the mention of a market price merely avoids an interest prohibition. Sema is correct. Kesef Mishneh explains Rambam that an item commonly sold in the market is treated as though it were within the seller's possession for this purpose. That is why money can create the level of acquisition sufficient for "Who exacted payment." Where the goods are not obtainable and are truly outside the seller's possession, the case resembles future property. Rambam expressly rules that if one sells future palm fruits and later retracts, he need not accept "Who exacted payment." The Gemara indicates that the curse rests on the fact that money acquires by Torah law and the Sages only withheld final rabbinic acquisition. Where the object could not be acquired by Torah law at all because it does not exist or is wholly outside the seller's possession, the basis for the curse is missing. I later found that the early authorities dispute this point explicitly. Some hold, like Sema, that "Who exacted payment" applies only where Torah acquisition by money was possible. Others extend the moral sanction more broadly. The Kesef Mishneh and Rambam's formulation support Sema.
