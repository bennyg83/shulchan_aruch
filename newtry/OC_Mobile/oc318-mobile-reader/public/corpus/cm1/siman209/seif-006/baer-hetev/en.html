In the market. In Bava Metzia 63b the Gemara states that one who gives money at an early market rate, meaning before a settled market price has emerged, where the seller has the commodity and sells it cheaply, must present himself to the seller at the threshing floor or say to him in the market, "I am relying on you." Then, if the seller retracts, he is subject to the curse of Mi Shepara. Without this, he need not receive Mi Shepara, because the seller can say, "I assumed that you would buy elsewhere." The Rambam records this in chapter 22 of Hilchot Mechirah. I do not know why the Tur and Shulchan Aruch omitted this law; I have not found it elsewhere. Taz.
To purchase. The Sema writes that this applies specifically if it is known and common for that item to be sold in the market. Then, even if the buyer knew that the seller did not presently possess it, since it is readily available for purchase the seller is obligated to make the effort to buy it for him. But if it is not commonly available in the market, this does not apply. For this reason they were precise here to write, "one who contracts at the market price," whereas in the preceding seif they ruled that a person does not acquire an item that is not in the seller's possession. The reason there is that the market price had not yet emerged at the agreed rate. The Maggid Mishneh likewise writes this. Sema. It does not appear so to me. The preceding seif says only that the buyer does not acquire, in the sense that either party may retract. Here, however, the issue is that the party nevertheless receives Mi Shepara. It may be that even where the item is not readily available he receives Mi Shepara. The phrase "the market price has emerged" is used here because otherwise the transaction would involve a prohibition, and it is obvious that one does not receive Mi Shepara for refusing to carry out a prohibited transaction, as the Rama writes in the gloss. As for the Sema's statement that the Maggid Mishneh supports him, I examined the passage and found no such implication. I later saw that Bedek HaBayit supports my view, writing: "Know that when we say that an item not in one's possession is like an item not yet in existence, this means only that the buyer does not acquire. Nevertheless, the seller is subject to Mi Shepara, as is clear from the Rambam, chapter 22 of Hilchot Mechirah." This implies that Mi Shepara applies in every such case. Shach.
