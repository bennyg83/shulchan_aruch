And if he sold the tree for its fruits. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 144, concerning one who contracted with another that the latter would supply a specified quantity of grain from which a specified amount of spirits would be distilled, and the question whether he could later withdraw on the ground that the object of the sale had not yet come into existence. Chatam Sofer writes that the facts were not stated with sufficient precision. If the arrangement was that the other party would distill spirits for him from this grain, the matter is not an attempted transfer of something not yet in existence, but rather resembles hiring a worker to press one's grapes. If he sold the grain on condition that the price would be paid with spirits of equivalent value, there is no insistence that the spirits come specifically from this grain. If he sold the grain to the buyer so that the buyer would produce a fixed quantity of spirits for himself and the seller would afterward take the remaining residue, this is like selling a palm tree for its fruits, and according to all opinions the buyer acquires. But if the seller retains ownership of the grain, personally produces the spirits, and sells to the buyer only the spirits that will emerge from this particular batch, it initially resembles selling the future fruits of a palm tree. Nevertheless, Chatam Sofer does not regard the cases as identical, because here completion is in human hands. It is therefore not considered something that has not yet come into existence, comparable to selling the wine of one's grapes at harvest time, and even stronger than the case of unripe produce discussed by Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov in Kiddushin 62b. He notes that the subject is lengthy and that he had not been asked to rule on that precise formulation. See also Nachalat Tzvi on this point.
