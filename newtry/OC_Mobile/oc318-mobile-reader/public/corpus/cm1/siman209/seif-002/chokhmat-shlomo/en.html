Seif 2. Note: Concerning one who purchases sealed etrogim and, when they are opened, finds them badly spoiled and suffers a substantial loss, whether the laws of onaah or mistaken sale apply: in truth, I have been young and have grown old, and I have never seen anyone bring a claim of onaah or cancellation of sale concerning etrogim. I have sat here for thirty-seven years, and this city is a primary center of the etrog trade, yet such a claim has never been heard. The reason is that merchants customarily buy them on this understanding, whether they prove good or bad. Although one who stipulates, "You shall have no claim of onaah against me," remains subject to onaah as a matter of strict law, established merchant custom can nevertheless govern the transaction, just as merchant custom can create an effective mode of acquisition even where it is not a Torah mode of acquisition. Thus a trade custom that such sealed etrog sales are not subject to onaah is effective. There is also a further rationale, based on the Sema in siman 209. Even according to the Rambam that gambling is not an asmachta, the seller of an unknown item is different where the seller knows what it contains and the purchaser does not. In gambling, neither side knows the outcome. With sealed etrogim, however, the seller generally knows no more than the purchaser. The situation therefore resembles gambling, and common practice follows the Rambam. This can also explain the Rama in siman 227, who cites an opinion that such an acquisition is effective, although the Sema wonders why the Rama did not cite that opinion here in siman 209. In siman 209 the seller knows and the purchaser does not. In siman 227 the purchaser knows that the seller too does not know, like one who scoops up an unknown amount of money while the other sees that he does not know its quantity. In that situation, where both lack knowledge, the Rama brings the opinion that the transaction is effective. There is yet another distinction. The reason a stipulation waiving onaah normally fails is that the injured party did not know the extent of the loss and therefore did not knowingly forgive it. That applies where he assumes the seller knows the true value. A purchaser ordinarily relies upon the seller not to deceive him, for a merchant does not wish to ruin his reputation and people do not generally defraud one another. He therefore did not truly forgive the loss. But where the purchaser knows that the seller also does not know, he necessarily considers the possibility of error. His consent can then be regarded as knowing waiver. Likewise, siman 209 may concern a case where the purchaser saw the item and relied upon his own assessment. Even if it was inside a box, one would not normally expect gold and jewels to be hidden among straw, so he relies on an estimate. Siman 227, by contrast, may concern one who saw neither the merchandise nor its setting and therefore had no basis for estimating its value. In such a case a sale by rough measure can be effective. In any event, as a practical ruling, the laws of mistaken sale do not apply to sealed etrogim in this manner. Go and observe common practice: if one buys one or two sealed etrogim for a fixed price and, after opening them, an etrog proves extremely valuable, does the seller seek to rescind for mistake? And if it proves worthless, does the purchaser do so? Certainly not. The trade custom is that such a sale is not subject to mistaken-sale claims. This is clear in practice.
