This is merely like one who gambles. See the Sema, seif katan 2, who says that this is the Rambam's wording and that the Tur records it in his name because the Rambam holds that gambling involves asmachta. His words here contradict what he writes in siman 34, seif katan 40, and siman 370, seif katan 3; his statements there are primary. When the Rambam writes, 'This is merely like one who gambles,' he does not mean that the law is derived from gambling, for nowhere does the Rambam explain that gambling itself is an asmachta. Why should that law be clearer than the present one? Rather, he means that this is no true sale but resembles a game of chance, and the purchaser does not rely upon it. This is why he uses the negative formulation, 'This is merely like one who gambles,' rather than, 'He is like one who gambles.' Nor is there a difficulty from the law that gambling is not an asmachta, because that case rests upon a different reason, as the Tur explains in siman 207, seif 17. Here one may say that the buyer relied upon his own estimate that matters would turn out in a particular way. The Sema's distinction is unnecessary. See Responsa Mabit, part 2, siman 78.
The same applies where two people exchanged, etc. Maharashdam, responsum 123, ruled similarly regarding one who sold a ship that had sunk. See there, though the matter requires study. See the Sema, seif katan 3, who cites Darkei Moshe as saying that those authorities disagree with what is stated here in the name of the Rivash. This is not compelling, because asmachta does not apply to a handshake or charity, as explained above in siman 207, seif 19. Nor does the acquisition mentioned in Terumat HaDeshen, siman 311, prove otherwise, as I wrote above at the end of siman 203. The Rivash's ruling is primary, for we have found no authority who disputes it, and there is no reason to distinguish between a case in which the seller knew and one in which neither party knew. Examine carefully.
