Seif 2. Everything that is in this house, see Rama seif 4. Concerning a sale of property that is not specifically identified, the Taz writes in siman 241 that the halachah recognizes acquisition of an unspecified item, as shown by the universal practice in documents to convey four cubits without identifying which four cubits. Nevertheless, the rule of siman 209, seif 2 appears fixed, as the Sema explains in siman 241, seif katan 13: the dispute in siman 241 concerns an item that is not quantified as between much and little, but where nothing is identified at all and the house might contain only straw, the undertaking is an asmachta. The Shach at the end of siman 203 likewise explains that the Terumat HaDeshen's case in siman 318, where householders exchanged all that they owned, can work only when the asmachta problem is removed. According to the Sema, an exchange undefined on both sides is disputed, but a sale of 'whatever is in the house' with no identification at all is invalid according to everyone. There is a minor textual error in the Sema in siman 241, seif katan 13: he apparently means siman 209 rather than siman 256. Siman 209, seif 2 does not present the dispute because where the entire house might contain only straw there is no acquisition according to any view. At present I do not understand why a house should differ from any other unidentified property. Even if the house is filled with straw, the house itself is certainly specific property of substantial value. Why should there be a distinction between land and movables? The Terumat HaDeshen also clearly discusses an exchange including the houses themselves in addition to their contents. Even if one house is empty, it may be nearly equal in value to the other house together with its contents. Why, then, does the Rama write that Terumat HaDeshen conflicts with siman 209? See Darkei Moshe there. The Rivash may follow the Rambam exactly, which is the first opinion in siman 241, seif 4, rather than the Tur. I previously wrote concerning sale of an unidentified item. I have now not seen an explicit ruling that the sale of 'whatever is in this house' is an asmachta even where the house itself is included in the sale. The Taz's proof from the established practice of conveying four unspecified cubits should show that an unidentified sale is effective. The case of 'whatever is in this house' may be different only where nothing at all is identified, because the house might contain nothing but rotten straw of no value. There is no reason to distinguish land from movables. The expression 'this cellar of wine' in siman 209 proves nothing, since that wording is used even where the cellar itself is not sold. The Rambam's language was not rejected by the Beit Yosef, but after the Taz's proof the law may be otherwise. Accordingly, the Terumat HaDeshen cited in the gloss at the end of siman 203 may accord with the accepted law, and the Rivash may simply follow the Rambam. Until the Taz, the dispute had not been resolved. Although the Shach at the end of siman 203 appears to hold that even if the house itself is certainly sold there remains asmachta, perhaps the Taz should be followed. The Chacham Tzvi corrected various passages of the Taz on Choshen Mishpat and objected where he found grounds to object; he lived later and had seen the Shach. His silence here may be an admission. Yet silence may not be decisive, because when reviewing many passages an author may comment only on those he examined closely. Perhaps the Shach's reason is that the custom concerning four cubits in documents is not decisive. This custom is already mentioned by Tosafot and thus was known to the Rivash. Still, it may prove validity because the halachah already determines that when such language is used the recipient receives only the least valuable four cubits. It is therefore as though four identified cubits of the lowest value were specified, since the documents speak of four cubits in one's house or courtyard and even the least valuable parcel has some value. Tosafot and the Rosh at the beginning of the fifth chapter of Ketubot explicitly state that the document says 'in his courtyard.' Although this does not appear to be the Rambam's position, customary practice may decide the matter. This differs from exchanging 'all that they possess,' where the subject matter may be wholly undefined and may encompass far more than the minimum. The Tur in siman 241 writes that whatever works for a sale also works for a gift. Yet one might argue that even if a sale of this kind is an asmachta, a gift is less subject to asmachta because a little is better than nothing, whereas in a sale the purchaser may have acted only because he assumed the contents were worth the price. This entire matter requires study. Elsewhere I wrote concerning chametz sale documents that simply sell the locations and everything in them. Since the parties' intent is apparent from their conduct, there is already a basis to uphold them. According to the Taz it is stronger still, because at least the locations themselves are sold, and the non-Jew also knows that there is valuable property there. According to the Shach, the matter may remain only doubtful. There is also an opinion that asmachta does not invalidate a sale to a non-Jew. Nevertheless, as the strongest formulation one should write even in a chametz sale document that the transaction is effective 'from now' before an important beit din, which removes asmachta according to everyone, as the Shach writes at the end of siman 203 regarding an exchange of all their possessions. According to the Terumat HaDeshen's approach that only the excess value is appraised, it may be as though nothing at all was identified, like the contents of a house that might be worthless straw, since the total assets of the parties might in fact be exactly equal and then there would be no excess on which the exchange could take effect. Yet if a gift is less subject to asmachta, such an exchange might again resemble a gift, because perhaps no monetary claim exists at all. If so, the Shach would not have needed to require removal of asmachta in the Terumat HaDeshen's case. One can answer that we should not multiply disputes: perhaps the Shach agrees with the Taz generally, but requires removal of asmachta there because it remains possible that the transaction has nothing on which to take effect, just as in siman 209, seif 2, which the Sema says the Taz too concedes. We have not found a distinction there between sale and gift. Urim VeTumim in siman 60 distinguishes in the opposite direction and says that a sale is more effective where the amount is unbounded. His intent must be to read all the cases he cites, such as 'whatever my net will bring up,' as having been made effective from now before an important beit din, for the Rambam explicitly rules that one cannot sell an unbounded obligation, as in siman 209, seif 2. Regarding siman 232, which the Sema cites in siman 241, the new editions of the Tur quote Perishah similarly. The Bedek HaBayit there explicitly explains that the rule is because the case resembles onaah. The Taz at the end of siman 227 gives a sufficient explanation why the Tur disputes the Rambam in siman 232 but not in siman 227. Hence no proof can be drawn from simanim 227 and 232 to simanim 209 and 241. The four cubits in documents might instead work through an admission, as Tosafot and the Rosh explain in Ketubot. One could say that the document merely records that he must have conveyed four cubits in an effective manner; the instrument is not intended to identify those cubits. Yet the authorities who follow the Rambam hold that one should not write an unspecified four cubits, as the Beit Yosef says in siman 241. Their concern may be that since the holder of a document has the weaker hand, we cannot interpret his admission in the most effective way. Perhaps he admitted only an unspecified transfer, in which case the lien is void. This strengthens the Taz's proof. Likewise, my earlier suggestion that four cubits is comparable to 'a house from among my houses' at the end of siman 214 is untenable, because Kesef Mishneh, cited in the annotations to the Tur in siman 241, explains that even according to the Rambam one must have a particular small and large house in mind. That does not apply to the four cubits in standard documents. Still, even according to the Rambam, the rule that 'a house from among my houses' is not sold may concern acquisition of the body of the property, because the seller can continually say 'not this one,' just as in the law of one who writes all his property to his slave, from which the Rambam derives the rule. Those who disagree either hold that the final halachah does not apply this continual deferral, or that such deferral exists only where the donor retained something, because he may point to every portion and say that it was the portion retained. In an ordinary sale, however, once the language conveys a portion of his fields, the least valuable field is automatically included and the sale attaches to it. If two least valuable fields are equal, whichever is later selected is revealed to have been the field included from the outset. The seller cannot keep deferring because the nature of a sale imposes an obligation not to do so. This differs from a gift, especially a gift with a reservation, where the donor reserves generously for himself and may reserve the right of choice. This requires study. On this basis, the Rama's statement at the end of siman 203 that an exchange of all their assets concerns only equal value may work even according to the Rambam. Siman 209, seif 2 fails because the house might contain only worthless straw, not because of continual deferral. But the rule in siman 241 regarding an unspecified field may be only because of deferral, and that does not apply in the Terumat HaDeshen's exchange where the exchange certainly attaches at least to the land itself. The Rivash may have discussed a case where no house was included in the exchange. I do not presently have the responsum before me. Alternatively, he may hold that the exchange is not limited merely to equal value. In any event, it appears that the rule of an unidentified kind in siman 209 and the rule of an entirely unidentified field in siman 241 are parallel. Since the Taz says that where the sale certainly attaches to some portion it is treated as identified, perhaps one no longer needs even the species to be specified. The Sema's statement in siman 241 that the house might contain straw may mean precisely that the species itself is unknown, whereas all the fields in siman 241 are of one species. On this approach, the practice of four cubits proves only that an unspecified item within a known species can be acquired. I later saw that this is indeed the Sema's language in siman 241, that the main issue is whether the species is known. In the Beit Yosef to siman 241 and the cited gloss in the name of Perishah there appears a reference to siman 127, which seems to be a scribal error for siman 207. The case in siman 230, where 'this cellar of wine' is sold even if it is all vinegar, does not contradict siman 209, seif 2, because that ruling follows Rav Hai Gaon, and vinegar may be considered one species with wine. Therefore, in chametz sale documents, if the different kinds of movables in the sold locations are not itemized, there remains a concern of asmachta, and it is preferable to write that it is effective before an important beit din. Still, all beverages and chametz expressly specified are certainly sold. According to the apparent ruling at the end of siman 203 regarding 'acquire you and a donkey,' the valid portion of a mixed transaction remains effective. Even according to those who hold that the entire transaction fails when part cannot take effect, one can perhaps distinguish a subject whose species is unknown: the sale does attach, but is weakened by asmachta only as to the unidentified portion, while the expressly identified species can be sold. Yet the Rivash cited at the end of siman 203 does not appear to agree, and the matter requires study. Perhaps even expressly listed categories remain subject to asmachta if the non-Jew does not know their details. Therefore the safest wording is still 'from now before an important beit din.' If the seller did not identify the item orally but delivered a document in which it was identified, and the purchaser did not read the document, perhaps everyone agrees that it is sufficiently identified, because the purchaser relies on a matter readily capable of being discovered. Perhaps even if the document itself does not identify it, but says that the movables sold are separately marked, that too may suffice. This requires careful study.
