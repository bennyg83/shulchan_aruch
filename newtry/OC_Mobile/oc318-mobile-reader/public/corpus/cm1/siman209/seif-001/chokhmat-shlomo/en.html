Seif 1. Note: See my responsum to the community of Tulchin, now printed in the laws of yibbum in my Even HaEzer, sixteenth edition, siman 152. There I proposed, with the help of Hashem, that the rule that a person cannot transfer something not yet in existence may perhaps be only rabbinic, while by Torah law a person can transfer even something not yet in existence. This is analogous to what the Shach cites in the name of the Bach in siman 207 regarding asmachta. See there also my explanation of Bava Batra 127b. There I was also asked by the late distinguished Rabbi Meshullam Yaakov, av beit din of Barshtov, concerning whether the sale of his established leasehold right in a liquor concession was effective. I answered as follows. Regarding acquisitions of a chazakah right, the Shach refers in Choshen Mishpat siman 140, seif katan 1; siman 175, seif katan 61, concerning the law of a neighboring owner; siman 192, seif katan 1; and siman 227, seif katan 5. Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 90, seif katan 51, treats such a right as land for certain purposes, and see also siman 100, seif katan 13. From all these sources it is evident that for some laws the right has the status of land and for others of movable property, but according to all of them it is capable of sale and acquisition. This is unlike the law of a neighboring owner. There the Sages did not grant the neighbor any proprietary share in the land; he merely has a preferential right to purchase it. A legally established chazakah right, however, is as though the enactment grants him a share or legal interest in the property, making him akin to a partner for this purpose. It is therefore certainly transferable. Indeed, it is more reasonable to treat it as land, as Beit Shmuel writes. In any event, the cited sources prove that sale applies to it. Even if one were to argue that a formal sale cannot apply, a relinquishment certainly can, for one may relinquish even a right relating to something not yet in existence, as stated in siman 209. At the least he possesses a sufficient legal interest for relinquishment to be effective. His Honor also wrote that the sale document states that he transfers his chazakah right and that the purchaser shall pay his debts, and suggested that this is a condition. That does not appear correct. The Gemara and Choshen Mishpat siman 253 discuss one who says, "Give to so-and-so, and he shall marry my daughter," and treat these as two separate matters. This shows that where one first states the benefit to the other party, the later statement is not necessarily a condition. Had he instead said, "He shall pay my debts and I sell him the chazakah," there would be greater basis to treat it as conditional, and even then the matter would depend on whether the formal laws of conditions are required, as discussed by the Shach in siman 253. But with the order used here it is not a condition. Nor does a compelling presumption help the seller. Since for the law of a neighboring owner this right is treated as movable property rather than land, it certainly does not possess the strong presumption that a person does not ordinarily sell his landed estate. For this issue it is therefore like movable property, and that presumption does not apply. The seller's only plausible claim is that he sold the right for only three years. That is a matter requiring an oath between the parties, and he can make that claim even though the document is written without limitation, as may be seen from simanim 60, 163, and 212, each according to its context. It appears that the seller is regarded as the possessor, since for several laws this right is treated as land, and especially because he has already leased the concession. He should therefore swear that his intent was not to sell it permanently. This is my inclination.
