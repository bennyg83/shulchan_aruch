Likewise, one can remove himself and his power from future property. This requires extended clarification. At the beginning of Perek HaKotev, the Gemara explains that an engaged man can write to his betrothed, "I have no claim or dealings in your property," and thereby remove future marital rights before marriage. Ran holds that such removal works even regarding Torah rights. The Gemara's reference to "I do not want the Sages' enactment" is needed only to show that rabbinic rights can also be rejected despite the Sages sometimes strengthening their enactments. Tosafot appears to ground the rule specifically in the fact that the right is a rabbinic benefit created for the person's advantage. Later authorities therefore questioned whether Tosafot would allow removal of a future Torah right. In my view, for practical halachah Tosafot too can allow it. The reason their sugya initially distinguishes Torah rights is that an attempted removal from a Torah entitlement can resemble conditioning against the Torah. But Rabbi Yehudah holds that in monetary matters a person may condition against Torah monetary rights. Once we rule like Rabbi Yehudah, a person can decline even a Torah monetary entitlement that arises for his benefit. This is reflected in Tosafot in Perek HaZorek. They distinguish inheritance from one's father because that inheritance is a Torah right already inherently awaiting him, while the husband's future inheritance of his wife arises through the marital relationship and can be declined before it fully enters his domain. The issue is not simply Torah versus rabbinic. It is whether the right has already come sufficiently into his legal sphere. Ran explains similarly that one cannot disclaim an inheritance from one's father because it is available to him at every moment and is treated as though already in his possession. A future marital right is not yet in his possession and can therefore be rejected before it arrives. There remains a conceptual problem. If removal from a Torah monetary right works because Rabbi Yehudah allows conditions in monetary Torah law, several authorities explain Rabbi Yehudah as meaning that the beneficiary can waive the money. But future property cannot ordinarily be waived before it exists. How then does this removal work? I believe the better reading of Rabbi Yehudah is broader. In monetary Torah law, the Torah imposed the obligation for the beneficiary's sake. He can say that he does not want the obligation to arise against the other party at all. The power is not merely an after the fact waiver of an existing debt. It is the ability to reject the legal entitlement itself. Ramban's language supports this. He writes that in monetary matters the Torah did not compel the other party to become liable irrespective of the beneficiary's will. If the beneficiary does not want the liability, no monetary obligation arises. This is why the formulation, "on condition that you have no claim against me," can work. This also explains the distinction in the laws of overreaching and Shemittah. "On condition that you have no claim of overreaching against me" can be effective because it limits the personal right between these parties. But saying, "on condition that this transaction has no law of overreaching" attempts to erase the Torah institution itself and is ineffective. Likewise, "on condition that you will not demand the cancellation of Shemittah from me" can work as a personal waiver, whereas, "on condition that Shemittah shall not cancel," attempts to uproot the Torah rule. Accordingly, removal of a future monetary right can work even when the right is Torah based, provided the person already has the necessary legal relationship to that future right and is rejecting the benefit before it enters his possession. This is the correct practical conclusion.
And his power from something not yet in existence. Maharit discusses a woman who deposited a chest and movables belonging to her husband and stipulated with the custodian that if he later had financial dealings with her husband, he should not seize those goods for a debt. The custodian later lent money to the husband and sought to seize the deposited property. Maharit first argues that removal works only from rabbinic rights, whereas the creditor's lien is Torah law. According to what I wrote above, this is not decisive. Removal can also work regarding Torah monetary rights. Maharit further argues that removal works only where the person already has some legal connection to the future right, like a betrothed man to the property rights he will receive at marriage. Before any debt exists, a future creditor has no such connection. Maharam's responsum, allowing a woman to remove her lien from property her husband will later acquire, does not contradict this. There the debt and lien relationship already existed. The later property had not yet been acquired, but the creditor already had a present legal connection to property the debtor would later acquire. Maharit also suggests that a person who disclaims a future right can retract before the right actually arises, because speech can cancel speech. I do not agree. Ran's treatment of a woman waiving a future fine and his discussion of future yibbum property show that where a valid removal is effective, it prevents the future right from ever arising and cannot simply be revoked at will. Otherwise the supposed removal would provide no real protection. Maharit's final argument is correct. Even if a creditor can remove a lien from particular future assets, that does not necessarily waive the underlying debt. So long as the property remains in the debtor's hands when payment is due, the creditor can collect from the debtor himself. Collection from the debtor personally does not depend upon the lien against purchasers. Removal of the lien matters chiefly if the property is later sold. To prevent collection from the debtor himself, the creditor would have to waive the underlying debt, not merely the asset lien.
From future property. Maharam's responsum concerning a woman who removed her lien from land her husband would later acquire applies specifically to later acquired land. It does not follow that she can remove herself merely by words from land the husband already owns. Once the property is already within the creditor relationship, it is treated as though the right has already entered her legal sphere and ordinary verbal removal is ineffective without a kinyan. This parallels the law of a married woman. A husband cannot simply disclaim by words property rights that have already fully attached during marriage. The effective disclaimer is made while she is still only betrothed, when his future marital rights have not yet entered his possession. There is still some doubt according to the opinion that even a betrothed man cannot remove rights from property that she will acquire only after marriage. A creditor whose debtor already owns some property may resemble a married man rather than a betrothed one, since he already has an existing lien relationship. If so, perhaps he cannot disclaim even property the debtor will later acquire. One can distinguish because several authorities allow the creditor to retract from the later acquired property arrangement until the property actually comes into existence, whereas the betrothed man's marital disclaimer is final. This can make the creditor's future lien weaker and more susceptible to removal. As to movables, there is reason to say a creditor can disclaim even existing future seizure rights because movables themselves can ordinarily be sold freely. But, as explained above, such removal matters only against third party transfers. If the debtor still owns the movables at payment time, the creditor can collect directly because personal collection does not depend on the lien. Sema correctly distinguishes waiver from removal by saying that removal requires some existing legal connection to the right. Before any debt exists there is no connection and no removal is possible, just as a person cannot disclaim marital rights before betrothal. His wording is imprecise only insofar as it seems to imply that removal is easier from property already acquired. The opposite is true. Once the property is already within the existing lien, verbal removal is weaker; its primary use is for property the debtor has not yet acquired.
