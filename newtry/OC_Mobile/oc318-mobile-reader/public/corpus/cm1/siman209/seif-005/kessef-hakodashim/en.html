I further found. Regarding a seller who sells what he does not possess and later exerts himself and buys it, as to whether the buyer can be compelled to accept, the matter appears analogous to simanim 183 and 269. If the buyer knew the seller did not own it, the seller is effectively his agent to buy, and the buyer should have to revoke that agency before two witnesses prior to the seller's acquisition. If the buyer did not know and believed the seller had the goods, but before the seller bought them the buyer learned that he did not and that he intended to buy, the buyer's silence may not create agency. Even if the buyer said 'buy it if you do not have it,' perhaps this still is not definite agency, for he may mean only that if it is later shown that the seller lacks it, he should buy it, without presently accepting the seller's assertion. If the merchandise rose in price, the presumption that the seller exerted himself to uphold the sale may not apply, as the Taz indicates. But if there is a signed writing stating that the merchandise is in existence, the seller later bought it, and it was then lost by accident, the seller's written obligation may make the price increase irrelevant to his motive, since he was already firmly obligated to perform. Still, the accidental loss does not fall on the buyer where the buyer did not know the seller lacked the goods, because no agency arose. If only the seller signed the writing, admits that he did not own the goods, and the buyer knew that fact, then the rule of 'he exerted himself and bought' may apply even where the price had risen, because the seller was under a binding obligation. If a seller owned some quantity, sold a portion, and later sold all remaining goods to others at different times and varying prices, perhaps the goods sold last are treated as the goods originally sold. In fungible goods he may divide without the partner's knowledge, and we do not presume wrongdoing. Even in nonfungible goods, perhaps his intent was to sell his own portion, though he had no right to divide unilaterally; the later sale then remains contingent on the first buyer's acceptance of that division. If afterward the market price fell, the first buyer's preference may reveal that he wanted the earliest identified goods attributed to him. This requires study. If one sold a quantity of which only part was in his possession and later bought the rest, the portion initially in his possession may actually be harder to validate than the later-acquired portion under the view that a transaction combining property in existence and property not in existence fails entirely at the outset. The later acquisition may revive the sale through the rule of exerting himself and acquiring for credibility, but how can that later act transfer the portion that was already present? Perhaps according to the authorities who understand ordinary sale language as including a future acquisition, the sale is like one made 'from now, to take effect when I acquire the remainder,' so that when the missing portion is acquired, the transfer of the whole relates back. This requires study. Where the later acquisition is of material that can be converted by one or two acts of labor into the product originally sold, one must consider whether the presumption that he bought in order to uphold the sale applies. Since that rule depends on practical inference, if he did not buy substantially the same merchandise, especially where commercial practice does not ordinarily transform what he bought into the contracted product, there may be no such inference. For example, if he sold strong covered liquor and later bought diluted liquor that is not normally redistilled into that product, there is reason to say he did not buy it to perform the prior sale. Likewise, even where the rule generally applies, if the seller was independently compelled to buy or manufacture the product, such as making liquor to provide feed for his animals or because of governmental dues, his later act may not prove he acted for the sale. I heard similarly in the name of my father-in-law. Perhaps he should nonetheless declare before beit din or witnesses that he is not acting on account of the prior sale. With a non-Jew, the rule that one exerted himself and acquired in order to uphold credibility does not apply even if the non-Jew is ordinarily very reliable in business, because the later acquisition operates through zechiyah or agency, and those doctrines do not apply in the same manner to a non-Jew.
