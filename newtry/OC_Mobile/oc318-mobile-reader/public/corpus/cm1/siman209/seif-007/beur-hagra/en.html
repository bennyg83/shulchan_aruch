Some disagree. Their reason follows the principle stated below: one who reserves rights does so generously, as stated there on 148. A shechiv mera, however, reserves nothing for himself; and we do not presume a generous reservation for his heirs, as explained above, because if he dies his heirs receive nothing under that formulation. The first opinion holds that the earlier case is different because he expressly reserves for himself, “for me” but not “for my heirs.” Certainly according to the Rashbam, whose explanation depends on surplus wording, the distinction follows.

Some say specifically, etc. Ramban challenged Rashbam’s explanation, s.v. “he shall give, etc.,” because the Gemara on 148a says the reason is that one who reserves does so generously, not because of surplus wording. Ramban therefore explains that where he reserves an undefined nonphysical benefit, the reservation functions only as a condition, and in Gittin chapter 7 and Kiddushin chapter 3 the rule is “for me but not for my heirs.” This differs from reserving a defined physical object that never entered the sale.
(Collected note) Some say, etc. Ramban’s wording in brief is that “for me but not for my heirs” was said only where he reserves to himself produce from the body he sold or gave, because the corpus belongs to the buyer and the reservation is like a condition. But, etc. The Gra agrees with Ramban’s ruling but not his reason. Just as one cannot sell an intangible right, even where the only interest in the object is that intangible right, so too one cannot bequeath an intangible right. The Gemara 148a expressly says “since an heir inherits it.” It is surprising that these great authorities overlooked that Gemara. See what was written in siman 212 seif 3. (End of note.)
