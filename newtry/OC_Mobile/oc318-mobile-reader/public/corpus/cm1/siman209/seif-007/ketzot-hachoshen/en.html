So too one cannot reserve future property. The Gemara asks whether one who sells an animal while excluding its fetus has retained something in the animal itself. If a fetus were wholly future property that could never be reserved, the question would seem impossible. The correct analysis depends on the status of the fetus. According to Rambam, as I wrote earlier, a fetus can be sufficiently in existence as the object of a transaction even though it lacks legal capacity to receive property. One can therefore reserve the fetal interest itself without invoking the rule against reserving future property. Terumat HaDeshen discusses selling a fetal interest in a firstborn animal to a gentile and recommends transferring the mother for the fetus, like transferring a palm for its fruits. He considers both money and meshichah because of the disputes concerning gentile acquisition. Even according to the view that the fetus is not independently transferable, transferring the mother's body for the fetus provides an existing object capable of acquisition. The question of timing remains. If the transfer takes effect only when the fetus is born, firstborn sanctity may take effect at the same time and precede the human transaction. One can instead structure the transfer to take effect with the first partial emergence, before the majority emerges and the sanctity becomes complete, according to the authorities who view firstborn sanctity as prospective rather than retroactive. Terumat HaDeshen also assumes that where the fetus itself is the subject of sale, the fact that it has no independent meshichah may leave room for money even if the mother can be physically pulled. The decisive mode can follow the primary subject of sale. This reinforces that the fetus problem is not a simple application of the rule against future property.
