The father consumed it for one year, etc. See Sema, seif katan 3, who cites the dispute among the authorities whether the son must be an adult, or whether even a minor suffices since he comes with the claim that his father purchased the field. The words of the Beit Yosef here require study. He brings in connection with this issue the dispute between Tosafot and Rashbam whether the son must already have been an adult during his father's possession or need only have been an adult at the time of his father's death. That dispute was never said concerning the present law, where the father possessed the property and the son continued afterward. In such a case it is obvious that if the son was an adult when his father died, the possession is effective. He is no worse than a purchaser who comes with the claim that he bought from the father, whose possession is effective when the transaction has publicity; the same is true of a son who comes by inheritance. Tosafot and Rashbam dispute a different case, where one possessed property in the presence of the owner's son. Rashbam holds that even an adult son is considered a minor regarding his father's affairs, and therefore may not have protested because he did not know his father's matters. Accordingly, Rashbam requires that the son have been an adult during the father's lifetime and during the possession against the father. This has no connection to the case where the father possessed and the son later continued the possession. The Beit Yosef's comparison therefore requires study.
Provided that he purchased by a deed. I have room for doubt. The Tur writes that when one possessed in the presence of a purchaser, even if that purchaser bought this field together with other fields and this field was explicitly identified, nevertheless the purchase lacks sufficient publicity because it was sold together with the others. What is the law in the reverse case, where the possessor purchased from the son all of the son's fields, including this field, with this field explicitly identified, and then consumed it during the third year? Does he have a chazakah, or may the original owner say: because such a bundled purchase has no publicity, I did not know that you had acquired this particular field and therefore did not think it necessary to protest, since you yourself possessed it for only one year? In my view this is demonstrated by the dispute below in seif 4 concerning possession in the presence of a purchaser, whether the purchaser's acquisition must specifically have been by deed or whether even a purchase without a deed suffices. According to the view requiring a deed in order for the purchase itself to constitute a protest, Tosafot and Ramban already asked that the baraita stating that possession "in the presence of the purchaser" creates a chazakah could have been established where the purchaser bought before witnesses. Ramban answers that the two baraitot were taught together. Yet if so, even after the Gemara answers that the seller sold his fields collectively, the two baraitot still do not appear to describe the same case. If the purchaser bought the fields collectively without this field being identified, the original owner never heard that this particular field had been bought, so why should he have protested? We must therefore understand "he sold his fields collectively" as the commentators and the Tur explain: this field was specifically identified, but since it was sold together with the other fields, the transaction lacked full publicity. The authority holding this view also maintains the reverse principle stated explicitly by Tosafot in the discussion of three regions for chazakah: the owner has a greater obligation to investigate than the possessor. An owner investigates and seeks information about who is occupying his property even in another country during an emergency, whereas the possessor does not investigate whether the owner is protesting. That is why a protest in the Galilee is ineffective, as Tosafot explain, and the Rosh writes similarly as discussed above. Accordingly, when the purchaser himself possesses the field, even though he purchased it together with other fields, once this particular field was identified, had the original owner investigated he would have learned that the purchaser acquired it and should have protested. By contrast, where someone purchases this field from the original owner together with other fields, there is no clear publicity because it was bundled with the others, and the possessor has no reason to investigate whether the owner sold the field or protested. Why should it enter his mind that the owner sold it or protested when the possessor knows that he acquired it lawfully? The two baraitot can therefore indeed be taught in parallel. This also resolves the difficulty all the commentators raise against the Tur. The Tur rules not like Rashbam, but like the alternate explanation that even when the field was specifically identified, the transaction lacks publicity if it was sold together with other fields. From where did the Tur derive this, when the Rosh's words are stated only generally? See the Bach and Derishah, who strain the Rosh's wording, but their inference is insufficient to establish such a significant rule. According to what I have written, however, the matter is clear. The Tur and Rosh hold like the view that even for possession in the presence of a purchaser a deed is required, and they follow their broader approach, like Tosafot, that the owner has a greater obligation to investigate than the possessor. They were therefore faced with Tosafot and Ramban's question and accepted Ramban's answer that the two baraitot were taught together. Yet unless this field was specifically identified, the baraitot would still not be parallel. One must therefore say, as above, that the field was identified but nevertheless the sale had insufficient publicity because it was included among other sales. Rashbam, however, who holds that the field itself was not specifically identified, follows the view of Rabbenu Yonah, Ramban, and Rashba that possession in the presence of a purchaser does not require a deed. The two baraitot therefore need not concern the same type of transaction at all, because the second baraita can involve a purchase without a deed. Hence there is no basis to introduce the novel proposition that even an expressly identified field lacks publicity merely because it was sold with other fields. Rashbam therefore understands the Gemara only where the fields were sold without specifying this field. This is also more logical. If a sale before witnesses alone has publicity, why should a written sale that expressly identifies the field lose publicity merely because other fields were included? Without a compelling source there is no reason to say so. The Bach writes that even the latter view accepts this rule, but my understanding does not agree. There is no basis to impose it without the necessity just explained, and Rashbam's reading of the Gemara's plain language is against it. Returning to the question with which we began, according to all opinions, when the purchaser is the one possessing the field, even if he bought it together with other fields, once this particular field was identified there is sufficient publicity and he acquires a chazakah. Even according to the latter view, which places less investigative responsibility on the owner than on the possessor, there is no basis to say that such a specifically identified purchase lacks publicity. This appears clear.
