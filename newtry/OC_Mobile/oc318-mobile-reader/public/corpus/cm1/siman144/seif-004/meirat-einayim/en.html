There is no greater protest than this. The Tur writes: the possessor should have preserved his deed forever. Meaning, the son's sale is stronger than an ordinary protest. After an ordinary protest, if the claimant remains silent for three years, that silence appears to retract the protest, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 146:5. Here, however, once the son sold the property to another, he can no longer retract the effect of the protest. Even if he later admits that his protest was improper and that the field belonged to the possessor, his admission cannot prejudice the purchaser. So writes the Rosh. Although the Mechaber does not explicitly write the word 'forever' here, he writes it twice in siman 146, seifim 6 and 9. The Tur also repeats it there. He further writes there in seifim 32 and 33 in the name of the Rosh and Rabbi Yeshayah that the requirement to preserve the deed forever applies only where the possessor does not know that the purchaser has a deed. Then he fears claiming, 'I bought it from you,' because perhaps the seller will come and challenge him. But if he knows that the purchaser has a deed, after three years in the purchaser's presence the possessor is believed to say, 'I later purchased it from the seller,' through a migo that he could have said, 'I purchased it from you.' The Mechaber writes this there in seif 21. One might ask: the Rosh says the son's sale counts as a protest only when made by deed, because a deed creates publicity. If so, why should the possessor lack a migo because perhaps he does not know of the deed? The answer is that a protest must be done in a manner capable of reaching the possessor, but actual knowledge is unnecessary. As the Tur and Mechaber write at the beginning of siman 146, even if the witnesses say, 'We did not tell anyone that the claimant protested before us,' the protest is valid because the claimant did his part in a manner by which the matter could become known. So too here: although the sale was by deed, because otherwise it would not be public enough to serve as a protest, the possessor may nevertheless not have heard of it. Therefore he may have refrained from claiming in beit din that he later bought the property from the purchaser who bought from the son.
Provided that he sold it by deed. Rashba, responsum 642, writes that if the claimant consecrated the property after the possessor had eaten its produce for one year, even if he consecrated it without a deed, the chazakah is nullified, because the remaining years were completed in the presence of one who does not know how to protest. See also the end of siman 149. Darkei Moshe cites this at the end of this siman.
Even without a deed. The Tur concludes with the reason: only with respect to purchasers who establish possession do we require a deed, because the claimant has no obligation to investigate whether they are establishing possession properly unless the matter becomes truly public. Here, however, the possessor has an obligation to investigate lest someone else sell the field. See the Perishah.
