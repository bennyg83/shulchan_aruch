That he sold it by deed. See Sema, seif katan 5, who cites Rashba's question that even if it was not sold by deed the chazakah should be nullified, because the three years ended while the property was owned by someone who could not effectively protest. At first glance, then, even an unwritten sale should have the same effect. Rama rules in siman 146, seif 4, that where the claimant secretly gives the property to another, even if that new owner protests, his protest is ineffective because the possessor can say, "I paid no attention to your protest." Since a sale before witnesses has no public voice, even if the buyer protests the possessor should similarly be able to say that he ignored the protest, so the three years ended before someone whose protest could not help. It appears, however, that the buyer's protest here is effective. The Gemara asks how it can be said that a sale before witnesses has no public voice, since a sale before witnesses allows collection from encumbered property. It answers that purchasers cause their own loss. Rashbam explains that purchasers investigate, whereas an ordinary claimant does not. The same must be true of a possessor. Otherwise possession against a buyer who purchased without a deed would not count as a protest. When the buyer protests before witnesses and the protest reaches the possessor, because the protest names the claimant, the possessor will investigate. Through investigation he will discover that the claimant sold the property, and he should then preserve his deed. By contrast, the secret gift in siman 146, seif 4, concerns a handwritten instrument that was merely authenticated, as I explain below. Even investigation will not necessarily reveal such a transfer, so the possessor can say that he ignored the protest. According to this, if one possessed land in Yehudah and at the end of three years the claimant sold it to a buyer in Galil, there is likewise no chazakah, because the buyer then could not effectively protest and the three years ended before someone who could not protest, just as with consecration. Yet there is an apparent difficulty from Ramban in Bava Batra 41, who explains that chazakah works against a minor because the proof is the owner's failure to object. During the first three years there is merely a weakness because the possessor did not preserve his deed. Ramban seems to imply that the underlying proof from possession exists immediately, even within three years, but is neutralized by the failure to preserve the deed. If so, in a case of consecration why is there no chazakah? Since the possessor did not hear of the consecration, there is no weakness from the missing deed, and the evidentiary chazakah should have arisen immediately before the property was transferred. It therefore appears that the primary explanation is Rashba's, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet on Bava Batra 29. Most people are particular about another consuming their produce for three years, but before that they are not necessarily particular. The possessor therefore continues to preserve his proof throughout the period, thinking that perhaps today or tomorrow the owner will object. Even in the case of the household of Bar Elyashiv, who are especially particular, no chazakah arises in less than three years because, since most people are not so particular, the possessor must still preserve his deed lest beit din conclude that this owner was like ordinary people and did not object. The matter depends on beit din's assessment whether this person would be particular, and until three years the possessor must preserve his deed. Ramban too can be understood this way. His statement about a weakness within three years refers to a person known to be especially particular, like Bar Elyashiv. There is no basis to say that Ramban holds chazakah is completed immediately. Even with freely roaming livestock three years are required although such property is not normally documented by deed, and all poskim indicate that the evidentiary chazakah itself is completed only at the end of three years. Therefore, in a case of consecration, although there is no weakness from the missing deed because the possessor did not hear of it, there is likewise no completed proof from possession, because the evidentiary period had not yet ended. At the end of three years the property belonged to hekdesh, and the owner's failure to protest at that point proves nothing. With freely roaming livestock, where the proof rests on failure to object and there is no deed issue, chazakah can be established outside the owner's presence because the owner should object even from afar. If he does not, that is evidence of sale. As for land where the owner is in a place from which protest cannot be heard, there is no chazakah even though he did not object. The reason appears to be that once protest cannot be heard, the possessor himself has a weakness from failing to preserve his deed, because he should fear that the owner may in fact be protesting but the protest is not reaching him. It is therefore like Bar Elyashiv, where the missing deed itself defeats the proof. According to this, regarding freely roaming livestock, where chazakah rests primarily on the owner's failure to object and there is no missing-deed weakness, there should be no difference between a place where protests are heard and one where they are not. Since there is no deed to preserve, the owner's failure to object before others proves sale. It is possible, however, that the Sages did not distinguish freely roaming livestock from land, and once they ruled that chazakah does not arise in a place where protest cannot be heard, one does not gain by the fact that such property is not normally documented, similar to Ramban's rule regarding a minor possessor.
