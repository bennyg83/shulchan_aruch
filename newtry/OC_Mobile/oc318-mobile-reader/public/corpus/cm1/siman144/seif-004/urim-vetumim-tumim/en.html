And some say, even without a deed. The Tur writes that the words of his father the Rosh, later in the episode of one who consumed for many years, incline toward the first view. The Perishah asks, and writes that this requires study: why did the Tur say merely "incline," when the Rosh there expressly writes that this applies specifically where the sale was by deed? The Knesset HaGedolah asks the same. It appears that the Rosh could indeed hold that even a sale before witnesses obligates the possessor to preserve his evidence, as the Ramban writes in his novellae, because witnesses generate publicity similar to a protest. A protest creates only a general report that so-and-so is protesting that the possessor is occupying his field unlawfully. The specific details of the protest are not widely known. Nevertheless, when the possessor hears that report, he must preserve his deed. According to this, when the property was sold before witnesses and the possessor then remained for another three years, even the view that a witnessed sale has publicity could agree that he ultimately acquires a chazakah. We rule that a protest must be renewed at the end of every three years. The Rosh's statement that when the owner sold the property to others he need not protest every three years because he cannot retract the effect of the sale and the possessor must preserve his deed refers to a case where the possessor clearly hears that the property was sold. If the sale was only before witnesses, however, the possessor does not hear the specific details of the sale. He hears only a report equivalent to a protest, that someone is challenging his ownership. After another three years pass without renewed protest, he no longer must preserve his deed. By contrast, a sale by deed creates a strong and specific public report that the property was sold to another, so the possessor must preserve his deed indefinitely. Therefore, in order for the purchaser's possession to operate against the original owner after only one year in the purchaser's presence, a deed may be required. Without a deed there is only a report comparable to a protest. Why should that matter to the original owner? He has ancestral title, and no one has yet possessed the land against him for three years. He did not necessarily hear of the actual sale. By contrast, when the original owner himself sold the property, the possessor hears the public challenge and must preserve his deed. This distinction is sound and removes the need for Ri's strained explanation that the possessor has a greater duty to investigate. Perhaps this is itself what Ri intended: the possessor must investigate because he hears a protest, whereas regarding the original owner, what significance does a mere protest-like report have? Accordingly, in the later episode of one who consumed for many years, it appears that he had consumed for four years after the sale. Had the sale been only before witnesses, he would still have acquired a chazakah, because although the publicity was comparable to a protest, a protest must be renewed every three years. Here, however, where the possessor consumed only one year in the purchaser's presence, it is possible that even the Rosh agrees that a sale before witnesses is at least equivalent to a protest and therefore prevents the chazakah. One can perhaps read the Rosh's wording precisely this way. He writes: "provided that the sale was by deed, for then it has publicity; but with witnesses without a deed it has no publicity like a protest." If his meaning were that a witnessed sale has no publicity at all, not even that of a protest, the words "like a protest" would be unnecessary. If instead he means that witnesses without a deed generate publicity only like a protest, but no stronger and clearer publicity, the wording is exact. I searched and found that the Pilpula Charifta of the Tosafot Yom Tov indeed explains "like a protest" to mean that if the owner remained silent and did not protest at the end of every three years, the possessor's chazakah becomes effective. Thus he understands the Rosh exactly as I have written, based on the phrase "like a protest," and the matter is clear. Still, the Pilpula Charifta is difficult. The Tur actually writes that the Rosh's words incline toward those who hold that a sale before witnesses has no publicity at all, not even like a protest. This also appears from what I wrote above, for the Rosh agrees with Tosafot that the owner has a greater duty to investigate than the possessor. Therefore a chazakah is heard in the Galilee but a protest is not, and the Rosh seemingly cannot be interpreted as the Pilpula Charifta proposes. Nevertheless, his words are valuable, because they reveal why the Tur had room for doubt and wrote only that the Rosh's words "incline" rather than deciding absolutely. There is another way to distinguish. Ordinarily, why should a possessor investigate whether the seller has sold the same field to another? Jews are presumed honest and do not commit wrongdoing. It would not enter the possessor's mind that the person who sold him the field would betray him by selling it again to someone else. As I wrote above, this is why a protest in the Galilee is not heard: the possessor has no reason to investigate. He therefore requires a strong public report, just as the owner does. That applies while the seller is alive. But when the seller dies and his son does not know his father's affairs, since even an adult is considered a minor regarding his father's matters, the possessor can reasonably think that perhaps the son does not know that his father sold the property and may sell it to someone else. He therefore has reason to investigate whether the heir has sold the land. Consequently, even a sale before witnesses may serve as a protest because he should have investigated, as Ri writes. The later episode of one who consumed for many years appears to involve the seller still alive, so it is not a completely decisive proof, and therefore the Tur writes only that the Rosh's words "incline" toward the first view. With these two possible distinctions, the words of the Maggid Mishneh in Hilchot To'en, chapter 12, halachah 8, are also resolved. Regarding "he consumed in the father's presence and in the purchaser's presence," the Maggid Mishneh writes that because the Rambam did not mention a deed, he holds like those who say that even a sale before witnesses constitutes a protest. The Kesef Mishneh objects that the Maggid Mishneh overlooked the Rambam in chapter 15, halachah 7, where the Rambam cites the episode of one who consumed for many years and writes that the purchaser had a deed in his hand, implying that a deed is required. It is difficult to say that the Rambam merely copied the factual wording of the Gemara. According to what I have written, however, the Maggid Mishneh understood that the Rambam distinguishes between a case where the possessor consumed only one year in the purchaser's presence and the later episode where he consumed four years afterward and the seller was still alive, in accordance with either of the two distinctions explained above. The reader may choose between them, and the matter is resolved. The Mechaber's wording can likewise be understood this way. Here he writes simply, "there is no greater protest than this," but omits the statement that the possessor can never acquire a chazakah. Below, in siman 146 seif 19, he does state that there is never a chazakah. See Sema, seif katan 4, who notices the difference. According to the above, it is clear. Here the Mechaber cites also the view that witnesses suffice, and according to that view, which treats a witnessed sale as equivalent only to a protest, the possessor certainly can acquire a chazakah after another three years, as the Pilpula Charifta explains. The Shulchan Aruch did not wish to lengthen the discussion with this distinction and therefore omitted "he never acquires a chazakah." Below, in siman 146, where he expressly rules that witnesses do not suffice, he writes that the possessor never acquires a chazakah. This also explains why here he cites two views, while below he rules expressly that witnesses are ineffective. In the later case all agree, in accordance with what has been explained. Consider this carefully. I later saw that the Lechem Mishneh also distinguishes between a sale by deed and one without a deed regarding the requirement to renew a protest every three years, though he states the matter briefly. In any event, the distinction explained here is clear and correct.
