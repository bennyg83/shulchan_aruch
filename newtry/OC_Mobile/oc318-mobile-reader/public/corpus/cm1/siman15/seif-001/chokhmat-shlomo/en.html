In my responsum on Even HaEzer siman 31 I distinguished between monetary cases and capital cases or witnesses to kiddushin. In the latter, I wrote, even the Shach concedes that one disqualified witness does not necessarily invalidate all the others where the valid witnesses themselves did not intend to join him. In my work on Even HaEzer, laws of kiddushin, siman 42, in a responsum to the province of Volhynia, I wrote that even the presence of a non-Jew can fall under the rule of one witness found disqualified, each view according to its own understanding. This follows a fortiori from other disqualified witnesses. That is true, however, only where the non-Jew understands the transaction taking place before him. Then, according to the Shach, if the valid witnesses also did not specifically intend to testify, his presence may invalidate the testimony even though he himself did not intend to testify. But if the non-Jew, and likewise any otherwise disqualified person, does not understand the event at all, his seeing it is legally insignificant, as though he did not see it. He never acquires the status of a disqualified witness and therefore does not invalidate the testimony. This appears correct as practical halachah. The authorities dispute whom the court asks, “Did you come in order to see or in order to testify?” Some understand that the valid witnesses are asked, others that the disqualified witnesses are asked. It appears to me that the intention of the valid witnesses is decisive. This may be inferred from Gittin 81b, where the Gemara asks why Rabbi Akiva did not allow a slave to sign a particular document, lest people say that a slave is valid for testimony, and then asks that the same concern should exist with a relative. One could have distinguished that a relative retains a general presumption of fitness and is disqualified only because of the particular relationship, so we could assume that he signed merely to fill the blank and did not intend to testify, whereas an ordinary slave or robber lacks such a presumption of fitness and perhaps intended to testify, invalidating the entire instrument. Since the Gemara does not make this distinction, it seems that the issue does not depend on the disqualified person’s intention but on whether the valid witnesses intended to join with him. There may, however, be room to reject this proof according to Rabbi Akiva, since in Makkot it appears that he does not distinguish whether the disqualified witness intended to testify at all. Further study is required.
There, Seif 1. I heard a question attributed to a great scholar: Why, when one of a group of witnesses is found related or disqualified, do we rule that all their testimony is void? The defendant denies the entire event and claims that the witnesses are lying. His admission against his own interest is stronger than one hundred witnesses, while his statements in his own favor are not accepted. If so, by denying that the disqualified witnesses saw the event together with the valid ones, he should be believed at least to his detriment that they were not joined, and the valid witnesses should remain valid. I answered that there is no difficulty in light of Tosafot in Kiddushin concerning an agent who also becomes a witness. Tosafot explain that even an oath cannot repair testimony where the witnesses are not believed through their testimony alone but require some additional external support, because the Torah says, “By the mouth of two witnesses a matter shall be established.” Their words themselves must be sufficient. Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 35 applies the same principle in related contexts. Here, according to the witnesses’ own account, they joined with the disqualified witnesses and their testimony is therefore void. It can become effective only by combining it with the litigant’s admission. Such witnesses are not believed by their words alone and therefore do not constitute valid testimony. This differs from one who says, “I never borrowed,” where witnesses testify that he borrowed and repaid. His statement that he never borrowed is treated as an admission that he did not repay, and he is liable. There, the witnesses do not need his admission to establish the loan. Their testimony regarding the loan is complete by itself, and the separate issue of repayment is defeated by his own admission. Here, by contrast, the witnesses testify to one indivisible matter and their own version makes them legally disqualified for this testimony because they joined the disqualified witnesses. Whether the defect originated in the relative himself or in the otherwise valid witnesses who joined him, the Torah has invalidated them for this testimony. They therefore cannot be rehabilitated by the litigant’s admission. This also explains why a personally interested witness is different according to those who permit his testimony once the interest is removed. Personal interest is not an intrinsic defect in the witness. It is an external impediment. Indeed, according to several authorities such a witness need not have been fit both at the beginning and the end. Once the impediment is removed, he remains a witness whose words themselves are capable of testimony. A disqualified group, however, has no such standing for that testimony. Even if a litigant’s admission is added, they would still be witnesses whose words are insufficient without outside assistance, which is not testimony. This is clear and correct. The rule also has obvious application where a person says, “I do not know whether I borrowed,” and witnesses testify that he borrowed, but the group includes relatives. In that case the entire testimony is void and there is no admission that can cure it. This also answers the approach cited in the introduction of the author of Shitrei Torah. Examine this carefully, for I have been brief.
