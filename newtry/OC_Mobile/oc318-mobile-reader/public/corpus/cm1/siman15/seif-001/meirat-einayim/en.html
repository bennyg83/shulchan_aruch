To adjudicate the case that came before him, etc. It appears priority does not depend literally on who physically entered beit din first. Rather, once a litigant presents himself before the judge and the judge prepares to hear his claim, that case is considered to have come before him first and takes precedence over one who later approaches the judge. I brought proof from Ritva in Derishah.

Of a Torah scholar. Even if the Torah scholar came later. Honoring a Torah scholar is also a positive commandment derived from "You shall fear Hashem your God," where the Sages expound the extra word to include Torah scholars. Thus there is a positive commandment to honor the Torah scholar, while there is also the commandment to hear the small case like the great case for the earlier litigant. The honor of Torah takes precedence. This is implied by the end of Shevuot 30a. See Derishah.
To search for merit on his behalf. When a Torah scholar litigates with an ordinary householder, we maintain the Torah scholar in his presumption of propriety. Therefore, we analyze his words carefully and seek a truthful interpretation that supports his position where possible. See what I wrote in siman 13 and below at the end of siman 17. It appears that in our times Torah scholars do not have this law, as Rambam, cited by the Tur and Mechaber at the end of this siman, writes that since improper courts became common, etc. All the more so regarding the litra of gold for humiliating him, releasing a vow by a single judge in Yoreh Deah 228, and adjudicating alone, since today we do not have the law of a sole expert judge as explained in siman 3:2. Although distinctions are possible, this appears to me correct.
Even if they already began the case. It appears this follows from the verse, "You shall hear the small like the great," which focuses on the act of hearing. Once they began the small litigant's case and started hearing the claims, one ordinarily may no longer postpone it for the later greater case. Since we rule that the positive commandment to honor a Torah scholar overrides this commandment, it follows that even if the court already began hearing the first case, it is deferred for the Torah scholar. Nevertheless, the Sages applied the verse even before formal hearing begins: if one litigant came before the judge first, the later one may not normally be heard first. This is why the Gemara and poskim speak of who "came before him first." Since this earlier priority is rabbinic, the Sages likewise allowed it to be set aside even for a relative of a Torah scholar, in honor of the scholar.
