To seek grounds in his favor. Sema holds that nowadays no one has the special status of a Torah scholar for this law, just as with the rule concerning a litra of gold. Bach and Kenesset HaGedolah do not appear to agree, and Shevut Yaakov, responsum 144, likewise maintains that the rule still applies. That appears correct. With respect to the litra of gold, the source speaks of one who humiliates an elder, meaning a sage recognized as an expert by the public, as suggested by the Scriptural expression "elders of the congregation." Unfortunately, such universally recognized authority may be uncommon in our generation. But the Gemara here speaks of a tzurba merabbanan, which Chazal explain as a sharp young Torah scholar. Thank God, people of that description are certainly still found in our generations. The Gemara says that one should seek grounds in favor of a tzurba merabbanan. It does not restrict the rule to an elder of supreme stature. Heaven forbid that we diminish the honor of Torah and those who study it. On the contrary, in an orphaned generation it is especially incumbent upon us to honor and elevate those who labor in Torah, and to strengthen their standing. Since people ceased giving proper honor to those who study Torah for its own sake, the number of true Torah scholars has diminished and Torah has weakened because it lacks support. People began treating every face as equal, scholar and ordinary person alike. We should therefore be especially careful with this rule and similar matters. Although not a conclusive proof, Maharik, responsum 25, also writes in his own generation that one should seek grounds in favor of a tzurba merabbanan. The law therefore remains applicable in our times.
But if he is his relative, etc. The source of this law is Ritva. Ritva asks about the passage in Shevuot where R. Yochanan sent a message to Rav Nachman: "Ulla is our colleague, my equal in Torah." The Gemara asks what practical purpose the message served and answers that it was to give Ulla's case precedence. Ritva asks from the incident in the chapter Shnei Dayanei Gezeirot. Rav Nachman gave priority to a man's case because he mistakenly believed the man was a relative of Rav Anan. The opposing litigant's arguments then became confused, and as a consequence Eliyahu HaNavi ceased speaking with Rav Anan for a period. Ritva answers that the Torah scholar himself receives priority. There is no concern that the opposing litigant's claims will become confused, because everyone knows the Torah scholar deserves precedence on account of Torah. But the relative of a Torah scholar does not receive such priority. Giving him precedence can make the opponent think the judge is showing improper favor, as in the incident above. Ritva then cites his teacher's view that a Torah scholar himself is given precedence even after another case has already begun, because of bitul Torah. A relative receives precedence only if the other case has not yet begun. Rama adopts the view of Ritva's teacher as primary law and rules accordingly. Bach and Kenesset HaGedolah object. According to them, if Ritva's teacher also grants priority to a Torah scholar's relative, Ritva's original question from Rav Nachman remains. They therefore understand the teacher's statement as belonging only to Ritva's initial assumption about that incident, not as the final halachah. In practice, they hold that a relative has no right of precedence. Shevut Yaakov, responsum 144, agrees with them and suggests that Rama may have had a corrupt text of Beit Yosef's Krakow edition. But anyone who examines Ritva's novellae to Ketubot will see explicitly that Ritva's teacher, and likewise Rabbenu Chananel, state as halachah that a Torah scholar's relative receives precedence so long as the other case has not yet begun. I believe the teacher's position can be defended strongly. According to Ritva himself, who says a Torah scholar's relative has no priority and may not be advanced because of concern that the opposing litigant's claims will become confused, what exactly was Rav Anan's wrongdoing for which Eliyahu withdrew from him? Rav Anan merely sent the man to Rav Nachman without explaining that he was a relative. If the law itself gives no priority to such a relative, why should Rav Anan have anticipated that Rav Nachman, an eminent judge, would make a legal error and advance him improperly? It is difficult to say Rav Anan was punished merely because his wording created a remote possibility that Rav Nachman would misunderstand the relationship and then independently violate the law. Rather, the law must be that a Torah scholar's relative does receive precedence before another case has begun. Rav Nachman believed the man was Rav Anan's relative and therefore correctly concluded that some precedence was due. His mistake was to advance the case even though he had already opened the orphan's case. The opposing litigant then saw that Rav Nachman was favoring the newcomer beyond what halachah allowed, and his arguments became confused. Rav Anan was held responsible because his ambiguous message created the mistaken impression that the man was his relative and thereby contributed to a procedurally distorted judgment. This fits the teacher's ruling perfectly. One may still ask: from where do we know that the Torah scholar himself is advanced even after another case has begun? Perhaps the same rule applies to scholar and relative, and Rav Nachman's mistake was simply that he advanced the relative after opening the earlier case. The answer comes from the Shevuot passage itself. R. Yochanan sent to Rav Nachman: "Ulla son of Rav Ilai is our colleague, my equal in Torah," in order to have him advanced. But even without that message, Ulla was the son of a Torah scholar, as the phrase "son of Rav Ilai" itself indicates. If a Torah scholar's relative or child already receives the same priority as the scholar himself, why was it necessary to add that Ulla himself was "my equal in Torah"? Necessarily there is a difference. A Torah scholar himself is advanced even after another case has begun because of bitul Torah. A relative receives precedence only before proceedings in the other case have begun. Therefore R. Yochanan specified that Ulla himself was a Torah scholar. This supports the teacher's ruling and explains the Gemara well. According to Ritva's own final view, by contrast, Rav Anan's responsibility is harder to explain. One would have to say that Rav Nachman inferred from Rav Anan's very act of sending the litigant that Rav Anan wanted him advanced, and Rav Nachman followed what he thought was Rav Anan's intention. That is forced. Therefore the teacher's explanation is preferable. There is another point worth clarifying. Why were Ritva and the other commentators troubled by the confusion of the opposing litigant's claims? Perhaps that litigant knew the truth, namely that the man was not really Rav Anan's relative, and therefore interpreted Rav Nachman's unusual precedence as favoritism. The answer must be that whenever a judge gives one case precedence, he is obligated to explain the reason to the parties, so that they do not suspect him of distorting justice. Had the opposing litigant known the newcomer was not Rav Anan's relative, then when Rav Nachman explained that he was giving priority because of the supposed relationship, the litigant could simply have corrected the mistake. If the opposing litigant also believed he was Rav Anan's relative, there should have been no reason for his claims to become confused if such a relative lawfully receives precedence. Thus the commentators' question is well founded, and the duty of the judge to explain the reason for unusual precedence follows as well.
