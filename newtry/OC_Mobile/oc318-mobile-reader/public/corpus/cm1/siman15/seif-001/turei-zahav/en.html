Seif 1, in the gloss “But if his relative comes to judgment,” etc. Ritva derives this from Ketubot 109. One might ask: since the other litigant’s arguments became stopped up, the conduct was improper, so how can Rama rule that honoring a scholar’s relative is effective? Ritva’s point is that in Rav Nachman’s case the litigant did not know the honor was for Torah and thought it favoritism, because the man was not truly the scholar’s relative. When the relationship to a Torah scholar is publicly known, the litigant understands the honor and his arguments are not chilled. Rambam’s priority of an orphan or widow over a scholar does not contradict this. Ordinarily a scholar is presumed to waive his honor. Rav Anan’s extra message implied that he insisted on priority for his relative, and that imprecision caused the ensuing error and punishment. Thus the rules are consistent.
