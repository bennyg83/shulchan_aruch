Kiddushin take effect with them. Rama in Darkei Moshe was uncertain regarding a rabbinic ervah: if a person betrothed a woman who is rabbinically forbidden to him and then another man betrothed her, does she require a get from the second man, since the first kiddushin are not a Torah impediment and perhaps the Sages did not strengthen them fully? The same doubt can be asked regarding those forbidden by a negative commandment, where we rule that kiddushin do take effect. If one man betrothed such a woman and a second man then betrothed her, does she require a rabbinic get from the second? The latter case must concern, for example, a mamzer who betrothed a mamzeret and afterward another mamzer betrothed her, so that the second man is not himself prohibited to her by a negative commandment. If an ordinary Israelite later betrothed her, it is obvious that his kiddushin do not take effect against the first. Likewise, in the case of a rabbinic ervah, the second man must be one to whom she is not rabbinically forbidden. The Tur, however, groups rabbinic ervayot together with doubtful Torah ervayot, saying that those rabbinically forbidden or of doubtful Torah status require a get. He also first says that Torah ervayot admit no kiddushin at all, implying that rabbinic ervayot do admit at least some form of kiddushin. Still, it is astonishing to suggest that although kiddushin with a shniyah is fully valid by Torah law, the Sages somehow weakened the marriage so that a second man's kiddushin could also take effect rabbinically. If so, why do we say in Yevamot 20b that if a man had relations with or performed chalitzah to a yevamah who is prohibited only by mitzvah or holiness, her rival is released through that act? If the first kiddushin were not fully effective rabbinically and left room for another kiddushin to take effect, then her yibum bond should likewise not be fully resolved, especially where there is another wife.
