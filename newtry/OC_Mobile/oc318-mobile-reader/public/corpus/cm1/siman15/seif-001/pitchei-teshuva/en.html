To seek arguments in his favor. The Sema writes that in his view the special rules relating to a talmid chacham do not apply in our times. The Bach, however, rules that this particular law applies even today. Although we may not generally accord contemporary scholars the full formal status of a talmid chacham, love of Torah and mitzvot obligates every judge to exert himself to adjudicate carefully and search for the merit of any genuine Torah scholar. The Shach inclines to this view, as does Knesset HaGedolah. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 144, likewise rules so, reasoning that even if full talmid chacham status is absent nowadays, the category of a sharp young scholar certainly exists. He writes that the same applies to giving such a scholar priority on the docket, since the Tur and Shulchan Aruch compare these rules. Tumim agrees and writes that in generations such as ours there is all the more reason to honor and elevate those who labor in Torah. Netivot HaMishpat likewise rules this to be primary. Birkei Yosef cites an unpublished commentary of Rabbi Uri Ashkenazi, who disputes the Sema and maintains that this law applies to contemporary talmidei chachamim. He distinguishes between laws that are merely honors or that would take money from others, such as the fine of a litra of gold, cases involving possible prohibition such as vows, a scholar’s judging alone lest he err, or restrictions introduced because unfit courts proliferated. In such matters, denying contemporary scholars formal talmid chacham status causes no direct financial loss to the scholar and avoids the danger of improper extraction or error. By contrast, where the law instructs judges to search for a scholar’s legal merit, or grants tax exemptions in Yoreh Deah 243, return of lost property based on visual recognition below siman 262, or a scholar-brother’s right to keep the profit from trade conducted with joint inheritance below siman 287, the sources show that contemporary scholars retain the relevant status so that they will not suffer loss. Birkei Yosef also questions Shevut Yaakov’s derivation regarding docket priority. If Shevut Yaakov’s premise is that full talmid chacham status is absent but a tzurba merabbanan still exists, no analogy from the Tur and Shulchan Aruch was needed, because Nedarim 62a itself uses the term tzurba merabbanan regarding priority. Yet even that argument does not establish the matter according to Mahari Weil, whose responsa show that in his view even the formal category of tzurba merabbanan is absent nowadays. Nevertheless, Birkei Yosef concludes that all agree regarding priority today, because the reason for priority is to avoid wasting the scholar’s Torah-study time, as Ritva explains. Any scholar who diligently studies Torah should therefore be advanced even today. This is also implied by the Sema, who restricted his discussion to the law of seeking arguments in the scholar’s favor and did not object to the priority rule stated immediately beforehand. As for seeking the scholar’s merit itself, Birkei Yosef concludes that the Bach and Rabbi Uri are correct that even Mahari Weil would agree here, and he sees support in Rama and Terumat HaDeshen, siman 341.
