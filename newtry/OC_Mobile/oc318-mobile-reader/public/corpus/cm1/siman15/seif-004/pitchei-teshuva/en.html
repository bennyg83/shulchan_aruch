The case of the orphan, etc. Beit Yosef cites Rashba regarding one who hires a minor and withholds his wages, asking whether beit din can extract the wages. Rashba answers that they certainly can. The rule that a minor’s acquisition is protected only for the sake of social peace applies to an ownerless object, because no other person transfers ownership to him. Wages earned under a hiring arrangement are different. Knesset HaGedolah explains that Beit Yosef cites this responsum here to teach that when a minor’s wage claim comes before a judge together with an adult’s case, one should not reason that the adult must be heard first merely because theft of a minor’s found object is only rabbinically prohibited for social peace and is not recoverable through judges. Rashba establishes that withheld wages are fully recoverable, so the minor’s wage case is like any other case. It follows that where the claim truly concerns a found object of a minor or other person lacking full legal capacity, whose protection is only for social peace, other cases receive priority. The Bach writes similarly. Ketzot HaChoshen uses this to address Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 151, concerning two persons who each have a claim against the other, one having incurred a Torah oath and the other only a shevuat heset, and asks who must swear first. Shevut Yaakov ruled that a lottery should determine the order. Ketzot argues that according to the Bach and Knesset HaGedolah, the Torah oath should be administered first. If one refuses a Torah oath, beit din descends to his property; one who refuses a shevuat heset is not directly dispossessed but is placed under ban until he swears. The latter therefore resembles a minor’s found object in its weaker judicial enforceability, and the Gemara in Shevuot HaDayanim compares these categories. Thus the Torah oath should be addressed first. Beit Yehudah disagrees with Ketzot. Urim VeTumim, siman 87, seif katan 24, in the name of Radbaz, part I, siman 216, and Shaar Mishpat in the name of Raavan, hold that if the plaintiff has seized property, it is not removed from him until the defendant takes the shevuat heset. According to those authorities, a person who owes the Torah oath may say, “I will not swear until you take your shevuat heset.” It makes no difference whether the other party is presently in possession of an object or, as it were, “in possession” of the outstanding oath. Ketzot’s contrary conclusion follows his own position in siman 87 that the plaintiff’s seizure is ineffective, but Raavan and Radbaz disagree.
