The orphan's case precedes the widow's. This follows the order of the verse: "Judge the orphan; plead for the widow." Sema.
The widow's case precedes that of a Torah scholar. All the more so, the orphan precedes a Torah scholar. See Tumim, where I reconcile Rambam with the Gemara and discuss why Rambam omitted the rule concerning a relative of a Torah scholar. Chavot Yair, responsum 124, records a dispute whether these priority rules depend solely on the status of the plaintiff, or whether they also apply when the defendant is the Torah scholar or other preferred party. In Tumim I support the view that priority is determined by the plaintiff. Maharshal, responsum 24, writes that the court should actively search for meritorious arguments for a widow just as for a Torah scholar. Bach writes that even if a Torah scholar sends only an agent or authorized representative to court, his case receives priority. It appears, however, that once another case has already begun, such an agent should not interrupt it. Ritva's teacher gave the reason for interrupting even after commencement only because the Torah scholar himself is being taken away from Torah study. That reason does not apply when he is absent.
A woman's case precedes a man's. A woman does not precede a Torah scholar unless she is a widow. A widow has the additional Torah protection of "You shall not afflict any widow." An ordinary woman has only the consideration that court proceedings are more embarrassing for her. The positive commandment of honoring Torah takes priority over that consideration. Bach; see Knesset HaGedolah.
