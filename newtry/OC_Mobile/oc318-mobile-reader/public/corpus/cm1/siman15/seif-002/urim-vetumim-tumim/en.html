An orphan's case is given precedence, etc. Over the case of a Torah scholar. Kesef Mishneh and Darkei Moshe ask from the incident where Rav Nachman advanced the case of the person he thought was Rav Anan's relative ahead of an orphan's case. All the more so, it would seem, the Torah scholar himself should be given precedence over the orphan. Commentators, including Lechem Mishneh, answer that this was precisely why the opposing litigant's arguments became confused: he saw Rav Nachman giving precedence over an orphan contrary to law. According to this answer, however, Ritva's distinction and his teacher's distinction between a case already opened and one not yet opened lose their proof. Perhaps the entire error in that incident was simply advancing anyone, scholar or relative, ahead of an orphan. If so, why does Rama adopt Ritva's distinction here but leave Rambam's rule that an orphan precedes a Torah scholar without qualification? It is possible to explain Rambam differently. Where there is only one beit din in the city and both an orphan and a Torah scholar arrive, the orphan has priority. The positive obligation toward the orphan is stronger than the scholar's precedence, and the Torah scholar should waive his honor for the orphan, whose right cannot similarly be waived. But where there are two courts in the city, and one of them is disqualified from hearing the Torah scholar's case because of kinship, the other court should take the Torah scholar first while the orphan goes to the other court. In that way both priorities can be honored simultaneously. This may explain the incident in Shnei Dayanei Gezeirot. Rav Anan was himself a permanent judge. The litigant first came to Rav Anan, but Rav Anan disqualified himself and sent him to Rav Nachman. Rav Nachman mistakenly believed the man was Rav Anan's relative. He therefore advanced that case, while the orphan could have gone to Rav Anan. A God-fearing person thus satisfies both obligations. If so, according to Rama we must say Rambam learned the orphan's priority from another source, not from the Rav Anan incident, because in that incident the confusion of the opposing litigant could not have resulted merely from advancing the relative over the orphan when another court remained available. This also explains why Rambam omits the rule that a Torah scholar's relative receives priority. Rambam may follow Ritva's own view that Rav Nachman's assumption concerning the relative was a mistake. The orphan issue itself is resolved because the orphan could have gone to the other court. There is another possible explanation. Even if the orphan's priority and the scholar's priority are both positive obligations, the Torah scholar is expected to waive his precedence for an orphan. Torah scholars are gentle people and should strengthen those who are distressed. Such waiver itself is their honor and glory. The orphan, however, is not in a position to waive the protection afforded to him in the same way. That logic applies to the Torah scholar himself. But according to Rav Nachman's mistaken assumption that a relative of a Torah scholar also receives priority because of the scholar's honor, there is no one present who can waive that honor. The scholar himself is absent, and the relative has no authority to waive another person's honor. Thus Rav Nachman could reasonably have believed that the relative's priority overrode the orphan's in that circumstance. Once we reject Rav Nachman's assumption and hold that the relative has no independent priority, the orphan again precedes the Torah scholar because the scholar himself should waive. A third possibility is that Rambam was uncertain about the precise nature of Rav Nachman's error. Perhaps he erred by advancing a Torah scholar or his relative over an orphan. Perhaps the error was that he treated a relative as having the scholar's priority at all. To avoid any possibility of causing an opposing litigant's claims to become confused and thereby distorting judgment, Rambam rules stringently on both points: he omits a relative's priority and also rules that an orphan precedes a Torah scholar. In truth, Rambam's omission of the relative's priority strongly suggests that only the Torah scholar himself receives precedence because of bitul Torah, not his relative or agent. This can also be understood from Nedarim 62, where the Gemara asks how we know that a Torah scholar's case is handled first. It derives the rule from "the sons of David were priests": just as a kohen takes first, so a Torah scholar takes first. It then cites "you shall sanctify him" regarding a kohen, including opening first, blessing first, and taking a fine portion first. Why do the poskim not generally cite a rule that a kohen's civil case must always precede an Israelite's case? The answer may be that the kohen's precedence is rooted in matters of sanctity. Ordinary civil litigation is not itself an act of sanctity. For a Torah scholar, however, precedence in court becomes a matter of sanctity because delay causes bitul Torah. Thus the analogy to the kohen extends to litigation only in the scholar's case. This fits Rambam, who holds "you shall sanctify him" is a Torah obligation. Otherwise it would be difficult to explain why Rambam omits judicial precedence for a kohen. Other authorities could perhaps answer that the kohen's present-day precedence is rabbinic because full priestly sanctity depended on Temple service. Hagahot Mordechai in Gittin cites Rabbeinu Tam that nowadays the full sanctity of kohanim does not apply because they are not fit for their Temple garments. But Rambam, who records laws even for the future Temple era, is better explained by saying that judicial precedence is not inherently part of the kohen's sanctity, whereas for a Torah scholar it serves to prevent bitul Torah. Rosh in his commentary to Nedarim likewise explains the Torah scholar's precedence in terms of avoiding interruption of his learning. It is also possible that the poskim omit a kohen's priority because present-day kohanim are kohanim by established presumption rather than verified genealogy. Chavot Yair asks against this distinction: we impose even capital consequences on the basis of established presumptions, so why should a kohen of presumptive status differ from one of verified lineage? The answer is that the Gemara records that thousands of slaves became intermingled among the priestly families, and that Eliyahu will in the future clarify the lineage of Levi, as described in the end of the laws of kings. Thus the general presumption has a known element of admixture and is weakened for creating new privileges of precedence. In any event, Rambam's position can be understood as above. There is a further practical question. The questioner in Chavot Yair held that all these precedence rules apply only to a plaintiff, not to a defendant. What benefit does a defendant gain by having his case heard first? Chavot Yair responds that a defendant also benefits by avoiding the indignity and wasted time of waiting around beit din. Shevut Yaakov, responsum 144, agrees with the questioner and cites Rashi in Yevamot 100, where Rashi explains the rule that a woman's case is advanced before a man's as referring to a woman who is the claimant. That proof is weak, but there appears to be a stronger proof from Shevuot. R. Yochanan sent to Rav Nachman that Ulla was "our colleague, my equal in Torah." The Gemara explains that the practical purpose was either to give his case precedence or, alternatively, for shuda d'dayanei. Tosafot, in the name of Rabbeinu Tam, explain that the opposing litigant was also a Torah scholar, which is why the message could matter for shuda: both parties needed to stand on equal footing. The Gemara cannot be uncertain about the historical facts of the incident, whether the opposing party was a Torah scholar. Rather, it is discussing possible purposes of the message: perhaps it was to secure precedence, or if a discretionary judgment arose, to ensure Ulla received the proper status. But if the opposing litigant was himself a Torah scholar, why was a message needed to advance Ulla's case? The opposing scholar should already have generated the same priority. Necessarily the opponent was the defendant, and a defendant does not receive the same precedence merely because he is a Torah scholar. This supports the view that the special priority rules generally operate for the claimant who needs the court to act, not for the defendant. One can answer Tosafot's separate question about seeking merit for a Torah scholar by saying that, according to Rashi's understanding, the opposing party was not a Torah scholar at all. Or, if he was, seeking merit for the defendant would be balanced by the message that the claimant too was a Torah scholar, leaving them equal. Still, regarding a Torah scholar specifically, there is room to argue that even a defendant should be advanced because of bitul Torah, as Ritva emphasizes. The matter requires further study.
