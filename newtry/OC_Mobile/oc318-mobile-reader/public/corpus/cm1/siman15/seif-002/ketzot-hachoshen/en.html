They give precedence. Beit Yosef cites Rashba concerning one who hires a minor and withholds the wages: the wages can certainly be recovered through beit din. The rabbinic rule that taking a minor's property is prohibited only for the sake of peaceful relations applies to a found object, because no other person's intent transferred it to him. Where another competent person transferred property to the minor, it belongs to him fully. Knesset HaGedolah wonders why this responsum appears in this siman and suggests that Beit Yosef wished to teach a procedural consequence. If a minor's wage claim arrives first and an adult's claim arrives later, one might have thought the adult's case should take precedence because taking a minor's found object is only rabbinically prohibited and cannot be judicially recovered. Rashba teaches that wages owed to a minor are fully judicially recoverable, so the minor's case is like any other and takes precedence if filed first. Conversely, a claim involving only a minor's found object would remain lower in priority than ordinary judicially enforceable claims. Bach makes the same point. This also helps resolve a question in Shevut Yaakov regarding two parties who owe one another oaths, one a Torah oath and the other a hesset oath. Which is administered first? A Torah oath has direct monetary enforcement: if the party refuses to swear, beit din may descend to his property. With hesset, property is not seized; he is placed under ban until he swears. The Gemara compares hesset to the rabbinic protection of a minor's found object. Therefore the Torah oath should be administered first, and only afterward the hesset oath. Rabbinic oaths that themselves permit collection from property, such as the enactments for a robbed or injured claimant, are different. Since the Sages instituted them with property consequences resembling Torah law, they rank procedurally like Torah claims and retain ordinary priority according to which claim came first.
