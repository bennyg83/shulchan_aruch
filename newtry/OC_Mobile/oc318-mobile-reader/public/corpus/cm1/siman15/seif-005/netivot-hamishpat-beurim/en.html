The judge may adjudicate, etc. Rambam, cited by Tur, writes that in monetary law a judge may decide according to matters established in his own mind. He then asks why the Torah requires two witnesses, and answers that when witnesses appear before the judge he must judge according to them even if he does not personally know whether they speak truth or falsehood. At first glance this is astonishing. If the judge personally saw the event while acting as a witness, he cannot later become the judge, and there can be no stronger assessment than his own sight. It therefore appears that an assessment known only to the particular judge is not sufficient to extract money. Its function is primarily to weaken a document, reverse an oath, or similar procedural matters. The Gemara in Ketubot itself says, 'Would we tear the document? Rather, we weaken it,' showing that direct extraction is not assumed. Where an assessment is used to extract from orphans, it is because the assessment is objective and universally compelling, such as where everyone knows a particular person was not wealthy enough to possess the alleged funds. Such a universally recognized inference is not treated as personal witness testimony, just as Tosafot explain in Yevamot. But where the inference is known only to the judge, the Torah's formal rules of testimony exclude reliance on it for taking money.
