Likewise, we do not extract from heirs. This refers to the rule later in siman 296. If a person dies leaving a silver object that he was not financially likely to own, and another person claims that he deposited it with the deceased, supplies identifying marks, and was not someone who regularly frequented the deceased's home so as to have learned those marks casually, the Gemara allows return based on this strong assessment. Rambam says that nowadays, because judicial discernment has diminished, courts do not remove property from heirs on such assessment alone. Sema notes that Rif and Rosh nevertheless recorded the Talmudic rule and therefore wonders why Rama did not cite them as an alternative opinion. It seems that Rama understood their citation differently. They were not disagreeing with Rambam's report of the later communal consensus not to act this way. Rather, even according to Rambam, if a judge nevertheless relied on the original Gemara and removed the property from the heirs, his ruling is not considered an error in a Mishnah or in judicial discretion. The judgment stands and the judge is not personally liable, because he followed an authentic Talmudic rule even though later authorities agreed not to use it. This explains why Rosh and other post-Rambam authorities still recorded the rule: it retains legal consequence even today. It also explains Rambam's own ruling in the laws of inheritance that orphan money should not be lent against an object bearing distinctive identifying marks, lest the borrower die and a third party later identify and take the object. Although contemporary courts generally do not extract from heirs based on such assessment, orphan property is protected against every reasonable risk. Since a judge who did act on the assessment would have a legally effective ruling, one cannot rely on the present practice when dealing with orphan assets.
Where one person robs another and the victim cannot recover through law. This means that strict law entitles the victim to payment, but because the wrongdoer is violently powerful the court cannot enforce it, or he evades appearing before beit din. If the victim later marries, recovery may become even harder. In such a case extraordinary measures can be taken, as in Piskei Mahari. But if recovery is impossible only because beit din lacks sufficient proof and has no authority today to decide based on mere assessment, then the court may not use extraordinary pressure, such as preventing marriage, to force payment. That would itself amount to extracting money through the very assessment that the court lacks authority to use. Rosh's rule regarding penalties likewise shows that today one does not excommunicate merely to achieve what the court cannot legally award, and this stronger coercion is certainly no better.
