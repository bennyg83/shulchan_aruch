A judge before whom comes a case that he knows is fraudulent. In the Derishah I explained that Rambam makes no distinction between fraud by the claimant and fraud by the defendant, and his wording implies that both are treated alike. Maharam and the Rosh distinguish between them. The Mechaber in Beit Yosef says Rambam and the Tur agree, yet here he begins with Rambam's wording and in the following seif concludes like the Rosh and Tur, distinguishing claimant from defendant. Rama says nothing here, perhaps because he interprets Rambam similarly or because he accepts the distinction in practice.
He shall examine and investigate. Maharik writes that even witnesses signed on a document should be examined where there is reason to suspect fraud, despite the general rule that signed testimony is regarded as already examined in beit din.
The matters are entrusted to the heart. Rambam and the Tur conclude with the verse, 'for the judgment is God's.' Matters of fraud depend upon the judge's discernment and he must direct his mind carefully because judgment belongs to God. Another explanation is that because a judge might excuse himself by saying, 'I see no fraud,' the text stresses that these are matters entrusted to the heart, to which the verse 'you shall fear your God' applies.
When the Rosh would see a compelling circumstantial indication, etc. If Rambam truly disagrees with the Rosh and Tur, then this practice reflects a different procedural theory. According to Rambam, the judge already knows the matter is fraudulent and investigation is intended to defeat the fraud. If this judge cannot expose it, perhaps another judge can, and therefore Rambam's text says, 'let one whose heart is complete in the matter judge it.' According to the Rosh, investigation aims to determine whether the claimant is fraudulent. Once he becomes known as fraudulent, the judge should withdraw from the case, and the Tur therefore says, 'let the King of the world judge him.' The Mechaber's combination of Rambam's phrase with the Rosh's practice appears internally inconsistent. See the Derishah.
