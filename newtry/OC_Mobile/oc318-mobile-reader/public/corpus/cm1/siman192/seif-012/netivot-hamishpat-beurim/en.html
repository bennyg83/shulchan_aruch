Only against the amount of his money. See Sema, seif katan 24, who writes that this is so even if the seller repeatedly demanded payment. I searched the cited Rivash siman 476 and did not find it. Ran and Raavad, cited by Beit Yosef, appear to say the opposite. Ran's language, however, requires explanation. He writes that if the seller did not repeatedly demand payment, the sale remains effective against the amount already paid, while if he did, the retracting party has the lower hand. If Ran agreed with Tosafot that whenever the seller cancels the unpaid portion the buyer may cancel the entire purchase by saying, "I want a complete purchase," that should apply even without repeated demands. If instead Ran holds, as Nimukei Yosef does, that where acquisition initially took effect only against the money paid the buyer cannot demand an all-or-nothing purchase, then repeated demands should not allow him to retract from the paid portion either. It appears that Ran follows his own view that repeated demands matter only where the seller thereby indicates that he allows the buyer to retract. The buyer's right to abandon the whole therefore does not come from "I want a complete purchase," but from the seller's own retraction as to the unpaid portion together with his consent that the buyer may withdraw. Thus, according to Ran, even in ten fields acquired only proportionately, the buyer may abandon the whole after such a demand because the seller has opened the door to retraction. The same would be true in the case of "my deposit shall acquire." But according to our halachah, where a purchase that originally takes effect only against the money paid remains binding to that extent even if the seller repeatedly demands the balance, the buyer cannot retract from the acquired portion. Sema therefore correctly writes that here neither side may retract from the part already acquired, even where the seller repeatedly demands payment. This is also clear from siman 190 concerning divisible movable property.
Some disagree regarding a gift. I am surprised Rama did not cite Tur's concluding words in the name of Rosh: unless the donor said, "Take possession of one and thereby acquire all of them." That shows that with such express language even a gift acquires all the properties according to everyone. Rashba in Kiddushin goes further and writes that even in a sale where the buyer did not pay for all the fields, if the seller said, "Take possession of one and acquire all," he acquires them.
