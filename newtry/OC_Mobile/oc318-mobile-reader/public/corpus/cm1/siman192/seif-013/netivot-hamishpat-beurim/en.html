The same applies to rental. One must determine what constitutes chazakah for rental. Maharit, part 1, siman 68, proves that rental and borrowing of land also require the formal chazakah of locking, fencing, or breaching. He cites Gittin, where the woman is told to go lock and open the place; Tosafot in Bava Batra and Bava Metzia, who require a kinyan for borrowing or renting a place; Ran in Kiddushin; and Tosafot in Bava Batra 87. At first glance these proofs are difficult because those cases concern objects belonging to the transferor inside the courtyard, while the renter placed nothing of his own there and performed no use of his own. Perhaps actual use by the renter with his own property would itself acquire. Ketzot HaChoshen raises this in siman 189. Maharit seems to rely particularly on the fact that in Gittin the Gemara said to lock and open rather than simply place one of her own objects there, and on the case in Bava Batra where her basket was already resting in the place yet Tosafot still required locking or fencing. Machaneh Ephraim, however, proves that a house can be rented by bringing one's utensils into it. Since the renter acquires only the right of use, acquisition occurs through the ordinary form of that use, like a vineyard path acquired by walking. He cites Bava Kamma 48, where permission to bring an ox into a courtyard is treated as transferring a place in the courtyard sufficient to make it jointly usable, showing that use alone can establish such a right. In my view Machaneh Ephraim's practical ruling is correct, but not because it is identical to a vineyard path. Raavad holds that barren rock is not acquired merely by spreading fruit even though that is its use. A vineyard path is different because one's body itself derives the benefit of walking, as Masat Binyamin explains. The better rationale is that Maggid Mishneh, chapter 5 of Hilchot Shekhenim, explains neighbor-use rights as mere waiver of a servitude. In these forms of use there is no full acquisition of the body of the land, only a servitude of use imposed upon it. Such a right can arise through commencement of the use itself, and the formal chazakah of locking, fencing, or breaching is irrelevant because the body of the property is not being acquired. But if the renter seeks such dominion that the courtyard should function as his own courtyard to acquire found objects or purchases for him, a mere servitude of use is insufficient. He must acquire a true right in the body of the courtyard for its fruits, and for that formal chazakah is required. Thus a person who merely has a right to drain rainwater, place ladders, or similar use plainly does not thereby own a courtyard capable of acquiring objects for him. This can also reconcile the law that a rented courtyard sometimes acquires for the landlord: where the renter merely introduced animals and thereby acquired a use-servitude, the body remains the landlord's; in siman 260, where the renter performed formal chazakah, the body for its fruits may be vested in the renter. This distinction resolves the sugyot. Gittin, Bava Batra, and Bava Metzia deal with making the courtyard legally his domain for acquisition or divorce, and therefore require full chazakah. Bava Kamma 48 requires only permission of use sufficient to remove the status of the injured party's exclusive courtyard; commencement of use suffices. Daily practice likewise shows that one who rents a place to store produce and actually places produce there cannot retract, and even after removing the first produce he may place other produce there throughout the rental term. Maharit therefore seems incorrect in saying that after the first produce is removed the servitude ends. The same is obvious from rights to ladders, rainwater, windows, and other uses that continue for the agreed term. Accordingly, a vineyard path acquired by walking can be understood as a case where only a right of passage was transferred, not the body of the land for digging or planting. The same is true of a synagogue seat: the synagogue and underlying land belong to the public, while the individual receives only a servitude of sitting at that place, so sitting itself acquires. Barren rock is different where the body itself is being acquired; Raavad therefore requires a formal act. It is also clear that this type of servitude cannot make a davar shelo ba la'olam effective. The limited ability to impose a lien on future-acquired property exists in loan law because of the need to preserve credit. Even where one rents "a house" or "a donkey" without specifying an existing item and the lessor must ultimately provide one, at the time the rental obligation is created there must be a legally cognizable obligation upon his person. If there is nothing at all to which it can attach, mere use-language does not create a present property right. A practical case was asked: a buyer purchased an old house intending to demolish it and build anew. Before receiving it by kinyan sudar, with the seller's knowledge he began demolition, removed two walls of the large winter room, cleared the debris from their sites to prepare new walls, and placed supports under the ceiling and roof so they would not fall. Before demolition the house was habitable for years. The buyer now wishes to retract, while the seller argues that he acquired by chazakah through demolition, clearing the sites like removal of mounds, and installing supports. In my view this is not chazakah. According to Rosh, digging foundations does not acquire because the improvement must be completed to the point of serving the intended use. If one merely digs in order later to build, the property is not yet improved for residence. All the more so, removing soil and demolishing walls is not chazakah merely because it is preparatory to construction. Even according to the view that digging foundations can acquire because it is an act in the body of the land, the present demolition does not. A building above the land is treated as one body with the land only when it is already a completed useful structure, with doors and fit for residence or as a usable courtyard. Then even beautifying the building can acquire the land. Before completion, however, the structure is merely loose building material, as the Gemara says. If partial building were automatically one body with the soil, then carving door sockets in an unfinished palace, drilling holes to join beams, or placing foundational members necessary for a roof should acquire the land, yet the Gemara says that building a palace without doors does not acquire. The reason is that until completion the construction is not yet a completed body of land-improvement. Moreover, demolition is not improvement at the moment of the act. Before demolition the land and house may have been worth one thousand, and afterward the land with dismantled materials only eight hundred. Chazakah requires that the act itself improve the property. Removing a mound improves the ground immediately; demolishing in order to rebuild causes immediate deterioration. It is contradictory to say that the existing building counts as part of the land because it benefits the land, while at the same time destruction of that beneficial building counts as improvement of the land. Once demolition begins, the building ceases to function as the completed improvement, yet the new construction is not ready. Clearing the debris does not help either. Removing debris one created himself is at most correcting one's own damage, and the seller's silence does not show consent to acquisition because the buyer is obligated to restore what he damaged if he retracts. Nor is the installation of temporary supports a proprietary improvement; it is merely preventing the roof from falling, comparable to driving away a lion, and in any event no better than unfinished construction. A scholar attempted to challenge this conclusion from the case in Gittin where one opens and then closes a door. There is no proof. If the seller explicitly tells the buyer to open and then close the door as an act of chazakah, the buyer is not independently liable for opening it, because the seller ordered him to do so. The case in Gittin also concerns a gift, where refusal to accept causes no compensable damage. My argument concerns a buyer who, in the seller's presence, first independently damages the property and later repairs it. The later repair is ambiguous: is he acquiring, or merely repairing what he already must repair? Locking is also stronger than merely preventing a loss because it visibly excludes everyone else like an owner. Nor is there proof from the rule that spreading bedding can acquire through beautification of property. According to the view that temporary beautification does not acquire, removing temporary refuse likewise cannot acquire, and removing an obstacle is in any event weaker than positive beautification. Even according to the view that bedding can acquire, the case concerns a completed house with doors where beautification is a natural use of the existing structure. Land intended for building or plowing requires a genuine physical improvement to the land itself, not temporary cleanup. Mordechai's proof from Shabbat concerning removal of a mound likewise shows that the object removed must be integrated into the ground in a way whose removal is legally treated as building or land repair. Simply removing something lying on the surface, or chasing animals away so one can plow, is no acquisition. The ground was already physically fit; one merely removed an external obstruction. Shemittah prohibitions based on appearance are no proof for ownership acquisition, which requires actual land improvement. In truth, these secondary debates are unnecessary. The decisive point is that digging foundations does not acquire under Rosh, and removal of mounds as an acquisition concerns an actual completed house or a field where the act produces an enduring finished land-improvement. The reading that a person who constructs an enormous palace from foundation to roof acquires nothing, but one who merely removes a mound in preparation for construction acquires, is no more plausible than calling stone gold.
