Likewise regarding rental, one must examine what act constitutes chazakah. Maharit, part 1, siman 68, proves that for renting or borrowing land one likewise requires the chazakah of locking, fencing, or breaching. He brings proof from the beginning of HaZorek in Gittin, where the Gemara says that she should go, lock, and open; from Tosafos in Bava Basra 85a, s.v. 'such as where her husband sold baskets,' and in the first chapter of Bava Metzia regarding 'its place is rented to him,' where Tosafos write that an act of acquisition is required for borrowing or renting a place; from the Ran in Kiddushin concerning a rock belonging to both of them; and from Tosafos in Bava Basra 87b, s.v. 'go and improve land for yourself.' These sources imply that specifically locking, fencing, or breaching is required. At first glance these proofs are difficult, for there the objects within the courtyard belonged to the transferor, and the acquirer placed nothing of his own in the courtyard and made no personal use of it. Perhaps when the renter uses the place with his own objects, he acquires. I later found that Ketzos HaChoshen raises this in siman 189. Maharit's intent appears to be that the Gemara in Gittin says she should lock and open, rather than merely place some object of her own inside; and the Tosafos in Bava Basra concerning the woman's basket provide a clear proof, because her basket was lying in the place, yet Tosafos still require locking or fencing. I saw, however, that Machaneh Ephraim proves that in rental of houses, bringing one's utensils inside does effect acquisition. Since the renter obtains only the right to the fruits, meaning the use, the mode of acquisition follows the mode of use, just as a vineyard path intended for walking is acquired by walking. He proves this from Bava Kamma 48, where one says, 'Bring your ox in,' and the Gemara says that he thereby granted him a place in his courtyard, making it a jointly used courtyard. Thus a chazakah consisting solely of use suffices even without locking, fencing, or breaching. I do not presently have that sefer before me and do not remember what else he wrote. In my humble opinion, the law certainly follows Machaneh Ephraim that rental is acquired by use alone. Above in siman 153 it is explained that drainage of rainwater, setting ladders, or inserting beams, when done before the owner who remains silent, creates a right. Thus use alone effects acquisition. Ketzos HaChoshen in siman 189 likewise writes this. Yet the reason given by Machaneh Ephraim, that it is like a vineyard path acquired by walking, does not appear correct to me. Even bare rocky ground, although useful only for spreading fruit upon it, is not acquired by walking according to Raavad. The reason a vineyard path is acquired by walking is that one's body itself benefits from it, as Responsa Masas Binyamin, siman 31, cited by the Taz here, explains. It therefore appears to me that the true reason is different. Maggid Mishneh, chapter 5 of Hilchos Shechenim, halacha 7, explains that acquisition through silence in easement cases works because it is only a waiver of a servitude. Thus such uses do not confer full ownership in the land, but only subject the body of the land to the specified use. Therefore the waiver is merely a waiver concerning the servitude. Since there is no ownership of the substance of the land but only a servitude, acquisition is accomplished merely by beginning the use, and the ordinary chazakah of locking or fencing is irrelevant. But for the place to count as one's courtyard so that the courtyard itself acquires a found object or a purchase placed within it, a mere servitude over the body of the land does not suffice. Certainly a courtyard in which one merely has permission to drain rainwater or place ladders does not acquire objects for that user. See Shach, siman 198, seif katan 6. Therefore, if one wants the place to count as his courtyard for acquisition of its contents, he must have an actual proprietary right in the body of the land for its fruits. Since he then has a right in the substance of the land, he requires the chazakah of locking or fencing, an improvement benefiting the land itself. This may resolve the ruling in siman 313 that a rented courtyard acquires for the landlord, upon which Sema, seif katan 5, wonders. One may say that there the renter did not perform the chazakah of locking or fencing, but merely brought animals inside. The body of the courtyard therefore was not acquired to him; only a right of use was created. In siman 260, however, the case is where he performed locking or fencing and thereby acquired the body of the courtyard for its fruits. Everything is now clear: Gittin, Bava Basra, and Bava Metzia concern acquiring through the courtyard or receiving a get through it, which requires a proprietary right in the courtyard's body, and therefore locking or fencing is necessary. Bava Kamma 48, by contrast, requires only permission to use the place. Once both parties have a right of use, it is no longer considered the injured party's exclusive courtyard, and beginning the use suffices. This accords with daily practice: when one rents a place to store grain, the landlord cannot retract after the renter has brought grain inside. Even if he rented it for a year and later removes the first grain he placed there, he may bring other grain until the end of the term. I am astonished by Maharit, who writes that even if the courtyard was acquired to him for those fruits, after he removes them it is no longer subjected for other fruits. This contradicts the law that even a right to place ladders, drain water, or maintain windows continues against others for the period of the permission. It is simple. Accordingly, when a vineyard path is acquired by walking, the case is where he sold only a walking right and not the body of the land for digging or planting. Since he receives only a servitude for passage, walking acquires it. By contrast, bare rocky land, where he acquires the body of the land as well, requires a full act of acquisition according to Raavad. It is also obvious that although such use-rights are servitudes, they cannot be created in something not yet in existence. The ability to obligate oneself with respect to something not yet in existence applies specifically to a loan, because of the concern for closing the door before borrowers, and similarly where one rents an unspecified donkey or house and becomes obligated to supply one. Even there, however, at the time of rental he must possess a house or donkey; once the servitude takes effect upon the existing property, his personal obligation extends to providing another. Where he has nothing at all, the obligation does not take effect. This too demonstrates that rental involves a servitude, as I have explained at length in the laws of rental.
Question 1: One purchased an old house intending to demolish it and build a new one in its place. Before the parties performed a kinyan sudar, the buyer, with the seller's knowledge, went and began demolishing and rebuilding. After demolishing two walls of the large winter house, he cleared their places and removed the debris in order to place new walls there. He also brought supports to hold up the ceiling and roof so that they would not fall because of the demolished walls. Before the demolition the house was fit to live in for several years. The buyer now wishes to retract. The seller argues that the buyer acquired it by chazakah through demolishing the walls and clearing their places, like removing mounds as Rama writes in siman 192, seif 11, and also by placing supports to prevent the roof from falling. This is the question.
Answer: In my humble opinion, none of this constitutes chazakah. According to the Rosh, cited in siman 275, seif 21, even digging foundations does not acquire. See the Rosh in chapter Chezkas HaBatim, who explains that the improvement must be completed for the intended use of the land. Here, he digs foundations in order to build a house, and the work is still not complete for his intended purpose of dwelling there. All the more so, removing soil and demolishing walls do not constitute chazakah, even though they prepare the property for construction. According to this opinion, removing mounds acquires only in a field intended for planting, or in a house in the manner explained below. Even according to the opinion that digging foundations acquires, it appears that he did not acquire here. Maggid Mishneh explains that digging foundations acquires because he performed an act upon the body of the land. One might argue that a building attached to land is considered one body with the land, as shown by the rule that drawing a design upon a building in the property of a deceased convert acquires the land as well; accordingly, demolishing walls attached to the land should resemble removing mounds from the land itself. This is incorrect. A building is regarded as one body with the land only after the building is complete and the land benefits from and needs the structure upon it, meaning that the building has doors and is fit for dwelling, or it is a usable courtyard. Then even drawing a design upon the building acquires the land, because the land and structure are truly one body. Likewise, removing an obstructing mound from such a completed building, or from the ground, where in the eyes of beit din this improves the dwelling, may acquire, just as drawing a design does. But so long as the building has no doors, it is not regarded as part of the body of the land; it is merely loose building material, as the Gemara says there. It has not yet become one body with the land.
Proof is that if even a partial building immediately became one body with the land, then one who found a palace without doors and carved the lintel and doorposts so that doors could be installed, and certainly one who drilled the holes on which the door would turn, should acquire. Such carving in the building is comparable to digging foundations and removing mounds, and it improves the land for its intended purpose, namely a building, for without that carving the structure is not ready to be completed. Likewise, during any construction one repeatedly cuts and drills in order to join one beam to another; on Shabbos, drilling is an act of building. Mordechai indeed derives the law of removing mounds for acquisition from the laws of Shabbos. Similarly, if one fills a depression in land so it becomes suitable for planting or building, he acquires, as the Gemara says concerning leveling one elevation against another. According to the proposed logic, then, he should also acquire by laying foundation timbers and joists attached to the ground so that the structure can be completed by adding roof and doors. But this is not the law. Rather, until the building is complete it is called mere building material and is not considered the body of the land; we require an act upon the body of the land itself. It would be absurd to say that building a palace without doors does not acquire, but removing part of that incomplete palace to replace it with something better does acquire. Nor can one say that because the structure was fit for dwelling before demolition it was part of the land, and therefore demolishing it for rebuilding counts as an act on the land. Drawing a design beautifies the building; demolition, by contrast, damages the building and the land at the moment it is performed. Before demolition the land with the building may have been worth one thousand, while afterward the land with the detached materials is worth only nine hundred. That is not an improvement of the land but a deterioration. Acquisition requires that at the time of the act itself the land be improved, as is explained repeatedly in siman 275. Here the act itself diminished rather than improved the property.
For this reason it is also entirely unlike removing mounds. When one removes obstructing mounds, the land is improved at that very moment. When one demolishes in order to rebuild, at the moment of demolition the property is not improved but damaged. This is obvious to anyone who walks upright; only one who is obstinate and refuses to acknowledge the truth could dispute it.
Moreover, one cannot simultaneously say that the building is part of the body of the land because it benefits the land, and that destroying that building counts as improving the land through the very act of destruction. As soon as he begins demolishing, the building loses its status as part of the land, and the property is still not ready for the new construction until the demolition is complete. Thus, at the point when any eventual improvement occurs, he is no longer performing an act upon the body of the land.
Additionally, even according to the view that digging foundations acquires, he certainly does not acquire until he has dug all the foundations of the walls so that the land is fully prepared for the building. Only in a field intended for planting can one stroke of a hoe acquire, because every small portion is itself fit to plant saffron; having improved that part, he acquires the entire field up to its boundary. But when he has dug the foundation of only one wall, the improvement for his intended purpose, the building, has not been completed. The same is true of preparing land for plowing: one needs at least the measure of a plow's pass, for in every case completion of the improvement is required; merely beginning it does not suffice. Likewise, concerning breaching in siman 275 one must breach enough to permit easy entry; beginning the breach is not enough. Here he demolished only two walls, while he intended to demolish all the walls before erecting the new structure, since one new wall does not ordinarily stand together with an entirely old house. The land was therefore not yet fully prepared for the new building, and he did not acquire. Moreover, we should not extend the view of Ri Migash, which is contrary to Tosafos and the Rosh, beyond what is necessary, especially since the halacha generally follows Tosafos.
Clearing away the rubble that fell there likewise does not constitute chazakah. The Gemara in Bava Basra speaks of cleaning a field, and Rashbam explains this as clearing twigs and weeds. It appears that this applies specifically where the twigs and weeds are attached or embedded in the ground so that, if analogous work were done on Shabbos, one would be liable for plowing. Then he performs an act upon the body of the land and acquires through that chazakah. Proof is that Mordechai derives acquisition by removing mounds from the law of Shabbos. This shows that chazakah is effected only by an act that would constitute a melachah on Shabbos; otherwise he could simply have said that removing mounds is no worse than clearing a field. Certainly removing movable items from a field so that it can be plowed is not an act upon the body of the land. It merely removes an obstacle, like driving an animal away from the field before plowing, and is inferior even to driving away a lion, which does not acquire. On Shabbos one is liable for leveling depressions only when the material is nullified to the ground, as explained in Magen Avraham, siman 313, seif 10; this is an explicit Gemara in Shabbos. Magen Avraham in siman 244, seif katan 8, likewise explains that removing manure from the street is considered labor because the manure was left there with intent that it remain and became attached to the ground. According to what was written above, clearing building debris in order to rebuild does not acquire, just as demolishing the walls themselves does not. Moreover, the debris resulted from damage the buyer himself caused, and he is obligated to repair what he damaged. The supports he placed to prevent the roof from falling are likewise comparable to merely driving away a lion, and they are no better than an unfinished building. End of the responsum. Another scholar wished to refute these words, and my response to him clarifies the matter. He wrote to me that the Rosh in chapter Chezkas HaBatim, together with the statement in Gittin that she should open and then lock, proves that even where one first caused damage himself and afterward repaired it, the repair counts as chazakah. In my humble opinion there is no proof at all. If the seller tells the buyer to open the door and afterward lock it as an act of chazakah, and the buyer retracts before locking, he surely is not obligated to relock it, for the seller instructed him to open it. Moreover, the Gittin case concerns a gift; if the recipient retracts, she is not liable for damage simply because she no longer wishes to accept the gift. My discussion concerns one who performed chazakah before the seller, so no express 'go, take possession and acquire' is needed, but first caused damage on his own in the seller's presence. If the buyer then retracts, he is certainly obligated to repair what he damaged. Therefore, even if he later makes the repair before the seller, we cannot know whether his intent is to acquire or merely to repair what he is obligated to restore. There is a great difference between being expressly instructed to damage and independently damaging and then repairing in the seller's presence. Furthermore, locking is superior to merely driving away a lion for another reason stated by Tosafos and the Rosh: it resembles the conduct of an owner, because he prevents everyone else from entering. As to the challenge from spreading bedding, which is said to acquire because it beautifies the land even though it is not a Shabbos melachah, I see no contradiction. According to the opinion that spreading bedding acquires through beautification, every act that beautifies the property may acquire. But according to the alternative view cited by Rama in siman 275, seif 15, that spreading bedding does not acquire because it is not enduring, the same applies where a temporary object lies upon the land and mars it: removing that temporary ugliness does not acquire. What difference is there between beautifying with something non-enduring and removing a non-enduring source of ugliness? Indeed, removing the ugliness is weaker, for it is merely driving away an obstacle. Moreover, even the opinion that spreading bedding acquires necessarily speaks of a completed house with doors, like drawing a design upon the property of a deceased convert, which also concerns a completed house. In land intended for building or plowing there is no concept of decorative beautification. This is obvious even to a beginner. The dispute is only whether, in a completed house, arranging bedding in the manner of nobles and kings is sufficient beautification. One view requires an enduring beautification such as drawing a design; the Mechaber holds that bedding also suffices. But on land intended for building or plowing, acquisition is not through beautification but through actual improvement of the land, and a complete improvement is required.
As for your honor's attempt to justify Mordechai's reason, that he invokes the law of building because his case concerns a house, I do not understand it. According to your honor's approach, the acquisition is through beautifying the land, and even if he did not remove a mound attached to the land but merely removed an object resting upon it, he would acquire. Why then did Mordechai specifically explain the law by saying that the act is one for which a person is liable on Shabbos? The Gemara's expression also does not fit your honor's approach. It should have said simply 'sweeping the land,' as elsewhere one speaks of sweeping a house. The expression used is derived from making the land itself clear or pure, by removing thorns and stones rooted in it, without whose removal the ground cannot be planted.
Nor do I understand your honor's proof from shemittah, where removing stones is prohibited. How can one prove from something the Sages prohibited because of maris ayin, since it appears that one is improving the ground, to a law of acquisition where an actual improvement of the land is required? Anything not considered improvement of the land for Shabbos is likewise not considered improvement for chazakah. Mordechai therefore properly needed to prove the case of removing an attached mound from the fact that it constitutes building on Shabbos. He specifically discussed a mound connected to the ground, rather than merely removing an object lying upon it. Necessarily, removing a loose object from the land, driving animals away, or even expelling lions so that the field can be plowed and planted is not chazakah, because the land itself was already fit and only an external obstruction was removed.
In truth, all these dialectical arguments are unnecessary, because I did not rely decisively on any of the foregoing points, each of which admits of further examination. The central basis upon which I rely, without doubt, is that digging foundations does not acquire, while removing a mound concerns a completed house with doors, just like drawing a design on the property of a deceased convert. Your honor sought to prove from Rama in siman 192, who writes, 'If there was a mound and he removed it, or a pit and he filled it, this is chazakah; but if he dug a little he does not acquire except in a field intended for plowing,' that the first clause must concern land intended for building. I do not understand this at all. We have never heard of a legal category called 'a field intended for building.' There are many other examples: land intended for spreading fruit or for a permanent threshing floor is acquired by leveling its surface, as Rashbam explains in Bava Basra. The simple meaning is that even in a field intended for planting, if local practice is to leave it fallow and he removes a mound that creates a permanent improvement, he acquires. But a small excavation that creates no enduring improvement acquires only where the field is presently intended for plowing and the slight excavation begins the plowing and prepares it for planting. When the field is currently fallow, a slight excavation does not acquire. I am astonished by your honor's proposal that one who builds a great palace from the foundations to the sky does not acquire, while one who removes a mound in order to place the stones and beams acquires. Your honor distinguishes that foundation trenches are made in order to be filled, whereas after removing a mound the ground remains permanently level. I wonder, for the case surely does not concern removing a mound in the middle of an incomplete house merely to beautify the ground; that certainly does not acquire in a house without doors. It must concern removing an obstruction from the place where he intends to build, so that he can construct properly. But that place too is then 'made to be filled,' for he will place other material there, stones, bricks, and beams. Moreover, Rama writes the parallel case of a pit that he fills. If he excavated down to the foundation and filled it with stones and bricks, then he himself created the pit and filled it, just as your honor analogized to opening and locking. If leveling the ground for building itself acquired, why did the authorities state without qualification that building a palace, even after digging foundations, does not acquire, without mentioning this novel exception in siman 275? Removing a mound in a completed house needed no special statement there because it is plainly no worse than drawing a design. In general, one who says that building does not acquire but demolishing the building does acquire is, in my eyes, like one who says that a stone is gold.
