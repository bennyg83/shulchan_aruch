And if the injured party claims: You certainly know, etc. Although Rambam and the Mechaber elsewhere impose an oath even without the claimant saying, "you know," that is where the defendant may in fact know. Here, unless the claimant asserts that the tortfeasor certainly knows, it is clear that he does not know, because he was not present. If the claimant himself admits that, no oath applies.
He pays what he admitted and takes a Torah oath concerning the remainder. Rambam rules this way. At first glance this is difficult, because the claimant alleges liability through a forewarned animal while the admission concerns an innocuous animal, apparently a different kind of claim. Yet when the claim is monetary in substance, an admission concerning property of a different form may still constitute partial admission if the defendant stands by it, just as where one claims a gold dinar by value and the defendant admits another item of value. The Gemara's comparison to wheat and barley concerns the defendant's ability to say that his admission was merely a deflection because he did not admit the very thing demanded. Thus, where he maintains the admission, or witnesses establish it, the admission can create Torah oath liability. This reconciles Rambam, Raavad, Maggid Mishneh, and the rulings above in siman 88. I later saw that Lechem Mishneh questioned Raavad from the Gemara, but in my view the difficulty is resolved by this distinction between the character of the monetary claim and the defendant's ability to retract a non-identical admission.
And he takes a Torah oath, etc. The Tur, citing Raavad, explains Rambam as speaking where one animal is innocuous and one forewarned, because a claim against the forewarned animal is a claim against the tortfeasor's general assets, so the innocuous animal is included among them. But where both are innocuous, each animal is a separate source of liability and there is no partial admission. In my view, however, Raavad's distinction is better understood through the rule that one who admits a penalty is exempt. With two innocuous animals, there is no oath for the unadmitted penal liability. The Maggid Mishneh's suggestion that witnesses might nevertheless create Torah oath liability requires study, because witnesses concerning one animal do not transform the remaining penal claim into ordinary monetary liability.
A Torah oath concerning the remainder. Although the admitted animal itself remains extant, this is not treated as heilech. Heilech applies where the admitted property already belongs immediately to the claimant, such as a deposit. Here an appraisal is still required, so the admission is not equivalent to immediate delivery.
And some say, etc. Above, siman 88, seif 12, I demonstrated against this view. Where there are witnesses, he pays the lesser amount established, and seizure is effective to retain the amount claimed even where there are no witnesses, as explained there.
For there is no oath at all, etc. Meaning that there is neither payment nor Torah oath on the remaining claim. The Sema understands the Rosh this way even where one animal is innocuous and the other forewarned. The Bach distinguishes and says that with an innocuous and forewarned animal there is a Torah oath. That is not correct. According to the Rosh, once the unadmitted liability is treated like an admission to a penalty, even witnesses concerning the collision do not create Torah oath liability on the remainder. Nevertheless, it is also difficult to say that no heset oath at all is required, for the defendant should at least swear heset to deny the injured party's definite claim.
