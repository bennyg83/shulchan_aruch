The burden of proof rests upon the claimant. The Rambam concludes: If there is no clear proof which one caused the damage, but witnesses testify that one of these two caused it, the tortfeasor pays according to his own admission. The Tur quotes him and comments that this is certainly correct where the injured party has not specified with certainty which ox caused the damage. But if the injured party says with certainty, "This one damaged me," then he has thereby admitted that the other did not. If he has no witnesses, he recovers nothing. He cannot recover from the ox he identifies because the owner denies that claim, and he cannot recover from the other because he himself admitted that it did not cause the damage. This resembles one who claims wheat and receives an admission of barley, where the defendant is exempt even from the barley. The Tur understands the Rambam to allow recovery according to the tortfeasor's admission even when the injured party states his claim definitely, and he objects. Thus the Mechaber follows the Rambam, while Rama cites the Rosh and Tur as an alternative view. Rama's wording that "there is no oath at all" requires explanation. He should seemingly have written that there is no payment at all, as the Tur says. Moreover, even in a wheat and barley case, although there is no payment and no Torah oath of partial admission, the defendant still takes a hesset oath. One must understand Rama's "no oath at all" as referring to the Torah oath and payment imposed by the Rambam. According to the dissenters there is neither Torah payment on the admitted item nor a Torah oath on the remainder. Rama is not discussing the hesset oath. The Maggid Mishneh reconciles the Rambam with the Tur's objection by distinguishing a case where witnesses testify that one of the defendant's oxen caused the damage. Since the injured party unquestionably has money due from this owner, the case is not like wheat and barley where there are no witnesses at all. The admitted portion then creates a Torah oath on the remainder. The Rosh and Tur may reject that distinction because the witnesses still do not identify which ox caused which damage. The Maggid Mishneh proves that the Rambam's first clause must concern witnesses, while his following clause concerning a large and small ox without clear proof concerns no witnesses at all. Otherwise the Rambam's own rulings would conflict. The Mechaber copies both clauses in succession. Rama's later comment that the following clause reflects the dissenting view must therefore be understood as objecting to the Mechaber's unqualified phrase "if he did not bring clear proof," which could imply that there is some partial evidence, namely witnesses that one of the oxen caused the damage. Rama notes that such a reading would fit the dissenters rather than the Rambam. He then adds that others distinguish, meaning the Maggid Mishneh, who understands the Rambam's later clause as involving no witnesses at all.
And he swears a Torah oath on the remainder. The Tur also cites this from the Rambam and then quotes the Raavad. The Raavad limits the Rambam's law to a case where one ox is innocuous and one forewarned. Since the forewarned ox's liability is against the tortfeasor's general assets, the innocuous ox is included among those assets, and the owner's admission can therefore count as a partial admission to one monetary claim. But if one ox is large and one small and both are innocuous, each liability rests only upon the body of the particular ox. Each is therefore a separate claim, and there is no partial admission. According to the Rosh, says the Tur, there is no Torah oath even in the innocuous and forewarned case, because it remains like a wheat and barley claim.
