The tortfeasor is exempt, for, etc. The Rambam and the Mechaber imply that even in the clause where the forewarned animal damaged the smaller animal, if there are no witnesses to the goring the tortfeasor is entirely exempt; the Tur likewise appears to hold this. The Nimukei Yosef at the end of Perek HaManiach, however, writes that where the forewarned animal damaged the smaller one, payment is due even without witnesses, because a forewarned animal pays full damages from the owner's general assets, which is ordinary monetary liability and can be admitted by the defendant himself. He explains that this is not a case of claiming wheat and admitting barley, because the injured party claims the value of the larger animal from the owner's assets and the tortfeasor admits the value of the smaller animal from those same assets. It resembles a claim for the value of a large slave and an admission concerning the value of a small slave, as discussed in Perek HaShoel. The Ramah likewise rules this way. The Gemara in Perek HaShoel indeed appears to support that view, as discussed above in siman 95, seif 3. It is difficult to say that the Gemara there concerns only a defendant who stands by his admission and does not claim that he was jesting, for the Gemara at the end of Perek HaManiach challenged why the claimant receives what the tortfeasor admits, since the latter can retract. The same challenge should have been raised in Perek HaShoel. That passage also indicates that the admission is of the same kind as the claim. It appears to me that the Rambam and Mechaber also agree that if one claims the value of a large slave and the defendant admits the value of a small slave, the admission is binding. Their reasoning here is different. Rabbah bar Natan's rule rests upon the assumption that an unexpected admission may merely be an evasive response. In the present case the tortfeasor generally was not present and knows nothing about the damage. He could have answered that he knows nothing at all. Therefore, when he says that the forewarned animal damaged the smaller one, we may treat that statement as a mere attempt to put the claimant off, just as a defendant who was not required to address barley because only wheat was claimed may later say that his admission of barley was made in jest. Since the tortfeasor was not required to admit any damage at all and could properly have said that he knew nothing, his statement can likewise be retracted as jesting. This is also the straightforward implication of the Gemara, which raises the challenge even from the clause involving the forewarned animal.

This is the opinion of the authorities who say, etc. In truth, this is not a separate opinion. This entire seif and seif 3 above are the language of the Rambam, and the present case concerns one in which there are no witnesses at all to the goring. The Maggid Mishneh explains it this way, and the Beit Yosef cites him. The Gidulei Terumah argues at length that the Rambam cannot impose liability where witnesses exist, because then the objection of the Baal HaTerumot and the Rosh would arise: The Mishna must involve witnesses, since otherwise the defendant would be exempt as one who admits a penalty. He further argues from the Rambam in chapter 3 of Hilchot To'en that Rabbah bar Natan's rationale is that the claimant is deemed to have admitted that no barley is owed, and such an admission cannot be contradicted even by one hundred witnesses. He therefore concludes that the Rambam's earlier ruling, that the tortfeasor pays as he says, applies only where the injured party himself is uncertain; if the injured party is certain, the tortfeasor is entirely exempt even with witnesses. This is wholly incorrect. The Rambam's language plainly concerns a certain claim. Immediately afterward he writes that if the injured party says, “You know that this animal damaged in your presence,” the tortfeasor pays what he admitted and takes a Torah oath concerning the remainder. There can be no more certain claim than this. The injured party is saying that both parties were present and saw the event. Moreover, a Torah oath is never imposed upon an uncertain claim. Thus the injured party certainly claims with certainty, yet the Rambam still requires payment of the admitted amount and an oath. It is unlike wheat and barley because witnesses are present, as the Maggid Mishneh, Tur, Sema, and Bach all understood. The Gidulei Terumah's objection from the Baal HaTerumot and Rosh also falls away, for the Maggid Mishneh expressly explains that the Mishna concerns a case without witnesses and follows the opinion that half damages are monetary rather than penal. I proved this at length above in siman 88, seif 12. Nor is his argument from the Rambam's rationale decisive. Even when the claimant's admission is the basis of the rule, that admission concerns only a case in which he actually claimed wheat alone and thereby excluded barley. Here he claims the monetary value of the damage and does not exclude the lesser value proven by witnesses. Therefore the Rambam's ruling stands as explained by the Maggid Mishneh and the Tur.
If the injured party seized it, he receives payment for the smaller animal, etc. Although one who admits a penalty is exempt, the Raavad already explained in his glosses that seizure is effective in penal matters where the claimant would otherwise have been entitled to prove the claim. This is discussed above in siman 1 and in the laws governing seizure of penalties. Here, however, because the injured party cannot prove which animal caused the damage, he receives only the amount admitted by the tortfeasor.
But he does not take according to his own words. This applies where he seized in the presence of witnesses. If he seized without witnesses, he is believed concerning the amount due and may retain according to his claim. The Tur and the other authorities write this explicitly.
