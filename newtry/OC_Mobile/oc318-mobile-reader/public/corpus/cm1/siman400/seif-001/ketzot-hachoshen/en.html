A Torah oath on the remainder. The Shach is astonished by Maggid Mishneh's statement that in a case involving two innocuous oxen and witnesses, the defendant can incur the Torah oath of partial admission. Damage by an innocuous ox is a fine, and there is ordinarily no Torah oath on a fine because a person who confesses to the fine is exempt. Tumim attempts to distinguish confession to the fine itself from confession concerning the source from which an already established fine can be collected. If witnesses already proved the goring and established a fifty-unit fine, and the only question is whether the defendant has an asset from which to collect, revealing that asset is not confession to the fine. That distinction is sound in a case where the fine itself is already fully established. But it does not solve the cases of two innocuous oxen. Where witnesses saw the goring but do not know which ox caused it, and the defendant admits which ox did so, his admission creates the very basis of collection from that ox. Tosafot expressly treats this as confession to a fine. Tur likewise rules concerning a pregnant cow whose fetus may or may not have participated in the goring that the defendant is exempt even from an oath, because if his own admission is needed to establish the fetus's liability, it is confession to a fine. Rashba states similarly that if the witnesses know the owner but cannot identify the damaging ox, the owner's admission identifying it is confession to a fine. Thus the Shach's objection remains correct. Whenever the defendant's admission establishes that this particular innocuous ox is subject to the fine, it is confession to the fine itself, not merely information concerning collection of an already fixed debt.
