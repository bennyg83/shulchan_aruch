The matchmaker. The regional enactments concerning matchmaking provide that the principals are believed to identify who began or completed the match and who did not. This likely applies after the fee is deposited with beit din and upon acceptance of a ban. Although monetary testimony does not always require every stage to have been conducted by fully qualified witnesses, each principal can potentially be financially interested because each matchmaker may claim against him. Even if the fee is fixed by enactment, the case resembles two claimants each asserting that money held for one of them belongs to him. An acceptance of a ban may therefore be needed. Where the money remains in the principal's hand, additional concerns arise because one claimant may be easy to deal with and another difficult. To avoid violating the prohibition against withholding wages, the principal can satisfy his obligation by having beit din credit the money to whoever is ultimately entitled to it. If the principals paid one claimant and stated before two people that he alone began and completed the match, then even though hearsay ordinarily cannot exempt a person from a rabbinic oath, where the matter will certainly become verifiable from the principals themselves there may be no present reason for an oath, ban, or other coercive measure. Even if the statement was made before only one person, the certainty of later verification may be enough to suspend an oath. If obtaining the information requires expense, the parties can write to the competent beit din in the other place and have the matter clarified. In ordinary brokerage even one witness may be unnecessary where the facts are readily discoverable through the merchant or correspondence; judgment can be delayed until the information arrives. Regarding regional regulations fixing brokerage fees, present practice in some places is to pay according to each measure of merchandise, which can produce more than the old schedule. It appears that the amount established by current accepted practice may be judicially collectible. Anything above the accepted rate, even if evidenced by a promissory note, may be asmachta unless the instrument was structured to overcome asmachta. In brokerage, unlike the ferryman case, the Sages' disapproval of one who does not keep his word may not necessarily compel payment of an excessive promise merely because the merchant was not forced to buy. If the document contains an oath or handshake undertaking, however, even where beit din cannot collect the excess, the obligor may be instructed that he must fulfill it to discharge his obligation before Heaven. If he argues that the note created a partnership rather than a brokerage fee, that characterization is doubtful. Even where funds were mixed, if only the merchant was capable of paying the full price of the merchandise and the broker was promised a fixed amount whether the goods produced profit or loss, this looks unlike a genuine partnership. A fixed future payment may also concern something not yet in existence, allowing retraction, and the broker lacks the capital necessary to bear half the commercial risk. If the parties had actually discussed partnership but in a form dependent on future property, there may be a moral issue in retracting, but where one party credibly says that no partnership was intended he need not pay more than the customary brokerage fee merely to satisfy a heavenly obligation. The regional fee schedule may not necessarily govern a brokerage assignment involving personal danger to the broker, which could resemble medical or rescue services. Likewise, a note stating that payment is for travel and effort does not automatically defeat a specific regulation covering brokerage within a stated distance. If the parties agreed that unless the merchant pays an unusually large brokerage fee the broker will instead become a partner in the goods, perhaps the ferryman limitation should not apply. Yet allowing such devices indiscriminately could undermine the fee regulations, because every broker could threaten to spoil a transaction unless he receives an excessive amount or a partnership interest. On the other hand, the enactment may have been directed only at an ordinary broker who is not himself realistically able to buy and trade the merchandise. A genuine merchant who could buy for himself may fall outside it. A doubt concerning the scope of an enactment should ordinarily be resolved by saying that the enactment did not extend beyond its clear case. Where the transaction is an exchange of goods for goods, it is uncertain whether the brokerage fee is calculated only on the principal commodity sold or also on the merchandise received in exchange. If the buyer would have paid cash had he possessed it, and goods were accepted merely because he lacked cash, perhaps no additional brokerage is due on those goods, particularly if the broker did not expend additional effort. Conversely, the received merchandise may itself be intended for profit, repayment of capital, or transport expenses, and then brokerage may also be due on it. Commercial custom may have to distinguish the cases. One person obtained from a local nobleman a letter relating to a lease of another village belonging to that nobleman. The prospective tenant's name was not written in the letter. He handed the letter to another person and promised him a sum many times greater than the fixed brokerage fee. That person then brought him before the nobleman, who expressly granted him the lease. It appears that only the ordinary brokerage fee is due. If, however, the nobleman had first written the lease in the broker's own name and the broker faced real risk of loss if no substitute tenant appeared, it requires study whether the service thereby became something more than ordinary brokerage.
