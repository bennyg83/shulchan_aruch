The householder then claimed that he sent the money through the same broker, while the broker denied receiving anything. The law in siman 188 concerning a minor provides a possible analogy. There, although the owner entrusted the minor with delivery, he is still liable when the shopkeeper returns goods through the minor, because the owner did not necessarily authorize the shopkeeper to use the minor for the return trip or to entrust him with other property. One could argue similarly here: even if the householder trusted the broker to carry the merchandise to him, that does not prove he trusted him to carry back payment, especially cash. On the other hand, the case of the minor may be exceptional precisely because a minor is not ordinarily a responsible custodian. Were the messenger an adult, entrusting him with outward delivery could imply sufficient trust for the return leg of the same transaction. Still, one cannot automatically infer that because a person trusted another with a given amount or type of property he also trusted him with an additional amount of money. It is possible that the shopkeeper had already entrusted goods of a certain value to the broker and was therefore willing to rely on him to that extent, but would not have expected the householder to place fresh cash in his hands without explicit authorization. There is also room to distinguish cash from merchandise. Another comparison is the accepted rule that a custodian who returns a deposit to the depositor's wife may be discharged, whereas a debtor who pays a loan to the creditor's wife may remain liable. Merchandise delivered by a broker resembles property placed for safekeeping or delivery, while payment of its price resembles satisfaction of a debt. Even though ordinary store credit does not become a formal loan until it is converted into one, for this purpose the distinction may still matter, particularly according to the reasons offered by later authorities for distinguishing debt from deposit. This requires careful study. There is an additional consideration from the law of a borrower who returns property to the lender's wife. Because all benefit is the borrower's, his duty of return can be stricter. Here, because merchandise is supplied for consumption or resale and equivalent money is later paid, the transaction may be even more debt-like. Yet the seller too benefits from the sale, so it is not literally a case where all benefit lies with one side. If the broker later became known as unreliable, a public deterioration in his trustworthiness could change the result. In any event, even if the householder was entitled to return money through him, or even if the physical merchandise was later returned through him, liability may depend on whether the shopkeeper knew of any newly arisen concern. The householder may also argue that he never performed meshichah with intent to buy, but merely allowed the goods to remain at his house while deciding whether to purchase. His statement that he would pay may likewise have meant only that the goods were present and under consideration, not that he consented to acquisition by his courtyard. This claim is even stronger if the goods were not yet at his home when he spoke but remained with the broker. He may say that although he then intended to buy, he retracted before the goods entered his courtyard. Even if the broker disputes this, the broker may have an indirect financial interest because his brokerage fee depends on whether the sale is treated as complete. The matter therefore requires careful adjudication.
