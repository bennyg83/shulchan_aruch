Seif 6: Every broker, etc.; and some say this applies only where he did not tell him, “Sell,” etc. Note: The same rule applies whenever the identity of the purchaser is a matter on which sellers ordinarily insist. For example, one selling half or all of a house while retaining neighboring property may care deeply who will become his neighbor. The same may apply to seats in a synagogue. If an agent sells to someone objectionable and the seller then says, “I do not wish to sell to this person,” his claim is comparable to a seller who gave no fixed price and later says that he was unwilling to sell for the price obtained. But if he said expressly, “Sell to whomever you find,” that is like saying, “Sell for whatever price you find,” and he cannot later retract. I so ruled in practice. As to a broker who was told to sell for one hundred and sold for less, I have discussed elsewhere the Netivot HaMishpat's questions, including the measure of liability if the item was not actually worth one hundred and the item was then lost through negligence or theft. I also reject Netivot HaMishpat's assertion that we rule like Rabbi Meir that one who deviates from the owner's instructions is treated as a robber; the Rosh and Maggid Mishneh, as reflected in the Sema and Shulchan Aruch, indicate otherwise. Seif 6, gloss: But if he told him, “Sell for whatever you can obtain.” Rashi in Bava Batra likewise indicates that once the owner says, “Sell for whatever you can,” he cannot later say that he never agreed to that price, even if the broker sold at the ordinary profit margin or more. This supports the view of the Raavad and Rama. I was also asked about Reuven, who told Shimon that Levi had merchandise and asked Shimon to broker a purchase. Shimon negotiated but failed to bring the parties to agreement. Reuven then sent a second broker, who succeeded in obtaining the goods at the very price the first broker had tried to achieve. The first broker demanded a fee. The Rama's source in the Rosh shows that the first broker receives the full brokerage only where the principal deliberately used a pretext to dismiss him and then completed essentially the same transaction. If there was no trick, but the second broker succeeded through additional persuasion, changed circumstances, or the seller's reconsideration, the first broker is not entitled to the full fee. However, neither is he limited merely to a grievance. If his efforts materially brought the parties closer and reduced the labor required of the final broker, beit din should assess the value of that contribution and award him an appropriate portion. The principal share belongs to the broker who actually completed the transaction, absent proof of evasive conduct designed to deprive the first broker of his fee.
