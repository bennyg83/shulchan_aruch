And afterward he sold it to Levi through another person. Responsa Noda BiYehuda, second edition, Choshen Mishpat 36, notes that the Responsa Rosh, which is the source of this rule, does not say that the property was afterward sold to Levi through another broker. It states only that he later sold it to Levi himself. The Rama added the words "through another." This addition requires analysis. If the seller later sold directly to Levi without any broker, it is clear that he deceitfully pushed away the first broker. But if the later sale was made through a second broker, it may be that the new broker offered sound reasons that persuaded the seller not to withhold the sale because of his dislike of Levi. Accordingly, even when the Rama says that where he sold through another he must pay Shimon his brokerage, the meaning is that he must pay the first broker the share due him. The rule teaches that the first broker did not lose all rights merely because his initial words were not accepted. The second broker, however, is also entitled to a share in the brokerage fee. If the first broker argues that the seller was already willing to sell at the earlier stage and merely dismissed him by falsely claiming that he did not want to sell to Levi, and then forcibly inserted another broker in order to deprive him, that argument is not sufficient even to require the seller to swear, because the first broker is not asserting the matter with certainty. Even if the seller did act deceitfully, Noda BiYehuda remains doubtful whether that deceit creates liability for the loss caused to the first broker, and inclines to exemption. He further distinguishes matchmaking from ordinary brokerage. In a commercial sale, there are ordinarily no substantive intermediate terms once the parties agree on price. If the broker brought them to agreement on price, the transaction is essentially complete except for the formal acquisition. Therefore, if the seller then dismissed the broker and inserted another, there is at least room to consider whether the first broker's loss must be paid. A match, however, contains many terms beyond the dowry, such as support, residence, clothing, gifts, and other details. A small unresolved matter may still dissolve the match. Therefore, so long as the first matchmaker has not brought both sides into agreement on all material details, large and small, the match is not considered completed through him. If one side then brings in a friend or relative to finish the matter, there is no deceit, even if the first matchmaker might also have been capable of settling the remaining points. Such occurrences are common. The first matchmaker has only a grievance and no claim to the finisher's portion. This does not mean that he loses the compensation due a person who began the match under the local enactment. Ateret Tzvi records from the communal register that the initiator and the finisher divide equally, and that one is considered the initiator only if he actually discusses the amount to be given. An intermediate person receives nothing. He notes, however, that the practice he observed was to divide into thirds: one third to the initiator, one third to the intermediary, and one third to the finisher. The parties deposit the matchmaking fee with beit din and are believed, under communal ban, to identify who served in each role. If one of them is a relative or otherwise disqualified, the other side makes the statement. If both sides are disqualified, their statements require an oath. If the original matchmakers are unrelated and the parties later bring their own relatives to finish the match, those relatives receive no matchmaking fee. The implication is that their share is then added to the initiators' portion. This differs from Noda BiYehuda only because Noda BiYehuda stated the strict law, while the communal register records an enactment. Chukei Derech likewise indicates that relatives disqualified as witnesses receive no fee unless beit din can clearly see that they performed a real service. Responsa Shev Yaakov 13 discusses a dispute between an initiator and finishers in a match and writes that the matter depends on local custom. In Prague, his birthplace, the custom was to give the initiator one third, and he followed that practice elsewhere. If the initiator worked only with one side, he receives half of that one third, namely one sixth. Although Responsa Rosh, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 185 concerning a broker, implies that absent deceit an initiator who failed to complete the matter receives nothing, Shev Yaakov distinguishes two situations. If the first person actually originated the proposed match and through him the possibility became known, after which other matchmakers entered, or the willing side appointed others whose words carried greater influence with the other family, then he has the legal status of initiator. The rule is that if one can fairly say that without his initiative the match would not have occurred to others, he is entitled to the initiator's customary fee. By contrast, if the finishers independently conceived the same match and reasonably believed themselves to be first, while the original initiator had done nothing that assisted them because the parties were not willing to proceed through him and his effort would otherwise have disappeared, he is entitled to nothing. Shev Yaakov derives this distinction from the teaching concerning Moshe's taking Yosef's bones from Egypt, though the burial was later attributed to Israel because others completed it. Where the completion would have occurred regardless of the initiator, the act is attributed to those who finished it. By contrast, the building of the Beit HaMikdash is attributed to David even though Shlomo built it, because David originated the undertaking, and without his initiative another king might never have done so. The same distinction governs matchmaking. See there, and see Pitchei Teshuva to Even HaEzer 50, seif katan 16, for additional rules regarding matchmaking.
