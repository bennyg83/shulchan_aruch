The broker takes an oath. See Shach, seif katan 6, through the words that the seller knows of the sale only from the broker. At first glance his words are difficult, because the Mishnah in Shevuot explicitly rules, regarding one who says, "I lent you a sela on a pledge, and it was worth two," that when the other admits part he takes a d'oraisa oath. This is so even though the lender knows that the pledge was lost only from the borrower's statement. Moreover, while the pledge still exists, a demand to return the item itself is treated as a claim concerning something measurable. Nevertheless, the partial admission creates a d'oraisa oath. According to what I wrote above, however, the matter is clear. There the claimant states with certainty how much the pledge was worth. For example, he says it was worth one hundred. The other answers that it was lost through negligence and was worth only fifty. The claimant's initial demand was certain: either return the object worth one hundred if it still exists, or pay one hundred if it was lost. He therefore asserted a definite, quantified demand of one hundred. When the other admits fifty, this is a partial admission to a measurable claim and creates a d'oraisa oath. It is not comparable to wheat and barley, because the claimant demanded either an object worth one hundred or one hundred itself. Here, by contrast, the principal does not demand one hundred because of the object's intrinsic value. He demands that amount only because he says, "I instructed you to sell it for one hundred." If the item were still present, his claim would not be a quantified demand of one hundred, because he does not allege that the item itself is worth one hundred. It may be worth only fifty. His monetary claim of one hundred arises only because the agent says that he sold the item for fifty, and at that point the principal says that the agent had been instructed to sell for one hundred. Thus, at the time of the original demand, the principal did not have a definite quantified monetary claim. It resembles an admission preceding a demand, and does not generate the ordinary d'oraisa oath of partial admission. Shach concludes that if the principal says, "I told you to sell it for one hundred, and you sold it for fifty," Raavad is correct. This too appears surprising. Since the sale can no longer be undone where the agency was undisclosed and the sale therefore stands, with the agent merely obligated to repair the deviation, the principal is effectively saying, "You became obligated to me for one hundred," while the agent admits fifty. Why should this not require a d'oraisa oath? It appears that Raavad follows his own view in siman 88. Where one claims one hundred as a loan and another one hundred as a deposit, and the defendant admits one of them, Raavad holds that there is no d'oraisa oath even though both were included in one claim. So too here. The fifty that the agent received for the object is deposit money belonging to the principal. The additional fifty that the agent owes because he deviated from instructions is a separate personal monetary obligation, like a loan. He admits only the fifty of deposit money, and therefore, according to Raavad, there is no d'oraisa oath. According to our ruling, however, where one claims both a loan and a deposit and the defendant admits one, a d'oraisa oath is required. Therefore here too, in such a case, the agent must take a d'oraisa oath.
And the buyer acquires. At first glance this is difficult. Rambam rules that where the agent disclosed that he was acting as an agent, the principal is believed to say that the agent violated his instructions. Even if the agent contradicts him, the principal can remove the sale from the buyer. If so, once it is established that the object belongs to the principal, and the principal is believed that the agent exceeded his authority, how can the sale stand merely because the agency was not disclosed? A person cannot sell what is not his. The agent's oath may protect him personally from payment, but one person's oath cannot function as testimony against another person so as to validate a sale of property that did not belong to the agent. The same difficulty exists in the rule of siman 182, seif 2, that when the agency was not disclosed, the sale stands. If the agent can repair the deviation, one can understand that because beit din can compel him to make the principal whole, the sale remains. But where the agent cannot repair the deviation, how can the sale stand? The principal said that he was unwilling to sell for less than one hundred, and the agent sold for fifty. Until the agent pays what the principal stipulated, he had no authority to make the sale. It is like selling another person's property. It therefore appears that the explanation is based on the following principle. The principal delivered the object into the agent's hands and thereby gave him practical control over it. The agent could, if he wished, hide the object and deny possession altogether. We therefore apply the reasoning of "he trusted him," similar to a third-party holder in Gittin 64. By entrusting the object to the agent, the principal effectively accepted that, as against outside buyers, the agent's acts with the object would stand, even if the agent later misrepresented the principal's instructions. The principal relied on the agent personally to correct any deviation. Accordingly, where the agent does not disclose that he is an agent, the buyer deals with him as though the object were truly his. The principal is treated as having empowered the agent's outward disposition of the object, leaving any later dispute about deviation between principal and agent. The sale therefore stands against the buyer. But where the agent discloses the agency, he is expressly notifying the buyer that he is unwilling to become the person who personally guarantees the validity of the transaction against the principal's objections. It is as though the agent says: "I am selling only subject to the principal's authority. Any objection by the principal will fall upon you, not upon me." In that case the buyer takes subject to the principal's true instructions, and if the principal proves or is believed that the agent exceeded them, the sale is void.
And if the buyer knew. See Sema, seif katan 9, who writes that in such a case the object is certainly the seller's. His meaning is as follows. If Shimon sells Levi an object known to belong originally to Reuven, and Shimon says that he had purchased it from Reuven, Levi's possession can nevertheless help even if Levi himself does not know the facts and can assert only an uncertain claim. Shimon had a possession-based chazakah supporting his purchase claim, namely the general chazakah that whatever is in a person's possession is his. Levi's present possession can therefore continue that chain of title and weaken Reuven's prior ownership chazakah. If the item is one ordinarily lent or rented out, whether Levi can stand in the place of witnesses who saw it depends on the dispute of the poskim explained in siman 72, seif 18, Shach seif katan 57. Here, however, the agent himself expressly disclosed at the time of sale that the object was not his. Although according to the agent's statement he had authority to sell, the usual chazakah that whatever is in a person's possession is his does not apply to that claim. Levi's uncertain possession therefore cannot defeat Reuven's prior ownership chazakah. I am nevertheless troubled. Why should this agent not have the law of a third-party holder? If one holds an object and says, "So and so instructed me to give this to you as a gift," the holder is believed, even when the recipient has no independent knowledge. According to many poskim, he is believed even where the original owner contradicts him and says the object was deposited with him. The reason, as explained in siman 56, seif 1, is that once the owner entrusted him with the property, he trusted him concerning what he would later say. Even those who disagree where the owner denies the very terms of the deposit agree where the owner admits the essential third-party arrangement. Here the principal admits that he gave the object to the agent to sell. That should be an admission concerning the third-party arrangement itself, and the agent should be believed as to the authority he says he received. Nor can one answer that the agent is financially interested because he receives brokerage compensation only if the sale stands. Shach in siman 56, seif katan 8, writes that a dispute over the amount of money does not necessarily make the third-party holder interested in the third-party issue itself. All the more so here, where the agent could have stolen the entire object and claimed purchase. His possible interest in a small brokerage fee should not disqualify him. Kiddushin 43 similarly says that an agent can become a witness because he could have claimed that he returned the money. The same reasoning appears in ordinary cases where an agent lends a non Jew's pledge to a Jew at interest. If principal and agent later disagree over the rate of interest or even over part of the principal, it cannot be that the principal is automatically believed against the Jewish lender merely because the agent received some benefit. Even if the lender must receive back his principal by the marketplace enactment, one would not say that he need not receive the agreed interest merely because the principal contradicts the agent. Likewise, where the agent gave the pledge on a deduction arrangement, it would be unreasonable to require the lender to return all fruits whenever the principal later disputes the agent's account. It therefore appears that the present case concerns one in which the agent actually was overreached, as implied above in siman 182, Shach seif katan 4. Since we objectively see that the transaction involved ona'ah and would ordinarily be void, the agent now seeks to testify that the principal had specifically instructed him to sell at fifty, so that no ona'ah claim exists. In that setting the agent is not believed. A third-party holder is believed when the act he performed at the time of delivery itself reflects and supports what he says the original entrustment authorized. Rashba in Gittin 64 explains that even after the object has left his hands he remains believed because the original principal trusted him regarding the act he was then performing. Here, however, the agent did not say at the time of sale, "The principal instructed me to sell for fifty." If the agent were absent, we would void the sale because of the evident ona'ah. With a broker, we do not presume that the principal authorized him both to improve and to impair, as Sema writes in seif katan 12. Only after the object has already left the agent's hands does he come and attempt to testify for the buyer that the principal instructed a fifty-price sale. At that point he is no longer believed to create a new factual basis validating the transaction. Raavad disagrees because he holds that with a broker we do presume, absent specification, that the principal delivered the object to him with authority both to improve and to impair. According to him, even if the agent were not present now, the sale would already stand. Therefore the agent is not being believed to create the sale, whether he now speaks to sustain it or undo it. But if there was no ona'ah and the principal merely alleges that the agent violated his instructions, even Rambam should agree that the principal is not believed without proof.
