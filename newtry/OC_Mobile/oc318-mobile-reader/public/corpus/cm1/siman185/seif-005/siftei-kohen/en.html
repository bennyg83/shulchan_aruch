The broker takes a Torah oath. The Raavad objects, writing that there is no Torah oath here, since the principal claims only the value of his object and knows that it was sold solely from the broker's statement; he does not claim a specified measure or any other definite item. The Beit Yosef writes that the Rambam's reasoning appears to be that, since the principal does not deny the broker's assertion that it was sold, but only says that he instructed him to sell it for one hundred, the broker is at least a partial admitter. His words are not conclusive, for the principal's essential claim nevertheless concerns his object. It appears to me that the Rambam spoke only where Reuven says: I told you to sell it for one hundred, and you indeed sold it for one hundred, so you now hold one hundred of mine; while the broker says: You told me fifty, and I sold it for fifty. In that case the broker takes a Torah oath. But if Reuven says: I told you to sell it for one hundred, and you sold it for fifty, his essential claim concerns the object itself, and in that case the Raavad's objection is certainly correct.
And if the purchaser knew. The Beit Yosef asks why the purchaser must know that this object belongs to Reuven; it should suffice that he knows it does not belong to the broker. One may answer that otherwise he need not return it, because he may say that perhaps the owner is not before him so that he may hold the broker accountable. Yet according to this approach that answer was unnecessary, for if he did not know it belonged to Reuven he could say to him: You are not my litigant, and he would not return the object at all. A difficulty remains: why must he have known at the time of sale that the object belonged to Reuven? It should suffice that at the time of sale he knew it did not belong to the broker and afterward learned that it belonged to Reuven. The wording, 'if the purchaser knew,' implies that he knew at the time of sale. It therefore appears to me that the Rambam requires specifically that, at the time of sale, the purchaser know that the object belongs to Reuven. This is also implied by the Rambam in chapter 2 of Hilchot Sheluchin, cited by the Tur in siman 182, seif 6, and by the Shulchan Aruch there, seif 2: specifically when he informed him that it belonged to a certain person; but if he did not inform him that he was the agent of that person, etc. The reason is that when the purchaser knows the principal, he must return the object, since he should have been concerned that the principal would object. But if he does not know the principal, even though he knows the seller is an agent, he may say: I assumed that because you did not identify the principal, there was certainly no objection, and that the principal would approve your act. This is likewise implied by the Rosh in Perek Get Pashut, by the Ran in Perek HaIsh Mekadesh, by the Mordechai there, and by Responsa Rashba, siman 182. Examine this carefully.
He must return the object, etc. The ruling in siman 182, seif 4, that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant presents no difficulty. Here the object is clearly identifiable and belongs to the principal, and the purchaser knew this; he must therefore return it. Above, however, the principal was the purchaser and sought to extract money from the seller, so we apply the rule that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. This is unlike the strained explanation of the Sema in seif katan 12. See also siman 182, seif 4, where my father-in-law resolves it differently. From seif katan 6 until here I found this written among the author's papers from his youth. See the Bach, who disputes the matter on another basis and therefore rules like the Rambam. His conclusion does not appear correct to me, for his words are strained. See Responsa Maharshadam, simanim 77, 110, and 204, and Responsa Mahari ben Lev, book 1, rule 13, siman 79, and book 2, siman 70.
