The broker swears. The Raavad disagrees and holds that he takes only a hesset oath. See the Beurim. The Raavad disagrees only where the owner does not state the object's value in his claim, but claims only by virtue of having instructed the broker to sell it for one hundred, while knowing that it was sold only from the broker's report. Such a claim is not one concerning a definite measure, so no Torah oath is required. The Rambam agrees in that case. The stringent oath applies where the owner says, "You said you would sell it for one hundred, and you say that is what you sold it for." See the Beurim. Even if the broker says, "You yourself sold it for fifty," and the owner does not independently claim one hundred except by virtue of his instruction to sell for one hundred, the broker is nevertheless liable to a Torah oath:
And if the purchaser knew. The Shach writes that the purchaser must have been told the principal's identity at the time of sale. Likewise, above in siman 182, it is necessary that the agent told him at the time of sale that he was specifically the agent of so and so. If he merely said that he was an agent without identifying the principal, it is treated as though he gave no notice. The reason is that once the purchaser knows the principal's identity he must consider that perhaps the principal will object. But if he does not know the principal, he can say, "I assumed that since you did not identify him, there was no special objection and the principal would uphold what you did." Shach:
He returns the object to its owner. Since the purchaser's claim is uncertain and the principal's ownership is certain, doubt cannot remove certainty. The agent is not believed to testify for the purchaser because he is financially interested. Sema. See the Beurim. This refers specifically to a case where the agent was subject to ona'ah. The Rambam and Raavad disagree in such a case. The Rambam holds that with a paid broker we do not presume that the principal authorized him equally to improve or impair the transaction. The sale is therefore presently void, so the agent is not believed to sustain it. The Raavad holds that even a broker is ordinarily presumed to have been appointed both for benefit and for loss, so the sale is presently valid. The agent is therefore not believed even when testifying for the principal, because one witness cannot extract money. But if there was no ona'ah to the agent and the principal contradicts him by saying that he had instructed him to sell above market value, the agent is believed like a third party entrusted with property, as in seif 56, because the principal relied on him. Even after the entrusted matter has left his hands, he remains believed concerning what he previously did:
Even if the broker admits it. The reason is that ordinarily we presume he was appointed both to improve and to impair, as in siman 182, seif 4, so the agent is not believed against the purchaser, who can say, "You are colluding against me." The Rambam holds that because a broker receives compensation, we do not presume that he was appointed equally to improve and impair. Sema. The Shach distinguishes that in siman 182 the principal is the purchaser and seeks to extract money, so the burden of proof rests on him. Here the principal is the seller and seeks to recover the object. Since it is established that the object belonged to the principal, we leave it in the presumption of its original owner.
