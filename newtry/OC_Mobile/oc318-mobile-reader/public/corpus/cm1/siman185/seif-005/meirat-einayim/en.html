And if he already gave him the fifty. Meaning, he is then denying the entire claim.
Or if he said to him, "Here it is." The law of "here it is" was explained in the Tur and in the words of the Mechaber above, at the beginning of siman 75 and the beginning of siman 87; see there.
The purchaser acquires. This also refers back to the first clause, where he took a Torah oath.
He must return the object to its owner. Even though the agent contradicts the seller and says that the seller told him to sell it for fifty, the agent does not have the status of a single witness, because he is an interested party. Thus, the seller's ownership is certain, while the claim that he instructed the agent to give it for fifty is doubtful, and a doubt cannot remove property from one who holds it with certainty. See what I write immediately below, and see the Perishah.
Or he consented to fifty and then retracted. Meaning, he did not expressly tell the agent to sell it for fifty, but he consented to fifty when the agent came and told him that he had sold it for fifty.
And he retracted after I purchased it. This too refers to the first clause, where he gave him permission to sell it for fifty, and now retracts because he sees that I purchased it. See the Perishah, where I wrote another explanation.
Even if the broker admits it. Raavad concludes his explanation by saying that the purchaser may say, "You are colluding against me." In the Perishah I wrote another possible reason: Raavad may hold like the Ramah cited by the Tur and the Mechaber in siman 182, seif 4, that the purchaser can say, "Bring me proof that you appointed him as an agent only to improve and not also if he causes detriment," and the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. Even though the agent admits that the owner told him to sell it for one hundred, the agent is not believed against me. Rambam, who holds that it must be returned, may hold that this rule was stated only regarding an unpaid agent, since ordinarily a person would not involve himself in agency unless the principal authorized him whether the result would be beneficial or detrimental. Otherwise, why would he expose himself to liability to the principal if he errs? A broker, by contrast, acts because of the brokerage fee. Alternatively, that rule applies only where the principal admits that he instructed him to sell without fixing a price. Here, however, the principal expressly says, "I stipulated with him that he should not give it for less than one hundred."
