He is an agent. One says to his fellow, 'Buy specified merchandise for me and I will pay you for your efforts.' Even without a formal kinyan, the other becomes his agent, and once the agent purchases, the merchandise belongs to the principal. The promised compensation for the agent's effort is due once the effort is performed, provided the amount is not grossly beyond the ordinary value of such services. If the person who actually makes the purchase is not legally capable of agency, there may nevertheless be liability under the law of guaranty where, at the principal's request, he pays more than the ordinary market value. If the merchandise was acquired by issuing a promissory note, guarantor liability may still arise, because a genuine enforceable obligation was undertaken even if the note later has technical defects, such as difficulty authenticating the signature. So long as the purchaser in truth became obligated through the note, it may be treated as though he paid money. If one obligates himself to another not to raise the price of an item that the other intends to buy, this may not be ordinary employment, because we do not ordinarily find a hiring relationship for pure inaction or silence. If the seller is actively pressing him and he must invent an excuse in order not to buy or bid, that is affirmative service and can be hired. Even then, the arrangement may resemble brokerage, where only the customary value of the service is due rather than every exaggerated amount promised, unless there is a settled practice to pay more. Yet even where such a practice exists, the principal purpose of the payment may merely be to remove a threatened obstacle rather than to compensate for the excuse itself, so the customary brokerage measure may still control. If money was paid for this undertaking and the anticipated sale later collapsed because the seller refused to sell at all, the result may depend on the wording of the parties' condition, whether the payment was knowingly made for the chance of success or only upon actual acquisition. If the wording is inconclusive, general commercial custom and the parties' own established usage may determine the matter. Brokerage and matchmaking practices, including local customs under which some compensation is paid even when the transaction later fails, may be relevant. If, however, at the moment the money was paid the seller had already withdrawn from the sale and neither party knew it, the payment was made in error and should be returned. Even though both sides might theoretically have been expected to investigate and stipulate, the resulting loss may fall upon the hired person, even if he currently holds the money.
