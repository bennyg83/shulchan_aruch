The broker is an agent. See Shach, seif katan 1, who says that regarding money the question whether the agency "returns" is not materially relevant. His words are difficult. Even with loans there are formulations that do not amount to zekhiyah, as explained in siman 128. There is also a practical difference where the principal expressly says he does not want the agent to acquire for the recipient. In a gift too, although siman 125 says that even where the recipient previously appointed the person as his receiving agent, if the giver says "take it along" the giver may still retract because the wording cancels the recipient's agency, there remains a difference where the giver first appointed the person as his delivery agent and the recipient later appoints that same person as receiving agent. Whether that works depends precisely on whether the first agency must return to the original principal. Gittin has the same question. One might answer that monetary law differs because appointment by the recipient does not ordinarily create a receiving agent capable of acquiring against the owner's wishes. In monetary matters acquisition generally requires the agency or consent of the property owner, as Ran explains from the Yerushalmi and as I cited in simanim 148 and 195. If so, making the giver's delivery agent into the recipient's receiving agent adds nothing, even if no return of agency is required. In my view, however, the matter is fundamentally unlike gittin. In a get, actual delivery from the husband's legal hand to the wife's legal hand is indispensable. The same individual cannot simultaneously serve as both the husband's and the wife's hand, just as the wife cannot make the husband himself her receiving agent. Therefore, when the husband's agent becomes the wife's receiving agent, one must determine whether his first agency has already returned to the husband. In ordinary kinyan, by contrast, once the seller's agent tells the buyer, "Go, perform meshichah and acquire," or in land, "Go, make chazakah and acquire," the seller's agency has completely ended. No further act by the agent is needed. The buyer may perform the acquisition even outside the agent's presence. The buyer can therefore appoint that same person as his own agent to perform meshichah or chazakah. The first agency has already ended before the second begins. Even an agent who purchases for himself can acquire according to some Geonim, as Shach cites in seif katan 3. Those who disagree do so because the legal concept of an agent ordinarily means acting for another, especially in negotiating the price, not because the same physical person can never switch roles. A proof can also be brought from Gittin 11 concerning one who seizes for a creditor. Ran explains that if seizure for a creditor works, the delivery agent can expressly make himself the slave's receiving agent after receiving the writ of emancipation from the master. At first glance this is difficult because if the slave had originally appointed him receiving agent and the master then said "take it along," the receiving agency would be canceled if "take it along" is not equivalent to acquisition. Necessarily, the distinction is that if the master first appoints him delivery agent and only afterward the slave makes him receiving agent, the second appointment can work if the law does not require the first agency to return. For a slave there is even stronger reason to permit it, because acquisition of freedom resembles acquisition of property more than divorce in certain respects. Rambam writes explicitly that the analogy of slave to woman concerns requirements such as writing for his sake and a detached document, whereas other aspects of the deed resemble a gift. A slave can even acquire freedom through chalipin. See Gittin 39. Thus the difficulty of "take your writ from the ground," which exists in divorce, does not control the same way. Accordingly, in monetary transactions, once the seller's agency has ended by the operative instruction to the buyer, there is no reason the same person cannot immediately become the buyer's agent for the act of acquisition.
He pays the fifty. This is difficult. It should seemingly say that he pays only the actual value, since even a complete damager pays only the value of the property. It is strained to say the case necessarily concerns an item worth one hundred where the transaction cannot be unwound because the error does not permit rescission. It therefore appears that the reason is that an agent who deviates from the owner's instructions is treated like a robber. We therefore presume the agent intended from the beginning to correct his deviation so that he would not become a damager or robber. An ordinary robber or damager, who plainly intended the wrongful act, pays only the value at the time of damage or robbery. But an agent who was originally acting for the owner is presumed to intend to make the principal whole to the full extent necessary to preserve the principal's transaction. The same is evident later in seif 5. It is also apparent above in siman 182, seif 6, where Sema writes that if the agent failed to disclose his agency, the purchase nevertheless remains for the principal while the agent must correct the deviation. This implies that even where the agent obtained a discount because he bought without warranty, and later must personally assume warranty, the principal need not return the amount of the discount to him. The reason is that once the agent used the principal's money in a manner that could otherwise make him a robber, we presume he intended to undertake whatever repair was necessary to cure the deviation.
