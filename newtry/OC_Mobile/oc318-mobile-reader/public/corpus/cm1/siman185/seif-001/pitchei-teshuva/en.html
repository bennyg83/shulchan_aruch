In the Shach itself, before stating that practical difference, he writes that although in monetary law there is ordinarily no difference regarding the phrase "take this to so-and-so," because with a loan or deposit such delivery is equivalent to acquisition for the recipient even without acceptance by the debtor or his agent, while with a gift it is not equivalent to acquisition even if the recipient appointed the carrier as his agent, as explained in siman 125, there is nevertheless a difference in the case of one who gives an article to another to sell. Dagul MeRevavah writes that these words require study. Siman 125 concerns a case where the recipient first appointed an agent to receive, and afterward the giver told him, "take this to him." By that instruction the giver displaced the agency of receipt and made him an agent only for delivery. The same rule applies to a get, as explained in Even HaEzer 140:6. But if the giver first said, "take this object to so-and-so," and only afterward the recipient said, "be my agent to acquire it for me," then just as the law is doubtful in a get, it is likewise doubtful in a gift. The Shach therefore requires study. Netivot HaMishpat raises the same difficulty. It also questions the Shach's opening statement that with a loan or deposit "take it" is equivalent to acquisition, because although with a loan the phrase may operate as acquisition, there are formulations that do not, and there is also a practical difference where the giver explicitly says that he does not want the agent to acquire for the recipient. Netivot then offers a partial defense of the Shach's wording, but disagrees with the Shach on the underlying rule. It argues that the case is not comparable to a get, where the defect is that the woman cannot be told to take her get from the ground and the agent's mission does not end until delivery into her hand. Netivot states simply that with a slave, taking a deed of emancipation from the ground is valid even though the laws of emancipation are compared to those of divorce, because the comparison concerns the text of the deed itself. The Taz in Yoreh Deah 267:44 also appears to hold this. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Even HaEzer 82, however, takes it as obvious that such a delivery is invalid and explains the Taz accordingly. Part of that discussion is cited in Pitchei Teshuva to Even HaEzer 141, seif katan 35. Responsa Be'er Yitzchak 1 likewise cites Ritva, Kiddushin 23, who writes that taking a deed of emancipation from the ground is ineffective. See there.
