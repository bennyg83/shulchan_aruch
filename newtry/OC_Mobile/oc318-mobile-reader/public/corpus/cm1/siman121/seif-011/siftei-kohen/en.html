The law in this matter, etc. We find a distinction between “take” and “give” only regarding acquisition for the recipient, because some authorities hold that “take” is not equivalent to “acquire,” whereas “give” is equivalent to “acquire,” as explained below in siman 125, seifim 1, 5, and 6. Regarding the oath, however, there is no distinction between them. So writes the Rosh in a responsum. See also below, seif katan 44.
And if he denies it, he must pay, etc. Nimukei Yosef explains that although the creditor could have exempted himself by claiming unavoidable accident, such accidents are uncommon. According to this, since we rule that one who holds collateral for a loan has the status of an unpaid custodian, as explained above in siman 72, and we do not extract money in a case of unresolved law, the messenger should be exempt because the creditor could have exempted himself by claiming theft or loss. The Rama’s ruling therefore requires study. Perhaps the Rama understood that theft and loss are also uncommon. There is some support for this from the beginning of HaMafkid, where theft, loss, and unavoidable accident are treated alike with respect to a custodian’s ability to exempt himself and thereby acquire the double payment. Still, it is difficult to know the Rama’s source, since Nimukei Yosef may simply be following his own view that a creditor holding collateral is a paid custodian. Perhaps the Rama reasons that because we rule that he is an unpaid custodian only because of a doubt in law, one cannot say that he could confidently exempt himself by claiming theft or loss, since he might fear that beit din would rule him liable or that the borrower would seize property, and seizure is effective in a legal doubt. This still requires study. Moreover, the Ran in Nimukei Yosef itself questions the reasoning that the creditor was compelled to trust the messenger because he could have pleaded unavoidable accident, and the Gemara in HaMafkid indicates that such a plea is also treated as practically available. Maharam of Tiktin raises objections from the laws of a storekeeper on his ledger and from a custodian who transfers an item to another custodian. Those objections are not difficult, because here the creditor sent the person specifically to take collateral, and whoever is entrusted with the collateral must necessarily be trusted with respect to a claim of unavoidable accident. Nevertheless, the Rama’s ruling still requires study.
