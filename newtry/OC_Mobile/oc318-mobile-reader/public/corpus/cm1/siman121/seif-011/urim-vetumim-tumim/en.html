For he should have taken the pledge before witnesses. See the Shach, who asks: with respect to the amount by which the pledge exceeds the debt, we rule that the creditor is only an unpaid custodian. What then would taking the pledge before witnesses accomplish? He could still claim theft or loss, which is common, so the lender must ultimately rely upon his honesty. It appears that there are two answers. First, he was negligent because he should have taken the pledge before witnesses and told the borrower not to return it except before witnesses. If the creditor later died and the pledge was found among his effects, the heirs would then have to return it. If it was not originally taken before witnesses, the heirs can say they do not know that it belongs to the borrower. Therefore, even if the creditor himself later denies the pledge, his failure to arrange witnessed possession was initial negligence that led to the eventual loss, and he can be liable because the unavoidable event arose from that negligence. Second, even without that reasoning, if the pledge had been taken before witnesses and the creditor later claimed an unavoidable accident, he would have had to take the full custodian's oath of an unpaid custodian. Now that he simply denies the pledge, only heset is imposed. A person may be trustworthy in the creditor's eyes for a full Torah oath but suspected with respect to a lighter oath. This is why authorities rule that if a person took only a lighter oath and afterward one witness appeared, he must swear again with the full custodian's oath. According to this second explanation, "before witnesses" need not be literal. One witness would also suffice, because one witness creates the stronger oath.
