And he shall impose a cherem. See Shach seif katan 17 through his words, "since the debtor does not recognize the emblem." This appears to be a textual error. It should read, "the handwriting written above the emblem," because the case is one in which witnesses identify the emblem. The issue cannot be whether the debtor recognizes the emblem itself. Shach's halachic position is as follows. Whether the creditor said "send it through him" or appointed an agent to bring the money, ordinarily an agent must have been appointed before witnesses. But if the creditor wrote in his own handwriting that the money should be given to a particular person, or even if another person wrote the document and the creditor signed his name so that it has the force of his handwriting, this works without witnesses and the named person is an agent. If, however, the writing is in another person's hand and the creditor merely places beneath it an emblem or personal mark, such as a drawing of a fish or similar sign, and he is not one of the prominent people whose emblem is treated as a full signature, the mark is not considered a signature. Money may then be sent through the bearer only if he was appointed before witnesses. According to Tosafot, even if the creditor admits that he gave the emblem to the bearer and appointed him, it is still not equivalent to an agent appointed before witnesses, but only to identifying a possible messenger. Shach disagrees in a case of admitted appointment. If the creditor admits that he appointed him and handed him the emblem, then even without witnesses he is treated as an agent appointed before witnesses. Shach's case is where the creditor denies that the emblem was given for agency, claiming it was given for another purpose, or that the bearer found or stole it. In such a case the creditor is believed, even though the bearer holds the emblem. With an ordinary written instruction and full signature, the creditor would not be believed to make such claims. Likewise, even if words directing payment to the bearer are written above the emblem, and witnesses recognize the emblem as the creditor's, but they do not recognize the handwriting, the creditor may claim that the writing is forged, that the bearer found the emblem and added the instruction himself, and the creditor is believed. It also appears possible to defend Rama. Since where a creditor sends an ordinary written note saying "send" and payment is made to the bearer, the debtor is exempt by merchant custom, as Sema explains, Rama may hold that the same applies where the creditor sends his emblem with the bearer and says generally "send it." Handing the bearer the emblem is analogous to handing him a written note, and merchant custom makes it effective.
