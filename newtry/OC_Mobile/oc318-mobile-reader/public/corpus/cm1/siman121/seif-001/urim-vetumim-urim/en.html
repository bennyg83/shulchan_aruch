If he said to him, etc. This need not be said before witnesses. Even if it was said privately between them, it suffices if the lender and borrower admit it. See Sema and Shach.
That he sent him a written instruction. See Tumim. If he expressly wrote, 'Send it through so and so,' then by strict law the borrower is exempt from liability for loss. But if he merely wrote, 'Send me what you have of mine,' then by strict law the borrower remains liable, and he is exempt only because of mercantile custom. Accordingly, in a place where there is no such custom, the matter is doubtful. I wrote that where the instruction is general, the mercantile custom may apply specifically to giving it to the bearer of the letter. See Tumim.
Even if the messenger is a gentile. He need not add, 'and be discharged.' See Sema. This applies specifically where the gentile is not his Canaanite slave. If he is his slave, whose person is owned by him, the sender must expressly say, 'and be discharged.' This is explicit in the Gemara, chapter HaShoel.
By the route people ordinarily use, etc. See Tumim, where I concluded that if he instructed him to send it before there was a time of emergency, and afterward unrest and danger arose, then even if he said to send it through a particular person, the sender remains liable. If the danger already existed when he gave the instruction, then even a general instruction exempts him, because he fulfilled what he was told. But where there are two routes and it is known that the specified messenger travels by the dangerous route, if the sender specifically named that messenger he is exempt, whereas if he did not specify him he is liable.
Through one established as a denier. A minor, mentally incompetent person, deaf mute, or gentile is always considered within the category of one established as a denier, even if we personally regard him as trustworthy. See Shach.
This is negligence, etc. Even if the messenger who had been established as a denier did not in fact deny anything, but witnesses establish that an accident befell him, the sender is nevertheless liable. This is a case whose beginning was negligence and whose end was unavoidable accident, for which one is liable where the accident resulted from the negligence. Had he not entrusted it to this person, the accident on the road would not have occurred to it. See Shach.
Unless he specified to him. The Eir Shoshan holds that it suffices if he said, 'Send it through whomever you wish, and be discharged.' The Shach holds that this does not suffice, because his intent is still presumed to be that it be sent through a trustworthy person. Rather, he must say, 'Send it even through a gentile,' or the like. The Shach's view appears correct.
