Rather, he sent him written instructions. The Ir Shushan adds that merchants customarily act this way. That wording indeed appears in the Rif and is cited by the Rosh and Beit Yosef, but it is a scribal error. This is a complete rule of law. Only where he wrote nothing specific and merely gave the letter to its bearer does liability depend upon commercial custom. This is the Sema’s explanation. The Rosh challenged the Rif on the ground that this is a complete legal rule, and Maharshal and the Bach cite that objection. Maharshal and the Bach also state that the wording is a scribal error. Even if the messenger is a non-Jew, and certainly where he said, “Send it and be discharged.” This is the Sema’s view, unlike the Baal HaTerumot, who writes in the name of Rambam that he must say, “Send it through this particular non-Jew and be discharged.” His proof from siman 341, seif 8, concerning a borrower who sends an item through his Canaanite slave, is no proof, because a slave’s hand is legally like his master’s hand and the item therefore remains in the borrower’s possession.
And by the route ordinarily used by people. The Sema writes that this limitation applies only where the creditor wrote or said, “Send it through whomever you choose.” If he specified a particular person, the complaint is not against the sender but against the messenger, who should not have carried it by a dangerous route. At first glance, however, since the creditor’s authorization did not contemplate such danger, even when he said, “Send it through so-and-so,” he authorized only the ordinary route. That seems correct when he appointed a specific messenger, and likewise when he said, “Send it through whomever you choose.” Yet Tosafot, cited below, imply that according to the view that a messenger appointed before witnesses is a valid agent, the debtor is exempt even if he sent the money through a dangerous route. All the more so here, where everyone agrees the person is an agent. It may be answered that Tosafot hold that even where the creditor said, “Send it through whomever you choose,” if danger is common and the debtor nevertheless sent it that way, he is exempt. According to the Baal HaTerumot and Tur, who hold him liable, the same liability applies where the creditor designated a particular person.
Through one who has been established as a denier. All the more so through a non-Jew, a deaf-mute, an incompetent person, or a minor. Regarding these latter categories, no prior record of denial is required. Even if a non-Jew is considered trustworthy, the sender remains liable, as stated below in siman 225. As to a deaf-mute, incompetent person, or minor, it is self-evident that they cannot acquire an established reputation for reliability, since they lack legal competence.
