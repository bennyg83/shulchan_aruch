Rather, he sent him a written message, etc. See Sema seif katan 2, who challenges the Ir Shushan for citing the Rif according to the Rosh's version: "If he sent him a letter saying, 'Send me that which I have with you through so and so,' and he sent it, he is not liable for its loss, for this is the merchants' practice." The Sema writes that this version is a scribal error, because it raises the Rosh's question: this exemption follows from strict law and not from merchants' custom. Rather, the proper version is that he wrote only, "Send it to me," without naming anyone, and the holder sent it through a person who was not established as trustworthy or the like. Even so he is exempt, not under strict law but because of merchants' custom. The Derishah, Maharshal, and Bach write similarly. I still do not understand why the Rif stated this as a separate case specifically regarding a written message, rather than adding it above where he ruled that if one said to another, "Send it to me through so and so," and he sent it, he is exempt. He could simply have added that even where he did not name so and so he is exempt by custom. What significance is there to writing rather than oral instruction? It seems to me that he indeed did not name so and so, but the holder delivered the item specifically to the bearer of that letter. Under strict law, since the bearer was not named, the holder should not give it to him, because he was appointed only to bring the letter and not to receive the money. Nevertheless, merchants customarily understand the writer to intend that the item be delivered to the bearer of the letter. They do not, however, customarily permit delivery to some other person. Accordingly, the case necessarily concerns a written letter and has no parallel in an oral instruction. This appears correct, for we should not extend an enactment that overrides strict law beyond its terms. Still, even according to the Rosh's version of the Rif one may explain that when the Rif says he sent him a letter, it means that he wrote it in his own handwriting but did not sign his name, merely sending an unsigned handwritten note. The Rif would then follow the Ritva and those who agree with him that where a person did not sign or state his name, even though it is in his handwriting it has no force, since he can say, "I wrote it merely as a draft." See above, siman 69, Shach seif katan 4. Therefore merchants' custom is required, as stated above in siman 69 concerning the custom of partners. This is precise.
And by the route people ordinarily use, etc. The Sema in seif katan 4 writes that this applies specifically when he said, "Send it through whomever you wish," whereas if he said, "Send it through so and so," the negligence is not attributed to the sender but to the agent. The Shach in seif katan 5 writes that there is no distinction. In truth, the source of the ruling in the Baal HaTerumot concerns a condition made initially that he may send through whomever he wishes, which is a valid monetary stipulation. The Raavad writes regarding this that nevertheless the original stipulation did not contemplate a time of emergency and dangerous roads. But if, after the emergency and danger arose, the owner expressly instructed him to send it, what else was he to do? If one says that he still should not have obeyed because perhaps the owner did not know of the danger, the same can be said even when he expressly named so and so, for perhaps the danger and difficult circumstances were unknown to him. Thus reason supports the Shach. Yet the wording of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, which state this rule in the case where he told him simply to send it, supports the Sema. It therefore appears that if there is a general emergency and all roads are presumed dangerous, and nevertheless he instructed him to send it, the sender is certainly exempt, for he carried out the depositor's will. Rather, for example, if from Jerusalem to Damascus the route through Tzefat is dangerous but the route through Hebron is not, then if he instructed him to send it through a particular person, even if it is known that this person is from Tzefat and will travel through Tzefat because his home and usual route are there, the sender is exempt because that person was specifically designated. But if he instructed him only generally and the sender chose a person whose route passes through the dangerous place of Tzefat, he is plainly negligent, since he should have sent it through someone whose route does not pass through danger. This resolves the matter. All the rulings of the Shulchan Aruch concern a situation where not every route is dangerous, but an alternative safe route exists. This is clear.
