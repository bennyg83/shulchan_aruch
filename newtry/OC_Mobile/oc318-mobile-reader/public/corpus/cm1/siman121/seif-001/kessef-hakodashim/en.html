Seif 1. (Even when the messenger is a non Jew. See Shach, seif katan 4.) In siman 121 the Shach rejects the view cited by Baal HaTerumot in the name of Ramban, which distinguishes a Canaanite slave because the slave's hand is legally like his master's hand. There may nevertheless be room to explain Baal HaTerumot's reasoning. When a creditor says, "Send the money through a non Jew," one could understand this as, "Send it through him and be discharged." Since ordinary acquisition or agency does not operate through a non Jew, the discharge would have to rest on waiver, which does not require a kinyan. A similar idea appears at the end of siman 340. If the lender tells the borrower to send the borrowed article through someone who cannot be his halachic agent, the borrower's responsibility can nevertheless terminate because the lender knowingly directed that method of return. This resembles the rule, "Place it on a rock and be discharged," where the debt or responsibility ends through waiver. At first glance, therefore, why should a Canaanite slave be worse merely because his hand is legally the master's hand? The Gemara in the chapter HaShoel, however, indicates that the laws of return correspond to the laws of initial delivery. This suggests that the discharge is not pure waiver. Rather, where valid agency exists, receipt by the agent is treated as receipt by the creditor himself. A Canaanite slave cannot acquire independently from his master, and the ordinary law of beneficial acquisition through him does not operate. Perhaps this is why the cited authorities hold that the slave cannot serve to effect the discharge. It is still possible that waiver should independently suffice, because when the creditor tells the debtor to send the property in a manner he knows will remove it from the debtor's control, his instruction can reasonably be understood as accepting the resulting risk. Yet perhaps waiver is ineffective unless he explicitly says "and be discharged." Without that phrase, the debtor remains responsible unless a valid agency relation exists. A Canaanite slave may be worse even than a minor. Although a minor lacks ordinary agency, he at least has some capacity to acquire for himself in certain contexts, whereas a slave's acquisition immediately belongs to his master. Thus the legal structure needed for receipt on behalf of the creditor may be absent. This distinction concerns repayment or return of property already owed. If, by contrast, a buyer tells a seller, "Send money through a non Jew and I will send you merchandise," there may be no liability on the seller for loss of the money because agency and beneficial acquisition do not operate through the non Jew. One might still seek to impose liability through guaranty, as in the case of one who says, "Throw a maneh into the sea and I will pay," according to the authorities who impose liability. Yet guaranty in uncertain future circumstances can involve asmachta, and the usual basis for enforceability is the benefit derived from the creditor's reliance. Whether that benefit exists in the commercial case depends upon the particular transaction and the seller's anticipated profit. The matter requires further study.
