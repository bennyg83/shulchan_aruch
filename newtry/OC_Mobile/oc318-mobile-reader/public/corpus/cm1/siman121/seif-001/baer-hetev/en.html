Exempt. The Ir Shushan concludes that this is because merchants commonly conduct themselves this way, and such a practice has legal force. The Rif indeed contains this wording, and the Rosh and Beit Yosef cite it, but the Sema writes that it is a textual error. The Tur, whom the authors of the Shulchan Aruch follow, shows that this is a rule of law. Only where the lender writes generally, "Send me my money," and the debtor hands it to the bearer of that letter does the result depend upon local commercial custom. The Shach notes that the Rosh asked this very question on the Rif, as cited by Maharshal and the Bach, and Maharshal and the Bach likewise call the wording a textual error. See Responsa Maharashdam 38.
A non-Jew. It is unnecessary to add, "Send it and thereby be exempt." Sema and Shach.
Commonly travel. The Sema writes that this limitation applies specifically when the lender said or wrote, "Send it by whomever you wish." If he said or wrote, "Send it to me through so-and-so," any complaint lies against the agent, not the sender, because the agent should not have transported the money by a dangerous route. The Shach writes that at first glance the underlying reason is that the lender never authorized use of an unusually dangerous route, so even if he named a specific agent, the authorization should extend only to ordinary routes. The same should be true if he appointed a particular person as agent and told him to forward it through whomever he wished. However, Tosafot cited later imply that according to the view that an agent appointed before witnesses is a valid agent, the debtor is exempt even if the money was sent through that agent by a dangerous route. All the more so here, where the named person is an agent according to all views. One could answer that Tosafot themselves may hold that even where one said, "Send through whomever you wish," the debtor is exempt if the agent used a dangerous route, whereas Baal HaTerumot and the Tur, who impose liability in such a case, would likewise impose liability when the lender named a specific person. Shach.
A known denier. All the more so a non-Jew, a deaf mute, an incompetent person, or a minor. Maharshal. The Shach explains that regarding a non-Jew and these persons, no established history of denial is needed. Indeed, even if a particular non-Jew is regarded as trustworthy, the debtor remains liable, as stated at the end of siman 225, seif 2. Regarding a deaf mute, incompetent person, or minor, it is obvious that no presumption of trustworthiness applies because they lack legal capacity.
And is liable. This is so even if the agent did not deny receiving the money but produced witnesses that it was lost through unavoidable accident. Once he was already established as a denier, entrusting the money to him was negligent at the outset, and when negligence at the outset is followed by unavoidable loss, liability remains, as stated in siman 291, seif 6. Shach.
Specified. The Ir Shushan says this means he expressly said, "Send it through whomever you wish and thereby be exempt." The Shach writes that this wording is insufficient. He must expressly authorize sending it even through a person known as a denier or through a dangerous route.
