Signs and letters, etc. This explains the statement that money is not sent by means of a signet. Above, in seif 3, the Mechaber wrote of a creditor who gave his seal as a sign, which some authorities likewise explain as the meaning of the signet mentioned in the Gemara. We must say that the Mechaber accepted both explanations as law. Above, there was no denial concerning the signet itself, but the creditor had not designated a particular messenger, as explained above. Here, by contrast, he expressly designated the messenger and instructed the debtor to send through him, but there is a dispute about the authenticity of the signet itself, for the creditor says that it never came from him, as explained in the text. Therefore the Shach's attempt in seif katan 17 to explain the Rama's statement that the creditor must accept a cherem as following the Rambam, whose words imply that if the creditor admits the signet he bears the loss, is not understandable. Here he expressly instructed, "Send it through Levi," as is clear from the Ramban, so if he admits the signet he plainly bears the loss. But above, in seif 3, where he did not designate the messenger but merely said, "Bring it to me," as explained, what would his admission accomplish? There was no appointment of an agent. And others deceived him, etc. This is the Rambam's explanation of the Gemara's term "signet." It is difficult to understand, however, because the Gemara says that even witnesses are signed upon it. How can the creditor then claim that others deceived the debtor? The suggested answer, that the witnesses testify only that the creditor customarily uses such signs but do not know that these particular signs came from him, is strained. It must obviously be known that he ordinarily uses these signs, for otherwise why would the debtor send money on the basis of signs not known to be the creditor's? Moreover, what is Rabbi Yochanan's reasoning in saying that money may be sent? If the creditor loudly protests that the signs did not come from him, no one could safely act, for anyone could forge another's signs and thereby cause his money to be paid out. It is also strained to explain that "witnesses are upon it" means that they authenticate it as recognizable as his signs, while the Gemara holds that authentication is effective only for handwriting because a person is not brazen enough to forge words of Torah, but with ordinary signs people may boldly forge and even witness authentication would not help. If that were the Rambam's position, he should have said expressly that even formal authentication is ineffective. Moreover, in discussing the signet itself the Gemara should have offered this remedy for Shmuel, namely that witnesses attest that it is his signet. There would then be no basis for asking Shmuel what remedy exists. It therefore appears more correct that the Rambam rules like Rabbi Yochanan, who says that money may be sent by means of a signet, as do the Raavan and some other authorities. Rabbi Yochanan permits this only when witnesses attest to the signet, because then the creditor cannot say, "Others deceived you." Shmuel still forbids it, as the other authorities write. Without witnesses, however, even Rabbi Yochanan agrees that the money may not be sent, for otherwise no one could safely retain his property, as explained above. This is logically sound. Why should we create such an extreme dispute, with Rabbi Yochanan permitting even without witnesses and Shmuel forbidding even with witnesses? The Gemara therefore properly asks Shmuel what remedy exists, because in his view even witnesses do not suffice. The Rambam consequently states the possibility that others deceived the debtor, a concern Rabbi Yochanan also accepts where there are no witnesses. It follows that if there are witnesses, so that the creditor cannot claim deception, the creditor bears the loss. Everything is thus resolved. The Hagahot Maimoniyot and Kesef Mishneh, who understood the Rambam to rule like Shmuel, require study.
And some rule that he must swear. See Shach seif katan 25 in the name of the Mabit, who rules that even where the document contains a clause of credibility, the creditor must nevertheless swear. The Shach questions this, and rightly so. Otherwise, what benefit would such a credibility clause provide? The debtor could always produce signs or the like and force the creditor to swear. If the case is one where the messenger is before us and says that the writing is indeed the creditor's handwriting, the issue depends on the dispute among the poskim above in siman 71 whether a credibility clause is effective against one witness. The matter still requires study.
