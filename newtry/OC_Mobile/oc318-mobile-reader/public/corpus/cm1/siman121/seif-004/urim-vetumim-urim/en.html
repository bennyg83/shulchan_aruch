The borrower shall take a hesset oath. This refers to an oral loan, where he can claim, 'I repaid,' and is believed with a hesset oath. The same applies if he says, 'Your letter reached me.'
That your handwriting came, etc. Even if the handwriting is no longer before us, since this is an oral loan he is believed to say that he had a letter in the lender's handwriting, through a migo that he could have said, 'It never happened,' or, 'I repaid.'
Or the borrower does not know, etc. This is a case of, 'I do not know whether I repaid you.' Even according to the view that one is liable on such a claim only where he should have known, here too he should have known and carefully verified whether the writing was truly the lender's handwriting. Before making such verification, he should not have paid.
And others deceived him. This means that the document expressly stated that he should send the money through that person. If the lender admitted that this was his writing, it is obvious that the borrower would be exempt. This differs from seif 3 above, where it was not stated that the lender appointed him as an agent, as explained above. See Tumim for what I wrote on this.
That he shall swear, etc. The Kesef Mishneh explains the reason by analogy to one who partially impairs a note, where we say that perhaps he did not carefully remember. The same applies here, perhaps he does not remember accurately that he gave it to the messenger. If the note contains a clause of trust, see Tumim, where I wrote that the matter requires study.
