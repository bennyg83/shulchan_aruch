The borrower takes an oath of hesset that it is the lender's handwriting, etc. This case is where the lender does not hold a promissory note against the borrower. The borrower is therefore believed through a migo, since he could have denied the loan entirely or claimed that he already returned it, as the Mechaber writes in seif 5. The apparent repetition in the Mechaber results from his sources. Here he copies the abbreviated language of the Rambam, while in the following seif he copies the fuller language of the Baal HaTerumot and the Tur. In the first clause, the Rambam writes that the borrower swears that the lender's handwriting 'came to him,' implying that the writing itself is no longer before us. This is a greater novelty, teaching that even without the document presently available, if the borrower asserts with certainty that he received the lender's handwritten instruction, he is exempt. In the latter clause, where the borrower does not recognize the lender's handwriting, the novelty is the converse: even if the writing is before us, the borrower remains liable because he cannot identify it as the lender's writing.
