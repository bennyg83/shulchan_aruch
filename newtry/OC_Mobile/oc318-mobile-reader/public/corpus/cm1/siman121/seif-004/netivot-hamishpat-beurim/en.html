Go and tell him. See Shach seif katan 10. According to his explanation, Maharshal disagrees with the Mechaber. The Mechaber holds that if the creditor told a person before witnesses, "If he gives the money to you, bring it to me," that person is also an agent appointed before witnesses. Maharshal holds that this is merely identifying a possible messenger, because Rashi and Ran imply that one becomes an agent only when the creditor expressly appoints him and says, "Be my agent to receive." The phrase "therefore, where he said" is Maharshal's own language, as appears in Yam Shel Shlomo. It seems that where the creditor admits the appointment, even without witnesses the messenger is an agent. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 104, s.v. Shaliach SheAsao, explain in one answer that there is no distinction between saying in the debtor's presence "send it through him" and conveying the same instruction outside his presence. When the creditor says in the debtor's presence "send it through him," witnesses are certainly unnecessary, so the same should apply when the instruction was relayed outside his presence. Although Tosafot in their second answer distinguish between the two, Maharshal himself writes that Tosafot in Perek HaShoel later retracted that distinction and accepted the first answer. The Rosh likewise cites only the first answer. Maharshal mentions witnesses only because without them the creditor could deny the instruction, not because witnesses create the agency. The Tur and Mechaber likewise do not mention witnesses in the first case, where one is told to tell the debtor to send through him, but only in the case of an agent to bring the money. Shach, however, infers from Rashi and Ran that even where the creditor says, "Tell the debtor to send through you," the messenger is not an agent unless the creditor appoints him before witnesses. Ramban's words cited by Shach in seif katan 15 likewise support that view. There is a textual error in Shach there; the correct reading is that the creditor must say before witnesses, "I hereby appoint you as my agent to receive money from so and so," or, "Tell so and so to send the money through you, for I have appointed you as my agent before witnesses." Both formulations are certainly effective, as Tosafot, the Rosh, and Ramban explain. Moreover, the Mechaber rules in seif 1 that a written instruction is effective, which necessarily means that an instruction given outside the debtor's presence can be effective. The principal rule therefore appears as follows. If the creditor merely says, "If he gives it to you, bring it to me," Maharshal's view is primary. But if he says, "Tell so and so that I sent you to tell him to send the money through you," the view of the Rosh, Tur, Mechaber, Tosafot, Sema, and Perishah is primary, and this works even without witnesses. The reason is not formal agency. Rather, Tosafot explain that since saying directly to the debtor "send through so and so" is effective, it makes no difference whether the creditor says it to him personally or sends someone to convey those words. This produces several practical distinctions between a person through whom the creditor instructs the debtor to send, and a true agent for receipt. Saying "send through so and so" operates, according to the Shitah Mekubetzet, through the law of arev, like saying "give a maneh to so and so and I will become obligated to you." This explains the dispute between Maharshal and Shach. Those who require witnesses understand the matter through agency; those who treat it through arev do not. Under the arev rationale, the message can even be conveyed by a non-Jew, a minor, a deaf mute, or a mentally incompetent person, as in seif 1, because that messenger merely conveys the creditor's words. The Rosh similarly writes at the end of the first perek of Gittin that a non-Jew who conveys an appointment is merely performing a mechanical act. By contrast, a true agent for receipt, where the creditor says "receive from so and so" and appoints him before witnesses, must be legally competent for agency, so a non-Jew, minor, deaf mute, or mentally incompetent person cannot serve. According to Shach, even "send through so and so" requires witnesses, because he does not compare it fully to arev. There, when one says "give a maneh to so and so," the money becomes the recipient's at once and leaves one domain for another by his instruction. But where he says "send through so and so," unless he also says "acquire it for him," the debtor can still take the money back from the messenger, as Shach writes in seif katan 14. It is therefore not a completed transfer and is treated as agency. Nevertheless, the primary view is that it works by the law of arev even outside the debtor's presence. This also resolves Tosafot's question in Bava Kamma 104. A messenger sent merely to carry the creditor's instruction may discharge the debtor through arev, but this does not necessarily constitute return of stolen property where Scripture requires the property to reach the injured party, especially where the robber swore falsely and must carry the money to him even in Media. In such a case, until the recipient acquires the money, it remains like the robber's deposit in the messenger's hands and does not count as the return needed for atonement. A true agent for receipt is different, because delivery to him is like delivery to the injured party himself. There is also a further distinction between an agent for receipt and one through whom the creditor said to send, regarding the death of the principal or revocation of the agency, as will be explained in siman 122.
