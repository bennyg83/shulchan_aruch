The following phrase, “Some authorities rule,” refers to the immediately preceding case, where the debtor does not know whether the document is genuinely in the creditor’s handwriting. If the debtor says with certainty that it was not the creditor’s handwriting, the creditor must take an oath and collect.
After imposing a ban. This applies where the messenger is not before us. If the messenger is present and contradicts the creditor, all agree that the creditor must swear and then collect, as stated below in seif 9.
Some authorities rule. Maharshal decides in favor of the first view, because no oath is imposed on the basis of an uncertain claim. The Tur goes further and rules that where the creditor says, “You sent it through a person who is unreliable,” and the debtor is uncertain, the creditor does not swear and collect. The view requiring an oath is difficult, for the creditor’s claim is uncertain and the debtor’s denial is certain. Nevertheless, since this is a case of one who swears and collects, some authorities require the creditor to swear when the debtor requests it, just as in other cases where the debtor says, “Swear to me.” The practical ruling follows the first view unless local practice dictates otherwise.

Responsa Mabit rules that even where the loan document grants the creditor full credibility and expressly exempts him from bans and oaths, if the debtor claims that the creditor sent the written instruction, the creditor must still swear that it was not his handwriting. Such clauses do not ordinarily waive an oath arising from a claim equivalent to a receipt or discharge.
