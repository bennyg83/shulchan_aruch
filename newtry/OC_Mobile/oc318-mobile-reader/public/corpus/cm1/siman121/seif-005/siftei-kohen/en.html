If it is a loan evidenced by a document. The borrower bears liability for unavoidable accidents and therefore lacks a migo. The Tur’s phrase, “If he is a borrower who has no claim by which to be exempt,” refers back to a loan evidenced by a document. Maharshal suggests that it includes a loan conditioned on repayment only before witnesses, or any similar case in which the borrower cannot plead payment. The Mechaber’s formulation, taken from the Baal HaTerumot, expresses the same point.
The borrower is not believed and must pay. Maharshal disputes the Tur and rules that the borrower is believed with a hesset oath, relying on Rambam’s ruling in seif 4. That proof is incorrect, because Rambam there speaks specifically of an oral loan. Kesef Mishneh likewise explains that the borrower says, “I repaid you by giving the money to your messenger,” and therefore swears hesset and is exempt, language appropriate only to an oral loan. Perhaps Maharshal challenged the Tur only because he understood it to concern an oral loan conditioned on repayment before witnesses. Even then his ruling requires study, for once the borrower cannot plead ordinary repayment, he likewise cannot be believed that the creditor authored the instruction. The Sema and Ir Shushan correctly state that his credibility exists only by virtue of a migo that he could deny the loan or plead payment. A depositary is likewise believed only through a migo, and the same applies here.

After the creditor swears. This means only where the borrower says, “Swear to me that this is not your handwriting.” The Raavad’s wording in the Baal HaTerumot indicates this, and it is also the view of Tur and Mechaber. They did not spell it out because the principal rule appears above in siman 82: even when a debtor pleads actual payment, the holder of a document need not swear unless the debtor demands an oath. The same applies here. Indeed, the Mechaber’s added words, “if the borrower claims, ‘Swear to me,’” clarify that the creditor’s stringent oath is required specifically upon that demand. The phrase concerning the messenger who says the money was lost is a scribal error and should read, “and the messenger says that it was lost.” The Rama’s gloss and Ir Shushan appear to connect the borrower’s demand to the messenger’s oath, but that cannot be correct, since a guardian’s oath is imposed even without such a demand. The added phrase therefore must refer to the creditor’s oath.
That the creditor swear. The Bach writes that even if the document contains a credibility clause, the creditor must swear, because the debtor’s claim functions like a receipt and resembles a claim of waiver, which is heard despite a credibility clause.
A stringent oath. The Sema calls the creditor’s oath a Mishnaic oath, like other cases of one who swears and collects. He distinguishes the earlier oath of a depositary who received the deposit under a document, calling it a Torah oath because the depositary is believed only through a migo that the property was lost through accident, which would require a guardian’s Torah oath. Maharshal makes a similar distinction. In my view this is incorrect. The Baal HaTerumot, Tur, and Mechaber call it an oath resembling a Torah oath, which everywhere denotes a rabbinic oath administered while holding a sacred object. The depositary’s oath here is also rabbinic with a sacred object. It is inconceivable that a plea of “I returned it,” a claim unknown to the Torah’s oath structure, should generate an actual Torah oath. See my discussion below, siman 296, seif 2.
Who says that it was lost through accident. Here, and throughout this siman, the same rule applies if a gratuitous messenger says that it was stolen or lost, provided he is not a shopkeeper or moneychanger, whose standard of liability differs as explained below in siman 292.
