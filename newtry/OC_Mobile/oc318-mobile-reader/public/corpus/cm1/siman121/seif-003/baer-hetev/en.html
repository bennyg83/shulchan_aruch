His seal. Tosafot write that this rule applies specifically to ordinary people. With leading authorities of the generation whose seal or likeness is well known, money may be sent on the strength of it. Shiltei HaGibborim and Maharshal write likewise. It is possible, however, that Tosafot said this only according to their own explanation that the term refers to a distinctive seal placed beneath a scribe's handwriting. If the creditor merely instructed orally that money be brought to him and handed the messenger his seal, perhaps even for leading authorities this is ineffective. If the creditor denies having appointed him as an agent to bring the money, it is obvious that the seal alone is ineffective even for a leading authority. It appears that this applies only where the seal itself serves merely as a sign. If, however, the creditor wrote that the money should be sent through that person, even if another person wrote the document and the creditor merely signed his name below, provided his signature is recognizable, the debtor is exempt. If the entire writing in the creditor's own hand would have been effective, the same rule applies here, as stated at the beginning of siman 69 and in many other places. Ibid.
He sent me. The Sema explains that the messenger told the debtor that the creditor had instructed him to tell the debtor to send the money because the creditor needed it. The messenger did not alter the message by saying in the creditor's name that it should be sent specifically through him. The creditor had not placed such words in his mouth; rather, the messenger merely said that if the debtor gave him the money, he would bring it. The Shach objects that he does not understand this explanation. If without witnesses the messenger does not become an agent even where he says in the creditor's name, 'Send it through me,' and the debtor does so, then what does the identifying sign accomplish here, especially according to the Sema's view that money is not sent merely on the strength of a seal even when the creditor admits the matter? The gloss disputes the Shach and states that the Sema's words are clear. The governing rule is that when the creditor says, 'Send it through him,' that instruction itself suffices and witnesses are unnecessary. Witnesses are required only where the creditor merely tells the messenger to inform the debtor that money is owed and, if the debtor gives it to him, to bring it. The Sema's point is that where there is no factual dispute among creditor, debtor, and messenger, giving the messenger a recognizable seal together with the instruction 'bring it to me' is not equivalent to an instruction given before witnesses. According to this reading, Rama's insertion requiring a general ban is difficult, because that requirement was stated concerning a different case in which the creditor denies the writing.
