And he shall impose a cherem, etc. The Sema in seif katan 6 rules that if the creditor, not in the presence of witnesses, told a messenger, "Tell so and so to send me my money, and if he gives it to you bring it to me," this is ineffective because there were no witnesses. The Shach agrees, and therefore in seif katan 9 he explains that the Shulchan Aruch's cases, where the creditor gave his seal and said, "Go and bring me my money," and where the messenger came and said, "So and so sent me to you," are all one matter and merely employ varying language. On this basis he questions the Rama's ruling that the creditor must accept a cherem: what purpose does such a cherem serve? Even if the creditor did send him, since there were no witnesses it is ineffective. In seif katan 17 the Shach writes that this follows the Rambam, who holds that money may not be sent on the basis of a signet because of possible denial, as stated above in seif 4. This is difficult. Apart from the unnecessary repetition of the same rule in two consecutive seifim, the Shach himself admits that the Rambam is a lone opinion on this point. How then could the Rama state the ruling without qualification, not even as "some say," when according to the Shach's explanation most poskim disagree? The Shach writes that he found support for the Rambam in the Raavan. I do not understand this. The Raavan expressly explains the statement that money is not sent by means of a signet, even when witnesses are signed upon it. The Gemara says "even" because without witnesses the debtor can certainly claim that someone stole the signet and brought it to him. Even where witnesses testify that it was delivered in their presence, so that there is no such concern, the money still may not be sent, because this is unlike a case where the creditor said, "Send it with my son," where he actually designated the agent. Here he did not. It is thus clear that the Raavan does not agree with the Rambam, for according to him the money may not be sent even where there is no possibility of denial. In truth, however, my mind does not accept the Sema's basic ruling at all. The foundation of his conclusion is faulty. He holds that where the creditor said, "Tell so and so to send me the money, and if he gives it to you bring it to me," the instruction is ineffective because there were no witnesses. It appears to me that once the creditor said that the debtor should send it and later admits that he said so, the instruction is effective. What difference is there whether he said, "Send it through so and so," or said generally, "Send it to me, and if he gives it to you bring it to me"? The latter necessarily means through that person. At the beginning of his discussion the Rosh uses the language, "Go and bring me my money," and does not say, "Tell him to send it to me." According to the Sema, the Rosh should have distinguished that the creditor did not say to send it through him. The fact that he said merely "send it" without naming so and so would not matter. Rather, these expressions are all one. Thus afterward Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Elazar say that an agent appointed before witnesses means one to whom the creditor said, "Go and bring me my money," without mentioning that he told the debtor to send it. This itself is difficult: perhaps Rav Chisda holds that he is an agent only when the creditor used language of sending, saying, "Tell him to send it," while if he merely said, "Bring me my money," even Rav Chisda agrees that the creditor meant only that if the debtor wants to send the money but has no messenger available, this person should go to him and bring it. If so, our Mishnah could concern such a case and there would be no need to interpret it as merely pointing out an available messenger. We must therefore say that when the creditor tells him to have the debtor send it and also says simply, "Bring it to me," this itself means that it should be sent through him. The Derishah writes similarly in the name of some of our sages. In such a case everyone agrees that he is an agent. The dispute must therefore concern a case where the creditor did not use language explicitly appointing him. Rav Chisda nevertheless holds that he is an agent, while the contrary view must interpret the case as one where the creditor merely pointed out an available messenger. So too Tosafot explicitly state that Rabbah and Rav Chisda dispute a case where the creditor told witnesses that the debtor should send through so and so, but because the instruction was not given in the messenger's presence perhaps he was not actually appointed. Yet even according to Rav Chisda, without appointment he is not an agent. There would then be no need to resort to the category of merely pointing out a messenger. It follows that according to Tosafot, whenever the creditor says, "Bring the money," he has made him an agent, and the only remaining distinction is a case where he merely points out an available agent. Accordingly, the Shulchan Aruch's case involving a signet means that the creditor said only, "Bring it to me," without language of agency, while the messenger acted improperly and falsely claimed that the creditor had used language of agency. The creditor is believed and the debtor remains liable. But if the creditor admits that he did use language of agency, witnesses are wholly unnecessary, for it is equivalent to saying, "Send it through so and so." That is why the Rama writes that the creditor must accept a cherem. This is precise.
