In the manner people ordinarily send money, see Sema, seif katan 4. The Sema distinguishes between saying, 'send it through so-and-so,' and, 'send it through whomever you wish.' Perhaps the reason is that when a particular person is named, the burden of caution falls entirely on the lender to urge that agent to guard against risks, whereas in the second formulation it does not. Yet the plain wording implies that when a particular person is named, even if the lender did not and could not urge him, while the borrower could have done so, the Sema nevertheless holds that the borrower or custodian bears no liability. This requires study. Urim VeTumim writes there that where negligence precedes an unavoidable accident, liability arises only if the accident could have resulted from the negligence. The Shach also appears to imply this. Perhaps there is a distinction between sending through one to whom ordinary laws of agency apply and sending through a non-Jew, where the lender's consent operates only as waiver. In the latter case perhaps the lender waived liability only so long as the borrower did not deviate from the instruction, which implicitly required ordinary care even if the later loss was not directly caused by the deviation. Since the money did not leave the borrower's ownership through the non-Jew, the waiver itself may be conditional on the money being delivered to the lender. Perhaps if the borrower retakes the money from the messenger before delivery, the waiver is retroactively void. If so, even the doctrine of negligence followed by unavoidable loss may apply here only where the accident is linked to the negligence. The phrase 'in the manner people ordinarily send money' may also imply that one should not send the messenger out at night, since Chazal advised traveling in daylight. Robbery may sometimes result from the negligence of sending him at night. If established custom permits sending at night, however, that custom may exempt the sender.
