He did not appoint him, etc. The Sema writes that this applies even where the creditor admits that he used the language of agency stated in the preceding seif: “I have money in the hands of so and so; tell him to send it to me, and if he gives it to you, bring it to me.” Yet he did not say this before witnesses, nor did he tell the debtor to send it through this particular person, which would have been effective even without witnesses. Rather, the creditor merely gave the person his seal, and that does not suffice. This is also evident from the Ramban, Tosafot, and Maharshal cited below in seif katan 15. There is, however, another view that if the creditor admits the agency, the debtor is exempt, as I explain at length below in seif katan 17.
Rather, he gave him his seal as a sign, etc. Tosafot write that this limitation applies to ordinary people. With leading Torah authorities whose seals are well known, money may be sent on the strength of the seal. So too write the Shiltei Giborim, and Maharshal rules this way. It is possible, however, that Tosafot said this only according to their own explanation that a recognizable emblem means that the person affixed his emblem beneath the scribe’s handwriting. If he merely instructed the messenger orally to bring the money and handed him the emblem, perhaps even with leading authorities this does not suffice. And if the creditor denies that he appointed him as messenger, the emblem alone certainly does not suffice, even for a leading authority. This also appears from the Shiltei Giborim.
His seal as a sign. It appears that this limitation applies only when the seal itself was given merely as a sign. If, however, he wrote that the money should be sent through this person, even if another person wrote the body of the document and the creditor signed beneath it with a recognizable signature, the debtor is exempt. If the entire text had been in the creditor’s handwriting it would certainly be effective here, as stated above in seif 5, and another person’s handwriting with the creditor’s signature beneath it has the same law, as explained at the beginning of siman 69 and in many places. This is also evident from Tosafot, who state that an emblem beneath the scribe’s handwriting is effective for leading authorities, deriving this from the Gemara concerning distinctive signatures; there it is clear that an actual signature is even stronger.
So and so sent me to you, etc. The Sema explains that the messenger says, “The creditor told me to tell you to send him the money because he needs it,” but the messenger does not alter the message by saying in the creditor’s name that the debtor should send it specifically through him. The creditor did not put that statement into his mouth; he merely said that if the debtor gives the money to him, he should bring it. I do not understand this explanation. If without witnesses the person is not an appointed agent, then even if he says in the creditor’s name that the creditor instructed the debtor to send the money through him, why should the sign help, since money is not sent merely on the basis of an emblem? Tosafot state expressly in Bava Kamma 104b that even where witnesses attest that the emblem is genuine, it is not enough unless they also attest that the creditor appointed this person to receive the money. Maharshal writes likewise. The Ramban, in Milchamot, explains that an agent appointed before witnesses has the legal hand of his principal, but a written emblem that merely says, “Give so much of my money to so and so,” without witnessed appointment does not itself create agency. Since the principal did not appoint him before witnesses, the writing serves only to indicate a messenger, not to place him in the principal’s legal position. The Sema’s wording can therefore be sustained only with difficulty, by saying that the messenger did not say that the creditor had appointed him before witnesses to receive the money.
And witnesses attest that it is his seal. This is so even if they testify that the creditor handed him the seal in their presence, so long as they do not testify that he appointed him as agent. Maharshal appears to say this as well. He adds that if the parties originally agreed that whoever presents that seal or sign should receive the money, and there are witnesses that it was delivered to this messenger, the debtor is exempt, because monetary conditions are valid. The Shiltei Giborim write likewise. It appears that the same is true even if there are no witnesses to the delivery, provided the sign is recognized or the creditor admits that this is the agreed sign, since the parties stipulated this from the outset.
And he shall impose a cherem, etc. The Sema questions the Rama, arguing that this entire case involves no contradiction between the parties, and the point is only that delivery of the seal is not equivalent to appointment before witnesses. He says that the Rambam and Tur stated the oath and cherem only in the separate case where the creditor denies the writing that the debtor says he received. The Agudat Ezov raises the same question. In my view the Rama is correct. The Rambam’s phrase, “even if special signs and letters known between them were written,” is itself his explanation of the Gemara’s term for the emblem; otherwise the Rambam would have omitted the law entirely. Hagahot Maimoniyot and Kesef Mishneh say this explicitly. Thus the Rambam understands Shmuel’s rule that money is not sent by an emblem to mean that when the debtor does not know the emblem himself, even witnesses attesting to it do not remove the concern of forgery. It follows that according to the Rambam, if the creditor admits that he sent the messenger to bring the money, even privately, and the seal is admitted or recognized, the debtor is exempt. This is also clear from the Semag, the Tur, the Bach’s understanding of the Tur, and Rabbeinu Eliezer ben Natan. Although I wrote above that Tosafot appear to require appointment before witnesses even when the creditor admits the instruction, the Rambam, Semag, Tur, and other authorities disagree and hold that the creditor’s admission of the agency is enough. The Shiltei Giborim likewise concludes that the debtor’s liability exists only where the creditor denies having sent the messenger; where he admits it, the loss falls on the creditor. This is precisely the Rama’s ruling, and it is correct.
