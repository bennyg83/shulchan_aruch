Even if the borrower releases them. The Shach in seif katan 52 asks that below, in siman 140, it is explained that if a tenant deposits the rent with beit din he is no longer considered interested in the testimony. See the Shach's strained answer. In truth, according to what the Rashba and Ran write in Kiddushin, the matter is clear. They ask: why not have the borrower release the agents from liability whether they delivered the money or not? They answer that removal of an interest is effective only when the witnesses derive no benefit from the removal, as with townspeople whose Torah scroll was stolen or a partner who writes, "I have no claim or dealings concerning it." Here, however, the agents derive a benefit from the release and are therefore not believed. According to this, in the case of tenants, if their testimony establishes that the landlord possessed the land and the land is then awarded to the challenger, requiring them to pay rent to the challenger, they have a claim for the rent against the landlord. Surely we do not deal with fools who will pay rent twice. When the tenants therefore deposit the rent with beit din in advance, they gain no substantive benefit from their testimony, only a reduction of inconvenience. Here, by contrast, if the agents do not testify that they delivered the money to the lender, they must at least swear to the borrower. If the borrower releases them from that oath, he confers a substantial benefit by freeing them from an oath they otherwise owed. They remain interested parties and their testimony has no force. This is precise.
For he is not believed as two witnesses. The Sema wonders: what difference is there between saying that two witnesses cannot exempt and saying that one witness can obligate the lender to take a Torah oath? The general rule is that wherever two witnesses would obligate payment, one witness obligates an oath. It appears that this is correct, but the Gidulei Terumah, page 133, writes, and it is also somewhat evident from the Shach, that the borrower has a migo that he could say, "I myself paid you," and the witnesses could testify consistently with him, in which case they would be believed. If one argues that the borrower does not wish to lie, he is lying now as well, and a migo involving brazenness is accepted in monetary matters. Tosafot, however, ask that this is a migo involving two people, and answer that where both witnesses gain financially from the testimony, they will coordinate their account and therefore we do not accept a migo involving two. According to this, one can say that the borrower is afraid to lie by claiming that he himself paid. Perhaps the witnesses truly did pay, in which event they gain nothing from supporting his false statement and may not coordinate their testimony. The borrower would then be contradicted: one witness could say that the borrower paid, while the other says that he himself paid, creating contradictory testimony and destroying the borrower's claim. Therefore the borrower says that he personally saw them pay. On that version they will coordinate their testimony in either event: if it is true, naturally so; if not, they both gain financially and will coordinate. Thus he has no migo. This reasoning applies to two witnesses. With one witness, however, the borrower plainly has the migo described above. He could say that he himself paid, and the one witness would support him and obligate the lender to take a Torah oath. There is no issue of a migo involving two. Therefore the lender is obligated to take a Torah oath. This resolves the matter and is correct.
