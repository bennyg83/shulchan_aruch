Or I delivered it to the lender. He need not deliver it before witnesses, since he was not instructed to deliver it before witnesses. See Shach.
The messenger shall swear. Meaning, although the borrower's claim is uncertain, since there is a definite claim by the lender and the messenger certainly received the money, the messenger must swear. See Sema and Shach.
While holding a sacred object. Since he is collecting money, and all those who collect through an oath take an oath while holding a sacred object.
Etc. I have already written that even though he is now swearing, a witness who must swear is nevertheless not a valid witness.
And even if he exempts, etc. For by the benefit of being exempted from the oath, they give their testimony, and there is therefore some personal benefit. See Tumim, where I explained why this does not conflict with the ruling below in siman 140 that a landlord may deposit the rent with beit din. There is a major distinction between the cases.
If initially, etc. This applies where there are witnesses that he initially trusted him, for otherwise he is not believed to say that he initially trusted him without an oath. See Shach. It appears that this applies to a borrower. A custodian, however, is believed with an oath to say that he was initially trusted, through a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable accident.
That it is effective. Two witnesses suffice to exempt the borrower. One witness obligates the lender to take a Torah oath. Although even without this testimony the lender ordinarily must take an oath resembling a Torah oath, there is a practical difference where he seized property and refuses to swear. According to many authorities cited above in siman 87, beit din does not remove the property from him in such a case. But where he is obligated to take an actual Torah oath, if he refuses to swear, the property is certainly removed from him.
For example, where he went away. This refers to a loan documented by a note, because with an oral loan the borrower is in any event exempt when he claims that he repaid. See Shach. The same applies to a deposit, where he has a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable accident, even where there is a document, as above. Nevertheless, the witness is useful to exempt a custodian from the oath taken while holding a sacred object, and in an oral loan to exempt the borrower from hesset, because a supporting witness exempts from an oath.
And obligates the lender to take a Torah oath. Although those who collect by swearing ordinarily take only a rabbinic oath, the borrower can first pay him before witnesses and afterward sue him on the basis of that one witness. This is the Gemara's case in Ketubot: if he is clever, he can bring him to an obligation of a Torah oath.
They are witnesses. This is the Rama's emendation. The Sema questioned it, but the Rama's words are correct. The Tur holds that they are believed because the borrower has a migo that he could say, 'I myself repaid,' and where the witnesses testify that they saw him repay, he is likewise believed to say that he saw the repayment. See Gidulei Terumah, Shach, and Tumim. According to this, the same applies where the borrower claims, 'You admitted to me that they repaid you'; the witnesses are likewise believed through this migo.
For he is not believed like two witnesses. But he is believed as one witness to obligate a Torah oath. The Sema and Shach ask: why should two witnesses not be believed, while one witness is believed? Wherever two witnesses obligate payment, one witness obligates an oath. See Tumim for a correct and clear resolution.
