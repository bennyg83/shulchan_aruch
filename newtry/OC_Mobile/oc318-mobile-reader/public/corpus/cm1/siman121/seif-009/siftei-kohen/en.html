But if he did not appoint him as an agent. Maharil rules concerning Reuven, who sent money with Shimon to Levi, his creditor. Shimon says he gave it to Levi through his wife, while Levi denies receipt. Maharil requires Shimon to swear that he delivered it to his wife, and holds that this is not an improper transfer by one guardian to another, because one who deposits does so with the understanding that the guardian’s wife and children may handle it. Shimon’s wife must swear that she gave it to Levi; Levi must swear that he did not receive it; and Reuven must pay Levi. In my view, however, once the wife swears that she paid Levi, Shimon is automatically discharged and need not swear separately that he gave her those specific funds. The separate oath is required in the Gemara’s case only because money is not individually identifiable and the mother did not know whether the stolen money was the deposit itself. With a specific object, only the second guardian swears. The same should apply here. Maharil further discusses a creditor who claims one hundred from a debtor. The debtor says, “I sent it to you, but I do not know through whom. If you swear that it did not reach you, I will pay.” The creditor cannot reverse the oath, because the debtor cannot swear that payment reached him. Maharil therefore requires the creditor to swear and collect. Beit Yosef questions this, holding that the creditor should collect without an oath because the debtor cannot identify the messenger. Darkei Moshe explains that even if the debtor names a messenger, the creditor collects without an oath when the messenger is absent and does not assert delivery, since the debtor cannot claim with certainty that the creditor was paid. This is also Beit Yosef’s intent and is supported by the authorities cited below in seif 10.
Carry it to so-and-so. Rambam, Semag, and the other authorities use this wording. Raavan distinguishes between “Go and pay my debt” and “Carry,” “Give,” or “Confer title.” If a debtor gives his debt to two persons before witnesses and merely says, “Go and pay so-and-so the debt I owe him,” without language of giving or acquisition, the messengers may still return it. But if he says, “Carry it,” “Give it,” or “Acquire it for him,” he cannot retract. If they then testify that they paid, they are interested witnesses, because had they said they returned it to the debtor they would be liable to the recipient. This distinction is correct. Raavan discusses the law before Rabbi Natan’s enactment. Later authorities, writing after that enactment, do not distinguish these formulations because in every case the messengers may be interested witnesses and may be required to swear. Their use of “carry” is therefore not necessarily language of immediate acquisition; it can mean, “Carry it while it remains mine until actual delivery.” A distinction remains where the debtor initially trusted them, or accompanied them and saw them perform the agency, so that no oath is required. In such a case they are competent witnesses only if he said, “Go and pay.” If he said, “Carry,” “Give,” or “Acquire,” they remain interested because the recipient had already acquired rights. Where Tur and Mechaber use “carry,” their rule must therefore be understood in a case with no witnesses to that instruction, so the messengers have a migo to deny receiving such language. Raavan, by contrast, explicitly speaks where the instruction was given before witnesses.
Or, “I delivered it to the creditor.” The Gemara states this rule regarding two messengers. Raavan limits it to two, because they may say, “We thought you selected two of us so that we ourselves would serve as witnesses, and you misled us.” Sending two messengers is unusual. A single messenger, however, cannot testify and should have understood that he must obtain witnesses before payment. Other authorities hold that even a single messenger is not ordinarily required to pay the creditor before witnesses. Tosafot, Rif, Rosh, Ran, and Maggid Mishneh support this. The special liability in the case of Avimi arose because the messenger was expressly told to obtain the debt document; by paying without witnesses and without recovering the document he acted negligently. Ordinarily, one who is told, “Give this money to so-and-so whom I owe,” need not deliver it before witnesses. Tur and Mechaber rule this way above in siman 58, and this is the primary law.
The messenger shall take a hesset oath. Although the borrower’s claim against him is uncertain, it combines with the creditor’s definite assertion that he received nothing from the messenger. The Ir Shushan and Sema write this on the basis of Ran. The same reasoning applies above, seif 8, where the borrower or depositary swears hesset to the creditor or depositor. Since the property was certainly in his possession and the messenger now denies responsibility, the parties’ claims combine. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 308, similarly explains that whenever it is known that another’s property came into a person’s possession, and that person claims it was lost, damaged, or otherwise removed from the owner, he must swear even though the owner cannot definitely contradict him. This is not because a definite debt coupled with doubtful payment is itself treated as a definite claim, for one who says, “It appears to me that you did not pay me,” cannot impose an oath, as explained above, siman 75, seif 17. Rather, the creditor’s definite denial combines with the borrower’s claim. See below, siman 126, seif katan 72. Maharshal cites a responsum concerning Reuven, who lent money to Shimon upon houses and deposited the instrument with Levi. Reuven demanded its return. Levi answered that he returned it through Reuven’s messenger, who brought a writing bearing Reuven’s signature. Reuven denied this and claimed that the writing was forged. The purported messenger answered that he had redeemed the debt from Reuven. Maharam ruled that the messenger need not swear, because neither Levi nor Reuven had a definite monetary claim against him. If Levi can recognize Reuven’s signature, Levi is exempt without an oath. If it cannot be recognized, Reuven swears that he did not receive payment and did not give that writing, and Levi must pay him. Maharshal explains that even though Reuven definitely denies having redeemed the debt, the messenger’s conduct causes Reuven no monetary loss, because Levi must in any event pay Reuven. Levi, who would suffer the loss, has only an uncertain claim against the messenger. In my view, if this is the responsum’s meaning, it is astonishing and contrary to law. The Gemara expressly rules that where a borrower sends money through a messenger, the messenger says he paid, and the creditor denies receipt, the borrower pays the creditor after the creditor’s oath, and the messenger swears to the borrower. All the authorities, including Tur and Mechaber here, rule this way. The messenger’s oath is required because the borrower’s uncertain claim combines with the creditor’s definite denial. The same should apply here. The correct reading is therefore that Levi claimed he returned the instrument through a messenger other than Shimon the borrower, or that the word “Shimon” is a scribal error. The borrower’s statement, “Far be it from me; I redeemed it from you,” then means that he paid Reuven’s messenger, who brought Reuven’s writing to Levi. On that reading, the messenger does not contradict Reuven directly. Maharshal further writes that Levi pays after Reuven’s oath only where Levi does not know with certainty whether Shimon’s assertion is true. If Levi definitely recognizes the writing and seal as Reuven’s, Levi swears hesset and is exempt. This is correct. Although Maharshal elsewhere says that one need not swear against an uncertain claim even in order to collect, here Shimon’s contradiction joins the claim, particularly according to Maharshal’s understanding that Shimon himself need not swear and therefore functions like one witness. According to my reading, in which Shimon does not contradict Reuven, the responsum necessarily follows the final opinion stated above in seif 4.
The messengers who gave the money to the creditor. Baal HaTerumot, gate 29, part 2, cites Rabbi Ephraim that the witnesses first swear that they acted as messengers, and the creditor swears when he seeks to establish that he remains unpaid. Rabbi Ephraim follows his position there, like Ri Migash, that the creditor takes only a hesset oath. The creditor must therefore first receive payment and only afterward swear hesset, because, aside from an oath reversed by the opposing party, there is no ordinary case of one taking property through a hesset oath. This is stated by Rambam, Semag, and Maggid Mishneh in the name of Ri Migash. The phrase “when the creditor seeks payment” means when he seeks to retain the payment he has received. According to our ruling, however, that the creditor first takes an oath while holding a sacred object and then collects, the creditor must swear first. The witnesses can say that perhaps he will refuse to swear and no monetary obligation against them will ever arise, whereas the creditor must swear in order to collect.
The creditor or depositor shall swear. This refers to a messenger who claims, “I delivered it to the creditor.” If he claims that the money was lost through unavoidable accident, the creditor ordinarily need not swear, since he did not appoint him as messenger. Alternatively, it applies even to a claim of accident where the messenger says that the creditor appointed him and the creditor or depositor denies it. Baal HaTerumot, gate 50, part 3, cites Raavad: where no letter was sent, there was no prior arrangement to send through that person, and a stranger tells the depositary that the owner appointed him before witnesses, but those witnesses are now overseas, if the depositary entrusted him with the property and it was lost, or the messenger claims that he paid the creditor while the creditor denies both payment and appointment, then, where the messenger claims an accident, the creditor takes a stringent oath that he never appointed him and never received the money, and collects from the depositary. The messenger must meanwhile take the Torah oath of a guardian against the borrower when he claims unavoidable accident.
While holding a sacred object. Rif in the chapter HaIsh Mekadesh is the source for requiring this form of oath. Beit Yosef cites no dissenter here, but Baal HaTerumot records that Ri Migash and Rabbi Ephraim disagreed and required only hesset; Tosafot Rid likewise disagrees with Rif. Nevertheless, Ran and Ritva there agree with Rif, as do the manuscripts of Rambam in our possession, Tur, Raavad as cited by Baal HaTerumot, and a responsum of Rosh at the end of rule 29. This is the primary ruling, and Maharshal rules accordingly.
Because he is interested in the testimony, since he must swear in order to be discharged. It has been suggested that although the messengers must in any event swear hesset to the borrower or depositary that they gave the money to the creditor or depositor, they prefer this oath because the borrower cannot contradict them. That is incorrect, since the creditor presently contradicts them and their oath is taken in his presence. Rather, the meaning is this: were we to accept them as witnesses, they would necessarily be exempt from an oath, because one who requires an oath before being believed cannot serve as a witness. If they were exempt from an oath, they would be interested witnesses, since they know that if they claimed to have returned the money to the borrower they would have to swear, whereas by testifying that the creditor received it they hope to avoid an oath. We therefore rule that they must swear; once that is established, it follows that they have no status as witnesses. Rashi and Ran similarly explain that they testify to payment because people suspected regarding money are not presumed suspected regarding an oath, and they would rather cause the creditor a monetary loss than swear falsely. Since they are not witnesses, the case follows the rule of the shopkeeper on his ledger: each party swears according to his own claim.
For he must swear in order to be discharged. Even though after taking the oath he would no longer have a financial interest, he still cannot serve as a witness. The Torah says, “By the testimony of two witnesses a matter shall be established,” implying witnesses who are believed by their statement alone. One whose statement becomes credible only after an oath has no valid testimony. Tosafot and Ran write this, and Ir Shushan agrees. Ir Shushan phrases it tentatively, perhaps because he adds that the Torah qualified witnesses through the single oath attached to testimony, namely the oath of one who hears an adjuration and fails to testify, but not where two oaths would intervene between observing the event and testifying, as in this case.
Even if he releases them. Mordechi gives one answer according to which a release is effective. There is no contradiction between Tur and Mechaber here, who hold that a release does not qualify them, and their rulings above, siman 37, seif 12, and below, siman 140, seif 9, that a landlord may return the rent to the tenant and thereby qualify him as a witness. There the landlord restores the tenant’s own money, which the tenant may use as he wishes. Here, by contrast, the messenger immediately became obligated to swear to the borrower. If the borrower releases him despite not knowing whether his statement is true, we are concerned that the benefit of the release, and the confidence thereby shown in him, may induce him to testify favorably. Bach gives another strained explanation; what I have written appears correct.
“And if you do not know what you are doing, then you will be able to do it, and you will not be able to do it, and you will be able to do it, and you will not be able to do it.”
"There is someone who says Demney in B'A witnesses to dismiss him from a crowd and says, 'This is a week', and it seems that there are witnesses who believed earlier in the borrower and they are not loyal to each other."
And he stood at a distance. This rule must refer specifically to a loan evidenced by a document. Otherwise, even without any witness, the borrower would take a hesset oath and be exempt, since he asserts payment with certainty.
And he subjects the creditor to a Torah oath. Although one witness does not ordinarily compel a Torah oath in order to collect, the borrower can bring about such an oath by paying the creditor in the presence of that witness and one additional witness. He may then reclaim the first payment on the testimony of the original witness, and the creditor will have to take a Torah oath to be exempt. This is stated in the chapter HaKotev and explained in Even HaEzer, siman 92, seif 13. Sema writes likewise.
They are witnesses, and the borrower is exempt. These words were missing from Mechaber and Rama supplied them, as they appear in Tur. Rama then writes, “It appears to me that he is not believed even in this,” meaning to amend Mechaber’s ruling that the borrower can render them witnesses. Beit Yosef asks: where there is one messenger, it is understandable that the borrower may be believed so as to impose an oath upon the creditor, since he does not thereby extract money; but how can he be believed to remove the creditor’s money from the borrower’s possession? In truth, if this objection were decisive, the borrower should not be believed even regarding one witness, because an oath can lead to payment. Sema answers that Tur speaks where, before any dispute arose, the borrower had already said, “I saw the messengers fulfill their mission.” This resembles the preceding rule that if he released them from an oath in advance, they are believed and we do not suspect collusion. Sema therefore proposes only a minor textual correction. In my view, this is strained. A release given before dispatch prevents any oath from attaching at all; a statement made only after they performed the mission would still be made in order to free them from the oath that had already attached. Rather, the rule is straightforward. Since the borrower personally saw them fulfill the mission, it is as though he himself paid the creditor. Their status as his messengers is irrelevant to the substance of their testimony. Had the borrower claimed payment and they merely testified that they saw him pay, their testimony would suffice. It is no worse when they say that payment was made through them. They also possess a migo that they could have omitted any mention of agency and simply testified that they saw payment, although this migo is unnecessary. Once the borrower himself saw the payment, their agency is not what establishes his claim. Only where he knows payment solely from their report does he require their agency, making them interested witnesses. This is also supported by Rabbenu Chananel’s interpretation cited below, siman 140, and accepted by the authorities. Bach likewise upholds Tur and Baal HaTerumot, though by a different and strained route. Ir Shushan prefers Rama and apparently applies the same view even to a single messenger, requiring only a lesser oath. Perhaps this was also Beit Yosef’s intention. In my view, however, the position of Baal HaTerumot and Tur is more persuasive. Still, the proof brought from the Jerusalem Talmud, where Rabbi Yirmiyah made the porter-messenger a witness, is inconclusive. That passage may precede Rabbi Natan’s enactment, when a messenger could serve as a witness. Baal HaTerumot’s proof must instead be from Rif’s citation of that passage. Yet Ran explains that Rif cited it for a case where the sender released the messenger from an oath beforehand. Rif may also have cited it to teach that even a hired messenger, like a porter, can serve as witness, a point relevant to marriage law. Thus the Jerusalem Talmud itself yields no proof here.
