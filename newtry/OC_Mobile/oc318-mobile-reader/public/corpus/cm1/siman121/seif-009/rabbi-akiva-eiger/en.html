[Shach, in the continuation] They have the same law. Note: It appears that his intent is based on the statement that a paid custodian acquires the double payment because he could have exempted himself by claiming that the item broke or died. We might ask that perhaps he did not wish to exempt himself with a claim of unavoidable accident because such a claim is uncommon. It must therefore be that theft is likewise uncommon, for by claiming theft he demonstrates that he is not concerned to make an uncommon claim, and for that reason he acquires the double payment. In my view, however, his words require great study. Even if we say that theft is also uncommon, the question remains why he acquires the double payment. Perhaps he did not wish to exempt himself by an uncommon claim because everyone would suspect him of wrongfully withholding another's money, whereas now that he pays, why should he hesitate to make an uncommon claim? It appears obvious to me that even if he actually claims that the animal died through its work and nevertheless pays, he does not acquire the double payment, because he did not wish to exempt himself by an uncommon claim. We must therefore say simply that the Sages assessed his intent as follows: with a wholly uncommon claim, such as death through work, he does not wish to exempt himself; with a claim that is only somewhat uncommon, such as unavoidable accident, he does not hesitate to make the claim. If so, I concede the Shach's proof. Examine this carefully. See Urim VeTumim, siman 108, seif katan 5, and Responsa Shev Yaakov, siman 27.
