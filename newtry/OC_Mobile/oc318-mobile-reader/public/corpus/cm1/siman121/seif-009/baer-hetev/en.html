And the lender. This entire gloss was missing from the Mechaber's text and the Rama supplied and corrected it in his glosses. The same law appears in the Tur, seif 12. Sema.
The agent. The agent takes a shevuat heset. Although the debtor's claim against him is uncertain, the lender's definite assertion that he received nothing from the agent joins with it and requires the agent at least to take a shevuat heset. Sema. See the Shach.
Received them. This refers to a case where the agent claims, "I delivered them to the lender." If he claims that the money was lost by unavoidable accident, the lender need not swear at all because the lender did not appoint him. Alternatively, it may apply even to an unavoidable-loss claim where the agent says the lender appointed him and the lender or depositor denies it. Baal HaTerumot in the name of the Raavad states accordingly. The agent must take a Torah oath against the debtor when claiming unavoidable loss. Shach.
To swear. Even though after taking the oath the agent would no longer have a monetary interest, he still cannot then serve as a witness. Once he required an oath before being believed, he is not treated as a witness. The Torah says that a matter is established "by the testimony of two witnesses," meaning persons believed through their words alone. One whose statement first requires an oath has no evidentiary force as testimony. Tosafot write this, and the Ran similarly, as cited by the Beit Yosef. This does not contradict siman 37, seif 12, or siman 140, seif 9, where a landlord can return rent to a tenant and thereby become a witness. There the landlord restores the tenant's own money for the tenant to use as he wishes. Here, however, the agent already owes an oath to the debtor, and when the debtor releases him from that oath without knowing whether the agent is telling the truth, we fear that the benefit of being trusted and released will influence his testimony. Shach.
That it helps. Two witnesses can exempt him from monetary liability, and one witness can obligate him in a Torah oath. The Shach writes that this applies specifically where witnesses establish that the principal exempted them from an oath beforehand. The debtor and the agents themselves are not believed to establish such an exemption because they remain interested parties. The Sema writes that we do not suspect collusion merely because of the exemption, since witnesses are not generally suspected of that. Once they were released from oath beforehand, they are like ordinary witnesses.
From afar. This rule necessarily concerns a documented loan. Otherwise, even without any witness the debtor could take a shevuat heset and be exempt because his claim is definite. Shach.
And obligates. Although one witness does not impose a Torah oath upon a claimant seeking to collect, as stated in simanim 84 and 108, the witness can create a situation that ultimately requires a Torah oath. The debtor may repay before this witness and one additional witness, and then sue the lender for the first payment that had been made before the original single witness. The lender will then need to take a Torah oath to be exempt, as stated in Even HaEzer 96, seif 13, and in the Sema there.
