His seal as a sign. See Beurim: the Shach holds that if the lender writes, "Send it through so and so" in his own handwriting, or even another person writes it and the lender signs his name, or if he is among the great sages and uses his recognized personal symbol, such as a fish or vessel, this is effective even without witnesses. But if another person writes it and he does not sign his name, but merely places an informal symbol and he is not a widely recognized sage for whom that symbol functions as a complete signature, the symbol is insufficient. Likewise, if he gave only a portrait or identifying image and the lender admits that he appointed the messenger to bring the money and gave him that identifying item, it is effective even without witnesses. But if the lender denies giving the identifying item, then even if the writing upon it says to send through the messenger and the messenger says it is the lender's handwriting, while the lender denies the handwriting and claims the item was found or stolen, the lender is believed in those claims, though he must swear or accept a ban. By contrast, where there is genuine handwriting or a complete signature, even if the text itself was written by a scribe, he is not believed to say it was stolen or found. This is the primary law.
