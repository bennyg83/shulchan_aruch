If Levi comes as the agent of Reuven, the lender, to Shimon, who owes Reuven fifty gold coins, and similar cases, the governing distinctions are as the Shach sets out. If Reuven sent Levi to borrow money from Shimon, but Reuven says, "I sent him to borrow only twenty," while Shimon says that Levi came as Reuven's agent to borrow fifty, then Shimon has against Reuven only a general cherem that Reuven did not send him to borrow more than twenty and did not receive more than twenty. If Shimon makes a definite claim that Reuven sent him to borrow fifty, Reuven takes a Torah oath against Shimon and is exempt, while the agent takes a rabbinic oath to Shimon that he gave fifty to Reuven. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 11, questions this. Why should Reuven be treated as one who partially admits a definite monetary claim? Even if Reuven admitted Shimon's assertion that he authorized a loan of fifty, Reuven would still not necessarily owe payment, because perhaps Shimon actually gave the agent only twenty. Although Shimon might be entitled to collect upon an oath under the rule of the shopkeeper on his ledger, where even the defendant's admission to the plaintiff's allegation does not permit collection without an oath, there is ordinarily no law of partial admission, as the authorities explain in siman 75. Beit Ephraim answers that the Shach follows his own position in siman 91, seif katan 26. There the Shach explains that the Raah and Ran do not disagree with Baal HaTerumot and the other authorities. The former speak where the workers deny receiving payment, while the latter speak where the workers admit receiving payment but the employer says he never ordered the shopkeeper to pay them. In that latter case, if the employer admitted giving the order, the shopkeeper would collect without an oath. So too here, the agent himself says that he gave Reuven fifty. Were Reuven to admit authorizing the fifty, Shimon would collect without an oath, because we rely on the agent's statement that he gave Reuven what Reuven authorized, and Shimon need not care whether the agent actually passed the money on to Reuven. Thus Reuven's denial that he authorized the full amount creates the law of partial admission. Beit Ephraim nevertheless questions the Shach's further ruling that the agent must take a rabbinic oath to Shimon that he gave Reuven fifty. Why should the agent swear when Shimon cannot personally claim with certainty that the agent failed to deliver the money to Reuven? If Shimon relies on Reuven's assertion that he received only twenty, then by Shimon's own account Reuven is denying the truth of Shimon's definite assertion that he authorized fifty, and Reuven is therefore discredited by Shimon's own position. How, then, can Shimon use Reuven's statement to convert his uncertainty into a definite claim against the agent and impose an oath? This requires study. See there further, and above, siman 91, seif katan 1.
