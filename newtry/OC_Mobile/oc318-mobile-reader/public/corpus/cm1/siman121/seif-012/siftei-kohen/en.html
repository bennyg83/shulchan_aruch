Levi who came as a messenger. Because several later authorities have written points here that appear legally unsound, and many new rulings follow from the matter, I must explain at length. Rambam rules: Levi came as Reuven’s messenger and took fifty from Shimon. Reuven then said, “I sent him to take only twenty, and he brought me only twenty.” Reuven swears that he authorized only twenty and received only twenty, like one who admits part of a claim. Levi swears hesset that he gave Reuven all fifty received from Shimon, and Shimon must pay Reuven from his own funds if he was indebted to him. Semag uses the same wording. Tur objects that an admission of part normally entails an oath to avoid payment, whereas Rambam appears to let Reuven swear and collect. Since the creditor appointed the messenger and the borrower says he paid the entire amount while the creditor admits receiving half, Tur would have the borrower swear and be exempt. Beit Yosef explains Rambam as addressing two cases. If Reuven sent Levi to borrow money from Shimon, Shimon says fifty was advanced, and Reuven admits only twenty, Reuven takes a Torah oath as one who admits part and is exempt from the balance. But if Levi was sent to collect Reuven’s debt, Shimon must pay again because Reuven says with certainty that anything beyond twenty was outside the agency and never reached him, while Shimon is uncertain whether Levi was authorized for the whole debt. Reuven then swears that he received only twenty and collects the balance. This explanation is difficult. An oath of partial admission cannot be imposed upon an uncertain claim. If Shimon claims with certainty that Reuven authorized fifty, then in the collection case Shimon too should swear and be exempt. It is strained to make the first clause certain and the second uncertain. One might say that Shimon makes no direct claim against Reuven and only Levi claims that he delivered fifty, so Levi is a proper litigant because he personally transferred the money. Yet Rambam’s plain language treats the entire passage as one case, namely Levi’s collection from a debtor who already owed Reuven. Baal HaTerumot also understands Rambam this way. Bach explains that Levi was never established as Reuven’s messenger by witnesses or written notice. Shimon merely relied on Levi’s own assertion. Levi therefore legally became Shimon’s messenger, not Reuven’s. Reuven swears and collects from Shimon, while Levi sues Reuven for the fifty he claims to have delivered; Reuven admits twenty and takes the oath of partial admission. Bach says Tur assumed the agency was established, in which case Shimon paid with authorization and should be exempt. This explanation is also incorrect. Once Reuven has sworn to Shimon that he authorized and received only twenty and has collected the remaining thirty, Levi has no further monetary claim against him. Levi suffers no loss, since he swears and is exempt from Shimon. Moreover, the twenty admitted by Reuven were received on account of the debt, not held for Levi; Levi’s only possible claim is for the other thirty, which Reuven totally denies. The case therefore is not one of partial admission. The correct reading is that Rambam applies even where Reuven admits appointing Levi, or witnesses establish the agency, but the exact amount authorized is uncertain. If Reuven simply said, “Collect the money owed me,” the entire debt would be included. The case is where witnesses remember that a specific amount was stated but do not remember how much. Reuven swears while holding a sacred object and collects another thirty from Shimon. Levi swears hesset that he delivered all fifty and is exempt. Once Levi has sworn, he has no further claim against Reuven. Maharshal likewise rules that where the debtor cannot definitely contradict the creditor, the creditor swears and collects, for perhaps the creditor authorized only twenty and received no more. He asks why Tur exempted the debtor, since the debtor could not know the scope of the agency. Tur can be understood as a case where Shimon definitely knows Levi was authorized for all fifty. If Shimon is uncertain, Tur too agrees that Reuven swears and collects, not because of partial admission, but because a creditor who seeks collection while contradicted by the messenger must swear. Rambam’s comparison to one who admits part is therefore not literal as to the source of the oath. It teaches that this oath has the procedural force of a Torah oath, even though the creditor collects after swearing. The ordinary distinctions between Torah and rabbinic oaths concern reversal, seizure of assets, and the opposing party’s suspicion. Here reversal is impossible because Shimon’s claim is uncertain; if Reuven refuses to swear, he cannot collect; and if Reuven is suspected regarding oaths, the ordinary rule for one who swears and collects would deny recovery. Rambam teaches that this oath is treated like the oath of partial admission. According to our law, however, where one who would ordinarily collect after a rabbinic oath is disqualified from swearing, he may collect without an oath. This applies all the more here. The debt is definite and payment uncertain, and Reuven would collect by law without any oath were it not for the messenger’s contradiction. Accordingly, if Reuven is suspected, he collects without an oath. Likewise, if he seized the money and refuses to swear, it is not removed from him; he is merely placed under the sanction applicable to one who refuses a rabbinic oath. The resulting law is as follows. Levi came as Reuven’s messenger to collect fifty from Shimon. Reuven says he authorized and received only twenty; Levi says he delivered all fifty. Whether Reuven admits the agency or it is proved by witnesses, if Shimon cannot definitely contradict Reuven, Reuven swears while holding a sacred object and collects another thirty. Levi swears hesset and is exempt. If Shimon definitely asserts that Reuven authorized all fifty, Shimon swears and is exempt where the loan was oral. If Reuven sent Levi to borrow from Shimon and Reuven admits only twenty while Shimon is uncertain, Reuven need only accept a general ban and Levi swears hesset. If Shimon definitely knows that fifty was authorized, Reuven takes the Torah oath of partial admission and is exempt, while Levi swears hesset to Shimon. If Levi dies or goes overseas after having contradicted Reuven in beit din, but no longer presses a claim, Shimon’s claim remains uncertain and Reuven is exempt after accepting a general ban. A responsum of Mabit rules that where Reuven instructed Shimon to deliver funds to Levi, Levi forwarded them, and Reuven later says a portion is missing, Shimon is exempt because Levi does not contradict him. Mabit makes Levi liable because he cannot identify the person through whom he sent the funds. In my view, if Levi does not know whether he ever received more than Reuven admits, this is an uncertainty whether he became liable at all, and he is exempt. Even if he knows he received more, there is room to exempt him where he had permission to send through any trustworthy person and definitely asserts that he sent everything through one whom he regarded as trustworthy. This differs from an ordinary deposit, which remains in the guardian’s responsibility until returned and requires him to know where he placed it.
The conclusion is as follows. Where Levi came as Reuven the creditor’s messenger to Shimon, who owed fifty, and took fifty, but Reuven says, “I sent him to collect only twenty and he brought me only twenty,” while Levi contradicts him, then whether Reuven admits the agency or it is established by witnesses, provided the witnesses do not remember the amount authorized, Reuven swears while holding a sacred object and collects another thirty from Shimon if Shimon cannot definitely contradict him. Levi takes a hesset oath that he gave Reuven all fifty and is exempt. Levi thereafter has no claim against Reuven. Shimon cannot improve matters by first paying and arranging for Levi to sue Reuven, for Reuven may accept only the unpaid thirty, or tender back the admitted twenty, leaving himself a total denier. If Reuven is disqualified from swearing, he collects without an oath. If he already collected and refuses the oath, the money is not removed from him, because the debt was definite and payment doubtful; he is placed under the sanction applicable to one who refuses a rabbinic oath. If Shimon definitely knows that Reuven authorized Levi to collect all fifty, Shimon swears and is exempt where the loan was oral. If Reuven sent Levi to borrow from Shimon, Reuven admits only twenty, and Shimon says fifty was borrowed but cannot definitely prove Reuven’s authorization, Reuven accepts a general ban that he authorized and received only twenty, and Levi swears hesset. Shimon may instead have Levi sue Reuven, in which case Reuven takes the Torah oath of partial admission. If Levi dies or goes overseas, even after contradicting Reuven in beit din, Shimon has only an uncertain claim and Reuven is exempt after accepting a general ban. If Shimon definitely knows that Reuven authorized a loan of fifty, Reuven takes the Torah oath of partial admission and is exempt, and Levi swears hesset that he delivered fifty. Mabit discusses Reuven, who had money with Shimon and ordered him to give it to Levi. Levi forwarded it, and Reuven later claimed that part was missing. Shimon said he gave everything to Levi; Levi said he did not know the amount but forwarded everything he received and did not remember through whom. Mabit exempts Shimon even from an oath, because Levi does not contradict him, requiring only a general ban, but holds Levi liable because he cannot identify his sub-agent and is therefore negligent. In my view, Levi is exempt where he does not know whether he ever received more than the amount reaching Reuven, for this is uncertainty whether liability ever arose. Even if he knows he received more, there is room to exempt him where he was authorized to send through any trustworthy person and asserts with certainty that he forwarded everything through someone he regarded as trustworthy. Unlike an ordinary deposit, the property left his responsibility when he properly exercised the authority granted him. The matter nevertheless requires study.
