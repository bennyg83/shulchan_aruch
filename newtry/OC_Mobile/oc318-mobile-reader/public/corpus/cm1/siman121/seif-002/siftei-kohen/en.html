Go and tell him that you intend to come here. Maharshal distinguishes as follows. If the creditor said, “If he gives the money to you, bring it to me,” this too merely places a messenger in the debtor’s path and does not appoint him as an agent. Rashi and Ran explain that an agent appointed before witnesses means that the creditor formally appointed him before witnesses to receive the money. Thus, if he says, “Receive it from so-and-so,” meaning, “Tell him to send it through you,” he becomes an agent. But if he says only, “Go tell him that you intend to come here, and if he gives it to you bring it to me,” he is not an agent. Indeed, Rashi and Ran expressly define one who merely provides a possible messenger as a person asked to present himself to the debtor and say, “If you wish to send the money to the creditor, send it through me.” Their words imply that even saying, “Tell him to send the money through you,” does not create an agency unless the creditor first says before witnesses, “I hereby appoint you as my agent to receive my money from so-and-so.” Ramban in Milchamot also appears to hold this way, although Maharshal seems to agree with the Rosh and Tur on this point.
Rather, he said to him, “I have money belonging to the creditor.” Even if he said this before witnesses, it does not discharge the debtor. The Gemara’s reasoning is that the creditor merely said, in effect, “He is trustworthy. Rely on him if you choose; send through him if you wish.” Tosafot ask why the debtor should be liable for an unavoidable loss on the road, since the creditor cannot say, “I do not want my deposit in another’s hands,” after identifying the messenger as trustworthy. They answer that even if the debtor himself carried the money and it was lost on that route, he would be liable, because carrying it through a dangerous place is negligence. He must guard it until he returns it in a settled place. According to that answer, even where the creditor has merely provided a messenger before witnesses, the debtor is liable only if the money was carried by a dangerous route. The other authorities, however, state without qualification that the debtor is liable. The correct explanation is that the creditor merely described the person as trustworthy but did not appoint him as agent or assume responsibility for him. This differs from a depositor who regularly entrusts property to that person, where the depositor has demonstrated actual reliance. It is possible that Tosafot agree that where the creditor merely provides a messenger, the debtor remains liable in every case.
