By whomever you choose. Anyone who sees this should wonder how this differs from Rama in seif 11. There, if the owner said merely "pawn it" and the agent pawned it to someone who later denies receiving it, the agent must pay, because the owner did not specify a particular person and the agent should have pawned it before witnesses. Here too, since the creditor said "send it by whomever you choose" and did not name a particular person, why is the debtor exempt if that person later denies receipt? It seems to me that there is a distinction. Here as well, if the creditor merely said "send it," the debtor would certainly remain liable. But when he says "send it by whomever you choose," the words are equivalent to saying "send it by such and such person." Thus there too, only if he said merely "pawn it" is the agent liable, but if he said "pawn it with whomever you choose," he is exempt. It also seems to me that Shach himself questions the rule there in seif katan 61. One may further ask against Ran's reason there, that an unavoidable accident is uncommon. Even if the pledge is delivered before witnesses and the pledgee is warned not to return it except before witnesses, he is still believed to say "I returned it" by a migo that it was lost through unavoidable accident, as explained in siman 133. It is difficult to say that once warned, a return without witnesses also becomes uncommon, for we advance such a claim for heirs through a migo of unavoidable accident. Rather, the principal reason is that pawning without witnesses constitutes negligence because, even if the item is later seen before witnesses so that the pledgee cannot claim return or unavoidable loss, he can still claim that he purchased it or holds it for the amount stated according to the relevant view. Had the item been pawned before witnesses, the owner could recover it upon proof. Therefore pawning without witnesses is negligence. See Sema in siman 72, who for this reason does not treat "I returned it" as a migo where the item could be seen. Since the initial act was negligent in that it was pawned without witnesses, even if the eventual denial occurs in a case where the item is not seen, it is at least a case of initial negligence followed by unavoidable loss that resulted from the negligence, because had he pawned it before witnesses the pledgee could not have denied it. Accordingly there is a distinction between a specific object and money. With money, where the doctrine of "seen" does not apply, lack of witnesses is not negligence with respect to a later denial.
