The messenger swears, etc., and the borrower or depositary swears, etc. The messenger and the borrower swear only where both are before us and contradict one another. Otherwise there is merely a general cherem, as stated below in seif 10.
To the borrower or depositary in the presence of the borrower or depositary. The wording is awkward, for if the messenger swears to the borrower or depositary it is obvious that the oath is in that person’s presence. The Ir Shushan therefore emends it to say that the messenger swears to the borrower or depositary in the presence of the creditor or depositor. The wording of the Raavad cited by the Baal HaTerumot, which is the source of this law, is better: he swears to the depositor in the presence of the depositary. This parallels the rule in Shevuot that workers swear to the employer in the presence of the storekeeper so that they will be embarrassed to lie before him.
And the borrower or depositary, etc. The same law applies even to a loan evidenced by a document. The authorities and the Tur state the rule without qualification, implying that it applies in every case. One cannot say that in a documented loan, because the messenger contradicts the borrower, the creditor should swear and collect. In the case of a storekeeper on his ledger, the storekeeper swears and collects from the employer even though the workers contradict him, because the employer authorized him to pay. All the more so here, the borrower is exempt because the creditor authorized him to pay the messenger.
An oath of heset, etc. Even though the creditor’s claim against the borrower is only uncertain, the reason is as explained below in seif katan 44. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 308, discusses Reuven who sent money through Shimon to Levi to purchase something. Shimon says he delivered it, while Levi denies receiving it. Shimon must take an oath of heset, while Levi is exempt as against Reuven because Reuven has only an uncertain claim. If Shimon wishes to sue Levi, Levi must take an oath of heset and cannot say, “You are not my litigant, since the money was not yours.” Before Shimon swears to Reuven he is certainly Levi’s litigant, because Levi’s admission could free Shimon from his oath; even after Shimon has sworn, since Shimon says that the money left his hand and was delivered to Levi, he remains a proper litigant. It follows that even if Reuven exempted Shimon from an oath from the outset, Shimon may still require Levi to swear, because he claims that he personally delivered the money to him. Terumat HaDeshen further writes that Reuven cannot compel Shimon to sue Levi, either before or after Shimon’s oath, unless Reuven fully releases Shimon from his own oath, because otherwise one of the two will certainly swear falsely. This last point does not appear correct to me. Reuven can say to Shimon: if you know your claim is true, swear; if Levi then swears falsely, that is Levi’s wrongdoing. A practical difference arises if Shimon dies or leaves the country. Even if he had previously contradicted Levi in beit din, once he is no longer suing Levi and Reuven has only an uncertain claim, Levi merely accepts a general cherem and is exempt.
