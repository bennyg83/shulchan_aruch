And if he gives it to you. The Shach concludes that this language merely identifies a possible messenger and does not actually appoint an agent. If one agent says, "Tell so and so that I sent you to tell him to send the money through you," the Sema holds that even without witnesses, if the lender admits the instruction, the messenger becomes a valid agent and the borrower is exempt if the messenger later denies receipt or suffers an unavoidable loss. The Shach rules that even here he is not a valid agent until appointed before witnesses. See Beurim: the primary practical law is that for an instruction, "Send through so and so," witnesses are unnecessary. In that case, even if the messenger is a gentile, deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, the borrower is exempt. But where the lender appoints an agent specifically to receive payment, the recipient must be legally capable of agency and must be appointed before witnesses. If the lender tells a second person, "If he gives it to you, bring it to me," the primary law follows Maharshal that this merely identifies a possible messenger and does not constitute an agency even if said before witnesses.
