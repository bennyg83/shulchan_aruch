If the agent denies, etc. The Shach in seif katan 42 cites the Terumat HaDeshen concerning Reuven, who sent money through Shimon to Levi to purchase merchandise. Shimon says that he delivered it, while Levi says that he never received it. Shimon must swear, because Levi's assertion makes the claim against Shimon definite and Shimon admits having received the money. Reuven, however, cannot require Levi to swear, even though Shimon says that he delivered the money to him, because Levi says that he received nothing at all. Shimon himself can sue Levi and require him to swear, since Shimon asserts definitively that he gave it to him. Nevertheless, Reuven cannot compel Shimon to make Levi swear unless Reuven releases Shimon from his own oath, because one of them would then certainly have taken a false oath. The Shach objects that Reuven can say to Shimon, "What concern is it of yours? According to your account you will swear truthfully, so why should you care whether Levi swears falsely or truthfully?" In truth, the Terumat HaDeshen chose his wording carefully. Shimon has a legitimate concern because one of them would certainly swear falsely, while observers cannot know which one swore truthfully and which falsely. Shimon would therefore remain under public suspicion as possibly having taken a false oath. What concern could be greater than that? Why should he place himself under such suspicion? At first glance, however, I find a difficulty. The entire purpose of imposing an oath is that we assume Jews are not suspected of swearing falsely, so a person faced with the oath will instead admit and pay. Reuven can therefore argue to Shimon: since you know that Levi received the money, impose an oath upon him, and when he reaches the point of swearing he will certainly admit and pay, for that is the very purpose of imposing an oath. There will then be no false oath at all. The true reason why Reuven must release Shimon from his oath is that Shimon can say: either Levi will admit, in which case I am exempt, or he will swear, in which case how can you then compel me also to swear when there is necessarily one false oath and the concern and stigma described above? According to this, if Shimon already swore, he can thereafter demand that an oath be imposed upon Levi, because Levi will certainly admit rather than swear falsely. Shimon is indeed required to sue him in beit din, but if Shimon sees that Levi is actually about to swear, he may waive Levi's oath and Reuven cannot prevent him, because Shimon can say that if Levi swears there will be the very stigma described above. This is precise. As for the Terumat HaDeshen's statement that Levi cannot be made to swear on the basis of Shimon's assertion because Shimon is an interested party, this requires study. Once Shimon has already sworn that he delivered the money to Levi, why can Reuven not require Levi to take a hesset oath? We rule above in siman 87 that one may impose a hesset oath where he says, "I was told so," even when the report came from a relative or otherwise disqualified witness. The Terumat HaDeshen sensed this difficulty and wrote that this applies only to other forms of disqualification, whereas one disqualified because of personal interest is different. I do not understand this. After Shimon has sworn there is no longer any monetary interest, as Tosafot ask, for he had a migo that he could have denied everything. If one answers that since he has now sworn, the Torah's decree is that a witness who must swear is not qualified as a witness, then his disqualification is no longer due to monetary interest but to a divine decree. He should therefore be like other disqualified witnesses, such as relatives, whose report can support a hesset oath. Why should his report not suffice to require an oath? The Rashba, in Kiddushin chapter 2, discusses a borrower who sent payment to the lender through agents. The agents say they delivered it, while the lender says he did not receive it. The Rashba rules that the lender must swear, and asks that the borrower has no definite claim because he does not know whether the lender received it, while the witnesses are interested in their testimony. He answers that since the witnesses assert the matter definitely and there is a presumption that an agent performs his agency, the claim is treated as definite. Alternatively, the Sages were more stringent with one who collects money and required an oath. According to the first answer, the agents' assertion is treated as a fully definite claim because of the presumption that an agent performs his agency. Even according to the second answer, that may be because we do not find that the Rashba elsewhere rules that one may impose an oath on the basis of "I was told," even from relatives or disqualified witnesses. But according to our ruling that such a report can support an oath, it appears clear as law that Reuven has a claim requiring Levi to swear. This is precise.
