He takes his two portions together. The Tur explains that where there are two brothers and one is the firstborn, their father's estate is divided into three shares. The middle share belongs to the firstborn in all events. They cast lots only for the two outer shares, and whichever one falls to the firstborn is taken together with the middle share, so that his two portions are adjacent. This is derived from the verse "a double portion," implying that the two shares come as one unit. According to the Rambam, even if the middle portion is better than one of the outer portions, the firstborn nevertheless receives it together with one outer portion. Otherwise, why would Scripture need to teach "a double portion"? According to the Rambam, even without the verse we ordinarily compel against Sodom-like conduct and would give him adjacent equal shares. The verse must therefore add the case where they are not equal. Conversely, regarding a yavam, where the law concludes that his shares need not be contiguous, the Rambam must be speaking where the middle share is better. If the shares were equal, even there they would be made contiguous under the rule against Sodom-like conduct. Rama then cites those who disagree, namely the Rosh and those with him, who hold that without the verse we would not compel contiguous division even where the shares are equal, because the other party has a legitimate lottery right. According to them, even a yavam with equal shares does not automatically receive adjacent portions.
