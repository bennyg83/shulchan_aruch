(Seif 4: he cannot compel his fellow.) The wording implies that this is so even where the portions are equal. This is surprising, because the Mechaber rules like Rambam, who holds that with equal portions, even one who is not a firstborn may compel the other under the rule against Sodom-like conduct. Our master cites this law in the name of the Rosh, who follows the "some say" in seif 1, unlike the Mechaber, who follows Rambam. The Maggid Mishneh also cites this law and writes that its rule is like that of a levir, whereas concerning two who purchased together he writes that their law is like that of a firstborn. This is consistent, because he is hinting that the portions are unequal, as in the law of the firstborn, who has an enhanced right even according to Rambam. The same must be true in the later case of three who purchased in partnership, where one cannot compel the others because the portions are unequal. The Mechaber's unqualified wording is therefore difficult. The Tur writes, "but if one field is near," etc. The Beit Yosef wonders why he says that one is near a river when the Gemara speaks of two fields on two irrigation channels. It appears that our master found the Gemara difficult. If the two channels are equal, then just as one field is subject to uncertainty, so is the other. What advantage does one have over the other? He therefore explains that one channel extends from the river to the first field, and from that channel another channel extends to the second field. The first field is thus described as nearer to the river by virtue of its channel. The meaning is that only the field adjacent to the drawn channel may at times fail to receive water, whereas the one directly adjacent to the river certainly will not.
