If the borrower wrote him a clause for subsequently acquired property. See the Maggid Mishneh at the beginning of chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh. He writes that requiring the borrower to write such a clause to the lender is specifically according to the opinion that its omission is not treated as a scribal error. The Mechaber ruled at the beginning of siman 112 that its omission is not presumed to be scribal error, and therefore likewise writes here simply, "if he wrote him a clause for subsequently acquired property." Even according to that approach, however, the clause is needed only from borrower to lender. The purchaser who improved the field retains half the appreciation even if the seller did not write him such a clause. I explained the reason below, and in the Derishah I brought proof from the Rif, Rosh, Nimukei Yosef, and Tur. The same is evident from Darkei Moshe and from Rama's first gloss in this seif, where he concludes that if the seller expressly wrote to the purchaser that the purchaser would not collect from subsequently acquired property, then the purchaser has no right in the appreciation at all. This proves that only an express exclusion defeats him. Ordinarily the purchaser has half the appreciation even without such a clause. For this reason Rama states the rule specifically regarding the purchaser and not the creditor. The Mechaber had already said concerning the creditor that collection of appreciation depends upon writing the clause, so without it he does not collect. The Rosh's statement in the first chapter of Bava Metzia that if the seller did not write the purchaser a clause for subsequently acquired property the lender collects all the appreciation means that the seller expressly wrote that the purchaser would not acquire the appreciation, as Rama writes here. This is clear. See the Derishah.

He seizes half the appreciation. The Tur explains that this appreciation is treated as though the seller acquired it and then sold it to the purchaser after selling him the field. The purchaser therefore also has a warranty claim against the seller regarding the appreciation. The situation resembles one who borrowed from one lender, then from another, and afterward acquired property and sold it, where the first and second lenders divide the property if both received clauses for subsequently acquired property, since neither lien attached until after both loans and they attach simultaneously. The wording is slightly adjusted here for clarity, but this is also the Rosh's explanation in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. The meaning is that the appreciation arose after the purchaser acquired the field. At that point the seller is regarded as a debtor both to the original lender and to the purchaser for the purchase price. Because of the concern that lenders not be discouraged, the Sages treated the purchaser's improvements as though the purchaser had transferred them to the seller and the seller then transferred them back to the purchaser. The lender's lien for his loan should certainly not be stronger than the purchaser's lien for the principal price paid for the field. Although the policy concern of keeping credit available does not apply to the purchaser, who entered the transaction for his own benefit, the purchaser's warranty claim is also against the borrower. The enactment therefore need only remove half of the appreciation from the purchaser, not all of it. In this respect the purchaser is stronger than the lender. Even without a clause for subsequently acquired property, he retains half the appreciation already in his hands. Such a clause is required for the lender only because the seller is treated as having acquired the appreciation and then resold it to the purchaser, and the lender cannot remove from the purchaser what the seller acquired only after the lender's loan and then sold without such a clause. But the purchaser is already in possession of the field and the appreciation arose while it was in his hands. His position is no worse than if the seller had not transferred it and the appreciation still remained with the seller, in which case he would share in it with the creditors even without a clause for subsequently acquired property. Nor should one argue that if the appreciation is treated as though the borrower acquired it from the purchaser, the situation is one where the borrower borrowed from one lender, then another, acquired property afterward, and wrote the first lender a clause for subsequently acquired property but not the second, in which case the first lender alone acquires it. Here the fictional acquisition of the appreciation from the purchaser and its resale to him occur at the same moment after the appreciation already arose in the purchaser's hands. For this reason no authority or the Mechaber states that the purchaser needs such a clause. From the purchaser's side, his position is no worse than that of a sole lender where the borrower later acquired property that still remains with him. See the Perishah, where I brought proof. The dispute cited by the Mechaber and Rama as to whether the creditor must reimburse the purchaser's expenses is likewise explained by the Rosh. Because of the policy against discouraging lenders, the Sages strengthened the creditor's lien so that he suffers no loss from what the borrower sold, treating the field as though it had remained in the borrower's hands and the borrower himself had labored and improved it. Therefore, according to this view, the creditor need not reimburse even the expenses. Although a person recovering property from a robber must reimburse the robber's expenses, creditors received a special enactment because of the concern for available credit. In addition, the purchaser entered into property already subject to the creditor's lien, and from the outset he relied upon recourse against the seller. The seller wrote him a warranty covering the principal, labor, and appreciation. Principal refers to the purchase price, labor to the appreciation caused by expenditure, and appreciation to increase occurring on its own.
