All of the appreciation. See Shach seif katan 5 through his citation of Giddulei Terumah, who asks: if so, what does the Gemara in Perek Mi Shemet attempt to prove from the Mishnah concerning the collection of appreciation? Tumim answers by observing that the Gemara there could not simply establish the Mishnah as referring to automatic appreciation. The Mishnah in Bekhorot states that a ketubah does not collect appreciation and that none of the listed parties collect appreciation, and the Gemara explains the latter clause as including automatic appreciation. This suggests that the earlier clause is dealing with appreciation produced through expenditure, as Rashi explains, and that only a ketubah, because of its special leniencies, does not collect it, whereas an ordinary creditor does. Therefore the Gemara must answer that the Mishnah follows R. Meir. The Nimukei Yosef in Bava Metzia, however, proves from that same Mishnah that a creditor may collect automatic appreciation from heirs. According to Tumim's explanation, one could ask that since the latter clause expressly adds automatic appreciation, the first clause necessarily concerns appreciation through expenditure. The answer is that Nimukei Yosef states this in the name of the Ran, and the Ran may follow the poskim cited by the Shach below who hold that appreciation beyond the amount of the expenditure may be collected from heirs and from a recipient of a gift. The distinction between a purchaser and a recipient of a gift concerns reimbursement of the expenditure, because the latter has no seller from whom to recover. As to after-acquired property, however, appreciation produced through expenditure is indeed after-acquired property, as the Shach explains. Still, the difficulty raised by Giddulei Terumah is not fully resolved, because the Mishnah in Bekhorot can be established as referring to the heirs' appreciation beyond their expenditure, as Nimukei Yosef explains. Even though that appreciation is after-acquired, after-acquired property acquired and then left to heirs is encumbered according to the law we follow, since an oral loan may be collected from heirs. The Gemara's question concerns purchasers. As to the Shach's statement that because an item is considered "in possession" for purposes of the firstborn, even though a firstborn generally requires something comparable to a gift and cannot take a davar shelo ba la'olam, it is likewise treated as acquired for purposes of a creditor, this may be understood as follows. Ramban explains that the Gemara's distinction is whether the property at division is substantially the same object that existed at death. We rule that a firstborn has a right before division. Therefore, if the cow itself is the same cow at division as at death, the firstborn already owns two shares of its body, and consequently receives two shares of its offspring as well. But when the body of the property has changed between death and division, he does not receive a double portion in that new body. Thus, when the underlying object itself is treated as possessed property for the firstborn, it is also treated as already acquired for purposes of a creditor. This is the Shach's comparison. Ketzot HaChoshen further challenges Maharit's reasoning that if a lien constitutes a proprietary acquisition, an after-acquired clause should not work because a creditor collects only from the time of collection, and one cannot say that "the land appreciated" as though this were analogous to a palm tree for its fruit, since in the palm-tree case one owns the body of the palm while a creditor has only a lien upon it. This argument is also stated by Tumim, and will be explained below, with Hashem's help.
If he expressly wrote it to the purchaser. See Sema seif katan 3 through his statement that the enactment preventing the closing of the door to borrowers does not apply to purchasers. His words are difficult, because no such enactment is needed against the purchaser. The most plausible explanation is that the Sema is discussing a case where the seller did not write an after-acquired property clause to the purchaser. Without the enactment protecting credit, the appreciation would not be treated as though the seller first acquired and then sold it, but only as though he acquired it and it remained with him, in which case it would certainly belong to the creditor's lien. The Sema concludes that the reason the appreciation is shared is that the acquisition and sale are treated as occurring together, and Chazal, because of the need not to close the door before borrowers, treated it as though the seller acquired the appreciation and immediately sold it. The difficulty is that this enactment is for the benefit of creditors, not purchasers, so why should they have treated it as though there were both an acquisition and a sale? It would have been enough to treat the seller as having acquired the appreciation without a subsequent sale. The Sema therefore explains that since the entire rule rests on an enactment, the enactment's force is sufficient merely to remove one half of the appreciation from the purchaser. In other words, Chazal did not create a rule under which only the creditor's lien exists. Since the deemed acquisition and sale occur simultaneously, the seller is treated as having retained part for the purchaser, as though he sold only one half. Nor can we say that the appreciation should be treated as later sold to a third party. That comparison can be made only in relation to the creditor. With respect to the purchaser himself, who is the one acquiring, it cannot be characterized as a later sale to someone else. And as against the creditor, the purchaser certainly did not waive the lien or rights that arose from his own purchase. See Sema seif katan 4. See Ketzot HaChoshen, who writes that if the debtor borrowed from another creditor only after he had already sold the field to the purchaser, the later creditor receives no share in the appreciation, because the body of the field was never encumbered to him, having been sold before his loan, and therefore he has no right in the field's appreciation. This conclusion is so obvious that it scarcely requires writing. What Ketzot writes in siman 116 concerning appreciation that arose while the field remained with the debtor as between an earlier and later creditor is more difficult. If the field simply rose in market value, the body of the land that was already encumbered to the earlier creditor increased in value, and that increase belongs to the earlier creditor's lien. But where a new improvement was created in the land, that new increment is after-acquired property and the creditors divide it.
The creditor does not reimburse him for the expenditure. It appears to me that if the purchaser says, "I will take my wood and stones," his claim is effective. The reason is explained in Shach seif katan 10, who distinguishes this case from one who improves another's property without permission. In Tur and Beit Yosef siman 375 the reason one who improves another's property may not later say, "I will take my wood and stones," is that when he initially intended to receive payment, his conduct is like saying, "Acquire this sum for so-and-so if he wishes." Once the owner accepts the improvement, his courtyard acquires it for him. This is also stated in the name of Rabbeinu Tam, that the improver cannot retract because the owner's courtyard acquired the improvement immediately. Since that rule depends on acquisition through the courtyard, it does not apply here. The body of the courtyard belongs neither to the seller nor, at that time, to the creditor, because a creditor acquires only from the time of collection. Accordingly there is no courtyard acquisition by which the creditor or seller acquires the purchaser's materials, and the purchaser may retract and say that he will take his wood and stones. I would add that under the later communal enactments, a creditor who can identify his merchandise has priority. Therefore the acquisition represented by the purchaser's actual expenditure should likewise be treated as earlier. The Shach writes in seif katan 10 that the purchaser may recover appreciation only on account of the principal purchase price of the field, not on account of the appreciation itself. That is true specifically of appreciation that is owed only because the seller personally undertook responsibility for it. According to the Rambam, a person cannot obligate himself in an unquantified amount at all; and according to the other poskim, even where such a personal obligation is valid, it cannot be collected from encumbered property. The purchaser's actual expenses, however, are like an ordinary oral loan. Relative to the seller, the purchaser is no worse than one who improved another's property without permission, since he improved the seller's property for him. Under our practice ordinary loans are treated alike for this purpose. See Shach seif katan 10, where there is a textual omission. It should read: "Do not say that he is like one who descends without permission.".
The principal from the seller's property. See Shach seif katan 12 through his statement that otherwise it would be as though the seller had encumbered it to the purchaser. It appears that his intent is as follows. Giddulei Terumah seems to hold that at the time of sale the seller is not considered to have sold the future appreciation at all. If he had sold the appreciation at that time and had undertaken responsibility for it, the purchaser's claim to the appreciation would itself arise from the original sale, as the Shach writes. Rather, according to Giddulei Terumah the future appreciation remains legally in the seller's domain and becomes encumbered to both the creditor and the purchaser. The Shach therefore asks why Rashbam needs to say that it is treated as though the seller acquired the appreciation and then sold it. It would be enough to say that the seller simply encumbered it to the purchaser. Moreover, if no obligation regarding appreciation arose when the seller received the purchase money, from where would a new obligation to pay for the appreciation arise at the time of collection? The Shach therefore explains that at the original sale the seller also sold the future appreciation and undertook responsibility for it. When that appreciation later comes into existence, the purchaser's acquisition would ordinarily take effect immediately, and the creditor's lien would never precede it. Rashbam therefore needed the rule that, when the appreciation comes into existence, the seller is treated as though he first acquired it and only then sold it to the purchaser, so that the creditor's lien attaches before the deemed sale. The Shach further writes at the end of his discussion that although as against the creditor the purchaser cannot acquire the appreciation merely by virtue of the sale, nevertheless as against later purchasers he may rely on the fact that the seller himself sold it to him. The meaning is that Chazal treated the appreciation as though the seller acquired and sold it only because of the enactment protecting the earlier creditor who lent before the purchaser acquired the field. Without that enactment the creditor could not collect the appreciation. Later purchasers cannot invoke that enactment against the purchaser, because with respect to them there is no need to protect the earlier extension of credit. As against them, therefore, the appreciation is regarded as belonging to the purchaser, and they cannot deduct from his principal claim the value of appreciation he received. And all the produce that he consumed. See Shach seif katan 17. Tumim writes that the principal reason a creditor collects appreciation is that the seller wrote to the purchaser an undertaking that he would stand and clear away claims against the sale. Since produce is not ordinarily included in that written undertaking, the creditor does not collect it. According to the Shach, however, automatic appreciation may be collected even without relying on that reason, and therefore the Shach needs his additional explanation that produce is movable property and is not subject to the creditor's lien. According to this, if the debtor expressly pledged movable property together with land, the creditor may also collect produce that is still in existence. Produce already consumed need not be returned, because a creditor acquires only from the time of collection. See Shach seif katan 18, where there are several textual errors. Four times the text states concerning produce that does not require the land that the creditor collects it; it should read that the creditor does not collect it. Likewise, in the citation from Ramban in Milchamot, where it says that the creditor does not collect produce that requires the land, it should read that the creditor does collect it. See also Shach seif katan 19, where the text should read: "even if the debt amounts only to the value of the land itself," as the Tur also writes.
