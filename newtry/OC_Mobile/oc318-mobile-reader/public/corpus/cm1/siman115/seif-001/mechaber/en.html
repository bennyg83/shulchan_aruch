The law of produce and appreciation when a creditor seizes property from a purchaser or from orphans. It contains 6 se'ifim.
When a creditor comes to seize property from a purchaser, if the borrower wrote an after-acquired property clause, the creditor also seizes the field's appreciation, whether the property improved by itself, such as when trees grew or its value increased, or improved through expenditures. If it improved by itself, he seizes the entire appreciation. {Rama: Some say he collects only half the appreciation in every case. This appears to be the primary view. If the seller expressly wrote to the purchaser that the creditor would not collect by virtue of the after-acquired property clause, the purchaser has no right to the appreciation.} If the property improved through expenditures, the creditor seizes half the appreciation above the expenditures. {Rama: Some say the creditor need not reimburse any expenditures when the debt equals the principal value together with the appreciation.} The purchaser then recovers the principal from the seller's property, including encumbered property the seller sold or gave away after selling him this field. But appreciation seized by the creditor may be recovered from the seller only from unencumbered property. All this applies only to appreciation that accrued while the seller was alive. If the property improved after the seller's death, the creditor has no right to it, and all of it remains with the purchaser. Produce the purchaser already consumed is not seized from him, but any produce still attached to the ground, even if it no longer needs the ground, such as grapes ready to be picked, may be collected by the creditor just like appreciation. If the purchaser wishes to remove the creditor with payment, he may do so regarding both the field and the appreciation, unless the field was expressly designated as the creditor's sole source of payment. In that case, he cannot remove him. See notes 40 and 271.
