If the borrower wrote an after acquired property clause. Whether general warranty is presumed to include after acquired property as a scribal omission is discussed above in siman 112 at length.
After acquired property. See Tumim. Baal HaTerumos holds that such a clause is required for the creditor to collect improvement. Without it, the creditor does not collect from the improvement. Many authorities, however, hold that with respect to improvement no express after acquired clause is necessary, because ordinary practice itself is equivalent to an express stipulation concerning improvement. Therefore, if the creditor is in possession, he may say that he relies on those authorities. See there.
He also seizes the improvement, etc. Rambam and the Mechaber hold that improvement arising automatically is part of the field's own lien and is therefore included in seizure of the land. Even without an after acquired clause, the creditor collects this natural appreciation. Improvement produced by expenditure, however, is treated as though the debtor later acquired it and then sold it to the purchaser. The creditor therefore collects such improvement only if an after acquired clause was written.
In every case he collects only half the improvement. According to the opposing view, all improvement is treated as though the debtor acquired it and then transferred it to the purchaser. Even natural appreciation did not exist at the time of the loan and is therefore after acquired property. Without an after acquired clause the creditor collects no improvement. The two issues therefore correspond: where he can collect the entire improvement, no after acquired clause is needed; where he collects only half, such a clause is required. The reason he receives only half is that the purchaser also has a claim to the improvement, making it analogous to two creditors whose liens attach simultaneously to after acquired property, and they divide. See Tumim.
He does not collect. Shach discusses Rambam's example of trees growing on the property and explains it as a case of a palm tree that was already present but grew thicker and larger. In such a case all agree that no after acquired clause is necessary and the creditor takes the entire increase. See Tumim. I reconciled the authorities whom Shach challenged and nevertheless concluded that Shach is correct in practical law. Where a palm tree simply grows thicker, such that a firstborn takes a double portion in that growth as explained below in siman 278, a creditor likewise takes the entire appreciation and no after acquired clause is needed.
However, if he wrote to the purchaser, etc. According to the view that general warranty is treated as a scribal omission, the case must be one where the deed explicitly excluded after acquired liability, or where it specified some forms of warranty and omitted others, so that we no longer presume an omission. According to the view that after acquired liability is not presumed, ordinary silence is enough to exclude it. This issue depends upon the major dispute whether, where one creditor has an after acquired clause and another does not, property still in the debtor's possession is awarded solely to the creditor with the clause. Certainly a purchaser's position should not be weaker merely because the property is already in his possession than it would have been while in the debtor's hands. Sema writes this correctly, unlike Shach. I discussed the underlying law at length above in siman 104 in both Urim and Tumim.
Half the improvement. The implication of the authorities is that the division is measured relative to the remaining debts. If the debtor borrowed one hundred ten, the field was worth one hundred and later improved by ten, the creditor first takes the field worth one hundred and then receives five of the improvement toward the remaining ten, while the purchaser keeps five toward his purchase loss of one hundred. In Tumim, however, I wrote at length that the purchaser, who is in possession, may rely upon the authorities who divide improvement proportionately by the respective monetary claims. Under that approach the purchaser receives ten shares of the improvement and the creditor only one, as Rosh writes.
The amount exceeding the expenditure. A purchaser who improved the property entered it with authorization, and therefore at minimum he is reimbursed his expenses like one who enters another's field with permission. See Tumim for my explanation preventing contradiction with the Talmudic passage cited by the great decisors.
And some say that he does not, etc. They reason that the purchaser never undertook the improvement expecting reimbursement from the creditor. He relied upon the seller, who expressly obligated himself at the time of sale to reimburse expenses if the property were later seized. The purchaser knowingly accepted that arrangement. Additionally, the Sages limited the purchaser's recovery for the benefit of creditors. When land is found to have been stolen, by contrast, the true owner must reimburse the purchaser's expenses because the purchaser had no reason to anticipate that situation and no special creditor enactment applies. See Tumim. The primary ruling follows these authorities, as Shach also concludes.
The principal from encumbered property. Why can the purchaser not say that the creditor had free property left elsewhere? One answer is that those assets were overseas, and the creditor cannot be forced to travel there, as explained above in siman 111 seif 11. Therefore the creditor collects from the purchaser, who then proceeds against the overseas property. See Maggid Mishneh and Sema. In Tumim I discussed this at length and gave several other situations in which the purchaser cannot raise the defense that free property was left available.
Even from encumbered property, etc. See Tumim. Although the half of the improvement left with the purchaser offsets part of his principal loss, the purchaser may nevertheless collect his full principal from encumbered property. Those later purchasers cannot say that free property was left because he already recovered part of his principal through the improvement. The improvement itself is not collectible from encumbered property. See there for the reasoning.
But the improvement, etc. Improvement lacks publicity, or it is not fixed in amount, or it was not yet written as a presently existing obligation. Even if the deed states that the seller will reimburse improvement, at the time of sale that improvement did not yet exist, and the writing therefore creates no public notice sufficient to bind purchasers. This is Rashi and Tosafos in chapter HaNizakin. Nevertheless, the creditor may collect improvement from free property even if the document did not mention it, because it is customary to write such liability and general warranty is treated as a scribal omission. Rambam states this explicitly.
Only, etc. If, after the purchaser's improvement was seized, the seller acquired new property and then sold it, see Tumim. According to the explanation that improvement is excluded because it is not fixed in amount, one may argue that the purchaser could collect from those later buyers. Therefore, if the purchaser already seized, he may rely upon the opinion that a sufficiently fixed improvement claim can be collected. See there.
From free property. If a kinyan was made specifically for the improvement obligation, I concluded in Tumim that it may be collected from encumbered property like the principal. See there.
All this, etc. According to the Mechaber, natural appreciation is collected in full without an after acquired clause. Therefore, even if the field appreciated after the seller's death, the creditor still takes all such natural appreciation. This clause also covers improvement produced through expenditure. According to Rama's dissenting view, the same rule here applies even to natural appreciation. Shach challenges this, but in Tumim I wrote that Rama's ruling is unquestionably correct in practical law.
After the death. The entire reason the creditor can ordinarily collect improvement made by the purchaser is that we conceptually treat it as though the seller acquired the improvement and then transferred it. That legal fiction cannot apply to improvement arising only after the seller's death. See Tumim.
And all fruits that he consumed. This is not limited to fruit already eaten. Even fruit still present, once detached from the land, is beyond the creditor's claim. Sema explains that these fruits resemble after acquired property subject to two equal claims, where whichever party collected first retains it. Shach explains that they are movable property acquired and then sold by the seller, to which the creditor has no claim. See Tumim. I wrote that neither explanation fully fits the practical law. The essential reason is that when the purchaser bought the land, he did not stipulate with the seller that the seller would reimburse him for fruits. Therefore the creditor has no claim to the fruit, and with respect to fruit the purchaser resembles a gift recipient with respect to improvement. Accordingly, even if the creditor's note contains both after acquired property and movable property together with land, while the purchaser's deed has no after acquired clause, the creditor still has no claim to the fruit. See Tumim.
That do not need the ground. See above in siman 95. Rosh disagrees and holds that only produce that still needs the ground somewhat retains the status of attached produce. Once it no longer needs the ground, it is treated as fully harvested.
The creditor collects. If the field was an apotiki, the authorities disagree whether the creditor also takes the fruit. Razah and those with him say he does not. Rosh and Tur, and according to what I wrote also Tosafos, hold that he does. See Tumim. I concluded that even Rosh and Tur agree that fruit already consumed by the purchaser is not recoverable. Their ruling concerns detached fruit still physically present, which the creditor may take.
And if the purchaser wishes to remove him, etc. See above at the end of siman 114. If the creditor will not receive his entire debt, the purchaser cannot remove him. See there for why the rule that an appraisal returns does not create a contradiction. Certainly, if the creditor says, 'I will accept the field in satisfaction of my entire debt,' the purchaser cannot force him to accept only the appraised value, as explained at the end of siman 114. See there regarding whether in such a case the purchaser can demand that a portion corresponding to his improvement be left to him in the land.
Whether from the improvement. See Tumim. Where the purchaser cannot compel the creditor to leave him any part of the land and has only a monetary claim against the creditor, one can argue that he does not receive half the improvement at all, and the creditor takes all of it. See there for the reasoning. The law requires study.
He cannot remove him. See below, siman 117. If the seller himself wishes to litigate with the creditor and remove him, he may do so. See Shach.
