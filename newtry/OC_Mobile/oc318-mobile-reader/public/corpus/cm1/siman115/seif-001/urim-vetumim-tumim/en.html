If the borrower wrote for him. Baal HaTerumot holds that in order for the creditor to collect appreciation, the document must contain an after acquired property clause. Many authorities, however, hold that with respect to appreciation no express after acquired property clause is needed, because a creditor ordinarily lends on the understanding that he will have a lien on later appreciation. According to them the Gemara's statement, "a creditor collects the appreciation, for proof is that this is what the seller writes to the purchaser," is especially well explained. The question otherwise is why the Gemara needs this independent reason when the creditor's document already contains an after acquired property clause. On the view that appreciation is different, the answer is that although ordinary after acquired property is not assumed unless written, appreciation of the very land already subject to the debt is treated as implicit. The purchaser's standard warranty language reflects the known fact that the creditor can take that appreciation. The Gemara in Bava Batra still properly proves from the law of appreciation that after acquired property can be subjected. If after acquired property were absolutely incapable of lien, an implied condition could not create a lien on appreciation either. But once we rule that after acquired property can be subjected, appreciation may be treated as implicitly included even without express language. Rambam's wording also points this way. In chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh he distinguishes between appreciation caused by expenditure and spontaneous appreciation, explaining how much the creditor collects in each case, but does not add the major distinction that one requires an after acquired property clause and the other does not. At the end of the chapter, when he speaks of Reuven who borrowed from Shimon, expressly subjected after acquired property, later bought land and sold it, the clause is needed because the land itself was acquired later, not because of its appreciation. The opening laws therefore indicate that appreciation of either type can be collected without a special after acquired property clause. The Maggid Mishneh says Rambam's rule assumes such a clause, but that may reflect only one of his proposed resolutions of the question how collection from encumbered property can occur without the purchaser saying, "you left free property in the debtor's hands." In one answer, the debtor later acquired and sold other land in circumstances where the after acquired property clause matters. That does not prove that every case of appreciation itself requires the clause. The Rif likewise states the rules of appreciation in Bava Metzia without saying that the creditor's document must contain an after acquired property clause. In Bava Batra, where he discusses half the appreciation, he again does not add such a requirement. His omission supports the view that appreciation is implicitly included. There is almost a compelling proof from the Gemara in Bava Metzia and Bava Kamma. Rav Nachman says in Shmuel's name that three persons have the improvement appraised for them and receive money, including a creditor against a purchaser. The Gemara asks that Shmuel himself says a creditor collects appreciation, and it gives a strained answer by limiting the debt to the value of the land. Why not answer simply that the document lacked an after acquired property clause, so the creditor had no lien on the appreciation and therefore had to pay for it? According to the opposing view one must say, with difficulty, that even without an after acquired property clause the creditor has some potential lien on appreciation because the land itself was originally subject, so the issue cannot be disposed of so simply. The view that appreciation is implicitly included is much more natural. Tosafos in Bava Metzia also supports this. They prove that in an express apotiki, even where the debt equals only the land's principal value, the creditor can take all the appreciation, paying only expenses. If appreciation required an after acquired property clause, what would the creditor's right be in an apotiki, where the agreement is that repayment will come only from this very field and there is no meaningful way to subject unrelated future acquisitions? The better understanding is that appreciation of the pledged field is itself included in the original lien. Accordingly, where the creditor is in possession he can certainly say that the halachah follows the authorities who do not require an express after acquired property clause for appreciation. This is especially strong where ordinary warranty language was written, for then there is substantial reason to treat the appreciation clause as an omitted standard term and therefore implied.
However. Tosafos in Bava Metzia, s.v. "resolve also with a creditor," appears at first to disagree, because they ask how collection from encumbered property can occur if the purchaser can say that free property was left with the debtor, and they do not use the Maggid Mishneh's solution that the creditor had no after acquired property clause. Yet this does not necessarily prove they reject the preceding principle. Tosafos may hold like Rashba that ordinary after acquired property language is itself treated as an omitted standard term. In an apotiki, however, although general future acquisitions are not naturally included, appreciation of that very property is clearly within the lien. The same distinction answers several difficulties raised by Maharsha. When the Gemara places a teaching about improvement under Rabbi Meir, one can say that under Rabbi Meir ordinary warranty and after acquired property are not presumed, while appreciation of the specific property may still be treated differently. Under the Sages, where ordinary warranty language is presumed, the after acquired property issue does not force the case into an apotiki. This allows the passages to be read without unnecessary contradictions. It may be even more accurate to say that even if the borrower expressly excluded general after acquired property, that exclusion need not include later appreciation of property already subject to the creditor unless he specifically excluded the appreciation as well. The ordinary intent of a clause excluding after acquired property is to protect later independent acquisitions, not to release the growth of the very field already mortgaged. If so, an apotiki is an especially strong example: although no general future property is included, the creditor still takes later appreciation because his lien rests on that field itself. In any event, the considerations strongly support the conclusion that the creditor in possession may rely upon the authorities who treat appreciation as included without separate after acquired property language.
When the property appreciated on its own. In Urim I wrote that this type of appreciation does not require an express after acquired property clause, because the creditor takes all of it. Gidulei Terumah asks from Bava Batra, where the Gemara says that if after acquired property cannot be subjected, how can the Mishnah speak of recovering appreciation, and it is forced to establish the source under Rabbi Meir. Why not answer that the source concerns spontaneous appreciation, which is unrelated to an after acquired property clause? Likewise, when the Gemara later says that if a creditor collects appreciation it should have stated half the appreciation, why not establish the source as spontaneous appreciation, of which the creditor takes all? The Shach answers weakly that the baraita's language "and it appreciated" indicates appreciation through expenditure. That is insufficient. The baraita itself is already textually difficult and elsewhere is corrected by the Gemara. More importantly, if its purpose is to explain the Mishnah's phrase "for appreciation of land," why would it select only expenditure based appreciation and ignore spontaneous appreciation, which is a principal form of the law? The later question, "it should have stated half the appreciation," can be answered differently. The Gemara can be asking precisely why the explanatory baraita chose only the case of spontaneous appreciation and omitted expenditure based appreciation, where the creditor ordinarily takes only half. Since the baraita is presented as explaining the Mishnah generally, it should have taught that significant distinction as well. The answer that it speaks of half the appreciation therefore remains appropriate. The earlier question requires a broader answer. In Bechorot the Mishnah says that a woman, daughters for support, and a firstborn do not take from "expected property" or from appreciation. The Rosh notes that an ordinary creditor likewise does not take expected property, so why were a wife and daughters singled out? He answers that the point is appreciation. Ordinary creditors do take appreciation, while a wife and daughters do not because of the special leniencies of ketubah law. The continuation includes spontaneous appreciation among the types they do not receive. This proves that the contrast with an ordinary creditor includes both expenditure based and spontaneous appreciation. A ketubah is not an apotiki, because Shimon ben Shetach's enactment made all the husband's property responsible. Daughters' support is even less capable of being formulated as an apotiki, for it arises as a condition of beit din. Thus the Mishnah itself demonstrates that ordinary creditors collect appreciation even in cases that cannot be reduced to the special apotiki theory. Once that source proves a creditor's right to appreciation, the Bava Batra discussion properly treats the right as evidence that after acquired property can in principle be subjected. Even if spontaneous appreciation has a stronger claim because it is treated as part of the original property, the Mishnah's contrast includes ordinary improvement as well. The Gemara therefore gives the comprehensive answer that fits all the sources rather than selecting only one form of appreciation. This resolves the difficulties.
And some say that in every case, etc. The Shach argues at length that where the appreciation is of the type "a palm grew thicker" or land was improved naturally by deposition of soil, everyone should agree that no after acquired property clause is needed and that the creditor takes the entire appreciation. His proof is from the law of a firstborn, who takes a double portion in such appreciation. Scripture calls the firstborn's extra portion a gift until it reaches him, yet he still takes this type of growth. This appears to show that the growth is treated as already present in the inherited property rather than as a new acquisition. At first sight this is a powerful objection to the authorities who require an after acquired property clause and grant only half even in such a case. But the argument proves too much. Even where blossoms later became dates, the Gemara explains the firstborn's exclusion by saying that the object changed, rather than simply that the dates had not been in existence. According to the Shach, one would then have to say that even dates are treated as though they had already been present, which would support the creditor here as well. The correct distinction emerges from Rambam's ruling, followed below in siman 278, that if a cow was grazing in the marsh and later gave birth, the firstborn takes a double portion in both the cow and calf. There is hardly anything more clearly not yet in existence than the calf. Rambam and the Mechaber below in siman 209 say explicitly that even a fetus is ordinarily a thing not yet in existence for purposes of acquisition. Rambam's ruling in Even HaEzer concerning betrothing an unborn daughter where the pregnancy is recognizable is only a stringency in matters of forbidden relationships, as he himself explains in his Commentary on the Mishnah. Why then does the firstborn take the calf? The answer is that a firstborn has a substantive right in the inherited property immediately upon the father's death. Once the pregnant cow is his property, later natural growth occurs within his property. The same is true where a palm already belongs to him and simply thickens. This is not a case of acquiring a future object through a contractual lien. A creditor is different. His lien does not make the field his property in the present. A creditor collects only from now onward. Except in a true apotiki, he cannot say that the field itself was already his and therefore that all later growth occurred in his domain. Thus the analogy to a firstborn is not decisive. Even under the Shach's reasoning, if later blossoms became dates, the purchaser analogy in siman 209 shows that actual ownership of the blossoms explains why later growth belongs to the purchaser. That rule cannot automatically be transferred to a creditor who owns no body of the land. Accordingly, the authorities who require after acquired property language even for this type of appreciation can be defended. The firstborn's rule rests on present ownership, whereas the creditor has only a lien. The Shach's proof therefore does not compel a different conclusion.
However. As a matter of practical law, there is still substantial reason to say that the growth of a palm or natural accretion to land should not be treated as ordinary "appreciation" at all. The Mishnah in Bechorot says that a woman, daughters, and a firstborn do not take appreciation, and then separately includes cases such as blossoms that became dates. It does not list the case of a palm simply thickening, because a firstborn does take that increase. If a woman collecting a ketubah also failed to take it merely because it was "appreciation," the Mishnah's grouping would be inaccurate: it would combine people whose rights differ as to that very increase. Since a woman's exclusion from appreciation is a special leniency in ketubah law, why should the Sages have distinguished between one natural appreciation and another unless the thicker palm is not legally classified as appreciation at all? It is more coherent to say that simple natural growth remaining within the same object is treated as the property itself, not as a separate improvement. Therefore it does not require after acquired property language, and the creditor takes it in full. This practical conclusion fits the structure of the Mishnah and avoids an artificial distinction.
Half the appreciation. Suppose the creditor is owed two hundred, the purchaser's principal purchase price was one hundred, and the property later appreciated by one hundred. The division of the appreciation should be proportional to the parties' respective claims, as I explained above in siman 104 regarding cases where the authorities say "they divide." The Rosh, however, in the first chapter of Bava Metzia writes that the division is according to the amount of each person's money. Thus if the principal is one hundred and the appreciation only ten, the creditor takes one part and the purchaser ten parts. He attributes this to Rabbeinu Chananel, even though in Ketubot the Rosh appears to rule unlike Rabbeinu Chananel and like the Rif. The matter can be explained through the Mordechai's presentation of Rashba. The reason they divide is not that the two liens literally arise at the same instant, for the first creditor's lien preceded the purchaser's. Rather, the debtor is regarded as having retracted from subjecting half to the first creditor because he wished to preserve some benefit for both parties. The Rosh's statement that the liens take effect together means that, because of this partial retraction, the effective liens on the appreciation arise together when the appreciation comes into existence. If the law depends upon the debtor's presumed intention to benefit both claimants, his presumed intention should be proportional to the amounts involved, just as with a deathbed disposition where intent determines the allocation. It is not plausible that a creditor with only ten zuz remaining should take five while a purchaser with a claim of one hundred also takes only five. The natural intent is that each should receive according to the size of his claim. On this conceptual account the Rosh's proportional formulation is clear. One might ask why, if the debtor retracted only from half the lien, a later creditor who collected first can ever retain everything. The answer is that the retraction itself can be understood as creating equal competing liens upon the relevant half in a manner that gives effect to prior collection. This is why the Rosh says the liens arise together. But the ordinary court division, where neither has yet obtained priority by collection, remains proportional to the amounts. The Tur's omission may indicate that he did not accept his father's special proportional rule as practical law. Another explanation is that Rabbeinu Chananel and the Rif's dispute elsewhere depends on whether property liens are of Torah law. If liens are only rabbinic and were enacted to prevent discouraging lending, reason supports proportional division: it would be absurd for one who lent one hundred to take the same amount as one who lent one thousand. But if liens are of Torah law, each debt creates a full legal lien on the property, and the Rif's equal division can be understood. The Rosh rules generally that liens are of Torah law, but in explaining Shmuel, who holds they are not, he follows Rabbeinu Chananel's proportional formulation. Even so, for practical law the purchaser can at least say that the halachah follows Rabbeinu Chananel, Ritva, Rashba, and Mordechai, who support proportional division. This is especially strong where the very after acquired property lien may itself be rabbinic. Therefore the creditor should not extract more than his proportional share of the disputed appreciation.
The excess over the expenses, etc. This is the view of the major teachers cited by Rambam. The Shach established from accurate manuscripts that Rambam himself does not agree with them, and this is correct, for it would be surprising for Rambam to depart from the Rif. Nevertheless, those teachers were major Torah authorities and their position deserves explanation. If an after acquired property lien works as strict law, then one can understand why the purchaser receives no reimbursement for expenses. The creditor can say: you chose to buy property already subject to my lien; if you spent money improving it, recover your expenses from the seller. But if after acquired property is not subject by Torah law and collection is only a rabbinic enactment to prevent discouraging lending, there is no reason to extend the enactment to deprive the purchaser even of his actual expenses. Reimbursing expenses does not discourage lending, because had the creditor himself wished to produce the same improvement he would also have had to spend money. Indeed, assuring the purchaser that he will at least recover expenses encourages useful improvement of the property. The question whether after acquired property is subject by strict law is connected to the dispute between Rabbi Meir and the Sages. Rabbi Meir permits transfer of future property and therefore can recognize the lien directly. According to the Sages, who ordinarily do not permit transfer of a thing not yet in existence, the lien can be understood as operating only through the Sages' concern for lending. Shmuel's own statement that a creditor collects appreciation is formulated according to Rabbi Meir in several discussions. Therefore Shmuel can say that the creditor takes the appreciation without reimbursing expenses. But our halachah follows the Sages while nevertheless recognizing after acquired property through the rabbinic institution, and the major teachers could therefore require reimbursement of the purchaser's expenses. This view may also explain the difficult passage in Perek HaMekabel where the orphans say, "we improved," and the creditor says, "your father improved." If a creditor collects appreciation from heirs even though they have no seller against whom to recover, as Tosafos understands from Bechorot, what practical difference is there who produced the improvement? According to the teachers' view, the difference is expenses. If the heirs themselves spent the money, they can demand their expenses. If the father improved during his life, they cannot. Thus their position is conceptually sound. Practical law nevertheless follows the majority of authorities that the purchaser does not receive expenses where he has recourse against the seller, as the Shach concludes.
From property, etc. Tosafos and Maggid Mishneh ask how the teaching can speak of collecting the purchaser's principal from encumbered property, since those purchasers can say that free property was left with the debtor. One answer of Maggid Mishneh is that the creditor's document did not include after acquired property while the purchaser's did, and the debtor bought and then sold another parcel. This works most naturally according to the view developed above that appreciation does not itself require an express after acquired property clause. Maggid Mishneh gives another answer: there is free property, but it is overseas. The purchaser cannot force the creditor to travel there first. The Sema cites this as practical law, though the source is not explicit in the Gemara and Maggid Mishneh himself expresses some hesitation. At first glance Tosafos's failure to give this answer might imply disagreement. But Tosafos's question can be understood specifically under Shmuel, who holds property liens are not of Torah law. If that is the framework, one can infer that ordinary later property may not have the same lien force, and the overseas distinction alone may not be enough. According to our ruling that property liens are of Torah law, the Maggid Mishneh's answer is much more plausible. This also resolves Maharsha's difficulty concerning why the supporting baraita for Shmuel must be an apotiki case. Under practical halachah the source can concern free property overseas and need not be reduced to an apotiki.
But. Tosafos's discussion itself deserves further explanation. They say that from the Mishnah's statement that principal is collected from encumbered property one might infer that after acquired property is not subject, because otherwise purchasers could always say, "you left free property." But even if that inference is accepted, the phrase "from free property" regarding appreciation remains difficult, for property purchased by the debtor after the first sale but still in his hands is free property and does not require an after acquired property clause to be collected from the debtor himself. The key is that the Mishnah says the reason for withholding appreciation from encumbered property is "for the welfare of the world," meaning because later purchasers lacked notice. If the only case in which the purchaser could ever recover from later encumbered property were where the debtor's remaining assets were overseas, that concern is weak. The first purchaser already knowingly entered a transaction where the creditor could choose to travel abroad and collect the entire debt there. He could not safely assume the creditor would leave those assets untouched. The purchaser therefore cannot plausibly say he lacked notice of the risk as to the entire transaction. Tosafos consequently seeks a normal case in which the creditor cannot reach the later property but the purchaser could, such as where the creditor's after acquired property clause did not cover it while the purchaser's warranty did. That is what creates a genuine need for the rabbinic market protection denying collection of appreciation. Once the Sages created the rule for the ordinary case, they can apply it uniformly even where the only available later property is overseas. Rabbinic enactments are not redrawn case by case. This explains why Tosafos insists on a structural scenario beyond the overseas property suggested by Maggid Mishneh, and it reconciles the two approaches. It also allows the baraita supporting Shmuel to be read without forcing every instance into an apotiki.
From encumbered property, etc. The Shach cites Gidulei Terumah's question. The half of the appreciation that remains with the purchaser is certainly applied toward his principal loss. If he later recovers the full purchase price from encumbered property, should not the amount already received through appreciation be deducted? The Shach initially suggested that the purchaser takes his half of the appreciation as compensation for the improvement itself rather than for principal, but he later retracted. The Rosh explicitly says that if the purchaser lacks an after acquired property clause he receives no appreciation, which would be inexplicable if his right were simply independent compensation for the improvement. At first I thought the Gemara itself supports the deduction. Rav Nachman says that a baraita assists Shmuel because it says the purchaser recovers principal from encumbered property and appreciation only from free property. Why is that baraita needed when the Mishnah already says the same thing? Perhaps Rav Nachman's proof is not that a creditor collects some appreciation, which is already clear, but that Shmuel means the creditor takes all the appreciation. Rav Nachman elsewhere holds that the first creditor has priority in after acquired property. If the purchaser retained half the appreciation toward his principal, then, as Gidulei Terumah asks, he should not also collect the full principal from encumbered property. The baraita's statement that he collects the full principal would therefore prove that no appreciation remained with him. Rav Huna, who holds the parties divide the after acquired property, cannot use the baraita this way and therefore interprets it as a purchaser from a robber. This gives good sense to the Gemara. Nevertheless, the unqualified rulings of the poskim imply that the purchaser recovers the full principal from encumbered property without deducting the appreciation. The reason is a form of alternative claim. Strict law would ordinarily allow recovery of both principal and appreciation from later purchasers. The Sages denied recovery of appreciation from them only because it is unascertained or lacks publicity. When the purchaser now seeks his principal, he can say: if you treat the appreciation I retained as something that reduced my principal, then give me that same amount of appreciation from encumbered property, because it has become fixed by the very accounting you invoke. If you say the later purchasers had no notice of the appreciation, then do not deduct it from the principal amount that everyone knew could be collected. Either way, the later purchasers cannot use the rabbinic protection concerning unknown appreciation to reduce the purchaser's undisputed principal claim. Tosafos in Ketubot uses a related form of reasoning regarding support obligations: even where the exact amount of support is not fixed, purchasers know that a ketubah claim may later consume the estate and therefore cannot always plead lack of notice. The logic here is stronger still. Accordingly, the ordinary ruling of the poskim is sound: the purchaser collects his full principal from encumbered property without deducting the appreciation retained from the creditor. Rambam says this explicitly in his Commentary on the Mishnah. If a person bought land for fifty and it appreciated to one hundred, and a creditor comes to collect, the creditor may take seventy five and leave twenty five to the purchaser, while the purchaser still recovers his full fifty of principal from encumbered property. This is decisive evidence that the appreciation is not deducted from principal for that purpose.
But. Even though the preceding reasoning fits the plain law, one can also explain Rav Nachman's use of the baraita in the opposite direction. The Mishnah by itself could be established in an apotiki or another special case. The baraita's explicit statement that the purchaser collects principal from encumbered property adds the important point that this remains so despite the simultaneous law of appreciation. Rav Huna then reinterprets it as a robber case because his view of the appreciation would otherwise create a different allocation. In any event, the decisive practical point remains the same. The purchaser's principal claim is not reduced by the half appreciation he retains. That result follows from Rambam's explicit example and from the overall structure of the market protection. The later purchasers assumed responsibility for the principal, while the Sages protected them from the uncertain appreciation. They cannot use that protective enactment to transform the retained appreciation into a retroactive payment of principal and thereby diminish the liability they knowingly accepted.
The appreciation, etc. Tosafos in Bava Metzia writes that the teaching's reference to collecting principal from encumbered property cannot be established where, after the creditor already seized the field and its appreciation, the seller later acquired another field and sold it. If the purchaser then sought to recover the appreciation from that later buyer, the amount of appreciation would already be fixed and public by the time of the later purchase, and it too should be recoverable from encumbered property. Tosafos in Bava Batra, however, in the reading adopted by Maharsha, appears to use precisely this answer: the seller acquired and sold new property after the creditor's seizure, yet the purchaser still recovers only principal from encumbered property. The two passages appear inconsistent. Ketubot 96 provides a useful parallel. Tosafos discusses a widow who sold land for support and later seeks her ketubah. Purchasers might say that she left herself a source of payment through the land sold for support. Tosafos gives one answer based on the land being treated as destroyed, and another by establishing that the purchasers whose land she now seeks bought only after she had already sold the first land for support. The support expenses were therefore already known before their purchase, yet she still does not automatically collect those amounts from them. This suggests a dispute about whether the Sages' enactment is applied uniformly even after the formerly uncertain amount has become fixed. The Bavli in Gittin 51 states in Rabbi Natan's name that if the first purchaser's appreciation preceded the second purchaser's acquisition, the first may collect from the second, because the amount was fixed before the second purchase. Tosafos and Rashba explain this under the opinion that the problem is lack of fixed amount, and Rashba says the Yerushalmi implies the same. That source strongly supports the Bava Metzia Tosafos: once the appreciation has been judicially determined before the later purchase, it is fixed and should be collectible from the later buyer. One can reconcile the Bava Batra Tosafos by saying that the Sages sometimes applied their enactments uniformly without distinction. This depends on the underlying theory. If property liens are of Torah law, strict law would permit collection from purchasers even without publicity, and the Sages removed that right only to protect later purchasers. We should then minimize the enactment: once the amount is fixed before the purchase and there is no unfair surprise, Torah law returns and the purchaser may collect. This is Rabbi Natan's reasoning. If property liens are not of Torah law, however, collection from purchasers exists only because the Sages enacted it to prevent discouraging lending. With unascertained obligations they left the matter at the original law of no collection. In that framework, applying a uniform rule even where the amount later became fixed is much more plausible, because there is no Torah right being taken away. Moreover, after acquired property is itself especially dependent on rabbinic policy. Thus Bava Batra can speak according to Shmuel, who holds liens are not of Torah law, while Bava Metzia's question can be directed to the view that liens are of Torah law. This also explains why one Tosafos passage is comfortable with an apotiki and another not, and why the different formulations need not be contradictory. As practical law, however, where the amount of appreciation was finally determined before the later purchaser bought, there is strong authority that it may be collected from that purchaser. If the creditor is in possession he can certainly say that he relies upon Tosafos and Rashba. This is particularly true under the view that the problem is lack of a fixed amount rather than lack of writing.
From free property. If the seller made a kinyan concerning responsibility for the appreciation, then according to what I established above in siman 60 from the Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer, the obligation may become collectible from encumbered property even according to the view that ordinarily an unascertained amount is not. One should not infer otherwise from Tosafos's suggestion that the baraita concerning a purchaser from a robber could involve a kinyan, while still saying appreciation is collected only from free property. Tosafos there follows its own position in Gittin that a kinyan helps where the defect is lack of writing but not necessarily where the defect is lack of a fixed amount. Other authorities disagree and hold that a kinyan can supply sufficient publicity even for the latter concern. This also resolves a difficulty in Tosafos's sequence. Why does the Gemara challenge from the latter clause rather than directly from the first clause concerning the robber? The first clause can be established where there was a kinyan but the obligation for fruits was not written into the instrument itself. The kinyan may not provide the same publicity as a written term if the transaction is not ultimately recorded. With appreciation, however, the obligation is normally written in the seller's warranty language, so if there was a kinyan it would be fully public and should be collectible from encumbered property. Therefore the Gemara's strongest question arises from the appreciation clause. According to the authorities who treat kinyan as sufficient even for an unascertained amount, practical law is that the purchaser can collect from encumbered property when a proper kinyan was made.
And all this concerns, etc. The Shach challenges the rule limiting a creditor's collection of appreciation where the seller has died. According to the Shach's own position that a creditor collects even appreciation created by the heirs, there is room for his challenge: why should the purchaser be stronger merely because the seller died? But according to the Mechaber and Rama's ruling, developed above, that a creditor does not collect appreciation created by the heirs, the limitation is straightforward. The purchaser should not be worse than if the field had remained in the heirs' hands. If the creditor would have no right to the heirs' later appreciation, why should he gain that right merely because the deceased had sold the land before dying? There is no need to invoke the purchaser's alternative claim, "if you remain silent, fine; otherwise I will return the deed to the heirs." Even authorities who reject that form of claim should agree that the purchaser's position cannot be worse than the heirs' with respect to appreciation arising only after the seller's death. Maharsha goes even further and suggests that even appreciation produced during the seller's lifetime may become unavailable once the seller dies and the purchaser has no one from whom to recover. That broader view requires separate analysis. At minimum, where the seller died first and only afterward did the land appreciate, the creditor should not collect. Even according to Tosafos, who allows collection from heirs' appreciation, one can argue that the purchaser's post death improvement is different because it is "expected" property. The heirs themselves may be bound by the mitzvah to repay their father's debt and are described as standing in his place. A purchaser has no such personal relation. If the seller died before the appreciation arose, the creditor's later claim against a purchaser can therefore fail as a claim to property not yet existing at the seller's death. There is also a deeper way to formulate the issue. When a person subjects after acquired property, one can ask when the lien actually takes effect. Perhaps the document creates an immediate lien now upon whatever later comes into existence. If so, the seller's later death does not cancel it, and the Shach's objection is strong. Alternatively, perhaps the document creates only a standing commitment that, when the new property or appreciation comes into existence, the lien will then attach. If the obligor has already died at that time, there is no person upon whom the new lien can take effect. A person's property is the guarantor for his obligation, and once the principal obligor has left the world the later object has no new lien to receive. This second understanding fits Rashba's rule that a person can retract from an after acquired property clause before the future property comes into existence. Death is an even more complete termination than retraction. It also connects to the dispute whether, when one borrower precedes another creditor and then acquires property, the first creditor takes priority or the two divide. If the first lien took effect immediately when the clause was written, the first creditor should have priority. If the lien arises only when the property comes into existence, both claims can attach together and division makes sense. The Shach himself explains one Talmudic question according to the earlier view that the first creditor has priority, and Rav Nachman indeed held that position. Under that view the present rule may fail. But our practical ruling is that they divide, which supports the theory that the lien takes effect only when the property comes into existence. On that theory Nimukei Yosef's rule concerning post death appreciation is coherent. There remains a conceptual puzzle. Suppose Reuven borrowed from Shimon in Nisan. Levi, Shimon's son, was born in Iyar. Shimon then died. Only afterward did Reuven's land appreciate and was sold. If the lien on appreciation arises only when the appreciation comes into existence, Shimon is no longer alive to acquire the lien, while Levi was not yet born when the original document was written and a person cannot ordinarily create a lien for someone not yet in existence. How does Levi ever inherit the right to the later appreciation? His father never acquired it, and it was not originally subjected to Levi. This point remains difficult and requires further study.
And all the produce. In Urim I cited the Shach's objection to the Sema. The Sema explains the purchaser's priority in detached produce through the rule of two successive creditors, where if the later party collected first, the collection stands. The Shach says that even if detached produce is still physically present, once it is no longer attached to the land the creditor has no lien upon it. This is not a decisive objection. The produce stands in the purchaser's domain. Rabbeinu Yonah's entire question concerning appreciation assumes that the purchaser's possession can count as prior collection, and he answers only that land requires a formal court collection. Movable property does not require that type of court award. It is therefore reasonable to treat the purchaser's existing possession of detached produce as prior collection. There is, however, a stronger question against the Sema. The Rosh carefully distinguishes land and movable property in Rabbeinu Yonah's rule and brings proof from the case where detached produce was left and Rabbi Tarfon treated it differently. If mere possession of movable property already supplied the entire answer, the Rosh could have said so directly. Moreover, where the creditor's document contains after acquired property but the purchaser's warranty does not, the Rosh says the parties do not divide. One might then expect the creditor to take the detached produce as well. If so, why does the Gemara ask whether a creditor ever has rights in produce when discussing the standard warranty phrase "beauty, improvement, and produce"? Shmuel's ordinary case can involve a creditor with the relevant after acquired property clause. Thus the Sema's formulation does not fully resolve the produce rule.
However. The Shach's answer that detached produce is movable property is also insufficient. If the creditor has a clause subjecting movable property together with land, why should he not collect the produce? The standard seller's warranty language would still need to mention produce in order to protect against a creditor whose document contained that clause. The Sages elsewhere take account of such clauses. The better reason follows the Gemara's own explanation concerning appreciation: proof that the creditor's ordinary expectation includes improvement is that the seller writes responsibility for improvement to the purchaser. Had the purchaser not had that right of recourse, the creditor would not ordinarily take the improvement, even with an after acquired property clause. This is why a recipient of a gift is stronger: the donor writes no warranty to him, so the creditor's ordinary assumption did not include taking his appreciation. Detached produce is different. Tosafos says that standard sale documents do not ordinarily include a warranty clause for produce. Therefore the creditor never lent on the assumption that he would take detached produce from a purchaser. In that respect the purchaser resembles a recipient of a gift. Even if the creditor's document expressly includes after acquired property or movable property together with land, the ordinary limitation remains: the creditor's lien was not understood to extend to produce that the purchaser could consume or sell before actual collection. Consequently, the creditor does not take detached produce merely by virtue of those clauses.
The creditor, etc. Where the field was made an apotiki, the Tur and Rosh say that the creditor has rights in its produce because it is regarded as his land. The Shach cites authorities who disagree and asks from the Gemara's statement that one does not recover "consumed produce" from encumbered property because of market protection. If a creditor can have rights in produce through an apotiki, why does the Gemara ask, "Does a creditor have produce?" Likewise, when the Gemara discusses the seller's standard language "improvement and produce," why not say that produce must be written because perhaps a creditor holds the sold field as an apotiki? One answer to the second question is that the Gemara's inquiry about standard drafting primarily concerns a purchaser from a robber. If it were directed to an ordinary creditor under Shmuel, even the ordinary lien language would have required separate specification under his view that warranty is not an omitted standard term. Tosafos in Bava Metzia makes this point. The first question remains difficult, especially because Tosafos elsewhere expressly establish a passage as an apotiki in order to avoid the purchaser's claim that free property remained. If the Rosh is correct that an apotiki creditor has produce, why does the Gemara still speak as though a creditor has no produce? I found this question cited in Asifat Zekenim without an answer. It is difficult to solve by saying Tosafos follows Baal HaMaor that even an apotiki gives no produce, because Tosafos themselves imply otherwise in their proof from the seller's standard warranty. Nor is it satisfactory to say that they silently rely on the purchaser's reimbursement of expenses. The proper distinction is between produce still present at the time of collection and produce already consumed. In a theft case, the purchaser who ate fruits must repay because he consumed property that never belonged to the seller. An apotiki is different. Until actual collection, the purchaser was entitled to use and even sell the produce in the ordinary course. The creditor's right in the apotiki does not operate retroactively to make every past consumption wrongful. Therefore, if detached produce remains physically present when the creditor comes to collect, he may take it as produce of his apotiki. But fruits already eaten long before are not recoverable. The Rosh's language supports this. In the robber case he says explicitly that the robbed owner takes all fruits the purchaser ate. In the apotiki case he says only that the creditor collects the field and its produce, without saying that he recovers fruits already consumed. The Tur likewise distinguishes its wording. This explains the Gemara's phrase "consumption of fruits," which means reimbursement for fruits already eaten out of the purchaser's pocket. An apotiki creditor does not have that right. Yet the seller still prudently writes produce in the purchaser's warranty because produce actually present at collection can be taken under an apotiki and the purchaser may then need recourse. This is also the most reasonable practical law. Since many authorities deny any produce right even in an apotiki, we should certainly not extend the Rosh's view beyond produce still present. Fruits already consumed remain with the purchaser.
And if the purchaser wishes, etc. The Shach writes that according to the Mechaber's understanding of the Rosh, even if the debt exceeds the value of the land and the creditor says, "I will accept the land in satisfaction of the entire debt," the creditor is not heard. The Shach rules instead like Ramban and those who allow the creditor to do so. I do not understand his formulation. The Tur's disagreement with Ramban below concerns heirs. With a purchaser, the Rosh and Tur agree, as explained at the end of siman 114, that so long as the creditor will not otherwise receive his entire debt, the purchaser cannot force him out merely by paying the appraised value. The Mechaber above likewise rules that the purchaser cannot remove the creditor unless the creditor is fully satisfied. Thus with a purchaser the practical result accords with Ramban: the creditor can insist on taking the property when partial cash payment would leave a remaining debt. See what I wrote above concerning why this does not contradict the rule of reversing an appraisal, and when the purchaser can demand a portion of the land corresponding to his improvement. There are circumstances where the purchaser cannot demand land for his appreciation because the creditor has increased the appraisal of the principal property rather than the improvement. In any event, the Shach's broad disagreement with the Mechaber here does not accurately reflect the purchaser case.
Whether from the appreciation. I am uncertain about a case where the purchaser cannot demand that a portion of land equal to his improvement be left to him, but instead the creditor takes the land and compensates the purchaser in money, as explained above in siman 114. Does the purchaser still receive half the appreciation, or does he receive nothing? The familiar question is why the Gemara in Bava Kamma and Bava Metzia asks against Shmuel, who says that one appraises improvement and pays money to the purchaser: "Does a purchaser have any improvement? Shmuel himself says a creditor collects improvement." Why not answer that the appraisal concerns the half of the improvement that remains the purchaser's? I earlier resolved this by saying that the Gemara may be following Rav Nachman and Shmuel's view that the first creditor has priority in after acquired property and therefore takes all. But it is difficult that two separate passages would state the question without even noting our practical rule that the parties divide. A better explanation is that the purchaser's right to half operates only while he has a possessory claim in the land itself. Nimukei Yosef explains the half division as a doubt concerning to whom the later lien attached. That produces division where neither party has complete possession. But if the creditor unquestionably takes the entire land and the purchaser seeks only money, the creditor is the possessor and the burden of proof lies on the purchaser. This resembles the rule that where an item is certainly destined to be cut down and the parties dispute only the value, the possessor retains the money absent proof. Even according to those who explain division because both liens attach simultaneously, once the creditor has actually taken the land, we rule that prior collection can stand, and with movable money simple possession is enough. The creditor is therefore holding the field, and the purchaser is the party seeking cash. The purchaser's uncertain competing lien should not extract money from him. This also resolves Tosafos's statement concerning an apotiki. One might ask why the purchaser cannot say: either the appreciation is mine, in which case leave me half, or if you say your land produced the growth and you take it all, reimburse my expenses. The answer is that an apotiki prevents the purchaser from removing the creditor from the land. The creditor need not claim "my land produced the growth" in order to take it. He can say simply: the property is my designated source of collection and you have no possessory right to defeat that designation. Once the purchaser is reduced to a monetary claim, the creditor's possession prevails. Accordingly, where the creditor necessarily takes the land and the purchaser can obtain only money, the purchaser may receive no share of the appreciation at all. This seems to explain the Gemara well. I have not found the point stated explicitly by the later authorities, so it still requires study. But the reasoning is strong, and the rule in seif 2, where the purchaser receives cash from the creditor while retaining a substantive right in the land, does not disprove it. There the land remains in the purchaser's chazakah unless and until the creditor pays. Here the legal structure itself leaves the purchaser no land and only a monetary demand.
