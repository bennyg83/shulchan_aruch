(Likkut) When he comes... According to the Rosh and Geonim, where a creditor collects from purchasers and the debt equals only the land's principal value, if the land was not made an apotiki he takes no improvement. If it was made an apotiki, he takes the improvement but reimburses the purchaser's expenditure, with the purchaser treated as one who improved another's field without permission. Where the debt equals both land and improvement, if there was no apotiki the creditor takes half the improvement without reimbursement; if there was an apotiki he takes all the improvement. Orphans and gift recipients differ because they have no seller from whom to recover, and therefore in some cases their position resembles a purchaser where the debt equals only the land's principal value. Rambam disagrees in several respects, particularly concerning reimbursement of expenditures and spontaneous appreciation. Tosafos has another position regarding orphans. The Rif largely follows the Rosh regarding purchasers but follows Rambam regarding orphans and gift recipients. (End of collected notes.)
(Likkut) The approach... Tosafos generally follows the Rosh concerning purchasers, with a special rule for orphans. They initially considered equating an apotiki case whether the debt equaled only the land or land plus improvement, but retracted. Their final purchaser rule is essentially that of the Rosh and Geonim, and their rule for a gift recipient also follows the Rosh. (End of collected notes.)
If he wrote... Bava Basra 157b. See above, siman 112 seif 1, where this depends on express language in the document.
Whether... whether... The Gemara in the first chapter of Bava Metzia speaks of "their labor and their improvement." Baal HaTerumos, citing the Raavad, explains "labor" as improvement created through expenditure and "improvement" as appreciation that arose on its own.
Except that if... and some say... Rif, Rav Hai Gaon, and the Rosh understand the first chapter of Bava Metzia as making no basic distinction between spontaneous appreciation and improvement caused by expenditure. Whatever portion of improvement is collectible in one category is likewise collectible in the other. With orphans and gift recipients, however, the Rosh may deny collection of improvement altogether in cases where there is no right of recourse. Rambam distinguishes: improvement created through expenditure may be only partly collectible, while spontaneous appreciation can be entirely collected. His proof is that Shmuel states that a creditor collects improvement, while Bava Basra raises a question whether after-acquired property is encumbered and resolves it through another opinion. Rambam therefore reads Shmuel as speaking of spontaneous appreciation, which does not require an after-acquired-property clause because it is treated as part of the land. Nimukei Yosef explains examples such as a palm that thickened, land increased by alluvium, or simple market appreciation. On this view the creditor may collect such appreciation even from heirs and gift recipients. The Geonim and Rosh reject this distinction and resolve Shmuel through the rules of after-acquired property. They also understand the Bechoros passage differently. The Gemara in Bava Metzia, which challenges from gift recipients, supports the Geonic approach, whereas Rambam requires a different reconciliation.
However, if... Because collection of improvement is rooted in the lien on after-acquired property, the purchaser ordinarily receives part of the appreciation where applicable. Express drafting matters for the creditor's lien, while the seller's ordinary responsibility toward a purchaser can include improvement even when not expressly written, because such responsibility is part of the standard sale. Rambam distinguishes these two contexts. Sema supports this reading, unlike the Shach.
The amount exceeding the expenditure, and some say... The Geonim read Bava Metzia to mean that where the debt equals land and improvement the creditor may take the entire improvement without reimbursing expenditure. Rambam follows authorities who regard that answer as strained and preserve the general rule that a purchaser who improved another's field is at least entitled to reimbursement of his expenditure. According to Rambam, Shmuel's statement about collecting all improvement refers to spontaneous appreciation. This is why Rambam and Shulchan Aruch do not explicitly frame every case by whether the debt equals land alone or land plus improvement, whereas the Geonim do.
And all this does not... The rationale for collection from improvement is that the seller is treated as having acquired the improvement and then transferred it to the purchaser. That rationale does not apply in the excluded case.
(Likkut) And all the fruits he consumed... This follows the view that even in certain robber cases fruits can be recovered, as explained in siman 373. Rashi disagrees. Here the phrase "he consumed" is not literal; even detached fruits still present are movable property and are not collected from a purchaser. The Rif says expressly that fruits not collected by a creditor include fruits already eaten or detached, while attached fruits are collected. (End of collected notes.)
But... even though... The Rif explains "improvement that has reached the shoulders" as produce no longer needing the land. This distinction fits the Gemara's initial assumption. Rashi explains it as produce close to harvest but still needing the ground. Rabbeinu Tam, Baal HaMaor, Rashba, Tosafos, and many commentators offer differing definitions. Kesubos supports the rule that whatever still needs the ground is land, while what no longer needs it is movable property. Rambam and Rabbi Yosef HaLevi draw further distinctions. Baal HaMaor and Rashba also have another approach, treating the phrase as improvement resulting from labor.
And if he wishes... but... According to the Geonim, where the property was made an apotiki, even if the debt equals only the principal land value the creditor takes improvement beyond the expenditure, because he can say that his own land appreciated. If the debt equals land plus improvement, he takes the entire improvement under their approach, while the purchaser seeks recourse from the seller. Rambam, although rejecting part of the Geonic analysis, also gives an apotiki stronger effect regarding improvement. The Maggid Mishneh records another approach under which the only distinction of an apotiki is the purchaser's inability to remove the creditor by paying money. The Beit Yosef appears to have followed this other approach in part, but elsewhere quotes Rambam's explicit apotiki rule. (End of collected notes.)
(Likkut) And if he wishes, etc. The Maggid Mishneh writes another approach at the end of halacha 6, that there is no distinction between an apotiki and other property except regarding the debtor's ability to remove the creditor by paying money. This follows the plain meaning of the Gemara's question, "This works well according to the one who says," etc., and Tosafos likewise understood it. The Rif, however, explains the Gemara's question like Tosafos's question, namely why the creditor should take the improvement at all. Tosafos there write that according to the conclusion, etc., and other poskim likewise write this. Rambam also writes there in halacha 6 that with an apotiki the creditor takes half the improvement without reimbursing the expenditure, while for the other half he takes it but reimburses the expenditure. This follows the Rif's approach that where the debt equals the land and the improvement, he need not reimburse the expenditure. According to our ruling, however, that by virtue of the after-acquired-property clause he takes only half the improvement, the other half that he takes is by virtue of the apotiki, and therefore he necessarily reimburses the expenditure on that portion. Rambam's words are thus correct according to the Rif's approach. According to Rambam's own method, however, he reimburses the expenditure from the entire improvement. As for a case where the debt equals only the land, Rambam says nothing, because the beginning of his discussion concerns a debt equal to the land and the improvement, as he writes at the beginning of the chapter, "How so," etc. See there. End.
