If the borrower wrote an after-acquired-property clause, etc. Some authorities hold that even an after-acquired-property clause is included through a scribal presumption. I discussed this above in siman 112. The Sema argues at length that even those who reject such a presumption in a loan nevertheless agree that, as between seller and purchaser concerning improvement, it is as though the seller wrote an after-acquired-property clause. His words do not appear correct. Nothing of this sort is implied by the Rif, Rosh, Nimukei Yosef, or Tur. Their ruling that a purchaser may recover the improvement from the seller even when improvement was not expressly mentioned rests upon the general presumption that the seller accepted responsibility for the principal and improvement customarily included in a sale. The seller personally undertakes to indemnify the purchaser for the field, its labor, and its improvement. That allows the purchaser to collect from property already owned by the seller. It does not prove that future improvement itself becomes encumbered through an unwritten after-acquired-property clause. The Rama's later statement that an express exclusion of after-acquired property prevents recovery merely addresses all views, since he already cited the opinion that such responsibility may otherwise be presumed. The primary rule is that there is no distinction between a lender and a purchaser in this respect. The Bach also rejects the Sema, and although his proof from Maharshal is not decisive, his understanding of the Rosh and of the law is correct.
After-acquired property, etc. The Mechaber's wording may imply that even automatic appreciation can be recovered only by virtue of an after-acquired-property clause. The Rambam's wording is different. He first states that a creditor collects improvement produced by the purchaser, whether through expenditure or automatically. If the appreciation occurred automatically, the creditor takes all of it; if it resulted from expenditure, he takes half. Only afterward does the Rambam give the case of Reuven, who borrowed from Shimon with an after-acquired-property clause, purchased a field, sold it to Yehudah, and Yehudah improved it through expenditure. This wording supports what I write below in seif katan 5.
He also recovers the improvement, etc. Below, seif katan 10, I explain that the reason is that the purchaser has recourse against the seller. Later I cite Tosafot and the other authorities concerning heirs, who resemble gift recipients because they have no one against whom to recover. The Gemara states that a firstborn does not receive a double portion of improvement and a wife does not collect it for her ketubah, and then asks from Shmuel's ruling that a creditor collects improvement. It answers that this is one of the leniencies of a ketubah. The daughters' maintenance is likewise a condition of the ketubah. The passage concerns improvement made by heirs, for if it concerned purchasers, the daughters could not collect even the land itself. Maharsha asks that if the purchaser improved after the seller's death, he too has no effective recourse. In my view there is no difficulty. The decisive consideration is the purchaser's intention when he invested in the property. He relied upon the seller's contractual responsibility for the field and its improvement, not upon payment from the creditor. This remains true even if the seller later died. Moreover, even improvement made after the seller's death may be recovered by the creditor, because the purchaser may still discover assets of the seller among the heirs or elsewhere and recover under the seller's undertaking. He therefore never improves the property with the expectation that the creditor will compensate him. Heirs are different, since all inherited assets already belong to them and they have no separate party against whom to recover. This is the correct distinction.
Others say that in every case, etc. Even these authorities agree that an express apotiki is different. The Maggid Mishneh writes in the name of Rashba and Rabbi Yosef HaLevi that, absent an express apotiki, a creditor takes only half the improvement, even where the appreciation occurred automatically and the document contains an after-acquired-property clause. Ramban likewise distinguishes an express apotiki. The reason is that, according to the authorities who permit collection beyond the amount of the debt from an express apotiki, the field is treated as though sold to the creditor, so he may say, "My land appreciated." Accordingly, even without an after-acquired-property clause, he takes the appreciation from the purchaser. Where the improvement resulted from expenditure, he takes the entire improvement but reimburses the expenditure, since it was his land that improved. According to the alternate view discussed below, however, an express apotiki never permits collection beyond the debt. Rashi and Baal HaMaor follow this approach. Under their view, even an express apotiki yields only half the improvement for the authorities presently cited, and only by virtue of an after-acquired-property clause. This explains the carefully abbreviated language of the Maggid Mishneh. The Beit Yosef's statement that Baal HaMaor agrees that an express apotiki permits recovery of all automatic appreciation is therefore imprecise. Baal HaMaor does not permit it. A practical difference remains even according to my conclusion below that ordinary automatic appreciation is treated as already owned by the debtor. Where the original item changed form, such as unripe grain becoming full ears or blossoms becoming dates, the resulting enhancement is after-acquired property. According to Baal HaMaor and those who agree with him, even an express apotiki gives the creditor only half.
This appears to me to be the primary ruling. The Rama apparently followed the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, which understood the Maggid Mishneh as reporting that Rashba, Baal HaMaor, Ramban, and the Geonim reject the Rambam's distinction between automatic appreciation and improvement through expenditure, and always award only half. The Tur likewise notes that the Rosh did not expressly distinguish the cases. In my view, however, the Rambam means that automatic appreciation is recovered even without an after-acquired-property clause, and his ruling is compelled by the Gemara and is supported by many authorities. The Gemara in Mi SheMet teaches that a creditor receives only half of improvement because it is after-acquired property. That reasoning concerns improvement produced through expenditure. Automatic growth is treated differently in Yesh Nochalin. A firstborn receives a double portion of automatic appreciation because it is regarded as property already possessed by the father, whereas he does not receive a double portion of buildings erected by the heirs or other improvement produced through their efforts. Rav Pappa distinguishes a palm that thickened or land upon which silt accumulated, which all agree is possessed property, from grain that matured or blossoms that became dates, where the item changed form. The Rif, Rosh, Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber all rule accordingly. Thus whatever is sufficiently possessed for a firstborn's double portion is likewise not considered after-acquired property for purposes of a creditor's lien. I found no clear source showing that the Geonim, Baal HaMaor, Ramban, or Rashba disputed this point. Their statements may concern automatic improvement involving a change of form, such as unripe grain becoming full ears, or the Rambam's example of trees that newly appeared. The latter should perhaps be read as trees that thickened. In any event, the Rambam's distinction is primary. Baal HaTerumot and the Semag also state it explicitly. The objection that the Gemara could have answered its question by establishing the case as automatic appreciation is not difficult, because the Mishna says that the purchaser improved the field, implying active improvement, and because the Mishna addresses improvement generally. The Beit Yosef also misunderstood Nimukei Yosef. Nimukei Yosef says only that without an express apotiki the creditor cannot collect more than the amount of his debt, even from automatic appreciation. He does not say that the creditor receives only half when the debt itself covers the full value. Earlier he expressly states that automatic appreciation, such as a palm that thickened, silt deposited upon land, or market appreciation, is treated as though it existed at the time of the lien. The creditor therefore takes all of it, even from heirs or a gift recipient. The Beit Yosef appears to have seen the conclusion without the earlier explanation. The resulting rule is clear. Automatic appreciation that does not involve a change of identity, including an increase in market value, a palm that thickened, or land upon which silt accumulated, is not after-acquired property. The creditor takes all of it from the purchaser. Whatever a firstborn receives as a double portion because it is not considered prospective property is likewise treated as already owned for this law.

Half of the improvement. The Rosh writes that if the land was worth one hundred gold coins and the improvement ten, the purchaser receives ten parts and the creditor one part. The Beit Yosef found this astonishing. Agudat Ezov, Maadanei Yom Tov, and the Bach explain that the Rosh follows Rabbenu Chananel's view that competing creditors divide proportionately according to their respective claims. Rabbenu Yerucham writes similarly. Since we rule in siman 104 against Rabbenu Chananel, the improvement here is divided equally in all cases. This is why the authorities state without qualification that the creditor takes half.
The amount by which the improvement exceeds the expenditure. Some printed editions of the Rambam, following the Maggid Mishneh and Tur, read that the purchaser receives the expenditure and the creditor only the excess. Other reliable manuscripts read like the Rif. The Beit Yosef initially accepted those manuscripts and later reconsidered. In my view the Rambam agrees with the Rif. Baal HaTerumot and the Semag also understand him that way. In any event, even if the Rambam held the alternate view, the Rif refuted it convincingly, the Gemara supports the Rif, and the ancient authorities overwhelmingly rule that the creditor takes the improvement without reimbursing expenditure. Rav Hai Gaon, Baal HaItur, Baal HaMaor, Ramban, Tosafot, the Rosh, Nimukei Yosef, Hagahot Mordechai, the Semag, Baal HaTerumot, and many others agree. This is the practical law.
Others say, etc. I have already written that this is the primary ruling. The Rif, Rosh, and Nimukei Yosef explain that the purchaser is unlike one who improves another's land without permission. Such a person improves in order to receive reimbursement from the landowner. A purchaser improves in reliance upon the seller, since the seller undertakes to indemnify him for the purchase, labor, and improvement if a creditor removes the land. He did not act in reliance upon the creditor. This is the main rationale. A purchaser from a thief is different, because the true owner himself would have had to reimburse the expenditure had the thief made it, and the purchaser succeeds to whatever right the thief could have asserted.
The purchaser then recovers, etc. The Maggid Mishneh asks why the creditor may collect from this purchaser when later encumbered property exists. He answers that the later property may have been acquired only after the loan and not covered by the creditor's lien, while it was covered by the seller's responsibility to the purchaser; or the seller wrote an after-acquired-property clause to the purchaser but not to the creditor; or the later property is in another country and the creditor need not incur disproportionate expense to collect there. The last rule applies even to unencumbered property. At first glance, he could simply have answered that the debtor acquired and sold the later property only after the creditor had already dispossessed the purchaser. Tosafot reject that answer because then the purchaser could recover not only the principal but also the now-fixed amount of improvement from those later purchasers. This also explains why the Maggid Mishneh does not ask about later unencumbered property, since it may have been acquired after the dispossession. Tosafot further answer that the original field was made an apotiki, so the creditor may take it despite later property. The Maggid Mishneh's first answer remains difficult. If the creditor has no lien upon after-acquired property, how can he collect improvement from the purchaser? The Bach answers that when neither creditor nor purchaser received an after-acquired-property clause, they share equally. This is not correct. The creditor has no basis to take after-acquired improvement already acquired by the purchaser merely because the purchaser also lacks a lien. Moreover, the Maggid Mishneh's case expressly includes an after-acquired-property clause in favor of the purchaser. The Sema and Ir Shushan cite only the answer involving property in another country, perhaps for this reason. The Bach also adopts a reading of Tosafot that a first purchaser cannot redirect the creditor to later purchasers of after-acquired property, because the creditor's lien attached first to the original field. This novel distinction is doubtful and is contradicted by the general language of the authorities, Tosafot in Bava Metzia, the Maggid Mishneh, Ir Shushan, and the Sema. The better reading of Tosafot does not support the Bach.
The principal, etc. This matter requires study. The Gemara and authorities imply that the purchaser recovers the entire principal from encumbered property, even though he has already retained half the improvement. Gidulei Terumah objects that the purchaser's right to half the improvement appears to arise from the seller's responsibility for the original purchase money. If so, that amount should reduce the principal later recovered from purchasers. It appears, however, that the purchaser's right to improvement is itself part of the sale. At the time of sale, the seller undertook to maintain for him both the body of the land and the future improvement, or their value if removed. Although future improvement cannot presently be conveyed as an existing object, the seller can obligate himself to deliver it or its value when it comes into existence. Thus the seller undertook two obligations at the time of sale, one for the land and one for the improvement. The Tur's statement that the seller is treated as having acquired and sold the improvement means that the improvement was included in the original contractual undertaking, not that a new sale occurred later. One might therefore say that the purchaser retains half the improvement because that improvement itself was sold to him, and still recovers the full principal from later encumbered property. Against the creditor, however, the purchaser may need to rely upon his lien for the principal because the creditor's lien to after-acquired improvement is prior. Since the rule against collecting improvement from encumbered property is an enactment only against affirmative recovery, the purchaser may retain what is already in his possession. The Sema and the Rosh's proportional calculation, however, indicate that the purchaser's retained half is credited toward his principal. If so, the broad statement that he recovers the principal from encumbered property is difficult. Perhaps even according to the Rosh he may recover the full principal from later purchasers because, as against them, the sale of the improvement itself is effective, though not against the earlier creditor. Alternatively, the authorities' phrase "he recovers the principal" may mean the principal less the half-improvement already retained. The point requires further study.
But the improvement, etc., he recovers from the seller's property, etc. A seller customarily writes that he will uphold, clear, and indemnify the purchase, its labor, and its improvement. The Rambam rules that this responsibility applies even if nothing was expressly written, because it is a known incident of a sale. The Maggid Mishneh states that the authorities agree. In my view this is explicit in the Gemara, which rules that the purchaser receives both his money and improvement even when improvement was not specified, because responsibility is presumed both in loan documents and deeds of sale. Although that passage discusses a field discovered not to belong to the seller, the ruling necessarily shows that responsibility for improvement is presumed in an ordinary sale as well. Rashi, Baal HaMaor, and the Rosh explain it this way.
The improvement, etc., he recovers from the seller's property, etc. This includes both improvement produced through expenditure and automatic appreciation. Thus if he bought the field for one hundred and its market value rose to two hundred, he recovers two hundred from the seller. We do not say that he paid only one hundred and performed no labor. This is explicit in the Rosh, the other authorities, and the Tur and Mechaber in siman 116.
Only from unencumbered property. He does not recover improvement from property sold to others. The Rambam and Rashi explain that the amount is not fixed; the Rif and Rosh explain that it was not written in a manner that generated public notice. See what I wrote in siman 60. Tosafot indicate that if later purchasers acquired property only after the creditor had already removed the improvement, the purchaser may recover from them because the amount was then fixed. The rationale based upon lack of public notice suggests no such distinction, since the original undertaking had no effective notice when written. The general language of the Rambam and other authorities also suggests that improvement is never recovered from purchasers, even those who bought after the dispossession. Maharsha understands Tosafot in Mi SheMet this way. Perhaps the Sages made a uniform rule that an obligation initially unliquidated cannot later be collected from purchasers merely because it became fixed. The practical law requires study.
All this applies only to improvement made by the property, etc. The Rama's language implies that even automatic appreciation is excluded if it occurred after the seller's death. Nimukei Yosef, however, says specifically that improvement made by the purchaser after the seller's death is excluded because after-acquired-property liability cannot attach to something the seller never acquired. Nimukei Yosef elsewhere treats ordinary automatic appreciation as property already owned, not as after-acquired property. Even regarding improvement made by the purchaser, his limitation does not appear correct. We do not literally say that the seller acquired the improvement from the purchaser. Rather, because the enhancement arose from the seller's land, it is treated as though acquired by the seller for purposes of the preexisting lien. If this reasoning applies while the seller lives, it should apply after his death as well. The creditor's lien preceded the purchaser's possession of the field. This is supported by the rule that a creditor collects from heirs the excess improvement over expenditure, and, according to many authorities, collects transformed natural growth such as unripe grain that became full ears or blossoms that became dates, even though all of this arose after the father's death. The reason is that the enhancement came from the father's land and therefore falls under the creditor's lien. The same should apply to a purchaser. The Talmudic discussions concerning appraisal of improvement for purchasers and heirs also imply that a creditor may collect improvement from a purchaser even when it arose after the seller's death. Otherwise the Gemara could have resolved its questions by establishing the case after death. The authorities state generally that a creditor collects improvement from a purchaser and do not distinguish between improvement made during the seller's lifetime and afterward. The primary ruling is therefore that even improvement made after the seller's death remains subject to the creditor's after-acquired-property lien.
All produce consumed by the purchaser. The phrase is not limited to produce actually eaten. Once produce is detached from the land, it is movable property and the creditor cannot recover it from the purchaser. The Sema explains this through the rule that when two creditors hold liens upon after-acquired property, the first to collect prevails. That is not the relevant principle. Even produce still lying before the purchaser is beyond the creditor's reach once detached. Ramban, the Maggid Mishneh, the Rosh, and the other authorities explain that detached produce is movable property, which a creditor cannot seize from purchasers. For the same reason, according to most authorities, produce that no longer needs the soil is treated as detached. If the creditor seized such produce, it is removed from him, because it is movable property acquired by the purchaser.
But all produce, etc., even though it does not need the soil, etc. This is the Rambam's language, but it is difficult because elsewhere the Rambam treats grapes and similar produce that no longer needs the soil as detached for all laws other than bailees. Lechem Mishneh leaves the matter unresolved. It appears that the Rambam follows Rabbi Yosef HaLevi's rationale concerning bailees: property entrusted for safekeeping was not delivered for immediate severance and therefore remains land. Likewise, when the creditor takes the land together with its produce, he may say that he presently intends to leave the produce attached. The ketubah case is different, because maintenance requires severing the dates while the palm remains with the heirs. For practical law, however, I concluded in siman 95 against the Rambam. Rashi, Baal HaItur, Ramban, the Rosh, Nimukei Yosef, Raavad, Hagahot Ashri, the Semag, Hagahot Maimoniyot, Mordechai, Rabbenu Chananel, and many others rule that produce no longer requiring the soil is treated as detached and cannot be collected by a creditor from a purchaser. Rashi explains that "improvement fit to be carried on the shoulders" means produce that has fully grown but still needs the soil slightly. Produce that no longer needs the soil is complete produce, not land-improvement. Ramban defines produce that needs the soil as produce that improves if left attached, while produce that deteriorates if left attached is considered detached. This is the primary definition. Baal HaMaor and Rashba go further and hold that a creditor does not collect even produce that still needs the soil, interpreting "improvement fit to be carried" differently. Their broader view is not primary, but it certainly rejects the Rambam regarding produce that does not need the soil. The Gemara concerning a firstborn also supports the distinction. A firstborn does not take transformed natural growth, such as unripe grain that became full ears or blossoms that became dates, because the object changed, though ordinary automatic thickening remains possessed property. Such transformed growth is nevertheless called improvement for a creditor, but complete detached produce is not. The conclusion follows the many authorities cited above: produce that no longer needs the soil is treated as detached and the creditor cannot recover it from the purchaser.
Even though it does not need, etc. I am surprised that the Rama cited no dissent, although the Beit Yosef himself records that the Rosh, Rashi, Ramban, Baal HaItur, and the Maggid Mishneh disagree, and the Tur also appears to do so. As shown in the preceding seif katan, many authorities hold that produce needing no soil is fully detached. The reason is that the creditor did not rely upon it, since the purchaser may sever it at will. This applies where the field was not made an express apotiki. With an express apotiki, according to Tosafot, the Rosh, Rav Hai Gaon, and the Halachot, the field is treated as sold to the creditor, who may say, "My land appreciated." The creditor therefore takes even produce that no longer needs the soil, though he reimburses the purchaser for plowing and weeding. The Tur states this expressly. It appears that under this approach the creditor may recover even produce already detached or consumed, just as an owner recovers produce grown on his land from a purchaser who bought from a thief. The Tur's wording indicates that the apotiki rule qualifies both consumed and detached produce. Under the approach of Rashi, Baal HaMaor, and the Maggid Mishneh, however, an express apotiki merely prevents the purchaser from redeeming the field with money. It does not treat the land as already sold to the creditor. Accordingly, even with an apotiki, the creditor does not recover produce, just as an ordinary creditor does not. Baal HaMaor states this explicitly.
If the purchaser wishes to remove him, and so forth. The Beit Yosef cites Baal HaTerumot: where the purchaser or the heirs wish to remove the creditor with money, but the land is worth only half the debt, and the creditor claims, “To me it is worth the amount of my entire debt,” some say that he cannot make this claim. The Raavad says that he can, just as in the case of the goats, where a claimant may assert a claim up to their value. If he says, “I will take the goats for one hundred,” he is heard, and the owner cannot say, “I will pay you only what the goats are worth to everyone else.” Ramban likewise ruled like the Raavad. The Beit Yosef concludes that the Tur in siman 72 follows the Raavad and Ramban. This also appears explicitly from Baal HaTerumot. His later difficulty with the Raavad concerns the goats, because the debtor does not admit owing so much. The Tur nevertheless disagrees there with Baal HaTerumot and follows the Raavad. Gidulei Terumah objects that the Tur there merely says that the possessor is believed, through a migo that the item was purchased, to say that the debtor owes him that amount; this would not apply here. The objection is unfounded. Even if he is believed that the debt is that amount, the utensil is presently not his, so why may he retain it for more than its market value? The Tur must therefore accept the Raavad’s principle, as the Beit Yosef understood. I am nevertheless troubled by the Beit Yosef. At the beginning of siman 109, the Tur plainly follows the Rosh in the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui, not the Raavad. One must say that in siman 72 the Tur follows the Raavad only with movable property. Movables are subject to the law of possession, and one who holds them for a debt is regarded as holding what is his until the debtor removes him by paying the debt. Land is not subject to that law of possession. The Beit Yosef is also internally difficult: here he appears to accept the Raavad and Ramban, while at the beginning of siman 109 he codifies the Tur and Rosh. As a matter of law, the primary ruling is that of Ramban, the Raavad, and Baal HaTerumot. Unless the purchaser removes the creditor by paying the entire debt, the creditor may seize the land and say that it is worth to him the full debt, even after the debtor has died and even though the debtor gains nothing thereby, because this land is encumbered for the entire debt. Rivash ruled accordingly, and this is implied by the Gemara in the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui, contrary to the Rosh and Tur in siman 109. Consequently, even if the purchaser seeks to remove the creditor before beit din has appraised and advertised this field on the debtor’s behalf, the same rule applies, contrary to the Sema in siman 114.
