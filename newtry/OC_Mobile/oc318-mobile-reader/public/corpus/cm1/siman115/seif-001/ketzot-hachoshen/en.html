Where the borrower wrote an after acquired property clause, the creditor can collect appreciation as well. The Gemara in Perek Mi SheMet uses the law of appreciation to prove that after acquired property can be subjected, because the improvement did not exist at the time of the loan. One might ask why this proves after acquired lien at all. A person who cannot transfer future fruits can nevertheless transfer a palm tree for its fruits. Perhaps the borrower likewise subjects the land for its future improvement. The analogy fails. One who owns a palm tree for its fruits has an actual property interest in the palm itself for that purpose and can say that his own palm produced the fruit. A creditor has no ownership in the land merely by virtue of lien. A creditor collects only from the time of collection onward. He cannot say, "My land produced the improvement." He reaches the improvement only if the improvement itself was subjected. Thus appreciation is true after acquired property for lien purposes. Maharit cites scholars who said that if a debtor subjects both present and future property, the first creditor's lien extends automatically to all later proceeds and substitutes of present assets forever, like a palm tree for its fruits. Maharit rejects this. Each future asset, whether it comes from an unrelated source or from sale of an existing asset, requires its own after acquired lien. Language subjecting the existing thing does not automatically subject all future substitutes. I would go further. Even if the document expressly says, in effect, "my present property and its future products," the analogy to a palm tree for fruits does not work for a lien. In acquisition, the buyer owns the body for the fruit and therefore the fruit is produced by his property. A creditor owns no body of property before collection. Even an express apotiki does not necessarily let him say the improvement is produced by his land. Therefore future appreciation must itself be subjected as after acquired property. This seems clear.
Where appreciation results from the purchaser's expenditures, the creditor takes only half of the appreciation. In my view even appreciation exceeding the amount of the expenditures remains in this category. It requires an after acquired property clause and is not treated like spontaneous appreciation. Nimukei Yosef indicates this in Bava Metzia and Perek Mi SheMet. Rabbeinu Yerucham, however, as cited by Beit Yosef, appears to say that the amount of appreciation above the purchaser's expenses has the law of spontaneous appreciation. If so, it can be collected from a gift recipient and should likewise be treated like spontaneous appreciation regarding after acquired property. The Shach appears internally inconsistent. In one place he treats appreciation above expenditures as still requiring an after acquired clause and collectible only in half, not as spontaneous appreciation. Later, regarding a gift recipient, he treats the excess above expenditures as spontaneous appreciation and allows collection. The two positions cannot easily stand together. I will return to this below.
The creditor collects half of the appreciation. The reason is that the appreciation arose only after both the creditor's loan and the purchaser's warranty claim had come into existence, so the borrower is treated as one who incurred two obligations and afterward acquired property, in which case both claims share. I was uncertain where Reuven borrowed from Shimon in Nisan, sold the field to Levi in Iyar, borrowed from Yehudah in Sivan, and the field appreciated only in Tammuz. It might seem that Yehudah should share because the appreciation arose after his loan as well. In my view he does not. The Sages subjected appreciation to the earlier creditor only because the body of the field had already been encumbered to him before the sale, and they strengthened his lien so that the sale would not reduce his recovery. A later creditor whose loan arose only after the field had already been sold never had a lien in the field itself. Since the borrower never owned the later appreciation, that later creditor has no lien in it either. Rambam's formulation supports this: he describes the parties sharing appreciation where both creditor liens preceded the purchaser's acquisition. This shows that the lien in appreciation follows the lien in the body of the land. Where the body was never subjected to the later creditor, the later appreciation is not subjected to him either.
The purchaser then returns and collects the principal from the seller. Maggid Mishneh asks why the first creditor can seize from the earlier purchaser when later encumbered property exists, since the purchaser can say, "I left you property from which to collect." He answers, under Rambam's view that an after acquired lien is not automatic, that the later property may have been acquired only after the loan and subjected to the purchaser's warranty but not to the lender. That answer is difficult if the lender needs an after acquired clause in order to collect the appreciation itself. Rabbi Betzalel Ashkenazi cites a view that appreciation is collectible even without an express after acquired clause, because the seller's standard warranty language is treated as though he expressly subjected the land and all future appreciation. On that approach Maggid Mishneh can be sustained. Bach offers another explanation: even if the lender has an after acquired clause, the purchaser cannot divert him from property that was already encumbered at the time of the loan to property acquired only later. The Shach objects that this is a novel distinction and reads Tosafot differently. In my view Bach's distinction is strongly supported by the sugyot concerning appreciation. The creditor's original lien is the primary lien on which he relied when lending. Although after acquired property can later become encumbered, the purchaser cannot compel him to abandon his original lien and satisfy himself from a later secondary lien. The Gemara's discussion of whether a purchaser can redeem the creditor with money also supports this. If the purchaser could freely shift the creditor from the original land to later acquired appreciation, he could satisfy the creditor from the appreciation itself to the amount of the debt and retain the balance. Yet the Gemara treats the creditor as entitled to take the original land and pay the purchaser for the protected appreciation. This indicates that the original lien remains primary. The cases where a purchaser threatens to return inferior land to the seller do not contradict this. There the inferior field itself was already among the debtor's assets when the original loan was made, and returning its document merely restores the creditor's original security. It is not a redirection from original property to truly after acquired property. Accordingly, where the later assets are genuine after acquired property, there is substantial support for Bach that the purchaser cannot force the creditor away from his original lien.
The purchaser collects the principal from the seller. Some authorities ask why he may collect the full principal after he already received half of the appreciation. In my view the half appreciation he received is compensation for his warranty concerning the appreciation itself, not payment of the principal purchase price. A purchaser's warranty claim is an independent debt. Since an earlier oral debt can have priority over a later written debt under the accepted ruling, the purchaser's warranty claim can remain collectible despite another creditor. The key distinction is that where a person lacks an after acquired clause, property acquired later was never subjected to him at all, and he can recover from the debtor personally only because of the debtor's obligation to pay. Here, however, the purchaser has an actual warranty obligation and, once the appreciation is fixed, that obligation can stand against other creditors according to the ordinary rules of priority. The fact that the purchaser had already obtained some appreciation does not extinguish the principal warranty claim. He therefore returns and collects the principal from the seller's available property.
If the purchaser wishes to redeem the creditor, the authorities dispute when the creditor may say, "To me the land is worth my entire debt." Sema distinguishes between a living borrower and heirs, allowing the creditor to insist on the full debt while the borrower lives because that benefits the borrower, but not after death. The Shach holds that the creditor may make that claim in all cases. I have explained elsewhere that even where the creditor does not expressly say the land is worth his full debt, the purchaser cannot redeem him merely at market value unless the purchaser himself is prepared to treat the land as worth more than the debt. Once he does so, he must pay the entire debt. This issue differs from the case of a pledge where the creditor claims a debt greater than the pledge's value without independent witnesses. There the creditor relies only on a migo from possession, and some authorities limit that migo to the pledge's actual value. Here the debt itself is established. The creditor therefore can insist that he will not release land specifically encumbered to him until the whole debt is satisfied. The episode of the woman holding a bundle of debt instruments likewise supports the rule that a creditor can retain pledged property until full payment of the established debt.
