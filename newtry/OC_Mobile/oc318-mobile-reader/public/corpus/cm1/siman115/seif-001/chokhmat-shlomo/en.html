Seif 1: When a creditor comes to seize property. Note: See Shach, who states a new rule that even if a husband wrote a clause subjecting after-acquired property, such property does not become subject to his wife’s ketubah, etc. With respect, it appears that he overlooked two glosses of the Rama in Even HaEzer siman 90. At the end of that siman the Rama writes that a woman who waived her power over her husband’s property before he acquired it, and he later acquired it, cannot seize it because a waiver is effective before the property comes into existence. If the Shach were correct that after-acquired property is never subject to her ketubah even when the husband expressly wrote a future-property clause, why should the Rama base the result on her waiver? Even without any waiver she could not collect from after-acquired property. Earlier in the same siman the Rama also discusses a Jew who held a non-Jew’s land as collateral, etc. According to the Shach, such property is after-acquired and the wife should have no ability to collect from it at all. These rulings therefore require study according to the Shach. The source of the present law is Bava Metzia 15a. The Gemara states that Rav holds a purchaser from a robber can recover both the purchase money and improvements, while Shmuel holds that he recovers the purchase money but not the improvements. The Gemara then asks what the law is if the seller expressly stipulated responsibility for the improvements. According to Tosafot there, Shmuel generally holds that in an ordinary sale, responsibility is not automatically read into the transaction; yet in a sale by a robber he agrees that responsibility for the principal is implied because the transaction is treated in some respect like a loan. This raises a difficulty. Why does the Gemara ask specifically about an express stipulation concerning improvements? It could have asked a prior question where the seller did not expressly mention improvements but did expressly accept general responsibility. Perhaps Shmuel’s concern that collection of improvements resembles interest exists only where responsibility was not stated. In that case, if the transaction were treated as a genuine sale there would be no implied responsibility, so the purchaser’s ability to recover the principal proves that the law treats the money as a loan. If the entire recovery is therefore based upon the loan character, allowing recovery of improvements would then look like interest, and the two positions would contradict each other. But if the seller expressly accepted general responsibility, even an ordinary seller from whom a creditor later seizes the field must reimburse the purchaser. Recovery of the principal would then not depend upon treating the payment as a loan. Perhaps in that case recovery of the improvements would no longer resemble interest. Or perhaps Shmuel’s concern applies in every case. The Gemara could have framed its question this way. Since it does not, the point requires study.
