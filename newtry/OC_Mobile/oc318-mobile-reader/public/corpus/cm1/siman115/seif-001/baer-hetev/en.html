Property acquired later. The Sema writes that this is according to the view that a clause encumbering later acquired property is not automatically supplied as a scribal omission, as explained at the beginning of siman 112. Even according to that view, however, such a clause is needed only in the borrower's obligation to the lender. In a sale, regarding improvement to the land, all agree that it is treated as though the seller wrote a later acquired property clause. The Shach challenges this. See there.
By themselves. The reason is that where the property improved automatically, the creditor can say to the purchaser, "You entered into property already subject to my lien. Had you not purchased it, it would have improved by itself in the debtor's possession and I would have collected the entire improvement for my debt." This does not apply to improvement produced by the purchaser's expenditures. Sema.
Matter. The Shach writes that even the latter authorities agree that where the field was made an explicit apotiki, the creditor collects the entire improvement. See there.
Primary. The Shach discusses this at length and proves that many authorities agree with the Rambam that automatically produced improvement may be seized even without an explicit later acquired property clause. He concludes that there is at least one clear rule: automatic improvement, such as a rise in market value, a palm tree growing stronger, land enlarged by sediment, and every type of increase in which a firstborn receives a double portion and that is not considered merely prospective with respect to him, is not treated as property not yet in existence for purposes of a later acquired property clause. The creditor collects the entire such increase from the purchaser. The Shach says this appears as clear as daylight.
Half. The Rosh writes that if the land was worth one hundred gold coins and the improvement was worth ten, the purchaser receives ten shares of the improvement and the lender one share. The Beit Yosef expresses surprise. Imrei Noam, Maadanei Yom Tov to the first chapter of Bava Metzia, and the Bach discuss ways to reconcile the Rosh, based on Rabbeinu Chananel's view in siman 104, seif 11, that creditors divide proportionally to the amounts of their claims. Since in siman 104, seif 14, we do not follow Rabbeinu Chananel, here the improvement is divided equally in all cases. This explains why the other authorities simply write that the creditor collects half the improvement. Shach.
At all. The Shach has already written that this is the primary ruling. The Rif, Rosh, and Nimukei Yosef explain that this case is unlike one who enters another's land without authorization, who may recover his expenditures from the owner. There he improves the property with the expectation of taking reimbursement from the landowner. Here he did not make the improvements intending to recover from the creditor, but because if the creditor removes the land from him he can seek reimbursement from the seller under the standard warranty clause. Even though one who buys a field that turns out not to belong to the seller may recover his expenditures from the true owner, as stated in siman 373, that case differs. Had the true owner removed the land directly from the seller, the seller himself would have been obligated to reimburse the expenditures, and the purchaser acquired no greater rights than the seller had. Here, however, the seller already owes the creditor an amount corresponding to the value of the improvement and expenditures. Shach.
From a later creditor. This case can arise where the later creditors are in another province. We do not require the lender to spend two hundred in order to collect one hundred merely so as to leave local property for creditors whose claims arose earlier. The same law applies even where there is completely unencumbered property, as stated in siman 111, seif 11. So writes the Maggid Mishneh. See the Shach.
The improvement. The Shach writes that this implies that both improvement produced by expenditures and automatic improvement may be recovered by the purchaser from the seller's property. For example, if he bought for one hundred and the property rose in value to two hundred, we do not say that because he paid the seller only one hundred and performed no act to create the additional value, he may recover only what he paid. Rather, he recovers two hundred from the seller. This is explicit in the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia and other authorities, and in the Tur and Shulchan Aruch at the beginning of siman 116.
Unencumbered. The Sema explains that for the sake of proper commercial order the purchaser may recover improvement only from the seller's unencumbered assets, because improvement has no public notice and later purchasers would not know to protect themselves from buying assets needed to cover it. The Rambam explains instead that the improvement is not a fixed amount. Rashi gives the same reason. The Rif and Rosh say that it is because the improvement is not written in the document. See the Shach.
To the purchaser. The Shach challenges this law at length. See there.
That he consumed. It need not literally mean that the purchaser consumed the produce. Once the produce is no longer attached to the ground it is as though he already collected it. The Sema connects this to the rule that where two loans arose before the debtor acquired property, if one creditor first collected, his collection stands. The Shach writes that this reasoning is incorrect. Priority of collection has nothing to do with the present rule. Even if the detached produce remains lying there, the creditor cannot seize it, because once detached it is movable property, and a creditor may not seize movable property from a purchaser. This is explicit from the Ramban and Maggid Mishneh and implied by the Rosh and others. For the same reason, according to most authorities, even attached produce that no longer needs the soil cannot be seized because it is treated as detached. Accordingly, if the creditor first seized detached produce or produce ready to be detached, it is taken back from him, because it is movable property acquired by the purchaser. The Shach says this is clear.
Need. The Rosh holds that the produce must still need the soil to some degree. Sema. See the Shach.
To remove him. See what the Shach writes in the name of Baal HaTerumot, and above siman 114, seif 3. The matter requires study. See what is written there in seif katan 2.
Does not. If the seller wishes to litigate with the creditor, he may remove him by paying money. So write the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia and the Tur at the beginning of siman 226. Shach.
