And some say. This is specifically Tosafos' view. Tosafos holds that a creditor may collect even improvement created by heirs and need not reimburse their expenses at all, because a son is treated as standing in his father's place, effectively like the borrower himself. If the debt does not equal the improvement, any excess over the debt belongs to the heirs. But where the property was made an apotiki, the creditor must at least reimburse the heirs' expenses. One might ask that the apotiki then disadvantages the creditor: without it he would take the improvement without reimbursing expenses, while with it he must reimburse. With respect to heirs we do not consider this objection decisive. The very rule that heirs must surrender their own improvement is itself a stringency against them, and it is enough to impose that stringency where there is no apotiki. Tosafos expressly distinguishes heirs from purchasers in this respect. See Tumim above. This applies even where no after acquired property clause was written at all, because such a clause is unnecessary against heirs. As a matter of halachah, all the later authorities reject Tosafos on this point. A creditor does not collect improvement created by heirs, and that is the accepted ruling.
