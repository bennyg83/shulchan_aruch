When does this apply? When he made it a specifically designated lien. This implies that even where the debt equals the land and its appreciation, and the field was specifically designated, the creditor must reimburse the heirs’ expenditures. All the authorities so rule, and the Gemara requires it. The Beit Yosef suggested that according to the Rif, if the debt equals the land and appreciation the creditor takes everything without paying the heirs, because the field was designated. That is incorrect. The Rif’s phrase that the debt equals only the land was stated with reference to a purchaser. It does not eliminate the heirs’ right to reimbursement for their own expenditures. Lechem Mishneh and the Sema likewise reject the Beit Yosef’s reading.
Some say, and he does not reimburse their expenditures at all. The Sema objects that no authority holds this. My teacher explained in the margin of his Sema that the gloss refers to the end of the siman, where the field was not specifically designated, and that it reflects Tosafot and the Rosh in HaMekabel. Bach and Maadanei Melekh explain similarly. In my view, the Sema certainly knew Tosafot, since the Beit Yosef cites them here. His point is that the Rama cannot have intended to rule that way. Earlier the Rama wrote that according to some authorities, if the field was not designated the creditor must prove that the father made the improvement. That plainly implies that if he fails to prove it, he does not take the improvement and must at least reimburse the heirs’ expenditures. Since the Rosh and the other authorities reject Tosafot’s position, the Rama would not normally rely upon it. It remains possible that the Rama first cited the moderate view and then added that some go further and deny reimbursement entirely. As a matter of law, however, Tosafot’s position was rejected above, and the Darkei Moshe also rules against it.
If the heirs wish to remove the creditor with money, they may remove him; and if they wish, and so forth. Rambam writes this, apparently even where the debt equals both the land and the appreciation. This is difficult. The Gemara’s question, “Let him say: give me a portion of land equal to the appreciation,” implies that when the debt equals the land and appreciation, the creditor takes the entire land. Although the discussion there concerns a purchaser, heirs should be the same for this purpose. The Rif also limits the heirs’ monetary option to a case where the debt equals only the land. Torat Chaim explains that where the debt equals the entire land and appreciation, removing the creditor from the whole field requires payment of the entire debt. Otherwise the creditor may say that the field is worth his full debt to him, following the Raavad and the authorities cited above. Even according to those who reject that claim where no additional land exists, this case is different: the creditor’s lien extends over the whole property, and if the heirs lack money, he should take the entire land and repay them only their proven expenditures. This creates difficulty in Rambam and the Mechaber’s statement that the heirs may instead take a portion of land equal to their improvement. One might explain that when they say they will remove him with money, they mean payment of the entire debt, but that is not the ordinary implication. Their reading may understand the Gemara’s challenge as applying even where the debt equals land and appreciation, as Rashi and Nimukei Yosef suggest. Nevertheless, the alternative reading better fits the sequence of the Gemara: the difficulty arises only after the case is reestablished as one where the debt equals the principal land alone. The practical law therefore requires further study.
To remove the creditor with money, and so forth. At first glance this means paying him the value of the land he would otherwise receive after deducting the heirs’ expenditures. According to the Raavad and the authorities whom I ruled primary above, however, the creditor may say that the land is worth the entire debt to him. They cannot remove him unless they pay the full debt, without deducting anything. The phrase may therefore mean that they remove him by satisfying the entire debt. Laws that emerge from this siman. Seif 1. When a creditor seizes land from a purchaser, he also seizes its appreciation, even if the land improved after the seller died. Spontaneous appreciation that is considered presently held property, such as an increase in market value, a palm tree’s growth, or alluvial soil, is taken in full. This applies even where the debtor excluded after-acquired property, and also to a wife’s ketubah and its conditions, because this is treated as property already in existence. Produce that changed form, such as fodder that became grain or blossoms that became dates, has the law of improvement caused by expenditure. Ordinarily the creditor takes only half of that appreciation and gives the purchaser no separate reimbursement for expenses, provided the after-acquired-property lien applies. If the debtor excluded such a lien in favor of the purchaser, the creditor takes none of that appreciation. Authorities dispute whether designation of the land as a specific lien changes these rules. The purchaser recovers the principal from the seller’s property, including encumbered property. Some say that the amount recovered from encumbered property is reduced by the half appreciation already counted toward principal. Appreciation seized by the creditor is recovered from the seller’s unencumbered property under the implied warranty of sale, but generally not from property sold onward, either because the amount lacked publicity or because it was not fixed. Some distinguish property sold after the appreciation was actually seized, when the amount became fixed. The creditor may seize from the purchaser only where the purchaser cannot direct him to other available property, such as where this field was specifically designated or the other property is elsewhere. Authorities dispute whether a purchaser may direct an earlier creditor to property bought later. Detached produce, or produce fully ready to detach and harmed by remaining attached, has the law of movables and cannot be seized. Produce still needing the ground has the law of appreciation. Where the field was specifically designated, some require the purchaser to return all produce. A purchaser may ordinarily remove the creditor with money, from both principal and appreciation, but must pay the entire debt if the creditor asserts that the land is worth that amount to him. If the debtor said that payment must come only from this field, the purchaser cannot remove the creditor with money. Seif 2. If the creditor takes only part of the land or appreciation, the remainder is divided only if it leaves the purchaser a legally usable unit. Otherwise the creditor pays the purchaser its value, unless he leaves enough for joint use and use is apportioned by time. Where the creditor’s share itself is too small to be useful, the land may be sold or leased and the creditor paid in money. If the field was specifically designated, some say the creditor takes all appreciation, even beyond the debt’s principal value, while reimbursing expenditures; others say designation changes only the purchaser’s inability to remove him with money, and the creditor still takes only the ordinary share of appreciation. If the seller himself litigates, or authorizes the purchaser to litigate for him, the seller may remove the creditor with money even from a specifically designated field. The creditor then takes no land or appreciation beyond the amount of his debt. Seif 3. A gift recipient who has no warranty recourse is reimbursed for his expenditures as one who improved another’s field without authorization, but the creditor takes all spontaneous appreciation and the net appreciation beyond those expenditures. A purchaser who expressly waived warranty has the same law. If the donor accepted warranty, the recipient has the law of a purchaser. Seif 4. Heirs have the law of gift recipients. Seif 5. Where heirs say that they made the improvement and demand reimbursement, while the creditor says that their father made it, the heirs bear the burden of proof, even without a specifically designated lien. The same applies to a purchaser where both sides claim the improvement under competing after-acquired-property liens. Seif 6. If the heirs prove that they made the improvement, they receive the lesser of their expenditures and the resulting appreciation. Where the field was specifically designated, they receive money rather than land. Where it was not designated, they may receive land equal to their expenditures and may remove the creditor with money from the field, but to defeat his claim that the field is worth the full debt they must pay the full debt. Some say that if the debt covers the land and all improvement and the heirs cannot pay it, the creditor may take the whole field and need only pay them money for proven expenditures.
