But a recipient of a gift, etc. An ordinary donee has no warranty from the donor, and the donor does not write that he will stand behind the gift. Since the donee therefore has no one against whom to recover, a creditor does not seize improvement from him. Even if the debtor wrote an after acquired property clause to the creditor, this improvement was not the debtor's improvement but the donee's, and because the donee has no recourse, he accepted the gift from the outset on the understanding that the creditor would not take his improvement. See Tumim for my reconciliation of Rosh's language.
Nothing from its improvement. This includes even appreciation beyond the donee's expenditures. After acquired property language is still required for such improvement, and in the case of a gift it does not help because the donee has no recourse against the donor. Shach questions this, but his objection is not compelling.
And if it improved automatically, etc. According to the Mechaber, natural appreciation needs no after acquired property clause and the creditor takes all of it because it is considered part of the land itself. Therefore the creditor may collect such appreciation even from a donee. The general rule is that wherever an after acquired clause is unnecessary, the creditor may collect from a donee.
And some say that even natural appreciation, etc. This is Rosh and Tosafos. They hold that for purposes of after acquired property and the rule of half the improvement, no distinction exists between automatic appreciation and improvement produced by expenditure. Therefore, regarding a donee as well, there is no distinction, and the creditor does not collect in either case. According to what I wrote above, however, the correct ruling follows Shach regarding a palm tree that simply grows thicker, where a firstborn takes a double portion. Such appreciation needs no after acquired clause, and therefore the creditor may collect it even from a donee. The two rules are identical.
For he has no warranty, etc. This differs from the earlier case where the purchaser's deed expressly excluded after acquired liability. There the purchaser can still return against the seller with respect to free property the seller possesses, certainly property the seller already had that is presently overseas. He therefore retains some recourse. Here, by contrast, the deed expressly gives the purchaser no recourse against the seller at all, making him equivalent to a donee.
