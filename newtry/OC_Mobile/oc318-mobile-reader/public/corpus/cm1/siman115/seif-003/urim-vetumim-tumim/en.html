But one who receives a gift. The Rosh explains that the recipient of a gift has a stronger position because we likewise find that a gift recipient has a stronger position regarding a pit or cistern and similar matters. The Shach asks what that has to do with our issue. Here, he says, the reason is simply that the gift recipient has no recourse against the donor because there is no warranty, and therefore the creditor cannot seize the enhancement. The Shach apparently did not have before him the Ritva's words quoted in Shitah Mekubetzet. The Ritva asks on the Gemara's question, "Is the power of a gift really stronger than the power of a sale?" Why is this a matter of stronger legal power at all? The purchaser accepted warranty and the gift recipient did not. The Ritva answers that once we see that this was the seller's presumed intent even when he sold the field, surely we should say all the more that a donor who voluntarily gave the field intended the same condition. The gift should be treated as though that condition had been expressly stated even though it was not articulated. The Gemara answers that since we find elsewhere that a gift recipient is accorded stronger rights, we do not infer this unstated condition in a gift. Shitah Mekubetzet gives the same explanation in the name of the Raavad. These are precisely the Rosh's words, and there is no difficulty.
Some say that even spontaneous enhancement cannot be seized, etc. The Shach in seif katan 30 argues at length for the Ramban's position that the entire Gemara's discussion distinguishing sale from gift concerns only whether the seizing creditor must reimburse expenses, whereas enhancement above expenses certainly belongs to the creditor even against a gift recipient, because the creditor has an after-acquired-property lien. I am certainly not one to raise my head against our great master the Ramban and declare his interpretation strained. Yet truth bears witness for itself, especially where the Rambam, Tosafot, and the Rosh disagree, and I am surprised that the Shach undertook to decide against so many great authorities. There is indeed a major difficulty with the Ramban's approach. The Gemara brings a baraita that a creditor seizes enhancement as support for Shmuel. According to the Ramban, the proof must be from the baraita's unqualified language, implying that the creditor takes enhancement without reimbursing expenses. Rav Huna then establishes the baraita as a purchaser from a robber. But the dispossessed purchaser is ordinarily entitled to reimbursement of his expenses, so the difficulty remains. The commentators struggled with this. The most plausible answer is that Rav Huna establishes it according to Rabbi Meir, who imposes a penalty. Yet according to the conclusion in Bava Kamma, Rabbi Meir does not penalize an inadvertent offender because of an intentional one, so one must say that the purchaser knowingly bought stolen land. Even then, whether an express undertaking for enhancement is effective is itself disputed below in siman 373, seif 1, and according to the Tur and Rosh it does not help. The strain is evident. How then can one decide halachah against the majority of the great authorities on this basis? The Shach further argues that an after-acquired-property clause attaches, so why is the Gemara's reason, "Know this because the seller writes such an undertaking," needed, and why should a gift be different? Many authorities already raised this question, especially in Shitah Mekubetzet. In truth, it occurred to me that according to Rabbi Meir, who holds that a person can transfer something not yet in existence, the Shach's question is strong. Enhancement, especially spontaneous enhancement comparable to fruits of a palm tree that are expected to come, can be subjected by strict law. But according to the Sages, future property is not subject by strict law at all. The effectiveness of an after-acquired-property clause is a rabbinic enactment to prevent the closing of the door before borrowers, as the Rashbam writes. What concern of closing the door exists in allowing a creditor to seize enhancement that the seller never labored to create and that the purchaser himself produced? What right did the seller ever have in that enhancement? On the contrary, allowing such seizure would close the door before purchasers, for who would buy and labor to improve property if strangers could take the fruits of his expense? It is therefore entirely reasonable that the Sages did not enact an after-acquired lien in this setting. The Gemara consequently gives the reason, "Know that this is so, because the seller writes such an undertaking." The purchaser entered the transaction on that understanding and accepted the risk because he could recover from the seller. But where he has no recourse, as with a gift, the Sages did not enact the creditor's right to take his enhancement merely to cause a second person's loss where there is no concern of closing the door. This also resolves a difficulty raised by Tosafot from the statement in Perek HaMekabel that heirs say, "We enhanced the property," implying that a creditor does not take enhancement produced by heirs, whereas the Mishnah in Bekhorot concerning a widow and daughters receiving maintenance implies that an ordinary creditor does take enhancement. According to what I have written, our halachah follows the Sages, and an after-acquired lien exists only because of closing the door. There is plainly no such concern in taking enhancement that heirs produced after the borrower's death. What claim does the lender have to their labor? But the Mishnah in Bekhorot can be understood according to Rabbi Meir, under whom the lien exists by strict law and therefore reaches the property and its enhancement even in heirs' hands. The Shach himself discusses at length a related distinction. Nor is it too strained to say that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir. According to Shmuel's unresolved question whether after-acquired property can be subjected, the Mishnah's statement that a widow does not take property that is merely prospective after death would otherwise prove the issue. If the property came only after death, it is after-acquired; indeed, even if it came during the husband's lifetime after the debt, it would still require such a clause. Since Shmuel says an oral loan does not collect from heirs, property not effectively subjected would be treated like an oral obligation. One is therefore already driven to say that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir. One might object that the same Mishnah says a firstborn does not take enhancement, even spontaneous enhancement such as unripe dates that ripened, and in Bava Batra 124 the Sages explain this by the Torah's language of "gift," meaning that a gift is not complete until it reaches the recipient. The Rashbam explains that one cannot transfer what does not yet exist. If so, according to Rabbi Meir a firstborn should take enhancement like Rabbi Yehudah. But the Rashbam's phrase need not be literal. The point is that the gift is not complete until it actually reaches him, because the giver could retract before then. Otherwise the Gemara's question on Rabbi Yehudah would be difficult, since Rabbi Yehudah himself holds that one can transfer something not yet in existence. The decisive point is therefore not the metaphysics of future property but when the Torah completes the firstborn's gift. The Mishnah can thus follow Rabbi Meir. Still, the fact that Tosafot did not offer this distinction suggests that they did not want to create a new tannaitic dispute between Rabbi Meir and the Sages specifically concerning enhancement of gifts. Even so, the Shach's question from after-acquired property is not compelling. The enhancement was never in the seller's possession at all. It is the clearest form of prospective property. Just as a firstborn cannot take it because it is prospective, so too there is substantial room to say that the seller could not subject it. Moreover, what claim can the creditor have to enhancement personally created by the purchaser? The Sages merely treat it, for certain purposes, as though it entered the seller's hand and he then sold it, as the authorities explain in Shitah Mekubetzet. The Ramban and Shach also argue from the Gemara's question: a baraita says the creditor takes enhancement and reimburses expenses, and asks, "According to Shmuel, how is this established?" This sounds as though without Shmuel the baraita would be easily understood, whereas according to the other authorities, who hold that absent Shmuel an ordinary creditor takes no enhancement at all, the baraita would seem even more difficult. One can answer simply that absent Shmuel we would establish it as a purchaser from a robber. Only according to Shmuel is that impossible, because Shmuel holds that a purchaser from a robber has no enhancement rights. Moreover, according to the Rosh and Tur, where an apotiki creditor is owed the value of the land and enhancement, he takes enhancement without reimbursing expenses, because an apotiki should not make him worse than an ordinary creditor. Thus without Shmuel one could establish the baraita as an apotiki. Everyone could agree that an apotiki creditor takes enhancement, but because without Shmuel an ordinary creditor does not, there would be no argument that the apotiki should enjoy the ordinary creditor's exemption from expenses. The apotiki creditor could therefore take enhancement but reimburse expenses. According to Shmuel, however, an ordinary creditor already takes enhancement without reimbursing expenses, so an apotiki creditor certainly cannot be worse. The Gemara therefore asks specifically according to Shmuel and answers that the creditor is not owed the value of the land and enhancement together, so the apotiki creditor takes more than his debt because his own land was enhanced, while reimbursing expenses. Even according to Tosafot, who hold that an apotiki creditor still reimburses expenses, their statement that he may take enhancement exceeding the amount of his debt is necessary. Their earlier question, "Why should an apotiki make him worse?" remains. The answer is that the legal treatment of an apotiki must be consistent. If enhancement exceeds the debt by thousands, the creditor takes it all because he may say, "My land was enhanced." Correspondingly, when enhancement does not exceed the debt, he must reimburse expenses because it is treated as his land. He cannot accept the favorable aspect and reject the unfavorable one. The Sages treated an apotiki as the creditor's own land for both consequences. Thus reimbursement of expenses and the right to take enhancement beyond the debt depend on one another. At the Gemara's initial stage, before the latter principle is known, everyone could say that an apotiki creditor owed the full value need not reimburse expenses because otherwise his apotiki would make him worse. Hence the Gemara's question according to Shmuel is sound. Accordingly, the majority position remains persuasive: a gift recipient's enhancement, including spontaneous enhancement, is protected unless the specific rules stated by the authorities apply. The Ramban's reasoning is weighty, but the halachah follows the majority of the poskim who disagree.
