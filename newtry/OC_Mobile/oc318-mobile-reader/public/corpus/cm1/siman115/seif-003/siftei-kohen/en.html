But a recipient of a gift, and so forth. The Gemara asks whether the recipient of a gift is truly stronger than a purchaser, and answers that he is indeed stronger. The Rosh compares this to the rule that one who gives a cistern gives the access path generously, while a seller does not. I do not understand the comparison. There the issue is the donor’s own generous intent; here the question is why the creditor may not collect the improvement. The simple reason is that the recipient has no one from whom to recover, and the law therefore protects him. Rashi explains precisely this: because the recipient cannot recover from anyone, we do not cause him the loss. Rif and the other authorities likewise indicate this. Therefore, even a purchaser who expressly waived warranty has the law of a gift recipient. With respect to an access path, however, even a purchaser without warranty is still a purchaser and does not acquire the path.
The creditor collects none of its appreciation; rather, we assess how much it was worth at the time of the gift. This is Rambam’s wording, also used by Baal HaTerumot. It implies that the creditor does not collect even the net appreciation exceeding the recipient’s expenditures. Rambam apparently reasons that the creditor takes spontaneous appreciation because the recipient can be told that, had he not accepted the gift, the land would have remained with the debtor and its increase in value would have been available for collection. This does not apply to improvement created by the recipient’s labor and expense, including the net gain beyond the expense, because that gain would not have arisen automatically in the debtor’s possession. Rabbenu Yerucham, however, writes that according to the Rif, where the appreciation exceeds the expenditure, the excess has the law of spontaneous appreciation. Ramban also understands the Rif this way. The Rif explains that the contractual warranty formula is what enables a creditor to seize both spontaneous appreciation and improvement produced by expenditure from a purchaser. His discussion shows that the excess over the expenditure is included in spontaneous appreciation. Although the Rif earlier wrote that spontaneous appreciation is collected even from a gift recipient, this is not contradictory. His later gloss represents his mature conclusion: the warranty clause is needed only for improvement created by expenditure, while spontaneous appreciation is taken even without such a clause because the land was already encumbered to the creditor. The Beit Yosef and Nimukei Yosef understand the Rif like Rashi, namely that the recipient may say, “What loss did I cause you?” and therefore the creditor does not collect any improvement produced by expenditure. In my view, that is not the Rif’s reason. The Rif’s position is that the land is acquired to the creditor for his lien. Accordingly, even the net excess over expenditures is collected from a gift recipient, as Ramban and Rabbenu Yerucham explain. Rambam disagrees because he follows the view that a purchaser’s expenditures are protected even against a creditor as one who improves another’s field without authorization. On that view, the warranty formula is needed for the excess beyond expenditures, and a gift recipient, who has no such formula, keeps that excess. Since I ruled above against that approach, the Rif’s view is primary: the creditor collects the net appreciation beyond the recipient’s reimbursable expense.
Some say that he does not seize even spontaneous appreciation. In my view, the principal rule is that the creditor takes every form of spontaneous appreciation. This certainly includes an increase in market value, a palm tree that grew, and land on which alluvium accumulated, which are regarded as property that came into existence in the debtor’s ownership. It also includes grain that matured from fodder and dates that matured from blossoms. A recipient of a gift has the law of heirs, and all spontaneous appreciation is collected from heirs. The Rosh’s argument that the Gemara should have expressly distinguished a sale from a gift is not decisive. The Gemara did not need to discuss spontaneous appreciation, because the creditor plainly takes it. Rava’s explanation, that the seller writes a warranty undertaking to the purchaser, was required only for improvement caused by expenditure. The law of after-acquired property also proves this. Once after-acquired property is encumbered, the creditor’s lien attaches to the appreciation regardless of whether the land was later sold or given away. The heirs and the gift recipient have no right to defeat an earlier lien merely because they have no recourse against the debtor. Ramban therefore rules that the creditor takes all spontaneous appreciation, including produce that matured naturally, and also takes the net gain where the recipient planted and the value exceeds the expenditures, because the land itself is pledged to him. The recipient receives his expenditures as one who improved another’s field without authorization, but the creditor takes the excess. This is supported by the Gemara in Mi SheMet, the first chapter of Bava Metzia, and the end of Yesh Bekhor. Nimukei Yosef, in the names of Ramban, Rashba, and Ran, likewise grounds the rule in the creditor’s right to say, “My encumbered land appreciated.” Baal HaTerumot, Rambam, and the Mechaber disagree concerning the excess produced by expenditures, but the position of Rif, Ramban, and Rabbenu Yerucham is more persuasive. The result is that in one respect a gift recipient is worse off than a purchaser. A purchaser ordinarily loses only half of certain improvement caused by expenditure, because his warranty and competing lien protect the other half. A gift recipient has no warranty lien against the donor, while the creditor’s earlier lien covers the land and its appreciation. Therefore the creditor takes the entire net appreciation, while reimbursing the recipient’s actual expenditures to the extent of the improvement.
