The phrase "where it appreciated through expenditure" is understood differently by the early authorities. Rif and Rambam hold that passive appreciation, such as market increase or natural growth, is fully collectible even from a donee. The special limitation applies to appreciation produced by the purchaser's expenditure. Rosh is more restrictive and does not allow collection of passive appreciation from a donee either. Ramban goes further. He writes that even where the purchaser spent money, any increase above the amount of the expenditure has the law of passive appreciation. The creditor therefore takes that excess, even from a donee, while the portion corresponding to the expenditure is treated as the purchaser's property and requires the seller's warranty arrangement in order to be reached. His reason is that the excess is effectively part of the land's own appreciation, like a rise in value. Only the actual investment represents new value introduced by the purchaser. This reading also clarifies Rava's proof from the seller's standard warranty language. The need for that warranty applies to the amount corresponding to the purchaser's expense, because those assets came from the purchaser and were never owned by the seller. Passive appreciation and appreciation above expenses are treated as part of the land itself and need no such constructive after acquired lien. Accordingly, under Ramban, the creditor's claim to appreciation above expenses is as strong as his claim to a rise in the land's market value. The special half appreciation rules apply chiefly to the value created by the purchaser's actual expenditure.
Some say the creditor collects even passive appreciation from a donee. The Shach goes further and says that appreciation above the purchaser's expenses is also taken from a donee. He reasons that only the amount corresponding to the actual expenditure is truly the purchaser's contribution; the excess resembles passive appreciation. I agree with Ramban that this is the correct classification, but not with the Shach's further suggestion that a donee is in a worse position than a purchaser so that the creditor might take all appreciation from the donee while only half from a purchaser. A donee owns the body of the field no less than a purchaser. If the creditor's lien attaches only when the appreciation comes into existence, the donee's ownership is at least as strong as the creditor's later lien. In fact, where the gift preceded the appreciation, the donor's transfer of the field and its future produce can constitute a withdrawal from the later after acquired lien. The difference between purchaser and donee is that a purchaser has a warranty claim against the seller while a donee ordinarily does not, not that the donee has weaker ownership in the appreciated field. Accordingly, if appreciation above expenses is treated like passive appreciation, the creditor may take it under Ramban. If it is treated as true after acquired appreciation, the donee should not be worse than the purchaser and the creditor cannot simply take all of it. The Shach's rule giving the creditor greater rights against a donee than against a purchaser is therefore difficult.
