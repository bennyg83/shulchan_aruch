Likewise orphans, etc. They have no one from whom to recover, so the creditor has no claim to their appreciation, even appreciation exceeding the expenses. But naturally arising appreciation may be collected according to Mechaber, because such appreciation does not depend on an after-acquired-property clause and is collectible even from a gift recipient. The Shach holds that appreciation exceeding expenses is collectible even from orphans. His words are not convincing. If a purchaser improved the land while the seller was alive, and the seller later died so that the purchaser has no one from whom to recover, the creditor nevertheless collects the appreciation. Since the appreciation existed while the purchaser still had a right of recourse, it was immediately as though the seller had acquired it and the purchaser then acquired it from him. Therefore, if the creditor has an after-acquired-property clause, he collects it. See Tumim. The Shach also innovated that an after-acquired-property clause is wholly ineffective in a ketubah. I wrote that this is not convincing, and we should not introduce a law with no source in the earlier authorities.
That he does not collect from orphans, etc. The Shach writes that Tosafot in any event allow a creditor to collect appreciation from orphans, while those who disagree distinguish between natural appreciation and appreciation through expenditures. Thus most authorities agree that natural appreciation is collectible from orphans, and only Rosh stands alone against them. He therefore rules that natural appreciation should be collected. This is correct as practical law.
