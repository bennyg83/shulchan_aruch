The creditor collects none of the appreciation. This too is Rambam’s wording and follows his approach. As explained above, however, Rif, Ramban, Rabbenu Yerucham, and the other principal authorities hold that the creditor takes the appreciation exceeding the expenditures. That is the primary ruling.
Some disagree. The Rama should not have presented this as a viable ruling. He apparently inferred it from the Tur and Rosh, who say that heirs have the law of a gift recipient, but they do not clearly rule this particular point. Even if they did, the rule should follow Rambam, the Mechaber, and the authorities discussed below. Tosafot state that a creditor collects appreciation even from heirs, notwithstanding that they have no one from whom to recover, because heirs stand in their father’s place. They prove this from the Mishna in Yesh Bekhor, which excludes a firstborn, a wife collecting her ketubah, and daughters receiving maintenance from appreciation. The Gemara explains that the exclusion concerning a wife reflects the special leniency of a ketubah, which implies that an ordinary creditor does collect appreciation from heirs. Tosafot distinguish heirs from a gift recipient, but their distinction is difficult. The Gemara in Mi SheMet derives collection from heirs through the rule of after-acquired property, and that reasoning applies equally to a gift recipient. Moreover, the Mishna’s discussion concerns spontaneous appreciation, and does not necessarily prove that heirs are liable for improvement created by their own expenditures without reimbursement. The correct reconciliation is as follows. All property and spontaneous appreciation that arose while subject to the debtor’s lien is collected from heirs and gift recipients. Where the heirs themselves expended money, they are treated as persons who improved another’s field without authorization and are entitled to reimbursement to the extent of the improvement. The creditor then takes the remaining appreciation because his lien preceded their acquisition. This is the position of Rif and Ramban. The rule does not depend upon a fictional warranty from the deceased father, nor merely upon the phrase that heirs stand in their father’s place. Rather, it follows from the creditor’s substantive lien in the land and after-acquired appreciation. The various passages in Bava Metzia, Mi SheMet, and Yesh Bekhor are consistent on this reading. Where the debt equals the land and appreciation, the creditor may take the entire property. Where the heirs prove that they made the expenditures, they receive the lesser of their expenditure and the resulting improvement. Where they cannot prove that they, rather than their father, produced the improvement, the burden rests on them, because the land stood from the outset for collection and is in the creditor’s presumptive right. The creditor is not required to prove that the father made the improvement. Accordingly, the principal law is not the broad view implied by the Rama’s “some disagree.” Heirs are like gift recipients in that the creditor takes all spontaneous appreciation and the net appreciation beyond their reimbursable expenditures. They differ from an ordinary purchaser because they have no warranty claim against the deceased estate that competes with the creditor’s lien.
