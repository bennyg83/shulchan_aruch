According to this, in the case where she has relations while a niddah, one must say that she admits it and is believed against herself to lose money, as Rashba writes. One cannot simply rely on witnesses of seclusion as witnesses of intercourse, because it is too severe to cause a monetary loss solely from seclusion. Why should she not be believed to say, "Although I was alone with him, when he came to have relations I told him that I was impure"? Rather, she must admit it or witnesses must have actually found them engaging in relations. Yet there is a difficulty. Here the Rosh rules that witnesses are required, and the Sage is not believed while she contradicts him. In chapter Shevuat HaEdut, however, the Rosh cites Ramban, who received from his teachers that whenever testimony is transmitted from one person through another and the original source later contradicts the transmitter, the original source is believed. Ramban proves this from our case: she says, "So-and-so the Sage ruled my stain pure," they ask him, and it is found false. Even though she is ordinarily believed, once she attributes the ruling to that Sage and he denies it, she is not believed. The Rosh writes that this is reasonable, and Ran likewise cites the Yerushalmi in Ketubot 503a: all of these require witnesses, "so-and-so the priest tithed for me, so-and-so saw my stain, so-and-so separated my challah, so-and-so released my vow"; they investigate and do not find confirmation. This implies that the correcting authority himself is not believed to say he did not correct the matter if she contradicts him. But the phrase "all of these with witnesses" can mean only that witnesses are required to prove she made such a statement to the husband, not that the priest or Sage is unable to contradict her. It is therefore surprising that Ramban's accepted rule, brought by both Rosh and Ran, is omitted here. Rambam's plain language also implies that the Sage himself is believed. Moreover, even neighboring women who testify that she wore the garments of a niddah establish her as a niddah despite her denial. Why should the Sage have less credibility? The Rosh in chapter HaMadir writes only a case where she says, "he corrected it for me," meaning, "I personally saw him correct it." Then he cannot contradict her, because this is not hearsay. But if she says, "So-and-so the Sage ruled me pure," where she herself does not know the halachic truth and lives only by his word, the Sage is believed to say, "I never told her it was pure," as Ramban rules. Thus, if she contradicts the husband, he must bring witnesses that his account is true unless she admits it. The phrase here, "or she contradicts the Sage," should therefore be understood as a case where she says, "I saw him tithe the pile," not where she says, "he told me it was tithed," or "he told me this piece was permitted," or "he told me this stain was pure." In all such cases the Sage is more credible than she, since her entire knowledge derives from him. The abridged rulings of the Rosh, however, do not appear to distinguish this way.
