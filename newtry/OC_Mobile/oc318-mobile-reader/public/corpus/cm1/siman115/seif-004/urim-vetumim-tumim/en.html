And likewise heirs, etc. The Shach returns to strengthen the Ramban's view that a creditor takes all enhancement above expenses both from a gift recipient and from heirs. He offers an answer to Tosafot's difficulty. In Perek HaMekabel the heirs say, "We enhanced the property," which implies that a creditor does not take enhancement they produced. Yet in Bekhorot, where the Mishnah says a widow does not take enhancement, whether spontaneous or produced by expense, the Gemara asks from Shmuel's rule that a creditor takes enhancement and answers that this is one of the leniencies of a ketubah; daughters receiving maintenance likewise do not take it because conditions of a ketubah are treated like the ketubah. This implies that an ordinary creditor would take the enhancement. The Shach says Heaven left him room to explain this. In my view his answer is difficult, and in trying to improve on the Ramban in Milchamot he has weakened the argument. According to the Ramban, Shmuel's novelty is only that a purchaser receives no reimbursement of expenses; enhancement above expense was already known to belong to the creditor even without Shmuel. The Gemara in Bekhorot therefore cannot be asking about heirs merely from Shmuel, because heirs are in any event reimbursed their expenses, while excess enhancement belongs to a creditor independently of Shmuel. The Ramban consequently explains that the Gemara asks a stronger question from a widow collecting even from purchasers: Shmuel says a creditor seizes the purchaser's enhancement without reimbursing expenses, whereas the Mishnah says the widow takes no enhancement at all. The Gemara answers that a ketubah is uniquely lenient. The widow takes no enhancement either from purchasers or heirs, and daughters likewise receive none from heirs, while an ordinary creditor takes the excess above expenses. That is coherent according to the Ramban. The Shach instead says that Shmuel also teaches that excess enhancement can be subjected despite not yet existing, as well as that the purchaser receives no expenses. The Gemara in Bekhorot therefore asks why the widow and daughters take none of the heirs' enhancement, for without Shmuel we would have thought future enhancement could not be subjected. It answers that the ketubah is treated leniently. This is the essence of his lengthy discussion. I do not understand it. First, the Ramban's principal proof is from the baraita saying that the creditor takes enhancement but reimburses expenses, followed by the question, "According to Shmuel, how can this baraita be established?" This implies that absent Shmuel the baraita is understandable. According to the Shach, however, Shmuel is precisely the source that future enhancement can be subjected at all. Without Shmuel the baraita would be even more difficult. The supposed solution therefore recreates the Ramban's original problem. Second, why does the Gemara need Shmuel to prove that the Mishnah in Bekhorot allows future property to be subjected? The Mishnah itself says that a widow does not take property that is prospective, even property inherited from the husband's father's estate after death. The implication is that if such property had entered the husband's possession during his lifetime it could be subject to collection. Otherwise the Mishnah's emphasis on prospective property would be unnecessary. As I wrote above, one must in any event understand the Mishnah as accepting a valid future-property lien, perhaps according to Rabbi Meir. What new proof from Shmuel is needed? Third, either enhancement produced by heirs after their father's death is treated as though it first entered the father's estate and then passed to them, as enhancement in a purchaser's hands is treated for certain purposes, or it is not. If it is, then even without a valid after-acquired clause we rule, with the Shach's strong agreement, that where a debtor acquired property and then left it to heirs, a creditor collects from the heirs without any after-acquired clause. The issue therefore has nothing to do with whether future property can be expressly subjected. If, however, the heirs' enhancement is not treated as though it entered the father's ownership, then no after-acquired clause can help. A person may subject his own future property, not property independently belonging to his children. This is also forced by Tosafot's concern with the statement in HaMekabel that the heirs say, "We enhanced." Why not answer that the case there is simply one where the creditor lacked an after-acquired-property clause? Enhancement produced by the father would still be collectible as property he acquired and left to heirs, whereas enhancement produced by the heirs would require such a clause. Tosafot did not answer so because either no clause is needed, or no clause would help. The Ramban in Shitah Mekubetzet who does require an after-acquired clause follows his own position that even property acquired and then left to heirs requires such a clause, a position we do not accept. The Shach's explanation therefore remains unclear to me, and the Ramban's answer is preferable. For practical halachah we return to what I wrote above: most poskim disagree with the Ramban, and that is the ruling. The Shach derives a further novel rule. Since the Gemara says that a ketubah is lenient and therefore does not take enhancement even though it contains an after-acquired-property clause, he concludes that such a clause is altogether ineffective for a ketubah. Thus, if a man marries and expressly writes an after-acquired clause, then acquires and sells property, the wife cannot collect her ketubah from that property. The Shach concedes that this is not implied by the Rif and Rosh, but he derives it from the sugya. This conclusion has no sound basis. Many authorities hold that spontaneous enhancement is collectible even without an after-acquired clause, yet the Gemara says that a widow does not collect even spontaneous enhancement. That itself proves that the leniency of ketubah is a special rule concerning enhancement, not that the after-acquired clause is generally void. One cannot extrapolate from one leniency of ketubah to another matter not stated by the Sages. There is also a direct difficulty from Bava Batra 157. The Gemara seeks to prove that an after-acquired clause is effective from the Mishnah concerning a house that collapsed, and answers that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir, who permits a person to subject future property. But the Mishnah speaks both of creditors and of a wife's ketubah. If the Shach were correct, even under Rabbi Meir the wife's after-acquired clause should be ineffective by the special leniency of ketubah, and the Gemara's answer would not resolve that half of the Mishnah. Nor can one answer that Rabbi Meir holds lien liability d'oraisa and therefore the Sages could not distinguish a ketubah, while according to the Sages an after-acquired lien is merely rabbinic and the concern of closing the door does not apply to marriage. We already established above that the Mishnah in Bekhorot likewise must be understood under Rabbi Meir according to Shmuel, yet it still applies the ketubah leniency to enhancement. Further, Ketubot 55 states in the name of Rabbi Yannai that conditions of a ketubah are like the ketubah itself, with one practical difference being enhancement. Rabbi Yannai himself holds that one can transfer future property, as appears in Yevamot 92. It is strained to say that Rabbi Yannai merely stated a general rule while the anonymous Gemara later attached the enhancement application only according to the accepted rejection of Rabbi Meir. Moreover, the Mishnah in Bekhorot itself implies that an after-acquired clause is effective, as explained above. The Shach's general rule therefore has no foundation. According to the Rosh, who requires an after-acquired clause both for spontaneous enhancement and enhancement produced by expense, the sugya can be explained simply. As to conditions of the ketubah, there is ordinarily no written after-acquired clause at all; the Sages treated those conditions as written obligations, but specifically did not extend that legal fiction to enhancement. As to the principal ketubah, even if it has such a clause, there is still no difficulty. We rule that where two liens attach to the enhancement simultaneously they divide, and in Ketubot, as the Rif and Rosh state, where the law would be division the asset is given to the weaker claimant. Between a ketubah claimant and an ordinary creditor, the ordinary creditor is considered weaker and receives it. Here the purchaser is the party competing with the wife, and the purchaser is the weaker claimant. The Gemara's phrase "one of the leniencies of ketubah" can therefore mean that the enhancement is given to the weaker purchaser. This can even illuminate the Ramban's own approach. The Gemara knew that an ordinary purchaser would prevail as the weaker party where division would otherwise apply, but Shmuel says the purchaser even loses his expenses, a result not based merely on the after-acquired clause but on his ability to recover from the seller. The Gemara therefore asks why the wife should be different and answers that this too is a leniency of ketubah. One might ask how this works according to the earlier version that the first lien alone attaches rather than the parties dividing. Even there the reason the first creditor is protected despite the debtor's later change of position is the concern of closing the door before lenders, as the Shach himself explains: who would lend today if the debtor could subject the same property elsewhere tomorrow? That concern applies to an ordinary creditor but not to a wife, since the familiar principle is that the desire to marry is greater on the man's side. Thus even under the earlier version there is room to treat the wife's position more leniently. In truth, this explanation assumes the view, discussed above in siman 104, that even if the creditor has an after-acquired clause and the purchaser does not, the parties divide. Otherwise there would remain cases where the wife's clause should prevail entirely. I have written all this to clarify the sugya. The practical rule is clear: an after-acquired-property clause in a ketubah is effective generally; the special leniency concerns enhancement.

The Maharsha asks: if so, how could the Gemara initially suppose that a woman collects her ketubah from purchasers after the husband's death? Necessarily the husband left no unencumbered property, because otherwise the purchasers could say, "We left you property from which to collect." Yet then the purchaser likewise has no one against whom to recover, which should make him comparable to heirs. His question can be answered. It may be that the husband did leave property subject to warranty, but the woman had written to one class of property, "I have no claim or dealings concerning it." She did not thereby cause herself a loss as against the first purchaser, because Tosafot in Ketubot 95, s.v. "there," hold that a wife may remove herself from one asset and nevertheless collect from an earlier purchaser. There is therefore no difficulty. Still, the Shach correctly challenges the Maharsha's underlying ruling. If the enhancement arose during the seller's lifetime, when the purchaser reasonably relied on his warranty claim against the seller, what difference does it make that at the later moment of seizure the seller no longer has assets? The purchaser's right was established when the enhancement arose. I am surprised that so great a master erred in this point. Perhaps the Maharsha meant a case analogous to a firstborn, where the property was enhanced only after the seller's death. Then the woman likewise concerns a purchaser who enhanced the land after death. In that case the Maharsha is correct, as I wrote above in the name of the Nimukei Yosef. But this is already fully prospective property. Since the Mishnah separately states that a widow does not take prospective property, there would be no need to state the point twice. The difficulty therefore remains resolved without adopting the Maharsha's broader rule.
