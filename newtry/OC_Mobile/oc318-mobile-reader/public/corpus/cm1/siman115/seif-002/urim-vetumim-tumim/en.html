And some say, etc. The Shach wonders why the Rama presents this as "some say," since the Rif and Rosh state it without any dissent. His question is sound. Perhaps the Rama understood that because the Rambam formulated the rule as a case where the creditor collected his debt together with half the enhancement, and did not copy the Rif's more general language, the Rambam limits the rule to that case. The reason may be explained as follows. Independently, one can ask why the purchaser can compel the creditor to pay him the value of his enhancement. Are they partners? The creditor is taking the land against the purchaser's will. See the Ramban in Milchamot. The answer is that the purchaser is treated as one who entered the property with permission, because through his enhancement the creditor became entitled to half the enhancement and thus received a benefit from the purchaser's labor. Strict law therefore requires the creditor to reimburse his share of the expense, since the purchaser entered with permission and for the creditor's benefit. But where the creditor has no right at all to the enhancement, the purchaser did not improve the property with the creditor's authorization or for his benefit. The creditor can then say, "What do I have to do with you? I will take my land from you," and cannot be compelled to pay. The Rif and Rosh, however, make clear that even in such a case the creditor must pay the purchaser. The Rama therefore introduced this ruling as "some say."
But if he made it an apotiki. See the Shach, who cites the view of the great authorities, the Rif, Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Tur, that with an apotiki, even if the debt equals only the value of the land itself, the creditor nevertheless takes all the enhancement, on the claim that his own land was enhanced, although he must reimburse the purchaser's expenses. The Shach argues at length against them, yet his own words acknowledge that mere reasoning is insufficient to reject the rulings of such great authorities. Indeed, all the arguments and responses already appear in the works of the earlier authorities. The Shach raises a strong difficulty against the Rosh. Tosafot, who hold that an apotiki creditor can take more than the amount of his debt, explain that at the Gemara's initial stage, when it answers that the creditor was not owed the value of the land plus its enhancement, one might have objected that he certainly cannot take more than his debt. The Gemara simply had a stronger objection. Thus, once the Gemara ultimately establishes the case as an apotiki, that objection disappears because the law is indeed that an apotiki creditor may take more than the debt. But the Rosh follows Rashi and explains the initial answer differently: the creditor is owed the value of the land together with the enhancement above the purchaser's expenses, but not the amount of the expenses. If so, even after the Gemara establishes the case as an apotiki, why not retain that explanation? Why force the conclusion that the creditor may take more than his debt? The final answer could simply explain why the creditor may remove the purchaser by paying money. This is a powerful question. It seems to me that the answer is as follows. Despite all the Shach's praise of this reading, its formulation is itself difficult. If the governing rule is simply that the creditor takes only up to the amount of his debt, without regard to whether that amount represents enhancement, and never takes more than his debt, what does the baraita teach by describing the purchaser's expenses? For example, suppose the debt is 110 gold coins, the land together with enhancement is worth 120, and the purchaser spent 10. The creditor takes 110 and 10 remains for the purchaser. If the purchaser had spent 12, he would lose 2, because the creditor does not deduct expenses from the amount of his debt. If he had spent only 8, he would still retain 10, because the creditor cannot take more than 110. Thus the expenses are irrelevant. The simple rule is merely that the creditor takes no more than his debt and the balance belongs to the purchaser, whether that balance represents expense or profit. Rashi sensed this and wrote that the baraita teaches a case where the expenses exceed the enhancement, so the purchaser recovers only the amount of the enhancement. Yet this too is difficult. If the creditor is still owed money, the purchaser's expenses never reduce the creditor's debt. If the debt is already fully satisfied, then even if expenses were one thousand and enhancement only one hundred, whatever remains after the creditor's debt belongs to the purchaser. The point therefore remains unclear. The Rosh does not mention Rashi's explanation that the case teaches what happens where expenses exceed enhancement. It is also strained to say that the baraita teaches this merely because it affects the purchaser's later claim against the seller, when the baraita is discussing only the creditor. I therefore suggest that the baraita specifically says that the purchaser receives his "expenses," rather than simply whatever enhancement remains above the creditor's debt, because there is a practical difference. If the residue is merely enhancement remaining after satisfaction of the creditor, then when the purchaser later seeks to collect his principal purchase price from other encumbered property, those purchasers can say, "We left you a place from which to collect," namely the 10 gold coins remaining in this field. Those 10 were themselves subject to the purchaser's claim for principal just as they would have been subject to the creditor had his debt exceeded 110. Why should the purchaser treat them as payment for enhancement rather than principal? He must first collect his principal from them. In this respect I do not adopt the reasoning I wrote above in seif katan 7; the Rosh, on this reading, holds that the amount is deducted from principal. But if the purchaser receives those 10 specifically as reimbursement of expenses, the later purchasers cannot deduct them from his principal claim. He received them in payment of the expense he incurred, and may still collect his full principal from the other purchasers. This is why the baraita says that the purchaser receives his expenses rather than merely "the remaining enhancement." If his expenses exceed the enhancement, however, even though some value remains in the land beyond what the creditor collected, the purchaser can characterize as expenses only an amount equal to the actual enhancement. Any further residue must be credited toward his principal. This reading is clear and highly persuasive. Still, one may ask: why should the fact that he spent money change matters? Those expenses are themselves a debt owed by the seller, just as if the purchaser had lent him money. His earlier debt for the purchase price has priority against this field. The later purchasers should therefore say, "Why do you take the remaining land in payment of the later expense claim? Apply it to your earlier principal claim, and leave the expense claim unsecured." The answer is that the purchaser entered with the implied permission of those later purchasers, because his expenditures were for their benefit. By improving the field he ensured that the creditor would have enough property from which to collect and would not turn to those later purchasers. The case at the Gemara's initial stage assumes that the creditor is owed the value of the land and the enhancement above the expense. Thus the purchaser's expenditures allowed the creditor to collect his debt in full from this property and protected the later purchasers. Since the purchaser acted for their benefit and with implied authorization, strict law permits him first to recover his expenses from the residual property, and the later purchasers cannot insist that the residue be applied to his principal. This reasoning works at the Gemara's initial stage. But once the Gemara establishes the case as an apotiki, the original problem returns. The later purchasers can say, "Apply the remaining land to your principal. You did not incur the expense with our permission or for our benefit, because whether this field was worth little or much, the creditor holding an apotiki could never have turned to us. What benefit did we receive from the creditor's taking the field and its enhancement above the expense?" At the initial stage they did benefit from enhancement above the expense because it prevented the creditor from collecting from them. In the apotiki case there is no such benefit. Accordingly, at the Gemara's conclusion one must adopt the answer of Tosafot and the Rif: with an apotiki, strict law allows the creditor to take even more than the amount of his debt, because he may say that his own land was enhanced. The Rosh's words are thus precise. These points fit together beautifully. One must only say that the Rosh does not accept the reasoning I attributed above to the Rambam, namely that half the enhancement is never deducted from principal. In truth, even apart from all this, the matter is nearly compelled. Suppose a field was worth one hundred and, through trees that grew or an alluvial deposit combined with expenditures, became worth two hundred. How should enhancement in an apotiki be understood? If the creditor is deemed retroactively to have owned the land, so that his own land was enhanced and produced fruit, then even where his debt equals only the original one hundred he should take the entire two hundred, exactly as the authorities ruled. But if he is not deemed owner until collection, then the enhancement occurred while the property belonged to the seller and purchaser. In that case the enhancement should be treated as a separate asset, as though the seller had acquired another field and then sold it to the purchaser. Why should the creditor take it even when his debt is two hundred? He wrote that he would have recourse only to this designated field. The enhancement is effectively another field acquired later. Consider a debtor who designated a field and stipulated, "You shall have payment only from this field," while trees in the field belonged to someone else. If the debtor later purchased those trees, would the creditor's lien automatically extend to them? Surely not. The parties expressly limited payment to the field originally designated. What difference is there between later acquiring the trees and the purchaser's later causing the field to increase in value, if that increase is treated as a new asset? Moreover, if one does not say that the creditor's own land was enhanced, then even an apotiki creditor could collect enhancement only if the debtor had written an after-acquired-property clause. The Shach himself states this in seif katan 22. I find this astonishing. How can an after-acquired-property clause operate with an apotiki? The debtor said, "You shall have payment only from this." How can a general lien on after-acquired property remain after all other security was expressly excluded? It would be strained to say that the debtor wrote a special lien over whatever future enhancement would arise specifically in that field. We have neither heard nor seen such a formulation, and it is certainly not the ordinary case. In an ordinary apotiki there is no meaningful general after-acquired-property clause, because all other security and methods of payment were excluded, as I wrote above in seif katan 1. The Shach's attribution of after-acquired property to an apotiki is therefore puzzling. The Baal HaMaor may appear to imply something similar, but it is preferable to read his words more narrowly than to posit a general after-acquired-property clause in an apotiki. Thus the Rosh's position is correct. At the initial stage of the Gemara we would have said that an apotiki creditor has no right at all to the enhancement, because there is no after-acquired-property clause and enhancement is treated as another field. Once the Gemara at its conclusion establishes that the creditor does collect enhancement from an apotiki, the only explanation is that his own land is deemed to have been enhanced. He therefore needs no after-acquired-property clause, and the enhancement is not treated as another field. Once that is so, the same reasoning yields that he may take more than the amount of the debt, because his own land was enhanced. Those who disagree must say that the parties' presumed intent at the time of designation creates a limited distinction: for purposes of avoiding the need for an after-acquired-property clause and preventing enhancement from being treated as a different asset, it is considered that the creditor's own land was enhanced; but not so far as to permit him to profit beyond the amount he actually lent and take another person's value. That distinction is strained. The Shach further argues from Tosafot. At the Gemara's initial stage, Tosafot say that the Gemara could immediately have asked: if the debt equals only the original land, how can the creditor take enhancement above the purchaser's expenses? Tosafot answer that the Gemara had another sufficient objection. The Shach says that their actual objection is only "this works according to one authority," and therefore leaves one view intact, whereas the objection that the creditor cannot collect beyond his debt would apply according to everyone. Many earlier authorities already raised this question, as recorded in Shitah Mekubetzet. It seems that the answer depends on the dispute whether a creditor acquires the property retroactively. All authorities who challenge the apotiki creditor's taking more than his debt ask, "Does he really collect retroactively?" They implicitly agree that if collection were retroactive, the creditor could indeed say that his own land was enhanced. Abaye holds in Pesachim that a creditor collects retroactively and can consecrate the property even before actual collection, which is more powerful than an ordinary apotiki. According to Abaye, therefore, the creditor can certainly say that his own land was enhanced and may take more than the debt because, once the debtor fails to redeem the property, it is treated as having stood in the creditor's ownership retroactively. The remaining question would then be only according to Rava, who holds that collection operates prospectively. Tosafot could therefore have asked: this works according to Abaye, but what can be said according to Rava? They instead wrote that the Gemara already had another "this works according to one authority" challenge. That challenge is even stronger on Tosafot's approach, because they identify the dispute whether the debtor can remove the creditor with money with the dispute of Rami bar Chama and Rava in the chapter Mi SheHayah. Abaye himself agrees there with Rava that the debtor can remove the creditor with money. Thus the difficulty remains even according to Abaye. Tosafot's formulation is therefore exact. According to the commentators who read Rashi as saying that the creditor is owed the value of the land together with the enhancement above the expense, but not the expense itself, I would explain why the baraita mentions expenses as follows. Suppose the enhancement is 30 and the expense is 10. The creditor takes 20 and leaves 10. The purchaser might have claimed that the 10 left to him represents partial payment of the enhancement itself. He would then still have claims against the seller for another 10 of enhancement plus 10 of expenses. When he later competes with the seller's other creditors over unencumbered property, where there is no priority among ordinary creditors and each receives proportionally to his debt, he would hold two distinct claims, one for expenses and one for enhancement, which did not arise simultaneously, as discussed above in siman 104. He would therefore receive two proportional shares. The baraita teaches instead that every amount left in his hands is applied first to expenses. He cannot designate it as payment of enhancement while leaving the expense claim outstanding. He therefore has only a 20 coin enhancement claim and receives only one proportional share with the other creditors. This is possible, but strained. The interpretation of Tosafot and the other authorities remains preferable.
His expenses. The Shach cites the Rambam in Hilchot Malveh, chapter 21, law 6: where the field was made an apotiki, the creditor takes the entire field. We then examine the half of the enhancement that otherwise remains for the purchaser. If that half exceeds the purchaser's expense, the purchaser receives his expenses from the creditor, because the creditor says, "My field produced the enhancement," and the purchaser collects the remaining enhancement from the seller. If half the enhancement is less than the expense, the purchaser receives from the seizing creditor only that half of the enhancement, and then collects from the seller only the half of the enhancement that was seized. The Shach rejects these words entirely and calls them astonishing. I do not understand what is strange in them. Only one word at the end may require correction. The Rambam follows Tosafot's approach that where the creditor is owed the value of the land together with its enhancement, even an apotiki creditor does not reimburse expenses, because an apotiki should not make his position worse than that of an ordinary creditor. But the Rambam also recognizes the difficulty I wrote above: what disadvantage is there? An ordinary creditor takes only half the enhancement, whereas the apotiki creditor takes all of it. The purchaser can argue either way. If the creditor says that his own land was enhanced, he should reimburse the purchaser's expenses. If he remains merely an ordinary creditor, he should take only half the enhancement. Why may he take all the enhancement and refuse reimbursement? The Rambam therefore holds that the choice belongs to the creditor. If half the enhancement exceeds the expenses, he takes the entire enhancement by asserting his apotiki status and saying that his own land was enhanced, but correspondingly reimburses the expenses. If the expenses exceed half the enhancement, he says, "Why should my apotiki make me worse than an ordinary creditor? I will take only half the enhancement and will not reimburse expenses." This is the Rambam's meaning. The rule is simple. Only at the end, where the Rambam says that the purchaser then recovers from the seller "the half of the enhancement," he seemingly should also have mentioned the expenses. This can be resolved by saying that he relied on the earlier rule, which makes the purchaser's right to recover expenses obvious. It is also possible that one word dropped from the text and that it should read "expenses and half the enhancement." Such a minor textual issue is no basis to reject the Rambam's entire ruling, which accords with reason. The commentators who were puzzled apparently had a version or understanding of the Rambam under which he followed his teachers that expenses are always reimbursed. According to the Shach's own testimony that the Rambam follows the Rif's approach, however, the Rambam's words are coherent. The practical halachah still requires study, because Tosafot's argument that an apotiki should not make the creditor worse remains difficult, as I have already offered several ways to resolve it, and many authorities hold that expenses must always be reimbursed.
