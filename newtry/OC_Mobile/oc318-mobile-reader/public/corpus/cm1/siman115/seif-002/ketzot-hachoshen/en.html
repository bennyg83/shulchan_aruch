The rule concerning a field large enough for nine kav comes from Rif and Rosh. If the creditor's debt is smaller than the value of the whole field, he takes only the amount encumbered to him and leaves the remainder to the purchaser. Baal HaMaor says that if the remaining parcel is too small to be independently useful and the purchaser does not want it, the purchaser can compel the creditor to buy it at value. Ramban disagrees with treating the parties like ordinary partners. The creditor never chose a partnership with the purchaser and cannot be forced to buy the purchaser's residue merely because his foreclosure created an inconvenient parcel. Ramban nevertheless agrees that appreciation is different. Where the creditor's debt equals only the principal value of the land and not the added appreciation, the appreciation belongs entirely to the purchaser. Since the creditor is taking the land itself and the appreciation cannot be physically separated, he must compensate the purchaser in money for the appreciation he is forced to take with the land. That is not because the purchaser can impose a partnership sale, but because the appreciation was never subjected to this creditor in the first place. This is consistent with the principle I established above. Appreciation is not automatically after acquired property, because the borrower never owned it. The Sages subjected it only when necessary to prevent loss to the creditor. If the creditor can collect his whole debt from the principal value of the land, there is no creditor loss and therefore no reason to subject the appreciation. The creditor must therefore purchase that appreciation from the purchaser at its proper value. The residue of the original land is different: that land was actually subjected, and the creditor simply takes the amount of his lien and leaves the balance, even if the remaining parcel is inconveniently small.
If the field was made an express apotiki, the Gemara teaches that the creditor's position is stronger. The early authorities divide sharply on the effect. Rif, Ramban, and Rashba understand that when the debt equals only the original land value, the creditor can still take appreciation beyond the purchaser's expenses because he can say, "My land is what appreciated." In an express apotiki, the land is treated more like the creditor's own designated property. The creditor therefore may take all appreciation beyond expenses, and he does not need an after acquired property clause for that portion. Rashi and Baal HaMaor understand the sugya differently. In their view the apotiki chiefly prevents the purchaser from redeeming the creditor with money. It does not automatically transform all appreciation into the creditor's property. They explain the baraita as a case where the creditor's debt actually reaches the principal plus the appreciation above expenses, so he takes that amount and reimburses only the expenses. A difficulty for Rashi is that if ordinary appreciation is shared between lender and purchaser under an after acquired clause, one rarely reaches a case in which the creditor can collect the whole debt while still owing all expenses to the purchaser. This can be resolved if Shmuel, whose statement the sugya is explaining, holds that the creditor takes the whole appreciation rather than only half. That makes the baraita's structure coherent. The practical difference is significant. Under Rif and Ramban an express apotiki can itself give the creditor all passive appreciation above expenses because the creditor can say the designated land appreciated. Under Rashi and Baal HaMaor, apotiki changes chiefly the purchaser's ability to redeem the land, while ordinary after acquired lien rules still govern the appreciation.
