["With respect to a field, etc., and with respect to a garden, etc. The criterion is whether, if they divide it, each resulting portion will still retain its recognized name under the laws of partition explained below in siman 171; see there. This rule comes from the Rif and Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, who derived it from the statement in the first chapter of Bava Batra that whenever property can be divided and each portion retains its name, they divide it, but otherwise one party compensates the other with money. Baal HaMaor writes that where the creditor's claim against the seller equals only part of the land, he takes land equal to his debt and the remainder remains for the purchaser, provided the remainder is suitable for the purchaser, or even if it is not ordinarily suitable but the purchaser wants it. If it is not suitable and the purchaser does not want it, the creditor must pay him its value. He compares this to the law of improvements where the debt equals the principal value of the land but not the improvements. The Ramban objects that the purchaser has no transactional relationship with the creditor. The creditor is not selling him this land and did not buy it from him; rather, he collects his debt from land that is subject to his lien. The law therefore does not compel the creditor to become a purchaser from the buyer. Even among partners, the rule of 'buy or sell' does not apply merely because one has a legally sufficient share while the other has less. If one already has a sufficient measure and does not wish either to buy or sell, why should he be compelled? All the more so here, where these parties never became partners. As for the Rif's ruling concerning improvements, the Ramban explains it differently. Where the land was not made an apotiki and the creditor's claim does not cover the improvements, the purchaser can say: since I am not removing you with money and you are seizing the land itself, yet my improvement was never encumbered to your debt, the improvement is mine and you have no right in it. Therefore either leave me the substance of my improvement in a form fit for me to take, or purchase it from me. When the creditor purchases the improvement, he cannot pay for it with an unusable fragment of land. The Ramban calls this a clear ruling. In my view, however, the Baal HaMaor is correct, and the Rif itself supports his view. First, since the Rif states simply that the purchaser receives money from the creditor, this implies that this is the law. The Ramban's distinction between improvements and the body of the land is strained. Second, the Rif expressly cites the rule that wherever division leaves each portion with its name they divide, and otherwise one raises the other with money. That source in Bava Batra 13 does not concern improvements at all. Nor do I understand the Ramban's objection from the absence of 'buy or sell' among partners. Here we are not proceeding under that doctrine, especially since the purchaser has no money with which to buy. Moreover, the Ramban's own assumption that where one partner has a sufficient measure he may take his portion against the will of the other is difficult. What difference does it make that one has a sufficient measure if division leaves the other with an unusable portion? True, the partner who lacks a sufficient measure cannot compel the other to buy or sell the entire property where the other can say, 'I will leave you enough to constitute a sufficient measure.' But if the first partner insists on taking his entire share and thereby leaves the other with less than a sufficient measure, the latter may prevent the division and say, 'What shall I do with the unusable remnant?' Therefore, if the partner or creditor refuses to leave any part of his own share in joint use, he must pay the other party money. This is explicit in the responsum of Maharam cited in the Mordechai at the end of the first chapter of Bava Batra, and is also explicit in the Tur and Mechaber below, siman 171, seif 11. The same is evident from the Rambam in chapter 1 of Hilchot Shekhenim and from the Tur and Mechaber below, siman 171, seif 3, who define divisible property as property that, when divided according to the partners' shares, leaves even the smallest share with the recognized name of the property. The plain meaning of the Mishnah in the first chapter of Bava Batra likewise states that a courtyard is not divided unless each receives four cubits. Even if one would receive four cubits, division does not occur unless the other also receives four. This is also the plain meaning of the baraita that where each portion retains its name they divide and otherwise one compensates the other with money. Nor is there a difficulty from the Gemara's challenge to Rav Nachman, who holds there is no general rule of 'buy or sell.' Since according to Rav Nachman partners continue using the property jointly, the baraita should have said that if it cannot be divided they continue in joint use. Its statement that one compensates the other shows that where the party with a sufficient share is prepared to leave enough of his own share in joint use, the one with the insufficient share may demand a buyout, and the first must therefore pay. The baraita also plainly speaks in all cases, whether one party has a sufficient measure or neither does. It would have been possible to distinguish partners from a creditor, but it appears that this is why the Rif cites the general baraita rather than the Mishnah, for the Mishnah on its face concerns partners, whereas the baraita states a general rule: whenever the remaining portion lacks its recognized name, compensation is made in money. Baal HaTerumot, gate 3, beginning of part 4, agrees with Baal HaMaor, and this also appears explicitly from Responsa Rashba, siman 1043. It is likewise the position of the Tur and Mechaber above in siman 103, seif 5, and this is the ruling we follow. Accordingly, it is clear that the Rif and Baal HaMaor discuss a case where the creditor refuses to leave any portion of his share with the purchaser for joint use. But if he is willing to leave enough in joint ownership to constitute a sufficient measure and to use it alternately in proportion to their respective interests, he may do so. Since the purchaser has no money, he cannot invoke 'buy or sell.' Had he money, even Rav Nachman, who rejects that doctrine generally, would plainly agree here that the creditor can say, 'Either pay me for my share or I will remove you from a sufficient portion.' There is no distinction in this respect between the body of the land and improvements. This appears clear to me."]

To the contrary, he should have said that they divide it, as explained below in siman 171, and all the authorities write that the creditor takes his portion in land. The Maggid Mishneh writes that from here they learned that where the debt is small and the amount of land that would satisfy it is less than a legally sufficient measure, beit din does not compel the creditor to accept such an unusable fragment in payment. ["An apotiki, etc. In the next seif katan I cite many authorities who explain that an apotiki is treated like property already sold to the creditor, so that the creditor can say, 'My land increased in value.' According to that approach, even without an after acquired property clause, and even if the document expressly provides that later acquired property is not subject to collection, the creditor collects the improvement because it is his land that improved. This was also explained above in seif katan 4. Tosafos at the end of Bava Metzia 14 likewise imply this, for they ask why, when the Gemara at the end of Mi SheMet seeks to prove that an after acquired property clause creates a lien from the rule that a creditor collects improvements, the Gemara does not answer that the case is one where the land was made an apotiki. They leave the question unresolved. However, in the next seif katan I explain that there is another view according to which an apotiki is not treated as already sold. According to that view, an after acquired property clause is still necessary even for an apotiki, and the creditor collects only half the improvement, as I wrote above in seif katan 4. This also appears explicitly from Baal HaMaor in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. According to that approach, Tosafos' question is resolved."]
["But if he made it an apotiki, the creditor collects even all of the improvement, etc. The Rama appears to mean that the creditor may seize even value beyond the amount of the debt, paying the purchaser only his expenses. The Tur states this explicitly in seif 8: if the field was made an apotiki, then even if the debt equals only the value of the unimproved field, the creditor takes the entire field with its improvement. If the improvement exceeds the purchaser's expenses, the purchaser takes his expenses from the creditor and the excess improvement from the seller. If the expenses exceed the improvement, he receives from the creditor only expenses up to the amount of the improvement and loses the remainder. This accords with Tosafos at the beginning of Bava Metzia 15b, who ask that since the debt equals only the land itself, why should the creditor take the improvement beyond the purchaser's expenses? They answer that the Gemara's conclusion establishing the case as an apotiki resolves the matter. The Rosh likewise writes that once the field was made an apotiki, it is for the creditor as one of his own fields, and he therefore takes all the improvement while paying only the expenses. This is also the view of the Rif in the first chapter of Bava Metzia and Rav Hai Gaon in Sefer Mekach UMemkar, gates 27 and 28. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh writes that this is the approach of Rav Hai and the Halachot and that the Ramban and Rashba agreed. He concludes, however, that another approach is possible: an apotiki does not strengthen the creditor's right to seize more than the amount of his debt; its special effect is only that he may remove the purchaser with money and need not accept land from him instead. The Beit Yosef cites this at the end of seif 8. Thus the Maggid Mishneh did not regard the view of Rav Hai and the Rif as compelled. Lechem Mishneh objects that according to this alternative approach, the baraita still says that the purchaser receives his expenses from the creditor and the creditor takes the excess improvement, which would mean that the creditor receives more than his debt. He suggests that once the Gemara answers that the case concerns an apotiki, it also retracts the earlier assumption that the debt equals only the land and instead assumes that the debt equals both land and improvement. This is not correct. Even though Tosafos in Bava Metzia 15b write along those lines, that is not the view of all the authorities, and it cannot be the Maggid Mishneh's intention. Otherwise, why would he call this an alternative approach in which an apotiki does not strengthen the creditor's right beyond his debt? On Lechem Mishneh's explanation, the apotiki would actually weaken the creditor in this respect because he would have to pay the purchaser's expenses. Rather, Lechem Mishneh overlooked the discussion of the Rosh, Rashi, and Baal HaMaor. The Rosh explains that the baraita's rule that the purchaser takes his expenses from the creditor concerns a case where the creditor's claim does not cover land and improvement, but only the land, and the excess improvement is greater than the expenses. Rashi in the first chapter of Bava Metzia similarly explains that the creditor pays the purchaser for the improvement because his claim against the seller does not cover both land and improvement, and that the baraita uses the language of 'expenses' to teach that where expenses exceed the improvement the purchaser receives only expenses up to the amount of the improvement. Rashi adds that all agree that where the purchaser has money he cannot remove the creditor when the field was made an apotiki. It is not difficult that the Gemara calls the payment 'improvement,' because in that context it necessarily means the purchaser's expenses. Baal HaMaor likewise disagrees with the Rif and holds that even with an apotiki the creditor does not collect more than the amount of his debt, citing Rashi as support, and the Ramban in Milchamot writes that this is Rashi's view although he disagrees with Rashi's explanation. Although the baraita is somewhat strained according to Rashi, his explanation nevertheless appears to me the principal one. The approach of Tosafos and their supporters is more difficult, because it makes the Gemara ask a weak question that could have been answered easily, when it should have asked directly from the baraita's statement that the creditor takes the improvement. The Ramban explains that the Gemara's question is why the purchaser does not take the excess improvement itself in land. Yet the plain sense of the passage is that the question is simply why the creditor can remove him with money rather than letting him take a parcel of land, and parallel passages in HaGozel Kama and HaMekabel use the same language in a way that clearly concerns taking land. It is also difficult on the Ramban's reading why the Gemara says that the question is satisfactory only according to the authority who holds that the purchaser cannot remove the creditor with money. To say that inability to remove the creditor makes the land equivalent to an apotiki is strained. With an apotiki, because the debtor expressly wrote that payment must come from this property, there is some basis to regard it as sold and to say, 'My land improved.' Without an apotiki, that reasoning does not apply. Moreover, the idea that an apotiki is treated as sold retroactively is not stated in the Gemara and is intrinsically difficult. From what time would it be sold? It cannot be from the date of the loan, for then the creditor would also take improvements made by the seller himself. Yet the Maggid Mishneh, when explaining the view of Rav Hai and the Halachot, writes that the creditor does not take improvements made by the seller or by the father, only improvements made by the purchaser or heirs. The Ramban and Nimukei Yosef likewise say so. The Rosh does write in the first chapter of Bava Metzia that one can explain the case where the father made the land an apotiki and said, 'If I do not pay you by a certain time, my field shall be acquired by you from now,' and the time passed only after his death. In that situation the land is treated as acquired from that later time and as one of the creditor's own fields. Tosafos in Ketubot 55a write similarly. This would imply that the land is treated as sold only where the apotiki contains such an express 'from now' clause. If the document is drafted that way and avoids asmachta, I agree that the creditor can take value beyond the debt. But the ordinary apotiki of the Gemara and the authorities is not described this way; the only language mentioned is, 'You shall have payment only from this.' One might therefore explain that this language itself is equivalent to saying that the land is presently sold to the creditor, subject to return if the debtor pays him money. The Rosh in Eizehu Neshekh may support that reading. Still, it is difficult to say that an ordinary apotiki is retroactively sold from the date of the loan. Numerous passages in the Gemara do not read that way. If it were already the creditor's property, then if he consecrated or sold it the act should be effective, yet the Tur and Mechaber above in siman 103, seif 6, explicitly rule that even with an express apotiki, if the creditor descends to the property and sells it, the sale is nothing. The Maggid Mishneh and Nimukei Yosef, citing the authorities above, likewise show that the property cannot be regarded as sold from the outset, because otherwise the creditor would also take improvements made by the seller or by the father. Nor can one easily say that the property becomes sold to the creditor only after it leaves the seller's possession, because the purchaser acquired it first. It is difficult to explain how it could then become the creditor's property, except by a strained distinction that it is deemed sold only for purposes of improvement. There is a further difficulty with the approach of Rav Hai, the Halachot, Tosafos, and Rosh that an apotiki is treated as sold and the creditor says, 'My land improved.' If so, the same rationale should strengthen the creditor concerning fruits. Even though an ordinary creditor does not collect fruits, a creditor holding an apotiki does collect them because they grew from his land, as I wrote above in seif 1, seif katan 18, citing the Rosh and Tur. Why then does the Gemara in the first chapter of Bava Metzia 14b ask, if the case concerns a creditor, how can he have a right to fruits? It should have answered that the case concerns an apotiki, where the creditor does take fruits. The difficulty is even stronger according to Tosafos there, who themselves understand that the case already concerns an apotiki, while Tosafos on 15b accept the approach of Rav Hai and the Halachot. The Gemara also asks from Shmuel's instruction to Rav Chinna bar Shilat to consult and write clauses covering land quality, improvement, and fruits: if the seizure is by a creditor, what right does he have to fruits? But if an apotiki gives the creditor a right to fruits, Shmuel could simply have instructed the scribe to include fruits because the purchaser might lose the land to a creditor holding an apotiki. Nor can one answer that fruits were included because of possible seizure by the original victim of theft, for then it would follow all the more strongly that a purchaser from a thief recovers improvement, since recovery of fruits from the thief does not create a concern of interest. One also cannot say that because the apotiki is considered sold, seizure by the creditor is retroactive and therefore resembles purchasing property that did not belong to the seller, creating an appearance of interest as with a purchaser from a thief. The Gemara according to Shmuel itself establishes the baraita, which allows recovery of improvement from the seller, where the land was made an apotiki and the debt equaled only the land without the improvement. This shows that no concern of interest arises, because the seller retained the ability to remove the creditor with money, and only now, when he fails to do so, is the land treated as passing to the creditor. I also find the basic rationale of 'my land improved' difficult. If the property is truly the creditor's and improved while in his ownership, then according to Shmuel, who says that a purchaser from a thief does not recover improvement because the land never belonged to the seller and payment would resemble interest, how can the purchaser here recover the improvement from the seller? One can force distinctions, but there is no need to prolong the matter. In conclusion, in my opinion the approach of Rashi, Baal HaMaor, and the Maggid Mishneh is primary both in the Gemara and in reasoning. Still, since many early authorities disagree, I am not worthy to decide conclusively against them. It nevertheless appears clear to me that the purchaser can plead that the law is established for him in accordance with Rashi, Baal HaMaor, and the Maggid Mishneh. Even according to the Rif, Rosh, and Tur, however, their rule applies only where the seller does not wish to litigate with the creditor, or where the seller died. If the seller wishes to litigate and remove the creditor, he may do so, and then he need pay only the amount of the debt; the creditor receives no improvement beyond that amount. The Rosh writes this explicitly in the first chapter of Bava Metzia regarding Reuven who sold a field to Shimon and Reuven's creditor came to seize it. Reuven may litigate with the creditor, and one practical consequence concerns the improvement, as explained. The Tur writes likewise below at the beginning of siman 226, as do the Ir Shushan and Sema there, seif katan 2. It follows that if the seller gives the purchaser authorization to litigate with the creditor, that authorization is effective as well."]
["Except that he pays the purchaser his expenses, etc. This applies where the loan equals only the value of the land. If the loan equals the value of both the land and the improvement, the creditor pays the purchaser nothing even for expenses. So the Tur states explicitly in seif 6, as the Sema writes in seif katan 16. The Sema's wording implies that the creditor takes all the improvement. The Bach, however, writes in seif 4 and seif 8 that according to the Tur, where the property is an apotiki and the debt equals land plus improvement, the creditor takes only half the improvement because he pays none of the expenses, whereas if the debt equals only the land he takes all the improvement but pays the purchaser's expenses. This is incorrect, and it cannot be reconciled with the Tur's approach that an apotiki is treated as sold and that the creditor can say, 'My land improved.' Rather, the Tur plainly means that where the property is an apotiki and the debt equals the land plus improvement, the creditor takes all the improvement and pays nothing, because he says, 'My land improved; let the seller reimburse you for your expenses.' Where the debt equals only the land, he pays the purchaser's expenses, because the purchaser is no worse than the seller himself, whom the creditor would have been required to reimburse for improvements under the law of one who descends into another's property without authorization. This is the view of the Rif, Rosh, and the other authorities cited above at the beginning of the previous seif katan and above in seif 1, seif katan 4. This is clear. According to the alternative approach cited immediately above, however, the only special effect of an apotiki is with respect to removing the creditor by money. The Rambam's language on this matter in chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh, cited by the Beit Yosef in seif 8, is difficult. The Raavad already objected that it cannot stand without a scribal error, and the Maggid Mishneh wrote that the text he found in Rambam's books was strange, that its reasoning was unclear to him, and that the Gemara could not be explained according to it. Lechem Mishneh attempted to reconcile the Rambam, but his words are not correct. Examine them carefully. The proper conclusion is as the Raavad and Maggid Mishneh wrote, that the text contains an error. For that reason the later authorities did not cite the Rambam's formulation, and the Mechaber likewise omitted it entirely from the Shulchan Aruch. He did well."]
