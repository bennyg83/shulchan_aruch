See Shach seif katan 22 through his words, "I have not reached the end of his reasoning, for it is obvious that here we are not proceeding under the law of gud o igud, because the purchaser has no right..." His meaning is that gud o igud is required specifically where one partner wishes to keep an indivisible property in joint ownership and the other demands either to buy him out or be bought out. In that situation the party invoking gud o igud must have the money and make that claim. But where one party has a share large enough to be divided and wishes to take his entire proper share, thereby leaving the other party with a portion too small for useful division and therefore substantially impaired, he cannot simply take his whole share and leave the other with a ruined remnant. Even if the other party does not formulate a gud o igud claim, for example because he lacks funds to buy the first party out, the party who wants his full divisible share must compensate him in money. Only where both are otherwise to remain joint owners and one seeks to terminate the partnership by gud o igud must he possess the necessary funds and expressly assert gud o igud. The Shach then writes, "Do not ask, if so, what is the Gemara's difficulty..." This does not refer to the immediately preceding sentence, but to his earlier statement that the law here is not based on gud o igud. According to the explanation just given, where one party has enough for division and wishes to take his whole portion, he must compensate the other even if the other makes no gud o igud claim at all. The Gemara's question from the teaching that if there is not enough for both they increase his share through money could therefore be answered by saying that the teaching concerns this case, while R. Nachman discusses a case where one wishes to continue joint use and the other demands gud o igud. R. Nachman could then hold that there is no law of gud o igud and that they continue using the property jointly.
But if he made it an apoteiki. See Ketzot HaChoshen, who challenges the Shach's ruling that Rashi's explanation is primary. Since the creditor takes only one half of the appreciation, and according to the view that the parties divide according to the proportion of their respective monetary interests the creditor will never recover his entire debt from the appreciation, why must he reimburse the purchaser for expenditures? As long as the creditor has not been paid his full debt, he should not have to reimburse expenditures unless the expenditures themselves exceed the unpaid debt. I do not understand why Ketzot separates the appreciation from the expenditure. The case can readily arise. Suppose the debt is one hundred ten zuz, the land itself is worth one hundred, the purchaser spends ten and improves it, and the land is now worth one hundred eleven. The actual appreciation beyond expenditure is only one zuz. The creditor collects the one hundred principal value of the land and still has ten outstanding, while the purchaser has a claim of one hundred. They divide the single zuz of appreciation according to their relative interests. With respect to that appreciation, the purchaser does not take equally with the creditor, as explained in Shach seif katan 12. The creditor takes the one zuz and the purchaser takes ten zuz representing his expenditure. Thus the purchaser recovers the entire expenditure even though the debt still extends against that value. Rashi's statement that the creditor's claim equals the value of the land and the appreciation beyond the expenditure follows the first version of R. Ashi in Bava Batra 117. According to the conclusion that they divide proportionally, the case can arise exactly as described. Ketzot also attempts to explain Rashi in Bava Metzia 15, where the creditor's claim equals the land and the appreciation, by saying that "appreciation" means only the amount beyond the expenditure. The commentators questioned why Rashi formulated the case in terms of the expenditure, and Ketzot answers from the Gemara's question: according to the opinion that a purchaser with money cannot remove the creditor, why can he not remove him by giving the creditor the land together with appreciation worth the amount owed and keep the remainder for himself? Ketzot explains that appreciation is not legally encumbered to the creditor by strict law, but only because of the enactment preventing the closing of the door before borrowers, under which Chazal treated it as though the seller acquired and sold it. Therefore the purchaser can remove the creditor only with property that is inherently subject to the lien, not with appreciation. If, however, according to Ramban, appreciation beyond expenditure is legally encumbered without an after-acquired clause, the purchaser should be able to use that portion to remove the creditor, unlike appreciation corresponding to the expenditure. I do not understand this. Even appreciation beyond the expenditure requires an after-acquired property clause, as the Shach expressly states, and this is obvious where the purchaser planted trees or built structures, which are after-acquired assets. What does not require a special seller undertaking is the appreciation beyond the expenditure. The expenditure itself requires the seller's usual undertaking because the purchaser is no worse than one who improved another's property without permission, as the Shach explains. The principal question also presents no difficulty. In an apoteiki the purchaser certainly cannot remove the creditor by tendering appreciation instead of the designated land. It is like a debtor who designates a particular field as an apoteiki and stipulates that if it is insufficient another field will also be encumbered. If the apoteiki itself is worth the full debt, the debtor cannot insist that the creditor take the other field instead, even though it too is encumbered. Even without an apoteiki, the purchaser cannot remove the creditor using the appreciation. The appreciation truly belongs to the purchaser; Chazal merely enabled the creditor to reach it because of the enactment protecting lending and treated it as though the seller acquired and sold it. Where the body of the land already contains value sufficient to satisfy the debt, the appreciation remains wholly the purchaser's, and he cannot force the creditor to accept payment from property that is entirely his in place of the land actually subject to the lien.
