If there is practical benefit, etc. Meaning that the remaining parcel retains the name and use of a field. These rules are explained below in siman 171.
Both of them. Neither can compel the other either to give him the parcel or to accept money instead. See Sema. If the debt is insufficient to take the entire field and only a small remainder would be left that no longer retains the name of a field, Razah likewise holds that the creditor must pay the purchaser money for that small remainder. Ramban disagrees. Shach rules like Razah, and this is correct.
The creditor gives him its value. Maggid Mishneh writes that if the debt is so small that assigning land of that value would leave a parcel without an independent name, the purchaser cannot be forced to accept such a fragment. Rather, the land is rented or sold for a period until the creditor can receive the money due him from its rental proceeds. See Shach.
And some say that the same applies, etc. One might have thought that because the purchaser's improvement did not benefit the creditor in a way that the creditor is required to compensate directly, since the creditor may not ultimately take that improvement, the creditor should never be compelled to accept money for the improvement. The authorities therefore state explicitly that the same rule applies. See Tumim.
But if he made it an apotiki, etc. In that case no after acquired clause is needed at all. An apotiki is treated like property already sold to the creditor, and therefore it is the creditor's own land that improved. Even improvement resulting from expenditure is collected without an after acquired clause. Most authorities hold this, and the dissenters are an insignificant minority. See Tumim.
Even all the improvement. Even if the creditor is owed only the original value of the land and the improvement exceeds the debt, most authorities hold that he takes the entire land because he can say, 'My own land improved.' Shach attempts to rule like the dissenters, who limit even an apotiki creditor to the amount of his debt. See Tumim. His proof is not decisive, and the lone opposing view is outweighed by the majority.
Except that he gives the purchaser his expenses. This refers to a case where the creditor is owed only the original value of the land, yet takes all the appreciation because he says, 'My land improved.' Even so, he must reimburse the purchaser's expenditures. If the creditor is owed both the value of the land and the improvement, one might say that he is no worse than a creditor without an apotiki, who takes without reimbursing expenses. See Tumim. I wrote that even where the debt equals both land and improvement, if the creditor wishes to take all the improvement he should reimburse the purchaser's expenses. Rambam appears to hold this way. The practical law requires study.
