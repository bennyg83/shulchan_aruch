Perhaps your father improved it. Although the creditor's claim is uncertain and the orphans claim certainty, the burden of proof rests on the orphans because we say, 'Here it is found, and here it was.' We presume the improvement arose while the property was in the father's domain. The same applies between a creditor and a purchaser. If the purchaser says, 'I made the improvement, so give me half,' and the creditor says, 'The seller made it and therefore all of it is mine,' the purchaser must bring proof, as Baal HaTerumot writes in the name of Baal HaIttur. This is also implied by Sema. See Tumim for another reason.
And some say that if he did not make it an apotiki, etc. With an apotiki, the heir cannot remove the creditor by paying money. The land is therefore certainly destined for collection, while it is doubtful whether the heirs have any right to part of it. A doubt does not remove a certainty. Without an apotiki, however, the heir can remove the creditor with money. The heir is therefore the possessor, and the burden of proof rests on the one seeking to extract. The Shach rules like Mechaber. See Tumim. His proof is not compelling.
