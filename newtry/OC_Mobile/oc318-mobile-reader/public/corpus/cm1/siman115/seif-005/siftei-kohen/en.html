The creditor, and so forth; the heirs must bring proof. From here to the end of the siman is Rambam’s wording. It implies that even without a specifically designated lien, the heirs must prove that they made the improvement. Rambam distinguishes a designated lien only later, regarding whether the heirs may remove the creditor with money. Lechem Mishneh likewise reads Rambam this way. The Maggid Mishneh reports that Ramban and Rashba limit the burden on the heirs to a case of a specifically designated lien, which shows that Rambam himself states a broader rule. The Rif in the chapter HaMekabel also supports Rambam. His limitation to a designated lien concerns the later question of payment and removal, not the initial burden of proof concerning who improved the land. If the heirs say, “We improved it,” and the creditor says, “Perhaps your father improved it,” the heirs must prove their claim. The land was already subject to collection, and the improvement is presumptively included in the creditor’s lien. This resembles other cases where the party seeking to withdraw value from the property that stands for collection bears the burden of proof. Some authorities reason that where no field was specifically designated, the creditor is merely a claimant against property in the heirs’ possession and therefore should bear the burden. That reasoning is not persuasive. The creditor’s lien exists independently of formal designation. Once the debt and lien are established, the land is legally destined for collection. The heirs seek to reserve for themselves the value of expenditures or improvement, so they must prove the fact on which that reservation depends. This is the better reading of Rambam, Rif, and the Mechaber, and it is the primary law.
