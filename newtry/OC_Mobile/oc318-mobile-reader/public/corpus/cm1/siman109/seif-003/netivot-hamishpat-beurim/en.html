For example, during a plague. This is the view of Tashbetz, cited by Beit Yosef and Taz. When property is sold at a time that is inherently unsuitable for sale, the sale resembles one made without proper announcement. Tashbetz proves this from Perek Shum HaYetomim in Arakhin. The Mishnah teaches that although slaves may be dressed to increase their value, a cow taken to the market, and pearls brought to a city, consecrated property is appraised only according to its place and time. This implies that only where value cannot increase without an affirmative act do we sell according to the present place and time. Where waiting until a plague passes will itself restore the normal market without any action, immediate sale at the depressed price is improper. Rashba, also cited by Beit Yosef and Taz, appears to say that during wartime property is appraised according to its current value. Beit Yosef therefore treats Rashba and Tashbetz as disagreeing. Taz reconciles them by distinguishing a plague, whose end and resulting recovery are expected and during which virtually no one willingly sells, from war, whose duration is unknown and during which people cannot indefinitely refrain from selling. A wartime market therefore remains a market.
In my view the authorities can be reconciled differently. Tashbetz himself seems to concede that where improvement requires an affirmative act, such as bringing pearls to a larger city, appraisal is according to the present location. Yet the Mechaber concludes this seif by saying that if a market is near the city, the property is brought to that market. Throughout the seif we are discussing property delivered to a creditor through judicial appraisal, not a voluntary sale to a third party, as appears from seif 5. It is also unreasonable that expensive merchandise located in a small town with few purchasers should be valued at a tiny fraction of its ordinary worth merely because the local residents lack funds to buy it. Taz in siman 101, seif 9, correctly says that appraisal considers the actual buyers available in the place.
The proper distinction is between appraisal and sale. The judicial appraisal itself is made according to the property's present place and time, meaning the place where debtor and creditor are situated. But if the property is to be sold and a significant market is reasonably near, it is taken to that market. We do not deliberately destroy the debtor's value by selling valuable merchandise cheaply in a small town when a nearby market will pay substantially more. Whether the market is considered near or far depends on the judgment of beit din. This resolves Tosafot's question in Arakhin. They ask how the Mishnah can imply that we do not wait, when consecrated property is generally announced for sixty days, and answer that movable property does not require announcement. Perishah questions this because Ketubot explains that movables are not announced due to concern they may be stolen, whereas Tosafot's answer sounds as though immediate sale is a Scriptural rule derived from the verse about valuation. The stronger answer is that the verse governs only appraisal. The command "he shall give your valuation on that day" fixes value according to that day and place. It does not require immediate sale. Sale occurs at the time and place that beit din considers appropriate. The Mishnah's language is exact: the entire chapter concerns appraisal. Thus, although the Sages instructed that slaves, cattle, or pearls may be moved or prepared in ways that enhance the eventual sale, the formal appraisal of consecrated property is according to its place and time because of the verse.
The same logic applies to creditors. Appraisal is based on the present local market. Sale, however, should occur at an appropriate time and place if that can be done without undue delay. Otherwise creditors would be deterred from lending if they could be compelled to take property at a value based on a distant city while bearing all the risk and expense of reaching that market. Conversely, the debtor should not be forced to suffer an avoidable loss through an improvident immediate sale. Beit din already grants thirty days in ordinary collection, and it may consider whether a market is sufficiently near. This also removes the apparent dispute between Tashbetz and Rashba. Tashbetz speaks about actual sale of land during a plague. Rashba expressly speaks about present appraisal. Their formulations may not have been intended in precisely this way, but the legal principle is sound. As to Bava Kamma's discussion of expected future price increases from Nisan until Tishrei, there the later market is considered too distant for compulsory waiting, as Ketubot explains. Judicial appraisal should therefore follow Taz in siman 101: value is assessed by reference to buyers actually available at that time and place. If the appraisers determine that no buyer would pay the theoretical full value, they must appraise at a lower cash price that in their judgment a buyer would actually pay even if he had no particular need for the item. Failure to find a buyer after an announcement does not prove the appraisal mistaken, because a potential buyer may have been absent or prevented by another circumstance. During plague or war, the same principle applies. If beit din can identify a reasonably near end to the disruption, it resembles a near market and they may wait, sometimes even longer than thirty days as explained in siman 100. If no end can be estimated, such as prolonged war in Taz's example, it resembles a distant market and they do not wait. This is the proper ruling.
The liability is upon the orphans. This means only upon the assets they inherited from their father, because the sale was made to satisfy their father's creditor. In seif 4, where property is sold for the orphans' own needs, the resulting liability falls even upon the orphans' personal assets. Who erred and sold. Seif 5 makes clear that this discussion concerns judicial delivery of property to a creditor. The rule applies only after beit din has already given the creditor a formal deed of final adjudication. If the error is discovered before that deed is written, even a slight error cancels the process, because increases or decreases in value before formal adjudication remain in the creditor's sphere only as explained in siman 103. Not to cancel the sale, but to return the amount of the overcharge. This applies whether the orphans were disadvantaged or the creditor was disadvantaged. If the orphans were overcharged and their benefit is served by leaving the transaction intact and returning the excess, we do so, because judicial sale should not leave them with less protection than an ordinary private transaction. The creditor cannot answer that if he receives the rights of an ordinary person then land is not subject to onaah. A judicial sale conducted without the direct consent of the orphans and creditor resembles an agency transaction, as Rosh writes. The threshold is one sixth, because that is ordinarily the degree at which people are considered particular about the price. In movable property the Sages determined that at exactly one sixth the transaction stands and the overcharge is returned, while above one sixth the sale is canceled. This reflects an assessment of ordinary intent. With land sold through beit din, because the judicial rule also turns on the point at which people ordinarily object, the natural result at one sixth would be that the sale stands and the excess is returned. The Sages allowed cancellation at one sixth only as a special protection for orphans. Therefore, where the orphans themselves are better served by retaining the sale and receiving the difference, the amount is returned instead of canceling. One cannot say that cancellation makes no difference because the orphans could simply repay the creditor with the land again at the corrected value. There can be cases where the land fell in value between the appraisal and discovery of the mistake, so that the creditor must retain it at the earlier appraisal while returning only the overcharge. Conversely, if the creditor was disadvantaged, there can be cases where cancellation benefits the orphans, such as when the land subsequently rose in value or where the ordinary right of return of an appraisal no longer applies. Less than one sixth. The usual time limit of enough time to show the item to a merchant does not appear to apply here when the error exceeded one sixth. If beit din, acting as the seller, was mistaken, the rule that a seller may retract until a comparable item comes into his hands does not fit judicial actors. If the purchaser was disadvantaged, we also cannot say that delay beyond the ordinary inspection time constitutes waiver, because the property was already appraised by three experts. What more was the purchaser expected to do? He reasonably relied on three professional appraisers. There is a further limitation. The law of onaah applies where the creditor or purchaser took the property at beit din's appraisal. If others bid above the appraisal and the creditor voluntarily raised his own bid and took the property at that higher price, ordinary onaah does not apply. With land, this is clear because the creditor added voluntarily and not under compulsion of beit din, making him like an ordinary purchaser. Even with movables, once expert merchants appraised the property, voluntarily paying more resembles a case in which one had time to consult a merchant and is presumed to have waived the difference. If, however, the appraisers themselves were mistaken, the purchaser can still cancel, because he may say that he intended to add a fixed amount above the true appraisal, not above an erroneous one.
