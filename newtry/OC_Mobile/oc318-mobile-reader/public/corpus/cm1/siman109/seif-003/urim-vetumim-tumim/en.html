At the time of collection. The dissenting view is that of the Tashbetz in a responsum, and his words require careful qualification. If there is such tumult from war or plague, may Hashem protect us, that no market price or ordinary buying and selling exists and every person is occupied with escaping for his life, it is obvious that property should not be sold then. Such a sale would resemble a sale without proper announcement, or a sale made at night or when workers do not ordinarily enter and leave. Indeed, Yirmiyahu was astonished when, with the Babylonian siege works already approaching the city, Hashem commanded him to buy a field. The Taz writes similarly. But this is not the case established by the Tashbetz's proof from the Mishnah about waiting to bring a cow to the market. His proof must concern a time when society continues to function and sales still occur, but property prices are severely depressed. He infers from Rashi that where one can wait for the animal to be brought to a major market, one does so, and from this he extends waiting to depressed land values. That inference is difficult. In the relation between debtor and creditor, the beginning of Bava Kamma says that if the creditor accepts payment according to his present legal entitlement he takes it now, and if he insists on other property he must take it at the future higher value. This implies that a creditor who takes the property he is legally entitled to need not wait until summer merely because everyone knows the field will then be worth more. The Baal HaTerumot, shaar 3, cites the Ramban that the same Gemara proves one need not wait for a later rise in value. Perhaps the Tashbetz distinguishes between a living debtor and orphans, following Tosafot in Eruvin who connect the rules of announcement with the rule of waiting for the marketplace. The Rambam requires a longer announcement process for orphans than for a living debtor, suggesting additional protection for heirs. Even so, the proof is not decisive. If the creditor himself says, "Do not sell now, because the low price will leave my debt unpaid; I am willing to wait," no one prevents him from waiting, especially when that also benefits the debtor. The dispute arises when the creditor demands payment now and refuses to wait for prices to rise. The Mishnah concerning consecrated property is different because there the consecrated estate itself is the claimant whose value we seek to maximize. Waiting for the market directly benefits the party entitled to the proceeds. That does not show that an ordinary creditor can be forced to postpone payment solely for the debtor's benefit. Nor is there a clear basis to distinguish orphans from a living debtor on this point without evidence. Tosafot's question from the sixty day announcement for consecrated land does not prove otherwise. Even if the announcement period is longer there to increase the return, it does not follow that every case must be delayed until the season of maximum value. The fixed announcement process can be required without indefinite market timing. Still, there is some support for the Tashbetz's distinction between a living debtor and heirs. Bava Kamma 7b asks that forcing a creditor to take property at a future high valuation would lock the door before borrowers, and the Gemara ultimately finds a possible application in a woman's ketubah, whose ordinary collection is from inferior land. Why not establish the case with adult heirs, from whom ordinary creditors also collect inferior land and where the concern of locking the door is weaker because lenders do not ordinarily anticipate the borrower's death? This may indicate that in the case of heirs the Sages indeed require waiting for a more favorable market, so the Gemara could not use heirs for the immediate choice between taking now and accepting the future high valuation. Rashi's language also supports this. He explains the ketubah case by saying that the husband gave her a divorce in Tishrei rather than simply saying that he died in Tishrei, suggesting that the rule would differ with heirs. Likewise, in Eruvin Rashi mentions estate property in connection with waiting to bring assets to a better market. The Derishah's attempt to limit that passage to a voluntary sale by an administrator is strained. The Talmud also says generally that consecrated property does not wait to bring an animal to market, whereas with orphan assets we do wait when the market day or market place is near. This suggests a real distinction between heirs and other sellers. Although the Rambam's reading of that Mishnah may differ, his commentary to the Mishnah indicates that one does not seek profit in consecrated property, which itself suggests that ordinary private property can sometimes be delayed for a better market. The Beit Yosef cites the Rashba against delaying a sale, but the Rashba's proof concerns a living debtor. In my view there is therefore room to distinguish heirs. The Beit Yosef may also have objected only where the delay itself creates a concrete risk of greater deterioration, as during war when land may soon become abandoned or damaged. In such a case land resembles movables, for which the Sages avoid delay because of the risk of loss. If no such risk is present, waiting for heirs may be appropriate when a substantially better market is near. The matter therefore depends on beit din's assessment. If the expected delay protects the estate without materially endangering the creditor or the property, the Tashbetz's ruling has substantial support. If delay creates a realistic danger of further loss, beit din may shorten even the announcement process, just as it does with perishable movables. Everything follows the circumstances seen by the judges.
But if they did not examine the appraisal carefully. See Sema, seif katan 11. We are dealing with the view of the Maggid Mishneh, whose explanation is that they did not carefully examine the appraisal and did not write an iggeret bikoret, meaning that this occurred at a time or in a place where announcement is not required. The Kesef Mishneh had difficulty with his explanation, for this is precisely the concluding clause, "and likewise if they sold land at a time," etc. Is it the Rambam's manner to repeat the same matter twice without any purpose? It appears that an iggeret bikoret is certainly the document of announcement that they give to a creditor when land has been appraised to him, so that he may consume the produce once the days of announcement have been completed. But what connection does a purchaser of the field have to the announcement? He merely has a deed of sale from beit din that he purchased the field, and from the time he gives the money he consumes the produce like any other purchaser. Accordingly, the Rambam is dealing with two forms: where the property was appraised to the creditor, and where it was sold to another. In both, if they erred by one sixth, the sale stands, but not if they erred by more. Both cases are necessary. One might have thought that a purchaser who acquired from beit din has the law of other purchasers, for whom there is no law of ona'ah with respect to land, whereas a creditor did not purchase the property, but it was merely conclusively assigned to him by appraisal, and there one can distinguish between one sixth and more than one sixth. Conversely, one can also say that with respect to a creditor, since his lien rests on the field and, in any event, an appraisal can be reversed, they are not so exacting, and therefore both cases are necessary. This is the Rambam's intent when he says, "but if they did not examine the appraisal and did not write an iggeret bikoret," which, as stated, refers to the creditor himself, to whom an iggeret bikoret is relevant; "and likewise if they sold," etc., which refers to an ordinary purchaser. Regarding both he says that the law depends on whether the error was one sixth, and the same law applies to both. It is true that in the first clause he should not have written "their sale stands" or "is void," but rather "their appraisal stands" or "is void." He was simply drawn after the terminology of the purchaser, although in truth the transfer to the creditor can also be considered a sale. This may be said according to the Maggid Mishneh. The Rambam in this clause adopted the language of the opening clause of the Mishnah, "the appraisal of the judges," etc. The Ramban, according to the Rambam's view, establishes it as a case where they appraised the property to the creditor himself, as cited in the Ran. The Beit Yosef, however, explains that these are two separate clauses. The first clause deals with a place and time where announcement is required, but they did not carry it out with the requisite care, while the second deals with a place where announcement is not required. In truth, the Rambam's wording points in that direction. The Shach, however, already wondered about this. The Gemara asks from the Mishnah, which states that if they erred by one sixth their sale is void. Either way there is a difficulty: if they made an announcement, then even if property worth two hundred was sold for one hundred their sale stands; and if they did not make an announcement, they erred in an explicit Mishnah and the transaction must be reversed. The Gemara answers that the case is a place where announcement is not required. Why did it not answer simply that they did make an announcement but did not perform it with proper care, as the Kesef Mishneh explains the Rambam? At first glance this requires study. It appears, however, that independently of this there is a difficulty in understanding what is meant by careful appraisal and announcement. If they appraised according to law through three judges, that itself is a proper appraisal. What additional exactitude is relevant? And if they did not act according to the law and established procedure of appraisal and announcement, the entire proceeding was erroneous and should be reversed. Why should the superior power of beit din make an error up to one sixth stand? It therefore appears that where the property is of a type, or the matter occurs at a time or place, for which announcement is not required, even if they nevertheless announced it, according to the Rambam their power is not thereby enhanced. The reason is that since there is no obligation upon them to announce, the matter is not incumbent upon them and they do not appraise and scrutinize as carefully, as the Sema writes in seif katan 16. This is straightforward. Accordingly, if they erred in the law and thought that a matter which truly requires announcement does not require it, yet as an added precaution and to strengthen the transaction they nevertheless announced it, then even in such a case, according to their understanding the duty was not incumbent upon them, and they did not examine and scrutinize it as carefully as required. What difference is there, from their perspective, whether in truth announcement was required or not? On this basis, the Rambam states two clauses. The first is where announcement was in truth required, but they erred and thought it was not required; therefore they did not examine and scrutinize properly, because we say that it was a matter they did not regard as incumbent upon them. Nevertheless, since they did announce it, their power is enhanced so that an error up to one sixth stands. The second is where the matter truly does not require announcement. The Rambam thereby teaches two substantial points: one case where they erred and thought announcement unnecessary, and one where announcement was in truth unnecessary. In the latter he teaches that an error of more than one sixth voids the transaction. One might have thought that since announcement is truly unnecessary, their power should be even stronger. He therefore teaches that even so, their sale stands only up to one sixth. This is precise. The Shach's difficulty is now resolved. Until the Gemara answered the foregoing question by establishing the Mishnah in a place where announcement is not required, and the initial assumption had been that announcement is required in every case, the Kesef Mishneh's answer, that they did not scrutinize properly, could not be given, because of the difficulty stated above: what additional scrutiny is relevant? Once the Gemara teaches that there are places and times where announcement is not required, however, the matter can properly be explained as above, that they erred and thought announcement unnecessary. The matter is therefore well resolved.
And if beit din wished, etc. According to the view of the Rosh in Perek Almanah and the view of the Tur, the explanation is that the purchaser deceived the orphans and their land was sold too cheaply. The choice is therefore in the hands of the orphans: either to void the sale, or to say, "Return to us the amount by which you deceived us." It cannot mean that the orphans deceived the purchaser, because the Rambam's view, as explained in seif 5, is that in such a case there is no recovery at all. Yet one must understand the Rambam's reason, "the power of an ordinary person should not be more stringent than theirs." What greater stringency is present here? Either way the purchaser can argue: if you come against me with the law of an ordinary private transaction, there is no ona'ah with respect to land; and if you come with the special power of the orphans, the sale is void. What basis is there for requiring return of the amount of ona'ah while maintaining the sale? It must be that the Rambam holds that the Sages equated movables and land in every respect concerning an appraisal by beit din, so that no distinction should be made among appraisals. With movables, certainly, if the orphans wish the purchaser to return the amount of ona'ah and maintain the sale, the sale stands and he returns the ona'ah, for their power should not be inferior to that of an ordinary person, regarding whom the law is that the purchaser acquires and returns the ona'ah. Therefore the same is true of land, since the Sages made no distinction among appraisals. The Rambam gave this reason with respect to movables, and the same law follows automatically for land. The Rambam inferred this from the Mishnah, which groups them together under "the appraisal of the judges." This includes movables as well, as the Gemara says regarding matters for which no announcement is made, meaning movables. Since the Mishnah grouped them together, it follows that one law applies to them. However, from the words of the Ran and other authorities it appears that the orphans were not the ones deceived. Rather, the purchaser was deceived, meaning not an ordinary purchaser but the creditor to whom the land was appraised in satisfaction of his debt. In that case, even though the law is that an error of one sixth voids the transaction, since the property was appraised to him against his will, as the Ramban writes, nevertheless, if the orphans wish to maintain the transaction and return the ona'ah to him, they may do so. This rule applies to movables and certainly to land. According to this, where the property is land and the orphans were deceived, perhaps they cannot compel the purchaser to maintain the sale and return the ona'ah, because he can argue whichever law favors him. The Rambam would then agree with the Rosh in this case. Why should we create a new dispute without necessity? For this reason, here in this siman, where the Mechaber copied the Rambam's wording exactly and without alteration, he did not bring the dissenting view of the Rosh. If the Rambam is explained according to the second interpretation, the Rosh does not disagree. In Even HaEzer siman 104, however, where the Mechaber explicitly wrote a case in which the orphans were deceived, he brought "there are those who disagree," meaning the Rosh, who indeed disputes that ruling. See there. It appears that according to the Rambam, if the orphans were deceived by more than one sixth but nevertheless wish to maintain the sale, the sale stands. This follows from the Rambam's ruling below in the laws of ona'ah that even where the error is sufficient to void the sale, the party who caused the deception cannot retract. The power of an ordinary person should not be greater than that of the orphans. Yet perhaps, according to the Sema's reason below that the deceiving party cannot retract because a sinner should not profit from his wrongdoing, that rationale does not apply here, since the transaction was carried out by beit din. This remains strained and requires study.
And regarding movables, the Gemara states that the reason is lest they be stolen. There is room to question the law where the movables are in the creditor's possession because he seized them as collateral. Perhaps in such a case the concern underlying the omission of announcement no longer applies, for the concern that they may be stolen is absent. The creditor is a paid bailee, either because of the benefit described by Rabbi Yosef or because he is holding them against money owed to him. What concern of theft is there, and unavoidable accidents are uncommon? From Tosafot, who ask that the movables of orphans are not subject to the creditor's lien and answer that he seized them against the money owed, it appears that they nevertheless hold that there remains a concern of an unavoidable accident, or that the Sages made no distinction, or that they were also concerned for a loss to the creditor.
