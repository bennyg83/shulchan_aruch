It is treated like an error in an explicit Mishnah. This is so even if there was no error in the valuation itself, because the procedure did not follow the enactment of the Sages.
For beit din should not sell, etc. See Tumim. This refers to a market in which merchants are still buying and selling, but property prices have temporarily fallen. These authorities hold that the property may be sold. If, however, due to great upheaval there is no functioning market at all, everyone agrees that it should not be sold. That is equivalent to selling without proper public announcement, or at a time when buyers do not come to market at all. In Tumim I brought nearly conclusive proof for Rashbatz, who disagrees and holds that property of orphans should not be sold at a depressed price, but should wait for a better market. Rashba, who gives the first view, may be speaking of the debtor himself, whose property is being collected, where the Sages did not require waiting. Therefore, even according to Beit Yosef, who disagrees with Rashbatz and holds that orphan property need not wait, this may apply only where there is a present concern that waiting until the turmoil passes could cause the land to deteriorate further for various reasons. Where no such concern exists, there is no clear proof that Beit Yosef would refuse to wait. I also wrote that according to Beit Yosef, in a case where waiting is inappropriate, beit din may shorten the announcement period if necessary to sell before the anticipated bad conditions arrive.
Liability rests upon the heirs. This means upon property they inherited from their father, not their independent property. The novelty is that one might have thought that a purchaser from beit din assumes the sale is secure and therefore resembles one who purchased without warranty. The ruling teaches that omission of warranty is treated as a scribal error, and the heirs' inherited estate remains responsible. This is from the Gemara.
Worth one hundred for two hundred. See what I wrote at the end of siman 66, that some authorities limit the rule specifically to this degree and not beyond it.
But if they did not examine properly, etc. See Tumim. Maggid Mishneh understands the entire discussion to concern a place where no announcement is required. Otherwise the concluding clause, 'likewise, if they sold land,' would simply repeat the same law. According to him, the first clause concerns beit din assigning the property directly to the creditor by appraisal and erring, while the second concerns a purchaser who bought following announcement based on the appraisal. The word 'sold' in the first clause is therefore borrowed language meaning appraisal. Kesef Mishneh instead understands the first clause to concern a place where announcement is required, but beit din failed to conduct it with proper precision. Their error is then governed by the one sixth rule. The concluding clause concerns a place where no announcement is required. In Tumim I explained that 'they did not examine properly' means that they mistakenly believed the property was of a type not requiring announcement, although they nevertheless announced it merely to strengthen the sale. Since in their view announcement was unnecessary, they did not perform it carefully enough, and the sale is protected only within one sixth.
If the error was less than one sixth, the sale stands. Here the Sages analogized land to movable property. Just as an error of less than one sixth in movable property is waived and does not indicate significant objection, so too here. But at exactly one sixth, since movable property law no longer treats the error as waived, they did not fully equate land to movable property. With ordinary movable property the sale stands and the overcharge is returned; here, because the error shows substantial objection, the entire judicial sale is void.
Or in a place where the custom is, etc. In such places people mock those who purchase property at judicial auction because most such property belonged to debtors whose land was being forcibly sold. Buyers therefore avoid judicial auctions, and for that reason the Sages dispensed with announcement there. This is from the Gemara.
The sale is void. Since beit din was not obligated to announce, it was not especially charged with meticulous announcement, and therefore the judicial sale does not receive enhanced protection.
Since they announced, etc. The opposite reasoning is that precisely because announcement was not required, yet beit din nevertheless undertook it, this shows that it exercised extra care, and its act should therefore receive greater force.
And they return the overcharge, etc. See Tumim. Rosh explains that the estate was underpaid, and understands Rambam to hold that the heirs may demand return of the overcharge while keeping the sale in force. Rosh disagrees. I wrote there that Rambam may hold that the Sages did not distinguish between appraisals of movable property and land. I also suggested that the heirs themselves are not the purchaser and may therefore return the overcharge and preserve the sale. This does not conflict with Rambam's ordinary rule that a purchaser who was underpaid cannot rescind, because here the case may involve a creditor who received land by appraisal against his will. If so, Rambam may agree with Rosh that heirs cannot insist, 'Return the overcharge and keep the sale,' where the estate itself was underpaid. Whether heirs may choose to preserve a sale even where the error exceeded one sixth is also discussed in Tumim and requires study.
Slaves, etc. There is concern that slaves may flee. With movable property and promissory notes, gathering a crowd for announcement may facilitate theft. This refers to the Talmudic rule where movable property was expressly encumbered with land or seized during the debtor's lifetime. Otherwise such property would not be subject to collection at all. See Tumim. Although a creditor who seizes is a paid custodian, the Sages were concerned even for loss to the creditor.
