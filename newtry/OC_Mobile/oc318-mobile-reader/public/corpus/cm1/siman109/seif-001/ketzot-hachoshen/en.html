Sema explains that when an heir wishes to redeem land already targeted for collection, he may insist on appraisal even if he does not presently have the money, so that if he later obtains money he need pay only the appraised value. A purchaser, by contrast, according to the Tur and Rosh, cannot force the creditor to accept less than the full debt merely because the field is worth less. The Shach follows Rambam, Raavad, Baal HaTerumot, and Rivash that neither heirs nor purchasers may discharge the creditor with less than the whole debt, because the creditor can say, "To me the land is worth my entire debt." The sugya in Ketubot supports the Shach regarding a purchaser. Where a creditor was owed one thousand and the debtor had sold two houses for five hundred each, the creditor took one house and then came for the second. The purchaser offered one thousand and said, "If the field is worth one thousand to you, fine; otherwise take the money and depart." Rava explains that the purchaser can do so because the creditor suffers no loss, one thousand is paid and one thousand is received. Tosafot derives that the purchaser's power to discharge the creditor with money rests on his saying, in effect, "The land is worth to me more than your claim." If he offers less than the debt, he has not established such a superior value and cannot remove the creditor from his lien. This reasoning applies to purchasers. An heir is different. Judicial appraisal is reversible for an heir even after appraisal and public announcement, under the rule of "you shall do what is upright and good." Therefore the heir does not need the special argument that the land is worth more to him than the debt. He can redeem the land because the appraisal itself is subject to reversion. Accordingly, an heir can pay the actual appraised value even if that value is less than the full debt, while a purchaser cannot remove the creditor unless he tenders the entire debt. This supplies a strong reason for the Tur's distinction between heir and purchaser.
