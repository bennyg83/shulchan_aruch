Afterward they announce it for thirty days, etc. The text later says that they may announce for sixty days. Since an announcement that extends over sixty days is made every Monday and Thursday, it acquires publicity equivalent to thirty consecutive days of announcement.
At the time workers enter. The Tur says: once in the morning when workers enter their work, and once in the evening when workers leave their work. In the Derishah I explained that the Tur follows Rashi. When the owner hears the morning announcement, he tells the workers as they go to their labor to inspect the field on the way and determine whether it is good or poor. In the evening the announcement reminds the owners of what they asked the workers to inspect, and they then go and ask them what they found. Tosafot explain the sequence in the opposite manner. The Rambam and Mechaber write that anyone who wishes to purchase the field should take workers to inspect it for him. Their wording implies the view of Tosafot: in the evening, when the workers return from their labor and are free, the owners hear the announcement and take them to inspect the field. See the Derishah.
How much it produces, etc. This wording does not appear in the Tur. The Rambam says, "how fine it is and at how much it is appraised." The Maggid Mishneh explains that "how fine it is" means how much can be produced from it, while "at how much it is appraised" means the valuation placed upon it by beit din, corresponding to the Mechaber's wording. Since the Maggid Mishneh interprets the Rambam's phrase "how fine it is" as "how much it produces," it appears that this refers to how much grain the field yields each year after sowing in the ordinary manner of fields. Its quality and value can thereby be assessed.

We heed the heir. This means not only that if the heir presently has money equal to the appraised value and wishes to redeem the property, we heed him. Since he inherited no more from his father, he need not pay more on his father's behalf, and he may redeem it for its appraised value. Rather, even if he presently has no money with which to redeem it, we still heed him. The reason is that once the borrower has died, there is no benefit to the borrower in having the lender accept the property for the entire debt. It is to the heir's benefit that it be appraised, so that if he later obtains money he may give the lender its appraised value and recover the field, since an appraisal is reversible even for an heir, as stated in siman 103. If, however, the lender accepts it for the entire debt, the heir would later have to pay the lender the entire debt in order to recover it, because the lender accepted it in satisfaction of the whole debt. One cannot say that it should then be appraised when the heir comes to redeem it, for the lender could claim that it was worth more when he received it and subsequently deteriorated. In siman 103, when beit din appraises the borrower's field and gives it to the lender, this rule does not apply so as to say that if the lender wishes to accept it for the entire debt without appraisal we do not heed him. There the borrower has no reason to object to the lender accepting it for the entire debt, because if the borrower later obtains money he must in any event pay the entire debt. Likewise, when a lender comes to collect from a purchaser and wishes to accept the field without appraisal for the entire debt, we heed the lender, as the Tur and Mechaber write below at the end of siman 114, seif 9. There the borrower is alive, and it benefits him for the lender to accept it for the entire debt so that the lender will not later pursue him. We therefore follow the borrower's benefit even if the purchaser wishes immediately to remove the lender by paying the appraised value. This is so even though, according to the Rambam, appraisal is reversible for a purchaser as well. We are concerned for the borrower's loss.
