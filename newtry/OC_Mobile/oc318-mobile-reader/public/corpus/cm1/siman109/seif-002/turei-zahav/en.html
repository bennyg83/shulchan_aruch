(Seif 3) Such as during a plague or during war. There is a major difficulty in Beit Yosef, from whom this ruling is taken. Beit Yosef cites Tashbetz concerning an orphan's land sold while a severe plague afflicted the city. Tashbetz ruled that the sale is void. Although the rule is that consecrated property is valued only according to its present place and time, and the same applies to a sale, as the later authorities write, that applies when the sale is made at a time fit for selling. A sale made when it is not fit to sell is like a sale without public announcement. This is indicated in the chapter Shum HaYetomim, which states that slaves may be sold dressed in clothing that enhances their value, and similarly regarding pearls and a cow. This implies that property is sold according to its present place and time only where it cannot increase in value without some act. Here, however, if they wait until the fury of the plague passes, the market will return to normal without any act. Selling now is therefore like selling at night or outside the customary hours when workers enter and leave. Beit Yosef objects from that very Mishnah, which states that a cow increases in value if one waits until the market day, although no act is required, yet nevertheless consecrated property has only its present place and time. He further supports his position from Rashba, who ruled that when a woman or creditor seeks to collect land after prices have fallen because of war, the land is appraised at its present value. The Gemara similarly says that if one takes payment now, he takes according to the present low price, and otherwise he must wait for the future high price. This is all the more true during war, whose duration and effect on prices are uncertain. I will explain that Tashbetz and Rashba do not disagree. The Mishnah in Arakhin teaches that although dressing a slave, waiting to take a cow to market, or bringing a pearl to a city may increase its value, consecrated property is appraised only according to its present place and time. Rashi explains that one who is assessed according to his means gives according to what he presently owns; a slave now worth twenty zuz is not valued as though thirty zuz were spent on clothing and he then became worth one hundred twenty. The same applies to a cow and to a pearl in a village. Tashbetz's point is that during a plague there is no market price at all, because no one sells then and all wait until the plague passes. The cow is different. Even outside market day there is a market price, since some people sell then, although most wait for market day. The same is true of a slave without fine clothing. Buying clothing requires an act, and the owner may choose not to perform it and may sell immediately, so the present price is a genuine market price with only a possibility of added profit. During a plague, by contrast, no act is lacking, the market will certainly return by itself, and no one sells at the temporary depressed price. Rashba discusses war, where a market continues to exist, albeit at a poor price, because people cannot know when the war will end and do not all wait indefinitely. Thus the wartime price is a true price. It follows that Rama's equation of war with plague is not correct. During war there is at least a market; during a plague there may be none, and the sale is then void. This is the proper ruling, not the first view as Rama understood it, and not merely as an alternative opinion.
