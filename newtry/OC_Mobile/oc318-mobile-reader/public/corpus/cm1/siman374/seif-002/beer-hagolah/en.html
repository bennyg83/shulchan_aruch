"The Rambam is there enough."
Kesef Mishneh writes that the reason we do not say that the moment the robber acquired the field it automatically became established in the first purchaser's hands, as the Tur writes and Rama cites, is that Rambam may speak of a case where immediately after acquiring it the robber sold or bequeathed it elsewhere. By that act he revealed that he did not acquire it in order to uphold his reliability toward the first purchaser. Ran, in the name of Raavad, explains similarly. Alternatively, the statement that he prefers to preserve his reliability is only an assessment of intent, relevant where the robber later dies. If he sells or bequeaths it, he has revealed the opposite intent. Since the present possessor holds the field by a valid acquisition from its owner, one who seeks to remove it bears the burden of proof. Accordingly, even if he did not sell or bequeath it but now contests the first purchaser and claims he bought it for himself, he is believed.
Tosafot likewise write so there.
