It is forbidden to seek stratagems, etc. Bava Kamma 40b discusses one who borrowed an ox believing it to be tam, but it was found to be muad and then gored. The custodian pays half the damage and the owner pays half. The Gemara establishes the case where beit din acted first and seized the ox. It then asks: why must the owner pay half? He should be able to tell the custodian, "Had you returned the ox to me, I would have driven it into the marsh and prevented collection." The Gemara answers that the owner can be required to pay from his superior property. It then asks: that works if he has other assets, but what if he has none? The answer invokes R. Natan's rule of direct collection through an intermediate debtor. Tosafot ask: what kind of argument is "I would have driven it into the marsh"? That would have been unlawful. According to R. Yishmael, who treats the victim merely as a creditor rather than as a co-owner of the damaging ox, the argument is somewhat easier to understand: hiding the ox would at most damage the victim's lien. But according to R. Akiva, who regards the victim as a partner in the ox, hiding it would be actual theft. Moreover, if the owner has assets and refuses to pay, beit din can place him under nidui. Tosafot answer that the owner's point is not that he had a right to commit wrongdoing. He means: "Through such conduct I could have pressured the victim into accepting a smaller settlement and waiving the balance." At first glance this appears contrary to Shulchan Aruch's ruling that one may not devise improper means to force another person into compromise. Terumat HaDeshen was troubled by precisely this. How can a litigant be allowed to seek dishonest stratagems in order to pressure his opponent? He therefore gives two explanations. First, the ox owner is making this argument only against the custodian. He says: "You caused me a loss. Against the victim I might indeed have acted improperly and hidden the ox until he compromised with me. Whether that would have made me liable to Heaven is not your concern. You nevertheless deprived me of the practical opportunity." Second, even if the owner would not actually have acted this way, the victim might have feared that he would. That fear alone could have induced a settlement, and the custodian's conduct deprived the owner of that bargaining advantage. Yam Shel Shlomo strongly rejects these answers. How can an ox owner appear in beit din and demand money from the borrower based on the claim, "I would have acted unlawfully and perhaps incurred liability before Heaven"? Would beit din listen to such a wicked claim rather than rebuke him? The second answer is even harder. It depends only on speculation whether the victim might have feared him and compromised. How can we extract money from the borrower based on such an imagined possibility? These are strong objections. Yam Shel Shlomo himself answers with difficulty that the owner has a grievance against the borrower because the borrower failed to guard the ox properly. But that does not explain why the owner should be permitted to use wrongful tactics against the innocent victim. What wrong did the victim do? The matter can be resolved differently. The Gemara later asks: the answer works if the owner has assets, but what if he has none? It does not simply establish the baraita as a case in which he has assets. This shows that the Gemara understands the baraita as applying generally. Because it was taught without qualification, it must work in every ordinary circumstance. The same interpretive principle can be used for the earlier question. At the beginning of Bava Metzia, the Gemara explains why suspicion concerning money does not necessarily imply suspicion concerning an oath. Perhaps the person believes he is withholding money because he has an old outstanding loan against the other party. Apply that here. Suppose the owner says regarding the victim: "He owes me an old loan. I have no witnesses, so I cannot collect it in beit din. Now that my ox has damaged him, if the ox were still under my control I would hold it back against my old debt." If he made that assertion openly in beit din, he would not be believed because he lacks proof. But if he retained the ox physically, he could hide it in the marsh and thereby preserve the practical leverage necessary to recover what he sincerely believes is owed to him. Before Heaven, according to his own truthful knowledge, he is not acting wickedly at all. He is taking steps to recover his own money. The custodian, by allowing beit din to seize the ox, deprived him of that practical ability. Tosafot's words now fit precisely. According to R. Yishmael, the question is easier because the victim is merely a creditor. The Gemara asks how the baraita can say without qualification that the owner pays half. Perhaps the owner has an old-loan claim against the victim. Had the ox remained in his possession, he could have hidden it and retained it in satisfaction of that claim. Even though hiding the ox would technically damage the victim's lien and could ordinarily create a Heavenly obligation, Heaven knows whether the owner's old-loan claim is true. If true, the victim has already received value corresponding to his damages through the unpaid debt, and the owner is not morally stealing anything. The owner can therefore tell the borrower: "You caused me to lose the practical ability to collect my old debt. You must bear that loss." The baraita's unqualified wording would then be difficult unless the law accounts for such a case. According to R. Akiva, Tosafot raise the stronger objection that the victim is already a co-owner of the ox. Mere assertion of an old loan would not authorize the owner to steal the victim's property, and beit din would force him to return it. Tosafot therefore add that he could have used continued possession to create enough procedural difficulty that the victim would agree to a compromise in order to avoid the burdens of litigation. There is no moral wrong in this particular scenario, because the owner is asserting a genuine old debt. Before Heaven, where the truth is known, he is attempting to recover what belongs to him. His procedural maneuvers are not being used to deprive an innocent person of money, but to obtain part of a claim he sincerely knows is valid yet cannot prove judicially. Thus the Gemara's question can be read broadly: how can the baraita state without qualification that the ox owner pays half? What if he has a genuine old-loan claim against the victim? In such a case, had the borrower returned the ox, the owner could have used possession and procedural pressure to induce a compromise and thereby recover at least part of his own money. This resolves both Tosafot and the objections of Terumat HaDeshen and Yam Shel Shlomo. It also yields the correct practical rule. If a person knows with certainty that another owes him an old debt but has no proof, and that person now makes a claim against him which he cannot defeat in beit din by asserting the old debt, he may use lawful procedural tactics that make the litigation burdensome enough to induce a compromise, thereby rescuing at least part of what he knows is his. There is no injustice in that conduct, because Heaven knows the truth. Such matters are entrusted to the heart, and the verse says, "You shall fear your God." But where he has no genuine old claim against the other party, Heaven forbid that he use dishonest schemes merely to exhaust the other person and force a compromise. One who does so is wicked, quarrelsome, and violent.
