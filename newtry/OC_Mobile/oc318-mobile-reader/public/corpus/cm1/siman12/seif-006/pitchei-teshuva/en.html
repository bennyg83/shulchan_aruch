And some disagree. The Bach rules like the dissenting opinion, that one may compel a person to act beyond the strict letter of the law when he has the means, such as a wealthy person. He writes that all batei din in Israel customarily compel a wealthy person to do what is proper and right even where strict law does not require it, and suggests that Rabbeinu Yerucham in the name of the Rosh may have spoken only of one who is not wealthy. The Shach below, siman 259, seif katan 3, appears to agree. Tumim explains that this compulsion means verbal pressure: beit din tells him, “You are obligated to do this, and if you refuse you are acting wrongfully.” All agree, however, that he may not be compelled by lashes, excommunication, or similar coercive measures, since strict law does not require payment. On that reading, even the Rosh may agree and there is no true dispute. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 168, says the same, contrary to Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 89, which rules that one may compel by excommunication. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Yoreh Deah, end of siman 239, cites that Tzemach Tzedek and appears somewhat sympathetic to it. Responsa Shav Yaakov, Even HaEzer, end of siman 29, sharply rejects a rabbi who sought to obligate and even coerce a litigant on the basis of lifnim mishurat hadin. He notes first that the Bach himself spoke only of a wealthy person. Second, the Rama records established customs by saying “and so is the practice,” whereas here he records one view anonymously and then “some disagree,” a formulation indicating that the first view is primary. Even in a full legal dispute we do not extract money from one who is in possession merely because most authorities disagree with him, since he may plead kim li. Here, moreover, the majority of authorities maintain that there is no coercion. Especially in the present era, it would be dangerous to authorize batei din to compel payment based on discretionary notions of what lies beyond strict law. It is possible, however, that Shav Yaakov objected only to physical coercion or excommunication, which he understood the Bach to permit, whereas he too would agree to verbal moral pressure, particularly where the poskim expressly state that one is obligated lifnim mishurat hadin.
