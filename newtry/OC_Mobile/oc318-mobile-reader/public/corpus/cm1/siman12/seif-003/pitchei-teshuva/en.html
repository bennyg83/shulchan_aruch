He should not incline the compromise. Rabbi Akiva Eiger notes in his glosses to Shulchan Aruch that just as an erroneous judicial ruling is reversed, so too an erroneous compromise is reversed, citing Knesset HaGedolah in the name of Maharit, siman 98. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part II, siman 144, discusses a compromise made by kinyan between Reuven and his partner Shimon. Reuven later objected that the compromise favored Shimon, for example, Reuven had been required to redeem with substantial money the oaths he owed Shimon, while far less was imposed for oaths Shimon owed Reuven and some were waived entirely. Reuven therefore claimed that the compromise was mistaken and should be voided. He also argued that the kinyan authorized the arbiters to compromise only regarding the disputed oaths and not regarding matters already conclusively decided, yet they compromised those as well. Shevut Yaakov answers that the first claim does not void a compromise made with kinyan. The arbiters can explain that in their judgment Reuven could not truthfully take his oath and therefore required a substantial monetary redemption, whereas Shimon could truthfully swear, and they may offer similar explanations. Matters entrusted to the heart are governed by “you shall fear your God.” Unless it is established that they accepted a bribe to distort the compromise, the compromise remains in force. The second claim, however, can void the portion of the compromise dealing with matters already conclusively adjudicated and not subject to an oath. That does not automatically void the balance of the compromise concerning the oaths, because we do not say in such a case that a compromise instrument partially void is wholly void. Maharit, siman 148, states explicitly that the compromise is void only where the arbiters erred, not where they did not err. If Reuven can establish, however, that the entire compromise was interdependent, so that he was charged in certain matters because he was granted relief in other matters already conclusively decided against him, then the entire compromise may possibly be voided. The matter requires careful consideration. See also Shevut Yaakov, siman 145, concerning an arbitrator accepted by two parties regarding a claim of three hundred reichsthalers, with authority to make a compromise close to the law. Where strict law would require payment of the entire three hundred, Shevut Yaakov writes that an ordinary compromise generally denotes an intermediate amount, but the arbitrator may adjust according to the circumstances to create peace. For a “compromise close to the law,” reducing the legal award by up to one third may still be called close to the law, for example two hundred instead of three hundred. This is not an absolute measure. If a smaller concession can produce peace, that is preferable where one party is legally entitled to the entire amount. Everything depends on the arbitrator’s judgment, but ordinarily he should not depart by more than one third from what strict law would require, because less than two thirds of the legal amount would already be considered far from the law.
