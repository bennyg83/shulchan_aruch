Even where the parties made a kinyan sudar accepting arbitrators, the arbitrators themselves may apparently retract from a compromise they orally announced while everyone is still engaged in the same matter, or one arbitrator may retract, because the statement of compromise is not itself a proprietary act in the subject matter and is weaker than the parties' own kinyan sudar. If one arbitrator says he assumed the majority would control the compromise and therefore remained silent when the other two stated their proposal, only later learning that compromise does not follow the majority, this appears to be a valid claim that he never consented. Perhaps even where he now independently becomes uncertain whether majority rule applies and asks the question, that doubt may justify setting aside the announced compromise. If the arbitrators first stated one compromise and, within the time of immediate speech, stated another because a litigant objected, the first compromise is canceled. The second is likewise ineffective unless they clearly stated it as their final ruling even if the litigant refuses, because their conduct showed that an objection causes them to reconsider. If two arbitrators accepted a kinyan to compromise and a third person merely happened to be present and announced a result, another arbitrator's silence is not necessarily admission, since he had no reason to answer someone who was not formally appointed. If, however, one of the appointed arbitrators announces the compromise in the name of the appointed panel and another remains silent, even after having previously proposed a different result, his silence while they remain engaged in the matter appears to be admission that he ultimately agreed. Where arbitrators give one party a choice between alternatives and he chooses one, he ordinarily cannot later retract once the matter has ended, all the more after the arbitrators disperse. Even while they are still discussing the matter, if money has already been deposited with a third party in accordance with his choice, that deposit functions like a proprietary act and he can retract only within the time of immediate speech, not merely because they remain engaged in the same subject. The depositary acts as agent or acquirer for the relevant party, particularly where land is involved and money fully effects acquisition. Compromises are not ordinarily conditioned on a written instrument, and one who resolves a dispute may be deemed to have acted definitively. If, however, the options were never presented as final, especially if the depositary and arbitrators themselves understood them as provisional, he may still retract.
