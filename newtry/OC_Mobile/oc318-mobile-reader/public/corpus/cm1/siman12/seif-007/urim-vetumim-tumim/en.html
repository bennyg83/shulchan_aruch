Some say specifically two. Meaning that two arbitrators are required. This follows Beit Yosef's understanding of Rambam. Rambam writes in Hilchot Sanhedrin 22:6: "The power of compromise is greater than the power of judgment. If two laymen judged, their judgment is not valid and the litigants may retract. But if they made a compromise and the parties made a kinyan from them, they cannot retract." Beit Yosef infers that because Rambam does not say even one person can make a binding compromise, although he has already written in several places that two cannot validly adjudicate, Rambam must require specifically two for compromise. Beit Yosef asks from the Gemara. The Gemara suggests that there may be three Tannaitic opinions regarding compromise: R. Meir requires three, R. Elazar son of R. Yosi HaGelili requires one, and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel requires two. R. Acha son of R. Ika answers that the opinion requiring two really agrees that even one suffices; it mentions two only so that there will be witnesses to the parties' acceptance. Kesef Mishneh gives a strained answer. If there were room to accept Mahari Sasson's position in responsum 71, another solution would be possible. Mahari Sasson says that if the litigants deny having accepted the judges, the judges themselves are not believed to testify that they were accepted. According to him, one would have to distinguish this from the rule below, siman 23, seif 1, that two judges are always believed like two witnesses to say, "We ruled this litigant liable and that one entitled." The difference would be that there the men first functioned as judges and only afterward testify as to what judgment they issued and how they conducted the proceeding. A judge can become a witness to what occurred in his judicial role. But where they must first testify that the parties accepted them as judges, their witness-role precedes the judicial role. Without that testimony there is no basis for their jurisdiction at all. They would therefore fall under the rule that a witness cannot become a judge. Even if the acceptance occurred by day, so that one might invoke "hearing should not be greater than seeing," formal designation as witnesses can disqualify them from later serving as judges according to most authorities. On this approach, the classic question of Tosafot and Kesef Mishneh becomes answerable. The Gemara says the power of compromise is greater than judgment. Why? If a kinyan was made, even judgment by one person can be binding, and certainly by two. If no kinyan was made, compromise is ineffective even before three. Where then is compromise stronger? The answer would be that the parties made the kinyan only before the two judges or arbitrators themselves, with no independent witnesses present. In judgment, even though a kinyan was made, the parties can later deny having accepted them. The judges cannot testify to the kinyan because a witness cannot become a judge. In compromise, however, the arbitrators may testify to the kinyan because there is no rule that a witness cannot become an arbitrator. Thus compromise is stronger than judgment. Rambam, who quotes this baraita about compromise being stronger than judgment, would therefore have to speak of two arbitrators. With one arbitrator, the party could simply deny the kinyan and one witness would not suffice. If the parties admit the kinyan or independent witnesses exist, then even judgment can be effective through the kinyan and there is no special superiority of compromise. This would explain Rambam's wording very well. However, Mahari Sasson's principle itself requires study. Perach Mateh Aharon objects to it, and although the foregoing analysis gives it conceptual support, the practical rule remains uncertain. First, Rambam himself apparently holds, as discussed above, that even one formally designated to testify does not necessarily become disqualified from judging merely because of the designation. He disqualifies only after actual testimony in beit din. Second, even according to those who disagree with Rambam, it appears that testimony merely about the parties' selection of judges is not part of the classic rule that a witness cannot become a judge. This is testimony about the source of their own appointment, not testimony about the substantive dispute. Tosafot in Bava Batra, concerning three people who entered to visit a sick person, imply this. They explain that the three can compel the heirs to accept their adjudication because the father effectively accepted them as judges for the inheritance arrangement. There are no other witnesses to that acceptance. The very men who know they were accepted can nevertheless become judges. This shows that we do not apply "a witness cannot become a judge" to the mere fact of judicial appointment. If so, Rambam's wording still appears to support Kesef Mishneh's inference, and the original Gemara question returns. I believe Rambam can be explained differently. Why did the Gemara find it so difficult to accept that there were three Tannaitic opinions? Perhaps there really are three. R. Meir could require three because he believes compromise demands three people knowledgeable enough to formulate a fair arrangement, as Tosafot suggest. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel could require two because compromise needs a majority-like decisional structure. In an ordinary three-judge court, even if one judge dissents, the other two form the decision. In compromise, because all arbitrators must agree to the compromise, two concurring arbitrators could suffice. R. Elazar could hold that compromise requires no such decisional structure and therefore one is enough. Why is three distinct opinions so implausible? The answer is that R. Acha son of R. Ika's entire discussion follows his own view elsewhere that two who judged have a valid judgment after the fact, in accordance with Shmuel. For him, there is a fixed assumption: compromise should not require more formality than judgment. R. Meir can still require three for compromise because R. Meir follows his own position that civil judgments require three d'oraisa through the comparison of disputes to negaim. But if Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel truly required exactly two for compromise, that would imply that ordinary judgment requires three while compromise needs two. Since both R. Meir and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel would then reject Shmuel's rule that two judges can render a valid judgment, the majority view would be against Shmuel. R. Acha, who himself follows Shmuel, therefore presses to interpret Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's "two" as nonessential. Even one arbitrator suffices; two were mentioned only for evidentiary convenience. But we do not rule like Shmuel. We hold that two who judged do not produce a valid judgment. There is therefore no need for us to force Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's wording away from its plain meaning. We can understand the three opinions straightforwardly. R. Meir requires three arbitrators, perhaps because proper compromise requires a group knowledgeable in compromise. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel requires exactly two. Judgment needs three because a beit din cannot be evenly divided; compromise, where the arbitrators must agree, can be formed by two. The first Tanna, who requires only one, is the minority against both of them. Accordingly, the Gemara's reinterpretation of "two" as meaning "even one" is a forced reading necessary only for R. Acha son of R. Ika's own position in favor of Shmuel. Since our halachah rejects Shmuel, we may read Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel literally. Rambam therefore rules like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that compromise requires two. There is an additional reason. R. Meir requires three and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel requires two. Both agree that one is insufficient. The first Tanna, who permits one, is a lone view against them. The poskim frequently rule in comparable situations according to the shared position of two Tannaim against the third, choosing the less stringent of the two majority views where appropriate. Rambam therefore correctly rules that two arbitrators are required.
If he gave a document regarding it. Sema, seif katan 18, understands this as explained in Urim: if a person gives a written undertaking to obligate himself according to whatever the arbitrators decide, the undertaking is effective. His proof is from below, siman 40, where a person can obligate himself by means of a document even though he owes the other person nothing. But if that proof were decisive, then even an oral declaration before witnesses, saying "you are my witnesses," should suffice, as explained below in siman 40. We have never heard such a rule here. Rather, there is a distinction. When a person accepts an unconditional obligation upon himself, not dependent on another person's future decision, even an oral declaration can be effective, and certainly a document. But where the obligation is uncertain and depends upon the future decision of others, a full kinyan is required because the matter resembles an asmachta, as I wrote below in siman 40. All the more so according to Rashba, responsum 1030, who expressly requires a kinyan here because the matter has the character of an asmachta. If so, how could a mere written document remove the asmachta problem? A kinyan is specifically required. Sema's ruling therefore requires study.
