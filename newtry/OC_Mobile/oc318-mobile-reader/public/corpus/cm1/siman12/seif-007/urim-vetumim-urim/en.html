They may retract, etc. Even the claimant who agreed in a compromise to waive part of his claim may retract before a kinyan. We do not say that ordinary waiver requires no kinyan. A voluntary waiver made entirely of one's own will indeed requires no kinyan. But here the waiver resulted from the arbitrators' intervention and was not independently chosen. The claimant can say, "The arbitrators persuaded me incorrectly, and I did not waive with a complete heart." Therefore a kinyan is required. Sema. Accordingly, where the litigants themselves reach a compromise directly with one another, this reason does not apply and an actual waiver can be effective without a kinyan. Maharam of Lublin writes so, cited by Shach. Likewise, if after the compromise a litigant independently says, "I waive it to you," that is a valid waiver. Darchei Moshe and Sema. Taz explains that this means the statement was not merely extracted by continued pressure from the arbitrators. If he yielded only to escape their pressure, he can still say his heart was never fully in the waiver.
A compromise requires a kinyan. If the parties made a formal handshake undertaking, beit din compels compliance with that undertaking while the party is alive. If he dies, the heirs are not personally bound by his handshake and may challenge the compromise. Maharshal writes that where the parties submitted to three judges who were not learned, they necessarily submitted for compromise rather than strict law and no additional kinyan is needed. Bach rejects this, correctly. Below in siman 22 it is clear that when disqualified judges are accepted to adjudicate and err, the parties may in some circumstances retract. Mordechai makes this explicit even regarding judges lacking legal expertise. See Tosafos and Maharshal's strained resolution.
Specifically before two. See Tumim. If the kinyan was made before two arbitrators and the litigants later deny it, Mahara Sasson holds that the arbitrators are not believed. See Tumim, where I wrote that in compromise even Mahara Sasson should agree that they are believed. Even in ordinary judgment, the better view is that the judges are believed against the litigants.
The same applies if he gave a document, etc. Sema explains that the Rama does not mean a document given after the exact compromise was already known, stating the fixed amount now owed. That is addressed by the Mechaber below in seif 13. Rather, before the arbitrators reveal their decision, a party writes an obligation upon himself in broad terms, such as, "I hereby obligate myself up to such and such amount, according to whatever the arbitrators determine between us." This can work because a person can obligate himself by document even where no prior debt existed, as explained below in siman 40. See Tumim, where I questioned whether the formulation is always effective.
So that it should not be a kinyan on mere words. For the defendant, a kinyan merely "to give" later is a kinyan on words and is ineffective. He must transfer or bind a concrete object or existing obligation. For the claimant, however, a kinyan to waive an existing claim can be effective. If the claimant holds a promissory note, the kinyan should relate to returning or surrendering the actual note if the arbitrators so decide. A kinyan merely to execute a future waiver document is only a kinyan on words. Taz in siman 203 goes further and argues that even a bare kinyan "to waive" resembles a kinyan on words and that the proper method is to bind the actual object or instrument itself. His position appears correct.
