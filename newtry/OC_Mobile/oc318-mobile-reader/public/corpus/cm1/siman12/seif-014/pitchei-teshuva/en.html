Waiver does not require a kinyan. Baer Heitev cites the Shach’s uncertainty, and the Shach below, siman 241, seif 2, treats the matter as a doubt in law. Tumim observes that if so, the familiar rule that one who sells a promissory note and then waives the debt has effected a waiver would apparently require a kinyan, yet no authority says so. He therefore distinguishes: where the creditor still holds the document, an oral waiver may be ineffective because had he truly intended to forgive, he should have returned the document. Where the document is in another’s hands, however, there is no such indication of incomplete intent, so in the case of a sold note the original creditor can waive without kinyan. The Shach below, siman 66, seif katan 74, states this explicitly in the name of Baal HaIttur. Responsa Brit Avraham, Choshen Mishpat, siman 1, letter 3, adds that if the creditor did not forgive the principal debt but merely extended the payment dates, all agree that the extension is effective without kinyan, for retention of the document proves nothing in such a case. See also Shaar Mishpat below, siman 120. The Shach further challenges the Bach’s explanation of Rabbi Yeshayah, cited by the Tur, that a waiver is ineffective while the creditor holds the document because Rabbi Yeshayah follows Beit Shammai that a document standing for collection is regarded as already collected. We do not rule like Beit Shammai. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 32, defends the Bach. See also Responsa Shaar Ephraim, siman 133, Ketzot HaChoshen, Divrei Mishpat, and Birkei Yosef. Noda BiYehudah there discusses a woman who waived her ketubah before receiving a get and later retracted while still holding the ketubah document. He writes that one cannot extract money from the husband where Rama in siman 241 rules that waiver does not require kinyan even if the document remains in the creditor’s hand. Even according to Rabbi Yeshayah, as explained by the Bach on the basis that a collectible note is like collected property, that reasoning may apply only where the debtor owns assets at the time of waiver. If the debtor has no assets and only his person is obligated to pay if he later acquires property, the creditor can waive that personal obligation even while retaining the document; if the debtor later becomes wealthy, the obligation has already been forgiven. In the case at hand the husband had no property, so the wife’s waiver was effective. He adds that if she used language of gift, saying in the vernacular, “I give him the ketubah,” no kinyan is needed in any event. Even if the debtor’s property were treated as belonging to the creditor, this is no stronger than a deposit in another’s possession, which may be gifted to the bailee without an additional kinyan, as in siman 241, seif 2. The problem of the word “waiver” with a deposit therefore does not affect an express gift. Thus the wife cannot collect the ketubah. See what I write in Pitchei Teshuva, Even HaEzer, siman 105, seif katan 4. Misgeret HaShulchan and Ateret Tzvi cite Maharshal that if a creditor admits that in his heart he had forgiven the debt, but now seeks revenge for some later reason and therefore sues, his claim is ineffective because a waiver in the heart is a waiver even while he retains the document. They infer this from the rule that a woman who does not demand her ketubah for twenty-five years is deemed to have waived it without an express declaration. Ketzot HaChoshen objects from Maharit, part II, siman 45, that an unexpressed waiver is ineffective unless what is in his heart is known to everyone. Netivot HaMishpat agrees, but distinguishes that this limitation applies to waiving a debt already incurred. In a case such as “live with me” or “eat with me,” if the owner says he intended from the outset to provide the benefit gratuitously, that intention is effective because it is equivalent to giving a gift at the time of the act.
