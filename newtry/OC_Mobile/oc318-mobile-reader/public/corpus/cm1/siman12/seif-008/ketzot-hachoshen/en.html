Waiver does not require a kinyan. The Sema writes that this is specifically where the creditor does not hold a debt instrument. If he holds a note, waiver without a kinyan is ineffective. Mordechai brings proof that even a documented debt can be waived without a kinyan from the rule that one who sells a debt instrument may later waive the debt. Chelkat Mechokek relies upon this proof. According to Rabbeinu Tam, however, the reason a seller of a debt instrument can waive is that the lender possesses two liens: a personal obligation upon the debtor and a lien upon the debtor's property. The personal obligation cannot be sold because it has no tangible substance, and therefore remains with the seller. The seller can waive that personal obligation, after which the property lien falls away because it is only secondary guaranty. A creditor holding his own note is different. The reason some authorities require a kinyan to waive such a debt is that a clear debt instrument is treated for certain purposes as though already collected. Although we do not generally follow Beit Shammai's doctrine that what stands ready for collection is already collected, a note is nevertheless treated as collected for some legal consequences. For example, an ordinary migo cannot extract money, while a claimant holding a note may sometimes extract on the basis of a migo because the assets are already encumbered. Knesset HaGedolah discusses where the note is treated as collected and concludes that where the claim itself is clear, the note has this stronger status. Accordingly, when the note has already been sold, the seller no longer possesses the lien on the debtor's property and will never collect through that note. Only the personal obligation remains with him, and he may waive it without a kinyan. But with his own note, because the property lien remains in the document and is treated in some respects as already collected, waiver without kinyan may not suffice. Ramban similarly explains that a documented claimant can use a migo to extract because the note places him in a position analogous to possession, though not literally complete collection. Even so, most authorities rule that waiver is effective even with a note. Misgeret HaShulchan cites Maharshal that if the creditor says that in his heart he already waived the debt but later chose to exact revenge and now wishes to collect, the mental waiver is effective even though he still holds the note. He proves this from a widow who loses her ketubah by failing to demand it for twenty five years, even without any express statement. This is surprising. We do not find ordinary legal effect for mere thoughts, whether in ownerless property, consecration, or vows, except for sacrificial consecration where the Torah expressly recognizes a willing heart. One might seek to explain it as removal of rights, but such removal works only before the right fully comes into one's possession, as with a betrothed woman, not with an existing creditor. Further, the Mishnah says that a receipt should not be returned because perhaps it was written and the writer later reconsidered and never gave it. "Reconsidered" implies that he initially intended waiver, yet the borrower did not acquire anything through that thought. Maharit resolves the broader question. Where a person's internal intention is universally obvious, it is not treated as legally ineffective unexpressed thought. But where no one can know his intention, thought alone has no effect. This is correct. Mordechai likewise explains vows made under compulsion: when the person inwardly limits the vow to today, that understanding can be effective because everyone knows the vow is made only because of the coercion. The widow's twenty five years of silence likewise evidences an intention universally understood as waiver. The same is true where one sues for wheat and the defendant admits barley. The claimant's failure to demand barley demonstrates obvious waiver. Similarly, ownerless status can sometimes arise from an unspoken but universally evident intent. By contrast, a creditor who merely intended privately to waive, without anything that made that intent known to others, has only an unexpressed thought, which is legally ineffective. Even writing a receipt does not prove present waiver, because he may intend to waive only later when the receipt is delivered.
