You are entitled, etc. This is the ruling of all the poskim. Semag, however, writes: "What is considered final judgment, after which one may no longer propose compromise? When they say, 'So-and-so, you are entitled; so-and-so, you are liable.' Yet we say in the first chapter of Bava Metzia in the name of Rav Nachman that this is not complete final judgment until they say, 'Go and pay him.'" Bach and Shach, seif katan 5, infer that according to Semag, until the court says "go and pay him," compromise remains permitted, because the case has not yet reached true final judgment. I do not think this is Semag's intent. It is difficult to say that Semag disagrees with Tosafot and all the other authorities on such a central rule without stating the disagreement explicitly. Rather, Semag agrees with the accepted ruling, as the Gemara itself indicates, that once beit din says "you are entitled" and "you are liable," compromise may no longer be proposed. Semag was troubled by Tosafot's question against Rashi: if beit din has already declared one party entitled and the other liable, what room remains for compromise? The case appears decided. Tosafot answer that perhaps the court may still reconsider. Semag found that explanation difficult and therefore sought another way to understand why compromise is still conceptually imaginable even after such a declaration. He brings the proof from Bava Metzia: even after beit din says "you are liable" and "the other is entitled," the debtor may not yet pay because he thinks the judges may reconsider and discover an error. For that reason, before they say "go and pay him," there remains enough uncertainty in the litigant's mind that payment is not presumed to have occurred. If the litigant himself still regards the ruling as potentially reversible, one could have thought that beit din may say, "Since the matter is not yet absolutely settled in your minds, go and make a compromise." The Gemara therefore teaches that this is already forbidden. But once beit din says "go and pay him," there is no remaining uncertainty in anyone's mind that the judgment might be reconsidered. At that point even the conceptual possibility of suggesting compromise disappears entirely, exactly as Tosafot's question assumes. Semag therefore does not permit compromise until "go and pay." On the contrary, he uses the Bava Metzia distinction to explain why the prohibition against compromise already begins earlier, at "you are entitled; you are liable." That is why he says the earlier stage is not "complete" final judgment. It is sufficiently final to prohibit compromise, even though it is not yet the strongest possible form of judgment for purposes of the presumption of payment. This, in my view, is clearly his intent, and the later authorities did not penetrate the purpose of his citation. Shach also writes in the name of Shiltei Gibborim that if the judges explain the nature of the compromise to the litigants and speak persuasively to them without coercion, compromise remains permitted. This is difficult in light of Rashba and Agudah, whose ruling Shulchan Aruch cites: after final judgment, a person who is not one of the judges may still bring the parties to compromise. That outsider certainly has no power to compel them. Who appointed him as ruler or judge? Necessarily his role is to speak to them, explain matters, persuade them, and obtain their voluntary agreement. The ruling nevertheless distinguishes sharply between an outsider, who may do this, and the judges themselves, who may not. This appears contrary to Shiltei Gibborim. It is strained to say that Rashba and Agudah speak only where the parties first made a kinyan accepting whatever compromise the outsider would later impose, while Shiltei Gibborim speaks where the judge merely explains a known proposal. Why should that distinction matter? The kinyan given to the outsider was also voluntary. No one forced the parties to accept his authority. Whether they voluntarily agree in advance to whatever compromise he will formulate, or voluntarily agree after hearing the precise terms, in both cases there is no coercion. Yet Rashba and Agudah permit the outsider while forbidding the judge. Therefore Shiltei Gibborim's leniency requires study. Bach likewise questions it.
And some disagree. This opinion is found in Mordechai. None of the proofs Mordechai brings establishes decisively that beit din may physically compel a litigant to act beyond the strict letter of the law. There is also a difficulty in Mordechai's wording. He says, "We compel him to act beyond the letter of the law if we have the ability to do so." What does it mean to make the rule depend upon whether beit din "has the ability"? The same limitation applies to ordinary law. If the court lacks practical power, what can it do? Unfortunately, since violent people have increased, the practical power of courts has weakened. If the court does have coercive power to enforce strict law, then according to Mordechai's supposed view it should have the same power to enforce compromise or conduct beyond the strict law. Why distinguish them? Bach explains "if we have the ability" as referring to whether the litigant is wealthy, but anyone who examines Mordechai will see that the distinction between rich and poor appears only afterward. It is not the meaning of this earlier phrase. It therefore appears clear that all the proofs Mordechai cites use expressions of "obligation," which can indicate a form of compulsion, but only verbal compulsion. This resembles the dispute in Yevamot and Ketubot over the phrase "we compel him to divorce." Sometimes "compel" means that beit din tells him forcefully: "You are obligated to do this. If you refuse, you are acting as a transgressor," without using lashes, nidui, or physical force. Likewise, where a person is expected to act beyond the strict letter of the law, the court may press him verbally, tell him he is morally obligated to do so, and characterize refusal as improper. But it may not compel him with lashes, nidui, or comparable force, because strict law does not require the act. See Tosafot in Ketubot 50, concerning the Gemara's phrase "I compelled them," where Tosafot explain that in matters beyond strict law the compulsion is only through words. This is precisely the distinction between strict law and conduct beyond the law. Mordechai therefore says, "if we have the ability," meaning if the person is one who listens to Jewish beit din and respects its words. In such a case verbal pressure can accomplish the result. But if he is not submissive to beit din and the principle "a servant is not corrected by words" applies to him, the court may not escalate to physical force or nidui merely because the morally preferable conduct exceeds strict law. Strict law is different. There beit din uses every lawful form of coercion available. Accordingly, Rosh may agree entirely with this. There may be no actual dispute between the opinions once Mordechai is properly understood.
