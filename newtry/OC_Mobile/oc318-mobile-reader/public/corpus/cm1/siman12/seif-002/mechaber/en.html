It is a mitzvah to say to the litigants at the outset, 'Do you want a legal judgment or a compromise?' If they choose compromise, a compromise is arranged between them. Just as a judge is warned not to distort the law, so is he warned not to incline the compromise more toward one party than the other. Any beit din that regularly brings about compromise is praiseworthy. This applies before the final judgment. Even after the judges have heard the parties' claims and know toward whom the law inclines, it is a mitzvah to propose compromise. But after the judgment has been completed and the judge has said, 'So-and-so, you are entitled; so-and-so, you are liable,' he may no longer arrange a compromise between them. A person who is not serving as a judge in the case may arrange a compromise outside the formal court session. If the beit din imposed an oath on one litigant, the beit din may arrange a compromise so that he can avoid the gravity of an oath. {Rama: A beit din cannot compel a party to go beyond the letter of the law, even if the judges believe it appropriate (Beit Yosef in the name of Rabbeinu Yeruham and the Rosh). Others disagree (Mordechai, chapter 2 of Bava Metzia).}
