Their wives were ill. Meaning, at the time the sage prohibited her or the witness testified. Nimukei Yosef explains that if his own wife was ill when he testified, we suspect that he intended eventually to marry the woman. By the same logic, where he and his wife were in a serious quarrel at the time he prohibited her or testified, he should likewise be forbidden to marry her, because we may suspect that he intended to marry her. On this basis one could interpret the Gemara opposite to Rashi. The mishnah says that where these men had wives who later died, they may marry the women in question. Rashi explains that because the wives were alive at the time of the relevant act, there was no suspicion that the sage or witness acted in order to marry the woman. The Gemara distinguishes divorce from death and says that if there had been prior quarrel, divorce likewise creates no suspicion. But according to the law stated above, one could explain oppositely: if there was already quarrel when he prohibited or testified, we suspect that he intended to marry her, so if he later divorced his wife he may not marry the woman; whereas the baraita permitting even after divorce speaks where there had been no quarrel at the time of his act.
