Both are powerful. See Bach, who labors at length to defend the sages of later generations who sometimes refrain from judging violent people even when both litigants are powerful. See there his interpretation of Tosafot, and see Kenesset HaGedolah for what he cites from Maharanach. In my view the proper defense of contemporary sages can be derived from Rashi's wording. Rashi explains "a difficult litigant" as a quarrelsome person who burdens the judges. On the phrase "if he is found to be powerful, he pursues him," Rashi explains: he pursues the judge in order to overturn the judgment. This indicates that "difficult" here does not mean a wicked person from whom the judge fears physical harm, denunciation to non-Jewish authorities, or similar retaliation. It means a contentious litigant who will overwhelm the judge with deceptive arguments, repeated claims, and confusion until he may disturb the judge's mind and cause an erroneous ruling. That is why there is a difference between before and after the judge knows which way the law inclines. Before he has reached clarity, the quarrelsome litigant may confuse him and distort his judgment. Once the judge already knows the law and has reached a settled conclusion, it is much harder to shake him. Even a powerful wind will not move him from his place. Accordingly, where both litigants are similarly forceful, the concern described in the Gemara is reduced, because neither side alone can overwhelm the process and the law can remain firmly established. But where the litigant is a violent person and the judge fears that he will bear a grudge, cause personal harm, denounce him, or fabricate accusations against him, the fact that the opposing litigant is equally powerful does not solve the problem. If the violent litigant harms the judge, what can the other party realistically do? It is far easier to cause harm than to undo it afterward. Chazal have already expressed the idea that causing harm is easier than removing harm. Therefore, in our generations, when unfortunately informers and violent people have become common, a judge may withdraw before undertaking the case even where both litigants are powerful.
Among transgressors. Bach asks from Sifrei, which indicates that even if the litigant threatens to kill the judge, the judge may not refrain from judgment. He gives a strained resolution. In my view there is no difficulty. Mahari Weil speaks only before the judge has undertaken the case. Rambam and Rashba hold that a permanent communal judge ordinarily may not withdraw even before formally undertaking a case. Mahari Weil says that where danger is common, the accepted practice is not to protest when judges decline to involve themselves at all. But once the judge has already undertaken the case, Mahari Weil would certainly agree that Heaven forbid he should withdraw because of fear or intimidation. Once he has entered the case, he is obligated to judge it. That is the case discussed by Sifrei. Even if the litigant threatens to kill him, the judge may not withdraw after he has already undertaken the matter. I later found that Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 143, rules similarly: after the judge knows which way the law inclines, he may not withdraw because of fear under any circumstances. This is the correct practical rule.
