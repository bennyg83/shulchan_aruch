A compromise requires a kinyan. Rabbi Akiva Eiger notes that if the compromise was made before the city elders or communal leaders, no kinyan is required, citing the Bach and Responsa Maharanach, part II, siman 49. Knesset HaGedolah cites the same Bach and Maharanach. This is puzzling, since the law is already explicit below, siman 22, seif 1, in the Rama, in the name of Teshuvot Maimoniyot at the end of Hilchot Sanhedrin: if the judges are municipal appointees or communal leaders, a litigant may not retract. The Shach there indicates that even three such officials suffice. The Bach and Maharanach themselves cite that responsum. The Bach further reports that Maharshal, Responsa siman 4, ruled that where two litigants came before three persons authorizing them to decide whatever they saw fit, or came before three laymen unlearned in law, so that their initial submission was necessarily for compromise, they cannot retract even without a kinyan. Maharshal argues that even the Rambam agrees, because the Rambam required a kinyan only where they initially came for strict adjudication and then accepted compromise. The Bach rejects this distinction, reasoning that according to the Rambam, even a compromise accepted before beit din can be retracted without kinyan, despite there being no claim of jesting, so the same should be true where the original submission was for compromise. He concludes that kinyan is always necessary unless the compromise was accepted before the city elders. Knesset HaGedolah cites Maharshal but strangely omits the Bach’s disagreement, and Urim VeTumim agrees with the Bach against Maharshal. Birkei Yosef discusses Responsa Masat Moshe, siman 62, which relied on Maharshal in a case where merchants were called before lay arbitrators expressly appointed to waive and compromise. Birkei Yosef argues that a careful reading of Maharshal shows that where private persons themselves enter between the parties to mediate and call the creditors before them, Maharshal agrees that kinyan is required. He dispenses with kinyan only where the parties themselves came before judges and their original appearance was either for law or compromise, especially where the forum consisted of laymen clearly suited only to compromise. Thus in Masat Moshe’s case, where the arbitrators summoned the creditors and mediated between them, even Maharshal would require kinyan. Moreover, Maharshal is not an uncontested authority: the Bach explicitly disagrees, Torat Chesed at the end of siman 122 rules against Maharshal, and the Shulchan Aruch, Levush, and Responsa Tomat Yesharim, siman 128, also point that way. A party in possession can therefore certainly plead kim li in accordance with the Bach and those who agree with him.
