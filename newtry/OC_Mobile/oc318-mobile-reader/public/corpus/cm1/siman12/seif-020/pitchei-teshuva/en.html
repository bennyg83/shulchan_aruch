And afterward he found witnesses. Dagul MeRevavah writes that the compromise is deemed mistaken specifically where the witnesses already knew the relevant facts at the time of the compromise but the plaintiff did not know that those witnesses existed. Had he known that testimony was available, he would not have compromised. If, however, at the time of the compromise there were no witnesses at all, the compromise was not mistaken. I do not fully understand the factual case he means. Perhaps his intent is that at the time of the compromise the witnesses said they did not remember, similar to Ketubot 20b, where Rav Ashi initially did not remember testimony for Rav Kahana and later recalled it. If Rav Kahana had compromised when Rav Ashi said, “I do not remember,” the compromise would not be considered mistaken merely because Rav Ashi later remembered. Alternatively, perhaps he means a case in which the witnesses were related through marriage at the time of the compromise and later ceased to be relatives. See what I write below, siman 33, seif 17, seif katan 11. The matter requires study.
