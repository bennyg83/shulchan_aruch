We presume that he delivered it. Baer Heitev explains that we place the witnesses on their presumption of proper conduct: they would not have signed an invalid moda'ah. Responsa Shivat Tzion, siman 111, notes that Maharit questions this rationale because witnesses to a moda'ah need not investigate whether the sale has already occurred; they may merely testify to the coercion, and the seller can later prove that the moda'ah preceded the sale. Maharit therefore offers another reason: where the timing is uncertain, the holder of the document bears the disadvantage. Bach similarly explains that when it is uncertain whether the moda'ah preceded or followed the sale, one leaves the land with its original owner and the sale fails. Shivat Tzion writes that Maharit's objection is compelling where the transaction is one without public notoriety, such as a sale of movable property or another private matter, since the witnesses had no duty to know whether it had already occurred. But where the moda'ah concerns a sale of land, which ordinarily becomes known, the Sema's reason is sound: had the sale already taken place, the witnesses presumably would have known and would not have written a future-tense moda'ah declaring that he was being forced to sell. Shivat Tzion further notes that although Darkei Moshe, citing an old Mordechai, mentions both sale and gift, Rama in the gloss here mentions only sale. Chiddushei Anshei Shem cites Rabbeinu Yitzchak and Maharam as agreeing in a sale but not in a gift, because witnesses to a gift need not investigate anything, since they need not write that they themselves know of the coercion. This may explain Rama's omission of gift. There is also a practical difference between Rama's own presumption and the Sema's presumption where the witnesses did not write the moda'ah but now testify orally that it was made and cannot recall whether before or after the sale. Under the Sema's reasoning, there is no presumption based on their signatures, so the moda'ah would not defeat the sale. Under Rama's reasoning, since the coercion itself is known, one may presume the moda'ah was delivered beforehand. Shivat Tzion applies this to a case where Reuven asserted claims against Shimon and Shimon produced releases in which Reuven waived all claims. Reuven produced witnesses that he had made a moda'ah because Shimon held documents with which he could inform against him, but the witnesses had not written the moda'ah and could not establish whether it preceded the releases. Shivat Tzion ruled the moda'ah ineffective. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak and Maharam, a moda'ah concerning a waiver, which is like a gift, is in any event not within the presumption. Even according to the old Mordechai, the Sema's rationale applies only where the witnesses actually signed. Here they did not, and Shimon is presently in possession, so Reuven must prove when the moda'ah was made. Ketzot appears to favor Bach's and Maharit's rationale and writes that where a compromise has the law of a sale and the claimant presents an uncertain moda'ah, the defendant remains in possession and the compromise stands. Ateret Tzvi adds that if the judges see that the witnesses themselves do not understand that a moda'ah must precede the sale, the matter is doubtful, and notes the communal enactment that a moda'ah be made only before the presiding rabbi. In my humble opinion, even if the witnesses clearly lacked that knowledge, Bach's and Maharit's reasons would still void a sale where the seller is the possessor; the main reason for such enactments is rather that witnesses must properly recognize the coercion, and lay witnesses may err about what constitutes halachic coercion. This requires study.
