See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 35, concerning one who delivered a promissory note to an agent with instructions to compromise with the debtor in a certain city and for a specified amount, but the agent deviated and compromised for less. This case was cited above, siman 123, seif 5, seif katan 2. Noda BiYehudah writes at the end that the creditor's declaration of protest is also difficult to validate. What recognizable coercion was there? The creditor could have authorized the agent to sue the debtor in the debtor's own city. Even if the agent says that the debtor was delaying him and was powerful in his city, the agent should have made the declaration of protest. Since the agent did not, the compromise stands, especially because the debtor can say to the creditor, "You are not my litigant; perhaps you sold the note to the agent or gave it to him as a gift." In a gloss, the author's son questions his father's statement that recognizable coercion is necessary, because Shulchan Aruch, siman 205, seif 6, rules that with forgiveness, recognizable coercion is unnecessary and it suffices that the giver revealed that he was not acting wholeheartedly. He suggests that because the matter was handled through an agent, it more closely resembles a compromise. According to what I wrote above, seif 3, in the gloss cited by Baer Heitev from the gaon Maharam Eizik, that a compromise has the law of a sale only in the sense that the coercion must be known, but once the coercion is known the compromise is void even without a declaration of protest, this answer does not resolve the matter.
