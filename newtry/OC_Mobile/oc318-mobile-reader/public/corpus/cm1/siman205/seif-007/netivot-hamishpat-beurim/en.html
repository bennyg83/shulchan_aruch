A person sometimes makes an exaggerated threat. According to Maharit's responsum, as he explains Rosh there, if the person delivered a moda'ah and showed the witnesses that he regarded the threat as coercion, that can itself be considered coercion. Mahara Chashon likewise writes this on page 13b. If so, Rama's ruling here appears difficult. The case concerns sale, as shown by the incident of the orchard, and therefore necessarily involves a moda'ah. On Maharit's approach even Maharik should agree where the person delivered a moda'ah. Mahara Chashon further writes there that if a person was threatened merely with being brought before non-Jewish courts, everyone agrees this is not coercion. Only a threat involving a sufficiently certain danger of harm, such as informing to the authorities or similar conduct, qualifies. Ordinary litigation before non-Jewish courts is not certain harm; many people sue there and do not prevail.
And he claims that the orchard is his. See Sema, seif katan 18, who questions Ir Shushan's answer that the witnesses were hidden behind a fence. If so, says Sema, the seller can later bring those hidden witnesses to prove what they heard. See the introduction to this siman, under the discussion of Rabbah and Rav Yosef, where I explained Nimukei Yosef. Ir Shushan's words can be sustained. We rule like Abaye and Rava in Bava Metzia 47 that a moda'ah can be written even against a person who is generally obedient to beit din. Rashbam explains that perhaps beit din will not be immediately available while the seller urgently needs money. Beit Yosef here and Mahara Chashon in the treatise on moda'ah and coercion rule this way. The question of Rashbam and the other authorities was not primarily why the seller himself could not later sue in beit din with the testimony of those who heard the coercive statement. Their question was against the coercer's conduct. If he openly says before witnesses, "If you do not sell it to me I will suppress the mortgage document," what does he gain by suppressing it when witnesses already know what happened? Ir Shushan therefore answers that the witnesses were hidden behind a fence. The coercer did not know they heard him, so from his perspective suppressing the mortgage document could be useful. Sema's objection, that the moda'ah should then be unnecessary because the seller can later sue on the basis of the hidden witnesses, is not compelling. A moda'ah may be written even against one who ordinarily obeys beit din, because perhaps beit din will not be immediately available, as Rashbam says, or because the coercer may still refuse to comply, as Ramban explains. For the practical halachah, see the Chiddushim, seif katan 19.
And they themselves are the witnesses to the moda'ah. This implies that the sale is canceled because of coercion together with the moda'ah. I find this difficult, because even without that combination the sale should seemingly be void. With movable property, if one stole an item, or even held a deposit and denied it, the owner ordinarily cannot afterward transfer it because movable property that has been denied is treated like stolen property. Regarding stolen property before the owner's despair, neither robber nor owner can consecrate it. Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 28, seif katan 16, cites She'erit Yosef that the owner cannot even transfer it to the robber or depositary himself. Bava Metzia 7 indicates similarly even regarding land: whenever a person cannot recover the property through beit din, consecration is ineffective. Rashi explains that this includes a case where he lacks witnesses. Here the coercer claimed that the orchard had already been purchased and the true owner had no witnesses. The owner therefore could not recover it through beit din. If so, how could he transfer it even to the wrongful possessor? The fact that our case nevertheless assumes the coerced sale could be effective indicates support for Beit Shmuel's other approach there, that the owner can transfer the property to the robber himself.
