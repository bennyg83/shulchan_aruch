Because a person may exaggerate a threat. See the Beurim, where this is difficult. It is explicit in the Rosh, Maharik, and Maharaash that if one threatened him and he delivered a modaah, all agree it constitutes coercion. But if he merely threatened to bring him before secular courts, all agree that this alone is not coercion, because the harm is not certain, since not everyone who litigates there wins. If, however, the disputed property is already in the coercer's hands and he refuses to litigate under Torah law, insisting only on secular courts, all agree that a modaah may be written:
A promissory note. Meaning a document from the renter establishing that the field is in his possession only by rental. Sema:
That he consumed it for three years. Then he is believed to claim purchase and that he lost his deed of acquisition. Sema:
If you do not sell it to me. This does not mean that he made the threat before witnesses, because then the lender could not deny it and the threatened owner could prove the threat through those witnesses. Rather, the threat was made privately. The owner then tells witnesses in advance that when he gives the coercer a deed of sale, the coercer will give him the market value, and when he later comes before beit din and produces the new deed of sale, the circumstances will demonstrate the truth of his prior statement that the coercer privately threatened to suppress the mortgage document unless he sold the property. Sema. See the Beurim. According to the Ir Shushan, even if the owner concealed witnesses behind a fence, a modaah is effective.
