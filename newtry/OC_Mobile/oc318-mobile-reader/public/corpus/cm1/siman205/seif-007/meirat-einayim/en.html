He denied it and claimed that the orchard was his, and afterward the lessor delivered a modaah, etc. This is the Rambam's language in chapter 10 of Hilchot Mekhirah. The Maggid Mishneh explains that the witnesses in the Gemara's case did not hear the original threat. They saw the later denial, after which the lessor delivered a modaah and sold. If the witnesses had heard the threat, there would have been no effective coercion, because the creditor could no longer deny the mortgage arrangement in their presence. The lessor could simply have sued him on the basis of those witnesses. Since he did not do so and instead sold, we would say that he resolved to transfer. According to this, the Rambam and Mechaber first state the incident to teach that even financial coercion of this kind can void a sale when the coercion is established and a modaah was delivered. They then write, "therefore, if he sued him," to explain how the coercion becomes established. The witnesses certainly did not hear the threat itself, because if they had, there would have been no continuing coercion. Rather, the lessor sues, the lessee denies and says that the orchard belongs to him, and the claimant tells beit din: I now deliver a modaah, because I will establish that his denial is only intended to force me to sell to him. He told me so earlier. You will know this is true when I agree to sell and he pays me the full value upon receiving a deed of sale. If afterward the lessor returns to beit din and it is shown that the lessee now holds a sale deed purchased from him, this establishes the truth of the earlier threat, namely that he said privately, "If you do not sell it to me I will suppress your document." In that manner the coercion becomes proven and the modaah voids the sale. Without the prior modaah, even though the coercion later becomes evident, one might still say that because he received the money he ultimately consented and transferred the property. The Tur, who abbreviates and cites only the opening of the Rambam's case, also means that the threat was not heard directly by witnesses but was later established through the sequence of suit, denial, and purchase. The Ir Shushan adds that hidden witnesses were placed behind a fence to hear the threat, apparently to answer the Maggid Mishneh's difficulty, but that does not help. If such witnesses heard him threaten to suppress the rental deed, the lessor should simply rely upon them if the lessee later claimed ownership. Beit din would then know from their testimony what had happened. See the Derishah for further discussion.
