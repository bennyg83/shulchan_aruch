Or they frightened him, etc. Rav Hai Gaon infers this from the incident of the orchard and from the statement, “Had he not admitted, he and his donkey would have been taken for royal service.” This is why the ruling speaks of a threat that can realistically be carried out, as in the orchard and robber cases.
Whether physical coercion. As in “they hung him, etc.,” and as the Gemara challenges from Gittin, “and they beat him, etc.”
Whether monetary coercion. As in the orchard and the threat to seize his donkey for royal service.
Some disagree, etc. They argue that the orchard case is different because the coercer already had possession of the land and needed no additional act. See Lechem Mishneh. They cite the end of HaMadir, which distinguishes verbal threats to punish a person, and there the Gemara says that words alone, etc., showing that a threat is not actual coercion. Rav Hai Gaon answers that beis din is different, because a person assumes that beis din will not beat someone or act improperly, unlike an ordinary private person.
Who is accustomed, etc. Bava Batra chapter 3 and Shevuos chapter 7.
Whether, etc. From the end of Gittin: a get coerced by a Jewish court improperly, etc., and by non-Jews, etc. The ruling here means that even if the threat is to use Jewish agents, it can constitute coercion; only a threat made by beis din itself is ordinarily not treated as coercion, as explained above.
