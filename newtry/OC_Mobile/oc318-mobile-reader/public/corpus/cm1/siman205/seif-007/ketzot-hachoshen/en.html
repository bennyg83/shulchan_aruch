One who coerces his fellow. Chavot Yair was asked whether the party who imposed the coercion can himself retract when the coerced party had delivered a valid moda'ah against the compromise or sale. He ruled that if the moda'ah was valid, the coercing party may also retract, because the transaction was never truly completed. Had he known the other side retained a later power to undo it, he would not necessarily have paid or entered the agreement. Chavot Yair distinguishes this from overreaching, where the one who overreached cannot retract if the injured party chooses to uphold the sale, lest the sinner benefit from his wrongdoing. He suggests that coercion is different because a coerced sale can still be valid under the rule that one forced to sell may nevertheless decide to transfer. I do not accept the suggestion that coercion involves no wrongdoing. One who takes another's property by force, even while paying value, is still called a violent taker. The sale stands only because the seller ultimately consented under the recognized rule, not because the coercion was permissible. Nevertheless, Chavot Yair's practical conclusion is correct. Once the seller delivered a valid moda'ah, the buyer never acquired the property. The buyer therefore may also retract because the transaction itself is void. Nor is the overreaching case a true counterexample. There too, the overreaching party's inability to retract applies only so long as the injured party has not retracted. Once the injured party actually cancels the sale, the other side can also withdraw. Here the seller's moda'ah is already his effective retraction from the coerced transaction. The sale therefore lacks legal existence and the buyer can likewise retract.
