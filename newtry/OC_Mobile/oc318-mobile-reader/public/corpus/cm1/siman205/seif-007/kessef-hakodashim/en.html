They coerced him to purchase. One held a handwritten document stating, “I sold grain in such-and-such an amount, and the buyer must receive it at such-and-such a place.” Later a second writing was made on the same paper concerning grain from another place at a higher price. The buyer claims that the higher price was not accepted willingly, but under compulsion because his money remained in the seller’s hands and Torah law then lacked effective governmental enforcement. One witness testified that the buyer acted willingly because the seller had offered to return his money. That testimony is not decisive. The buyer can say that because he urgently needed the grain, the second arrangement was preferable to receiving his money back. His failure to deliver a modaah does not defeat the coercion claim, for perhaps he did not know to do so or could not do so secretly. Moreover, there was no kinyan sudar, handshake, or signature of the buyer on the second writing. Although he gave the seller the original paper so that the second writing could be added, that act by itself creates neither acquisition, personal obligation, nor waiver. One might have thought waiver applies, but waiver does not ordinarily operate upon a specifically identified object. Here the grain was not yet identified as particular kernels or sacks, even if the seller had no other grain at that location, and until delivery it remained unselected from the non-Jewish owner’s larger stock. On the other hand, since the writing uses language of sale, there is at least an attempted conveyance of the body of the grain, and the principle of bereirah may identify retroactively which grain was sold. Still, there was no effective waiver or new acquisition, especially in view of the coercion claim. The buyer’s statement that he would pay the higher price may therefore have been mere placating words. The grain at the location named in the first writing had originally been good but spoiled before the second writing. The parties had discussed that the grain delivered must be good. The first writing’s wording does not conclusively show otherwise, because it can mean only that the grain was to be brought to that village for the buyer to receive it there. Nor is there an overwhelming presumption that the transaction was limited exclusively to grain physically present in that village. The parties disputed the oral terms. The buyer said he had a choice between grain from the first village and grain from another village where the seller also then had stock. Although the seller immediately sold the grain in the second village and only afterward obtained the grain described in the second writing, there is still room to say that he undertook, on the strength of trust between them, that if the grain in the first village spoiled and the other stock was sold, he would provide from grain later acquired. If so, a kinyan sudar would be required for the higher price. Although written terms ordinarily prevail over conflicting oral terms, where the writing is itself ambiguous one may follow the oral explanation. Since the seller denies the buyer’s version and says the agreement concerned only the grain at the first location, it appears that according to the buyer’s account he need pay only the price stated in the first writing.
