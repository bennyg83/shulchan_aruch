A compromise has the law of a sale. Baer Heitev refers to siman 12 and the Bach for the apparent contradiction. There, if Reuven frightened Shimon and Shimon made a compromise with a kinyan and cancellation of moda'ah, Shimon may retract, implying that no prior moda'ah is necessary. Here, however, compromise is treated like a sale, seemingly like a coerced sale, which remains valid unless a moda'ah was delivered. Bach resolves that the present rule concerns a genuine disputed claim, where one does not know who would prevail. Each side effectively sells to the other whatever rights he has in exchange for the amount fixed by compromise. If, however, Reuven clearly has no legal claim and merely frightens Shimon into a compromise, that is like coercing a gift; the kinyan and cancellation of moda'ah were themselves products of coercion and Shimon can recover what he paid. Ketzot similarly distinguishes a genuine doubtful case, including one involving factual denial or an oath, from the clear case in siman 12. Netivot discusses this further. The gloss in Baer Heitev in the name of Rabbi Ozer offers another distinction, namely that compromise is treated like a sale only insofar as proof of coercion is required. See Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 25, at length. Responsa Chemdat Shlomo, siman 13, raises a further question where a person was forced to issue a written obligation and received some benefit in return. Perhaps the principle that a coerced sale is valid applies only where property is immediately removed from his possession, as in a sale, kiddushin, or divorce, but not where coercion merely causes him to assume a future obligation. He later found Hagahot Maimoniyot, end of Laws of Oaths, explicitly stating that the rule of coerced sale applies only where something is actually transferred out of the person's possession; a liability created by coercion is ineffective. On this basis he suggests another reconciliation with siman 12: there the coerced party had not yet paid, so the arbitrators' imposed obligation was ineffective; here the compromise may concern something already transferred or a specific object immediately vested in the other party.
