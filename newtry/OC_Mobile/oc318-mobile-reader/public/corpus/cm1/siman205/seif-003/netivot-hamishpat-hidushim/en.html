A compromise has the law of a sale. In a compromise each side waives some portion of his claims, so it resembles a sale in which consideration is received. This is unlike siman 88, which compares a compromise document to a gift document, because there the disqualification depends upon what was effected by the document itself. Sema. See Beurim: if the compromise arose from a genuinely doubtful legal issue, both coercion and a protest declaration are required to void it. Coercion alone or a protest declaration alone does not suffice, because it resembles a sale. But if the law clearly favored one party and the other coerced him into compromise, it resembles a gift and is void because of coercion alone. A protest declaration without coercion still does not void it. If the compromise arose because the parties contradicted one another factually, and witnesses later establish that the law was with one of them, the compromise is void even without coercion and without a protest declaration, as explained above in siman 12. The same applies if one party later admits that the law was actually with the other. But if he later admits only the amount for which they compromised, such as where the claimant demanded one hundred, the defendant denied everything, they compromised for fifty, and the defendant later admits the fifty, the claimant may not afterward impose an oath concerning the other fifty that had been denied. The rule that coercion alone does not void a compromise applies only where he was coerced merely to compromise generally, while he voluntarily agreed to the amount. If he was also coerced concerning the amount of the compromise, coercion alone voids it. See Shach, seif katan 5, concerning one who swore to give a gift to another and at the time of the gift made a protest declaration before witnesses stating that he was giving only because of the coercion of the oath. It is as though the recipient has not yet acquired the gift, and the giver may seek release from his oath. See Beurim.
