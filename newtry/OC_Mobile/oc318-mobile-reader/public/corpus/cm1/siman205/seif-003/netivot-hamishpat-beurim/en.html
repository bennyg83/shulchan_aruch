A compromise has the law of a sale. See Maharit, part 4, siman 98, who writes that this applies specifically where the underlying law was genuinely uncertain. In that case coercion without a moda'ah does not void the compromise. But where the law was clear that the defendant was exempt and the claimant forced him into compromise, the case is like one who was forced to give a gift. Rashba's responsum cited by Beit Yosef at the end of this siman says likewise. One might ask that even where the law was initially uncertain, if beit din later clarifies that the law was entirely with one party, the compromise should be a mistaken waiver, like siman 12 where parties compromised and afterward witnesses arrived showing that the compromise rested on error. This is not so. According to Rosh in Bava Metzia 66, only ona'ah, where the person did not know at all that he was giving away his own property, is not considered waiver. Where he knew that he was giving from his own property but erred in the law, the waiver is valid. That reasoning certainly applies here. Even according to Tosafot there, who hold that mistaken waiver is generally ineffective and that the special case of sale of a davar shelo ba la'olam works only because one wishes to maintain his credibility, the present case is different. Here the parties knowingly entered the compromise in order to purchase freedom from the existing doubt. They bargained over the uncertainty itself. If the law is later clarified, this does not retroactively turn the compromise into an error, because they originally contracted precisely to eliminate that uncertainty. Still, the compromise has the law of a sale only where the coercer forced the person generally to compromise but did not force a particular amount, and the amount was afterward agreed by the parties without coercion. If the coercer forced him to accept a specified compromise amount and did not release the pressure until he agreed to that amount, the compromise is certainly void. Even in an ordinary sale, where the coercer also dictates the price, the sale can be void, as stated in seif 4. If the compromise resulted from a factual denial, such as Reuven claiming one hundred from Shimon, Shimon denying it, and they compromising for fifty, then if witnesses later prove the full debt the compromise is void, as explained in siman 12 and in Rashba's responsum. There are two reasons: it was a mistaken compromise, and it resulted from the coercion created by the false denial. The fifty received is not sale consideration but merely partial return of what was wrongfully withheld. The same should apply if the defendant later admits the entire debt. The admission of a litigant is equivalent to one hundred witnesses, and the compromise is void. But if the defendant later admits only the amount of the compromise, for example he was sued for one hundred, denied, compromised for fifty, and later admits that he truly owed fifty, while the plaintiff now seeks an oath on the remaining fifty and claims that he waived the oath only because of the pressure created by denial, Rosh, responsum kelal 72, siman 8, indicates that the compromise stands. The denial itself does not prove coercion. Perhaps the truth was exactly as the defendant later says: because the plaintiff demanded more than was owed, he denied the whole claim, and once the plaintiff agreed to the true amount, he admitted it. There is room to question why this should be treated like a sale at all once the defendant admits the fifty. He returned only what was already owed, so the plaintiff received no new consideration for waiving the oath on the other fifty. Rashba in siman 12 says that returning only the claimant's own money is not sale consideration. The waiver of the oath should therefore resemble a gift, for which a moda'ah alone can help. It appears that Rosh holds that where it is not objectively known that the waiver was truly gratuitous, and it is possible that the claimant accepted fifty because that was in fact the amount he believed he could prove or recover, we do not undo the compromise. Were I not hesitant to disagree, I would say that Rambam's principal rule that compromise resembles a sale was stated only regarding a moda'ah delivered without coercion. Even in a genuinely uncertain case, perhaps compromise is sale-like only for that limited issue. According to Rashbam's rationale, a moda'ah by itself voids a gift because it demonstrates coercion. In a compromise there is no such demonstration. One can easily say the person made the moda'ah only to preserve an option: if later the law proves favorable to him he will undo the compromise, while if it proves unfavorable he will retain it. Likewise, even according to other authorities, it is improper to let one party compromise while secretly preserving a unilateral right to withdraw. Therefore a moda'ah without coercion does not void a compromise. But regarding actual coercion without a moda'ah, the reason a sale stands is that, because of the money, the seller fully resolves to transfer. In a compromise, the coerced party can say, "In my view the law was clearly mine. What I received was only part of what was already mine," like Rashba's rule concerning partial return of stolen property. On that logic a compromise should be void under coercion even without a moda'ah. Nevertheless, Maharit and other poskim explicitly rule that where the law was genuinely uncertain, compromise is like a sale even regarding coercion without a moda'ah. Their view therefore governs. Still, wherever it can reasonably be shown that the party knew the law and compromised only because of coercion, the compromise should be void. See Shach, seif katan 5, citing a responsum of Rashdam. I do not have that work before me, and the statement as quoted is difficult. Shach also cites Bedek HaBayit concerning one who swore and then gave a gift. Shach writes, "meaning that at the time of the oath he said..." This is difficult and appears to be a scribal error. It should read, "at the time of the gift he said..." The point is as follows. If a person gave because he was obligated by his oath, that is ordinarily still a valid gift. Beit din can compel him to fulfill his oath. If he does not wish to violate it and expressly gives the property as a gift, the gift is effective. The case must therefore be one where, when giving, he explicitly said that he was not giving willingly, meaning that he was not voluntarily transferring ownership. So long as there is no willing transfer, the recipient has not yet acquired the gift. A practical difference is that so long as the recipient has not acquired it, the giver can seek release from his vow or oath. Once the gift has already vested in the recipient, release from the oath cannot retroactively take the property away. Therefore, where he delivered a moda'ah and afterward obtained release from the oath, the recipient must return the gift. As to the underlying question whether an oath or penalty is itself considered coercion for purposes of a gift where no moda'ah was delivered, because the person brought that pressure upon himself, it appears that with a get many authorities treat later regret under an oath or penalty as coercion and therefore potentially as a coerced get, as discussed in Even HaEzer siman 134. But with a gift, once he says "I want it," the gift should stand. A get is different because genuine internal willingness is required. Rambam explains that when beit din lawfully compels a get, we say the person's inner will is also to obey the Sages. That reasoning does not apply to a person acting because of a private oath or penalty. Perhaps internally he remains unwilling, and therefore the poskim are stringent. With an ordinary gift, however, once he says "I want it," we do not inquire into an unexpressed inner reluctance.
