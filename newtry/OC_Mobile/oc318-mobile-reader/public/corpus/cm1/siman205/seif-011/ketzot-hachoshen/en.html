If we do not know of his coercion. Rosh distinguishes sale and gift. In a sale, one who is forced to sell nevertheless transfers validly unless he delivered a moda'ah. Therefore, if he later cancels the moda'ah, the coerced sale stands. In a gift, one who is forced to give does not transfer at all even if he later cancels the moda'ah, provided the coercion itself is established. If the coercion is unknown and the only basis for invalidating the gift was the moda'ah, then canceling the moda'ah allows the gift to stand. According to Rashbam and Ramban, however, the Gemara's statement that a moda'ah concerning a gift is "mere revelation" means that the very delivery of a moda'ah ordinarily shows that coercion must have existed. If he were freely giving, why would he gratuitously deliver a moda'ah instead of simply refraining from the gift? On that approach, even when we do not have separate proof of coercion, we ordinarily infer that the gift was coerced. Therefore, even if he later cancels the moda'ah, the underlying gift remains a coerced gift and is invalid. Rashbam's discussion of divorce confirms this. He says the Sages required a husband to cancel all prior moda'ot precisely because a moda'ah itself ordinarily indicates coercion. The case concerns those divorces where beit din may properly compel him to give a get. Such coercion is analogous to a compelled sale because he is legally obligated to give the get. Therefore explicit cancellation of the moda'ah can restore the validity of the get. This resolves the apparent difficulty and fits Rashbam's reading.
