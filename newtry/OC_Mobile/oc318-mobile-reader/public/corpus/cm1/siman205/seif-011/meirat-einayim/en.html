The moda'ah is void. We say that because of the coercion together with the money, he fully resolved to transfer ownership, as explained above. A prior moda'ah causes us to say that the sale is not valid because he revealed that he did not wish to transfer ownership. But when he afterward nullified the moda'ah, it is as though no moda'ah had ever been delivered. In the Derishah I wrote that Rif, Rambam, Rav Hai, and Ramah hold that whenever a moda'ah was first delivered, even an ordinary subsequent nullification is ineffective where we know that the nullification itself was compelled, as the Tur cites in their names. The Rosh disagrees. This is unlike Beit Yosef, who writes that Rif and Rambam agree that an unspecified nullification is effective. See there. The Mechaber here follows his own view.
To nullify every moda'ah that emerges from within a moda'ah, etc. Specifically this wording must be written. It is not enough for the document to say that he nullifies all moda'ot forever, because that does not include nullification of a prior moda'ah directed against a later nullification. See Darkei Moshe. Nevertheless, even according to the first view, it is effective if the acquisition document states that he disqualifies every witness who will testify that he delivered a moda'ah before them. See Darkei Moshe and Even HaEzer siman 134.
So long as we know of his coercion, etc. The reason has already been explained: with a gift, a mere disclosure that he does not desire the gift is enough to nullify it. The same applies when he acknowledges an obligation he does not actually owe. Even if he says before witnesses, "Be witnesses against me that I owe so-and-so such-and-such," where it is known that he does not owe it and is merely creating a liability against himself through this admission, the law is like a gift.
