They forced him to buy. But if they forced him to sell, the sale can be effective even where, instead of cash, they forced him to accept movable property of equivalent value as consideration. Just as we say that because of coercion and money he fully resolves to transfer, so too because of coercion and money's worth he can fully resolve to transfer. He can sell the item received for cash, so it is effectively monetary consideration. One might argue that the coerced seller does not acquire the object the coercer gives him by barter, because one who is forced to buy does not acquire; if the seller does not acquire the consideration, perhaps the coercer should not acquire the property sold either. That is not so. When one sells in exchange for money's worth as payment, the seller does not acquire the very body of the item through chalipin. Rather, the item creates a payment obligation on the buyer, like the rule that gold obligates silver in Bava Metzia 45. See Taz in siman 250, seif 3. Since the coerced seller is not acquiring the object itself as a reciprocal barter item, the case is not one of "forced to buy." He is being forced to sell for consideration, and he can fully resolve to transfer. Accordingly, if the coercer specifically designates an object and says, "Sell me your property for this particular object," in a form treated as actual barter under siman 203, then the law is different. Since the coerced party does not acquire the barter object because that would be a forced purchase, the coercer likewise does not acquire the property from him. This resembles the reciprocal-barer principle of siman 203. It further appears that even if the coercer gives only a pledge securing the sale price, the sale is effective. According to many authorities, even merely becoming personally obligated in the price can suffice, as explained in seif 1 in the gloss. When a pledge is actually delivered, everyone should agree that this is equivalent to receipt of money. See Even HaEzer siman 42, where an opinion is cited concerning one who was forced to accept kiddushin, holding that the kiddushin can be valid because even a person forced to buy may acquire. Maggid Mishneh and Beit Shmuel question why this opinion was not brought here in Choshen Mishpat. In my view it was cited there only because of the stringency of kiddushin. It appears further that where a person coerces another to buy, although the coerced buyer may cancel the transaction, the coercing seller cannot cancel it. The law resembles siman 200, seif 11, where the seller cannot retract. Here too, the transaction is effectively suspended upon the buyer's will.
