A compromise has the law of a sale. The rule that a compromise is treated like a sale for purposes of modaah appears to apply only where some money was actually paid immediately. If only a promissory note was given, the principle of “because of the coercion and the money he resolves and transfers” may not apply. At least where the party delivering the modaah remains in possession, he may plead kim li in accordance with the view that actual cash is required. Shach, seif katan 2, in disagreeing with Sema, means only that we should not say that a deed of sale without a promissory note for the purchase price is never treated as a sale. According to the view that cash need not be immediate, a deed of sale can suffice where the agreed payment will be made later. But there certainly are authorities who hold that without actual cash payment the compromise does not attain the status of a sale. Plainly, money paid to satisfy an undisputed amount already fixed by clear judgment does not count as the consideration that makes the remaining compromise equivalent to a sale. Even in a place where Torah judgments lack governmental enforcement and secular courts take bribes, it still appears that whatever the judgment itself requires immediately is the payer’s own debt and cannot be used as the consideration for the compromise. If the obligor had already openly refused to pay, perhaps the eventual payment could have some character of consideration, comparable to the case of a threatened orchard where coercion must first be disclosed. If the judgment allowed an extended time for payment and he voluntarily paid earlier, perhaps that early payment can function as consideration. Likewise, if there was prior conduct showing evasion, that may amount to recognized coercion. These points require study. The statement that compromise is like a sale for modaah may also be limited by value. If the concession in the compromise is slight and the transaction concerns movables in which overreaching can be recovered, the compromise should perhaps be treated as a sale only up to the value of what the other party gave up; beyond that it is like a gift. One had copper in the hands of a craftsman who had agreed to make utensils for him at a fixed rate per unit of weight. After about a year of delay, the craftsman entered a partnership with another. He told the owner to demand half from the new partner and refused to release even his own half unless the owner gave him a writing that nothing further was owed. Before giving that writing, the owner delivered a modaah that he was compelled to do so because the craftsman refused to pay him. Were it not for the dispute about the wage, where the craftsman claimed that the owner must now pay more, the release would be a gift and mere disclosure of intent would suffice even without proof of coercion. Because there was an element of compromise over the wage, however, one might think that modaah would work only upon proof of coercion. Here the circumstances themselves establish coercion. Although a regular beit din existed and had power to compel the craftsman, a person is willing to pay to avoid litigation and to sacrifice money he knows he could ultimately collect. The indignity and burden of litigation can itself be substantial, especially for one who lives far from the city and will suffer delays and evasions. Since the craftsman admits that he refused to release the owner’s half until receiving a release, the coercion is evident. Even absent known coercion, this may still be treated as a gift. The rule that an employer may deceive workers who wrongfully refuse to perform indicates that where the employer was not legally required to promise the higher wage, the later promise can be defeated even without a formal modaah. Although the sources do not specify that the deception must be accomplished by kinyan or writing, had there been no first agreement the worker’s performance after the higher promise would itself create an acquisition. Where witnesses know of it, that is comparable to a writing. In our case, however, the earlier agreement remained in force, so the worker’s later performance creates no acquisition regarding the increased wage. A written release is potentially stronger because it is a fully effective act of acquisition, especially where a waiver requires no kinyan at all. All the more so if under ordinary law the craftsman could claim that the wage had increased, even though he was a contractor whose hand is on the lower side. If he negligently delayed the work until prices rose, that does not necessarily give the owner a claim against him for the increase. If the owner did not know that the craftsman had taken a partner and told the person he saw in the shop to bring the movables for the work, and that person was in fact the partner, then if the craftsman knew the owner was unaware of the partnership, the entire obligation remains upon the original craftsman. If the owner knew of the partnership, then although the partnership arose after the original hiring, telling the partner to bring the goods to the shop may count as delivery to both. If the owner says he did not know and the craftsman says he assumed the owner knew, since the original agreement had been made only with the craftsman, it was for the craftsman to inform him or ascertain that he knew of the partner. Still, money cannot necessarily be extracted on that basis. It is like “I do not know whether I became obligated,” while as to half the craftsman says, “Here it is.” Although partners are guarantors for one another, before half has been demanded from each there is no present obligation beyond half. This requires further study. One who claims that he was coerced when he executed a written obligation while present in the city generally has no claim, because he could have delivered a modaah. Even if he says he was under great pressure, he could have delivered a modaah afterward before witnesses that same day. Since in seif 9 we presume that an undated modaah preceded the gift, there is basis to place the burden on the holder of the document. Although Sema explains that presumption by saying that witnesses are presumed to have acted properly when writing the modaah, a reason less applicable where the witnesses would discover that the modaah came after the gift, that explanation may be needed mainly where the recipient of the gift is already in possession. In a written gift or release, the holder of the writing bears the weaker hand. This requires further study. At times a plea of coercion against a document may also be believed through a migo of “I paid,” where the document is merely in the obligor’s handwriting without a clause of trust and the due date has passed, according to one view in siman 69. Even then, however, the objection remains that he should have delivered a modaah. He cannot say, “I relied on later pleading coercion through a migo of payment,” because the holder of the writing might have pressed him for immediate payment within the due period, when he could not yet claim to have paid before the debt came due.
