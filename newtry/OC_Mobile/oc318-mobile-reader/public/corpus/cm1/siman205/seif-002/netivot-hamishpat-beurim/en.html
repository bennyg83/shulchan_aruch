But with a gift. See Sema, seif katan 8, who writes that a moda'ah is effective even where there was no coercion at all. Rashbam, Tosafot, and many other authorities, however, hold that the reason a moda'ah is effective for a gift even without witnesses recognizing the coercion is that we presume there must in fact have been coercion. Otherwise, why would a person give a get or gift and at the same time deliver a moda'ah against it? Beit Yosef cites their view. At first glance this creates a difficulty. Why does cancellation of a moda'ah help even when one cancels all prior moda'ot forever? In a sale, both coercion and moda'ah are needed to void the sale. Once he cancels the moda'ah, the later statement cancels the earlier one, leaving only coercion without a moda'ah, and a coerced sale for money remains valid. But with a get or gift, either coercion or a moda'ah can suffice to invalidate the act. If the very fact that he delivered a moda'ah proves he was coerced, then even after the moda'ah is canceled the coercion remains. A coerced gift is no gift. Upon examination this is not difficult. There are cases in which coercion exists but a moda'ah is nevertheless required. The Gemara on Bava Batra 40 tells of a man who went to marry a woman and she said that she would marry him only if he wrote all his property to her. He delivered a moda'ah before giving the property. Coercion of that type does not itself void the gift, because it is self-imposed pressure. Yet self-imposed pressure together with a moda'ah does void the gift. Rashbam can therefore agree that even for a gift the presumption created by an unexplained moda'ah proves at most some form of coercion, perhaps only self-imposed pressure. That is enough when combined with the moda'ah, but once he cancels the moda'ah only the self-imposed pressure remains, and that alone does not void the gift. Tosafot write that a moda'ah is written without the person's knowledge as a rabbinic enactment to protect the coerced person. Even if a gift moda'ah proves only self-imposed pressure in some cases, where the rationale of protecting a victim does not naturally apply, the Sages instituted a general form of moda'ah and did not distinguish. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that if witnesses know with certainty that there was no coercion, they do not write a moda'ah; only in an ordinary unexplained case do they write it because we presume some coercion. This is correct. But Ketzot further writes that according to Rashbam, cancellation of a moda'ah does not work for a get or gift. That is certainly incorrect, as is clear from all the poskim in Even HaEzer. The explanation given above resolves the difficulty.
A gift or waiver. The same is true that coercion alone can void a gift, because one who was forced to give has not made a valid gift. This applies specifically where the coercer explicitly forced him to give this particular gift. But if he was subjected to general pressure and he himself chose to give this gift in order to escape the pressure, the matter depends on the two explanations Beit Yosef cites from Rashbam concerning the law of sikarikon in Gittin. The first explanation is that in the wartime case the coercion was not directed at the gift itself. The person voluntarily gave his property in order to save himself from danger, and such a gift is valid. The second explanation is that because the king had effectively abandoned them to death, they no longer expected to return to their property. Mahara Chashon in his treatise on moda'ah and coercion concludes like the first explanation. Therefore, if one is subjected to general coercion and of his own initiative gives a gift to escape it, the gift is valid. But if he had previously delivered a moda'ah, the gift is void. The same applies to a sale. If the coercer did not specifically compel the sale but imposed general pressure, while it was known that a sale to him would bring relief, the sale can be void when the seller had delivered a moda'ah. This rule applies where an actual gift reached the recipient. If instead the person merely executed a promissory note obligating himself to give money in the future in order to escape general coercion, he is exempt. The rationale that under pressure he fully resolved to transfer does not apply where he has not yet actually given the money. See Mahara Chashon in the treatise on moda'ah and coercion.
