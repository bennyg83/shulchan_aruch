See Responsa Shivat Tzion, siman 111, who cites Responsa Maharit, part 2, siman 43, as challenging this reason. The witnesses to a declaration of protest need not investigate whether the sale has already taken place; they testify only to the coercion. The seller can afterward establish that the declaration preceded the sale. Maharit therefore gives a different reason: since there is a doubt when the declaration was made, the holder of the deed has the weaker hand. Bach, seif 6, gives a similar explanation: because there is doubt whether the declaration was made before the sale or afterward, we do not remove property from its existing possessor, and the land remains in the presumptive possession of its original owner; the sale is therefore void. Shivat Tzion writes that Maharit's objection is correct where the declaration concerns a matter that does not become publicly known, such as a sale of movable property or another private transaction. There the witnesses indeed have no duty to investigate whether the transaction has already occurred. But where the declaration concerns a sale of land, which ordinarily becomes known, Sema's reasoning is correct. We maintain the witnesses in their presumptive integrity, because if the sale had already occurred they presumably would have known of it and would not have been permitted to write a declaration stating in future tense that the seller "is being coerced to sell." They would have been obligated to distance themselves from falsehood. Shivat Tzion further notes that Rama here mentions only a sale, even though Darkei Moshe cites the rule from an old Mordechai whose wording includes "sale or gift." This may be because Chiddushei Anshei Shem, printed on Mordechai to the chapter Chazakat HaBatim, siman 544, also cites the old Mordechai and comments that Rabbeinu Yitzchak and Maharam agree regarding a sale but not regarding a deed of gift, because in a gift the witnesses are not required to investigate anything, since they need not write that they themselves know of the coercion, unlike in a sale. According to this, Rama in his gloss may have adopted that distinction and therefore omitted gift. Yet according to Sema's reason, that we maintain the witnesses in their presumptive integrity because they would not have signed improperly, the same reasoning seems applicable to a gift as well. One would have to say, according to this approach, that Rama's mention of sale is not exclusive. I find this explanation difficult, because Rabbeinu Yitzchak and Maharam can also accept Sema's general reasoning and nevertheless distinguish a gift: in a gift the witnesses record only what the giver told them, not facts they are independently certifying, and therefore they have no duty to investigate. According to Bach and Maharit, by contrast, the same rule plainly applies to a gift. This requires study. There is another practical difference between Rama's stated reason and Sema's reason. If the witnesses did not write the declaration but later testified orally before beit din that so and so delivered a declaration of protest before them, and they do not know whether it preceded or followed the sale, Sema's presumption regarding signed witnesses does not apply, because there was no signature. Under Sema's reasoning, the oral testimony would therefore not invalidate the sale. According to Rama's reason, however, once we know of the coercion we presume that the declaration was delivered beforehand, and the sale is void. On this basis Shivat Tzion rules in a case where Reuven had claims against Shimon, and when Reuven sued him, Shimon produced releases from Reuven waiving all claims. Reuven brought witnesses who testified that he had declared before them that he was compelled to give Shimon the releases because Shimon possessed writings with which he could falsely inform against him. The witnesses testified orally in beit din but had not written a formal declaration, and it could not be established whether the releases preceded the declaration or vice versa. Shivat Tzion ruled that this declaration has no effect. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak and Maharam, cited by Chiddushei Anshei Shem, who disagree with the old Mordechai regarding a declaration concerning a gift, the declaration is certainly void, because a gratuitous waiver is comparable to a gift, as stated in Shulchan Aruch, seif 10. Even according to the old Mordechai, who equates gift and sale, that is where the witnesses signed the declaration, for then Sema's presumption applies that proper witnesses would not have signed after the transaction. Here, however, the witnesses did not sign but only now testify orally in beit din, so that presumption does not exist. Nor can one say that because the matter remains doubtful, the holder of the releases, Shimon, has the weaker hand. Shimon is presently in possession, while Reuven seeks to extract money from him through the declaration. Reuven therefore bears the burden of proving when the declaration was made. Until that is established, Shimon's releases remain fully effective. This is Shivat Tzion's conclusion. At first glance, in that case the declaration concerned not a gift of land but a waiver of monetary claims. Even if the declaration had been signed, it would still be ineffective so long as it was not established that it preceded the releases, according to Shivat Tzion's own opening principle that where the subject does not become public, Maharit's reasoning is correct and the witnesses have no duty to investigate. One must therefore say that Shivat Tzion added the point about unsigned testimony only for greater force: in such a case even Sema would agree. As a matter of law, however, even a signed declaration would not suffice, and the claimant would bear the burden of proof. See Ketzot HaChoshen, whose words likewise imply agreement with the reasoning of Bach and Maharit. He writes that according to this, in a compromise, which has the law of a sale, if the claimant produces such a declaration, we leave the money in the defendant's possession and the compromise remains valid. He means this even where the declaration was signed. See Ateret Tzvi, and similarly Maamar Mordechai, seif katan 12, who cite Sema's reason that we maintain the witnesses in their presumptive integrity. Ateret Tzvi writes that if it appears to the judges that the witnesses do not know that the declaration must precede the transaction, there is room for doubt. He therefore approves the enactment found in several communities that a declaration of protest is effective only when made before the rabbi who heads the yeshivah. In my humble opinion, even if it is certain that the witnesses were unaware of this rule, the sale would still be void on the basis of Bach and Maharit. Rather, the reason for those communal enactments appears to be that the witnesses must know that the person is actually selling because of coercion, as stated above, seif 1. Frequently witnesses do not know whether the claimed pressure truly constitutes coercion, as Maharam Alshich, siman 71, cited by Netivot HaMishpat, seif katan 6, writes that witnesses are prone to error in such matters.
