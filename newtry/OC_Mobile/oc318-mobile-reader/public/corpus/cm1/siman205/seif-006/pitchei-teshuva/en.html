Or in a waiver, even though he is not coerced. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 35, discusses a creditor who delivered a note to an agent with authority to compromise with the debtor in another city for a specified amount, but the agent compromised for less. At the end he questions whether a moda'ah made by the creditor helps, because there was no recognizable coercion. The creditor could have authorized the agent to sue the debtor in his city, and if the agent claimed the debtor was evading him or was powerful locally, the agent himself should have made the moda'ah. Since he did not, the compromise stands. Moreover, the debtor can tell the creditor that perhaps he had sold or gifted the note to the agent and therefore the creditor is not his litigant. The author's son questions his father's requirement that coercion be recognizable, because Shulchan Aruch, seif 6, rules that for a waiver one need not establish coercion; it is sufficient that the giver reveal that he is not acting wholeheartedly. He suggests that a transaction through an agent resembles compromise more closely. Yet according to the distinction cited earlier in Baer Heitev in the name of Rabbi Ozer, that compromise is like a sale only in requiring proof of coercion, even this answer is insufficient, because once coercion is established no moda'ah would be necessary.
