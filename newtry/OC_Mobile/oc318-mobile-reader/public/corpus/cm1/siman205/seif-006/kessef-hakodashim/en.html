His wife refused to make a kinyan sudar consenting to the sale unless he gave her an increased ketubah of six hundred gold coins beyond what otherwise belonged to her. She had married him as a widow and claimed that she already had an additional ketubah in that amount because she had brought property worth that sum into the marriage. It was uncertain whether that document had been lost, and the husband denied her claim. The buyer at one point said he would buy without her kinyan sudar, and at another point said he would not buy because she threatened continual conflict with him. The husband delivered a modaah, then made a kinyan sudar on the additional ketubah, after which she made her kinyan sudar consenting to the sale on condition of that ketubah. The kinyan on the ketubah also contained a nullification of prior modaahs. The husband then sought to retract. It appears that this is governed like an ordinary sale in which a modaah was expressly nullified, so that the modaah and proof of coercion no longer help. Even if the buyer now offers to buy without the wife’s kinyan sudar, the two kinyanim already made cannot simply be undone. Although a coerced sale is effective only where the seller receives fair value, here, despite the fact that the wife’s right is a lien on land and the certain amount of her ketubah may have been much smaller than six hundred gold coins, the buyer refused to purchase without her consent. This resembles the principle that even a small plot of land can be especially valuable to its owner. The cash received from the buyer was presently worth far more to the husband than the uncertain future benefit of the additional ketubah, and the sale price itself was substantial. The amount given in exchange for her kinyan should therefore be viewed in light of that practical value. The widespread practice of giving the wife consideration for her kinyan may itself help against a claim of modaah, since the transaction then resembles a sale, and land is not subject to the ordinary measure of overreaching. There is also room to regard this as the type of self-induced coercion discussed at the end of siman 205, because the husband’s need for money brought about the situation. One may distinguish, however, because there the very sale from which he needed money was the pressured act, while here the kinyan on the ketubah itself was directly against his will, even though without his need for money no such pressure would have arisen. This requires further study. In another matter, witnesses had made a kinyan sudar for a customary shtar chatzi zachar. They later realized that no chalitzah document was relevant because the groom was an orphan from his father and had no brother. The father of the bride then delivered a modaah and, before the document was written, informed the witnesses that he had done so. It appears clear that in such a case there is no obligation to give a shtar chatzi zachar merely because the original engagement terms mentioned it “in exchange for a chalitzah document.” The reference to a chalitzah document is an obvious mistake where no brother can exist, and the paired obligation of a shtar chatzi zachar is likewise mistaken. The phrase “in exchange for” itself demonstrates that where there is no chalitzah obligation there is no shtar chatzi zachar obligation. If the father nevertheless now wishes voluntarily to give a shtar chatzi zachar, a new kinyan sudar is required and the document must bear the later date. The standard clause in such a document nullifying modaahs does not overcome a prior modaah known through witnesses in what is fundamentally a gift. Nor is it enough that he now says that he waives the modaah or wishes matters to be treated as though it had never been delivered. A new kinyan is needed. Although the ordinary concern of an antedated document is less severe in a shtar chatzi zachar because collection from encumbered property is limited by its conditions and exclusions, an earlier date could still create confusion in relation to another shtar chatzi zachar given in the interval. Even if the witnesses to the later document heard of the modaah only from the bride’s father, and his statement by itself would ordinarily require formal testimony in beit din, the matter is likely to become known and the modaah will presumably invalidate the earlier document. Therefore the new document should bear the later date.
