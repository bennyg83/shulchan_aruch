And he took the purchase money. Meaning, as the gloss states, according to the opinion that requires, etc. The phrase “and he took” excludes a case where the buyer forced the money upon him. Likewise the seller must have taken the item being sold. A forced taking is not a sale, as stated at the end of HaKones, at the beginning of Bava Metzia, and in Kiddushin chapter 4. This is the force of the phrase “and he took the purchase money,” equivalent to the seller expressing “I want it.”
(Collected note) One who was coerced, etc. The ruling does not mention the statement at the end of HaKones that he must say “I want it.” The reason is that the Gra understands that passage as following the opinion that the prohibition of coveting is de'oraisa in the particular manner discussed there. According to the sugya in Sanhedrin chapter 3, however, a coercive purchaser violates only a rabbinic prohibition. Accordingly Rav Huna’s rule that a coerced sale is valid is not difficult, for a rabbinic wrong does not necessarily invalidate the monetary act even if he did not expressly say “I want it.” The Rambam, Hilchos Gezeilah chapter 1, likewise records only that such a person violates “Lo sachmod,” even if the seller ultimately consents, and that the property is not recovered in beis din. Such a person is also not disqualified from testimony because ordinary people do not fully understand the prohibition of coveting. Tosafos there, s.v. “chamsan,” writes similarly, though they explain the distinction between robber and coercive purchaser within a rabbinic framework. See below siman 359 seif 9. There is no need to derive the law from that passage, for even persistent pressure is forbidden as stated there in seif 10. The point there concerns testimonial disqualification, already explained in siman 34 seif 13. The Rambam omitted that detail. (End of note.)
Even, etc. There.
Whether, etc. Land is the case of the Gemara there. Movable property is included from the statement, “What is the difference between a robber and a coercive purchaser, etc.? But Rav Huna said, if they hung him and he sold, etc.” The primary case of robbery is movable property, as indicated by the verse and other sources. The Gemara also does not answer by saying that coercive purchase applies only to movables.
Although, etc. See Beer HaGolah. The Rif in HaNizakin explains that “he did not accept the money” means that the money was not actually handed over. It cannot mean that no money was given because acquisition occurred through a document, for we rule like Shmuel that even a document does not acquire in such circumstances unless money is involved, as stated there and in Bava Batra. Therefore the case must be as explained here.
Some disagree, etc. As stated there: “When did they say this? When witnesses say, ‘He counted money before us, etc.’” Although Rav Huna disagrees with Rav Bibi on another point, he does not disagree here. Certainly according to the Rambam’s approach Rav Huna does not disagree with Rav Bibi at all, as explained below in seif 8. The Rambam distinguishes a known robber from an ordinary coercive buyer. A robber’s admission can be attributed to coercion, as stated there 47a, whereas this person has not been established as a robber. See Rambam, end of Hilchos Gezeilah chapter 9, and below seif 8.
Some say we require, etc. As stated, “Shmuel concedes where he gave money,” and the halachah follows Shmuel in monetary law.
Some disagree. The Rif in HaNizakin explains “he did not accept the money” as meaning that the money was not actually handed over, and explains Shmuel’s concession to mean that the seller submitted to a monetary sale and was told, “Come take your money,” even if the money had not yet been physically delivered. This differs from the cases of a woman and a sikarikon, where no consideration is given at all.
And even, etc. As stated there: “A robber has no chazakah. What is considered a robber, etc.?” This person too is treated as a robber regarding this field once he made a moda'ah, for according to all views the coercion is thereby established.
