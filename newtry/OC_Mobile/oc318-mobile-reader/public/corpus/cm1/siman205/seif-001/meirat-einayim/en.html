One who was coerced, etc., even if they hung him until he sold, etc. In the Perishah I wrote and proved from the wording of Rif, Rambam, and the Mechaber that in their view, even if we neither saw nor knew of any coercion affecting the seller, and the seller appears not to be selling willingly, nevertheless once he sold and accepted payment we say that he fully resolved and transferred ownership, and the sale is valid. The wording of the Tur and Rosh does not imply this. Rather, whenever it appears from the seller that he is not selling willingly, even if he accepts the purchase money, the sale is invalid unless the sale resulted from coercion. In that case we say that because of the coercion together with the money, he fully resolved to transfer ownership to the purchaser. See there, and see what I write below in seif 7.
And he took the purchase money. Meaning, even if because of the coercion he did not expressly state that he now consents to sell willingly, we nevertheless say that because he accepted the purchase money, the combination of the coercion and the money caused him to resolve fully to transfer ownership, so long as he had not previously delivered a moda'ah. The Tur writes that even if he did not count the money but merely took it and threw it into his purse, thereby revealing that he accepted it against his will, the sale is nevertheless valid.
Even if they hung him, etc., until he sold, etc. The text gives an extreme form of coercion, and the incident in the Gemara likewise involved such a case. All the more so, lesser coercion does not enable him to void the sale by saying, "I was coerced."
Even though he did not take the money, etc. Meaning, there are witnesses that he admitted before them that he received the money, and now he claims that the purchaser never actually paid but that he was forced to admit payment because of the coercion. We do not accept this claim, because he should have delivered a moda'ah. Those who disagree hold that once we know he was coerced regarding the sale, we presume that he was also coerced into the admission.
But if he gave him a note, etc. Meaning, the purchaser converted the purchase money into a loan obligation against himself and gave the seller a promissory note, and now wishes to pay the purchase price reflected in the note. Even so, we say that at the time of sale the seller did not fully resolve to transfer ownership if he had not then received actual cash.
If he delivered a moda'ah. "Moda'ah" means notification: he informs the witnesses of the circumstances and how the matter occurred. Some explain the term as related to suffering, as in the verse concerning the men of Sukkot, meaning that he discloses his suffering to the witnesses and explains that because of it he is compelled to sell. With a gift, waiver, or get, even though actual suffering is not required for a moda'ah, as explained below, the same term remains because in a sale or compromise its paradigm involves suffering. See the Perishah.
The witnesses must know that he is selling, etc., and that he is coerced, etc. The wording implies that they must know that the sale itself occurred only because of the coercion and that he did not fully resolve to transfer ownership. In the Perishah I wrote that we do not require so much. It is enough that they know coercion was brought upon him to force him to sell, and that this coercion had not ceased at the time of sale. It is possible that the Mechaber also means this: he first writes that they must know he is selling because of the coercion, and then explains that this means they know he remains coerced as before. Even if they do not know that he failed internally to resolve to transfer ownership, this suffices.
