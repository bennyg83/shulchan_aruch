One who was coerced. See Sema, seif katan 1, through his statement that whenever it is apparent from the seller that he is not selling willingly, even though he received the purchase money, etc. At first glance these words are unclear. To explain them, Bava Kamma 62 at the end of HaKones establishes the rule of one whom they hang and compel to sell as a case where he says, "I consent." Beit Yosef on the Tur and the Perishah write that the Gemara's phrase "he says, I consent" is not literal, for if he silently accepts the money that itself is equivalent to saying "I consent." Their explanation is very strained. The Gemara asks concerning a chamsan who pays: if he paid, why call him a chamsan, since a coerced sale is valid? It answers: one case is where the seller says "I consent" and one where he does not. According to their view, that silence is already equivalent to "I consent" unless he expressly says "I do not consent," the Gemara should have contrasted a case where he expressly says "I do not consent," not merely one where he failed to say "I consent." Mahari Sason, cited by Maharahash in the Kuntres HaModa'ah VeHaOnes, also notes an apparent contradiction in the Maggid Mishneh. In chapter 4 of Hilchot Ishut he writes that in a forced sale one need not expressly say "I consent," while in chapter 1 of Hilchot Gezelah, regarding one who covets another's slave, he says that one must specifically say "I consent." Maharahash further asks why Bava Batra 48 does not answer the proof from sikarikon by saying that the case is one where the seller did not say "I consent." He also asks why Bava Kamma says, "If he paid, do you call him a chamsan? A forced sale is valid." Even if the sale is legally effective, the buyer could still have committed a prohibition and deserve the name chamsan. Lechem Mishneh in chapter 10 of Hilchot Mekhirah asks similar questions. Maharahash further notes that Rambam, chapter 10 of Hilchot Mekhirah, writes, "When is this said? With a coercer who is a chamsan," implying that a chamsan must first issue a moda'ah, whereas Bava Kamma indicates that no moda'ah is required. In my humble opinion the resolution is as follows. If the seller is subjected to bodily coercion or monetary coercion in order to force the sale, then by means of the benefit that the sale releases him from that coercion, together with the money he receives, we apply the principle that because of the coercion and the money he fully resolves to convey. Once the Sages established a compelling presumption that he genuinely resolves to convey, no express declaration is needed. Where there is a clear presumption, unexpressed intent is legally effective, as the Rashba writes in Kiddushin 50. Indeed, even in the severe area of permitting a married woman through a get we rely on the presumption that because of the coercion he fully resolves to divorce. Beit Yosef is therefore correct that in an ordinary case of "they hang him and he sells," the Gemara's requirement that he say "I consent" is not literal, because the circumstances themselves establish his consent. A chamsan is different where he did not subject the owner to any separate coercion, but simply seized the object and offered its price. The sale itself does not release the owner from another threat, so there is no basis to say that because of coercion and payment he fully resolved to convey. There is no compelling presumption of consent. He must therefore expressly say "I consent." Once he says so, that case is stronger than an ordinary forced sale. At the moment of sale the buyer commits no further coercive act, because the seller voluntarily consented without being forced to make that declaration. There was no external threat compelling him to say "I consent," and we therefore regard it as genuine willingness. This resolves the apparent contradiction in the Maggid Mishneh. In Hilchot Ishut, discussing a forced transaction where there is genuine external coercion, he says that an express "I consent" is unnecessary because the circumstances themselves establish consent. In Hilchot Gezelah, discussing a chamsan who merely takes the item and pays without separate coercion, he correctly requires an express declaration. It also resolves Rambam's statement in chapter 10 of Hilchot Mekhirah that a chamsan requires a moda'ah. There Rambam speaks of a coercer who also subjects the seller to some independent coercion, so that absent a moda'ah we presume that because of the coercion and the payment he consented. Even if he says "I consent," a prior moda'ah can show that this statement itself was made from fear. Bava Kamma, by contrast, speaks of a chamsan who imposed no separate coercion. If the seller does not say "I consent," the sale is void even without a moda'ah. If he does say it, a moda'ah should not help, for no one forced him to make that declaration. There is also a proof that in an ordinary forced sale no express "I consent" is required. Bava Batra asks, concerning the rule that a robber who produces proof of ownership is not allowed to retain the field, what novelty is taught, since the Mishnah already states the law of one who purchases from a sikarikon. Rashbam explains that the baraita must concern a case where the owner admitted receipt of payment, because if actual money was given, even a robber's purchase can be valid. If an express "I consent" were required, the Gemara could simply answer that the baraita concerns a case where money was paid but the seller never said "I consent," and its novelty would be that the sale is void while Rav Huna teaches that the seller's admission does not force him to return the money, because otherwise he would not have admitted. The fact that the Gemara does not answer this way supports the conclusion that an express declaration is not required in ordinary coercion. Maharahash's question why the Gemara says that one who pays is not a chamsan can likewise be answered. The Gemara's reasoning is that if the act itself remained prohibited, the transaction should be ineffective under Rava's principle in Temurah 4 that when the Torah says not to do an act, an act done in violation is ineffective. Rambam can nevertheless rule that a chamsan violates a prohibition while the sale is effective because, as the Maggid Mishneh explains, where the seller expressly says "I consent" the prohibited coveting and pressure occurred earlier. At the moment of the actual sale, once the seller voluntarily agrees, no prohibited coercive act is occurring. But at the stage of the Gemara's initial assumption, that a forced sale could be valid even without any declaration of consent, the Gemara properly reasons that if the buyer's paying transaction itself were prohibited it would not take effect. With respect to a get, Rambam requires the husband to say "I consent" even though the Gemara compares a coerced get to a coerced sale. The comparison is only with respect to the principle that coercion can lead a person to fully resolve to act. The two areas remain different. A get also requires true internal willingness. Rambam himself in chapter 2 of Hilchot Gerushin explains why a coerced get is not void, namely that the person's evil inclination temporarily overpowered him and coercion restores his true will to perform what the law requires. That rationale has no application to an ordinary sale. A sale does not require the same inner willingness once the legal circumstances establish a completed transfer. Accordingly, the Sema's language can be understood as follows. From the Rif and the Mechaber one might infer that even where we did not observe any separate bodily or monetary coercion, but merely saw that the seller was not acting willingly because the buyer took the item against his will and paid for it, the sale may nevertheless be valid if the seller himself accepted the money and put it into his purse. In that circumstance, his taking the money could indicate genuine consent. The Bava Kamma case where failure to say "I consent" invalidates the sale would then be where the seller did not himself accept the money, but the buyer forcibly threw it into the seller's purse or chest. According to the Rosh and Tur, however, where there was no independent coercion apart from the buyer's seizure of the object, the sale is not valid merely because the seller received the money. It is valid only if we know that he expressly said "I consent." Since he was under no separate threat compelling that statement, it demonstrates actual consent. The Sema understood the Rif and Rambam's words, "one who was coerced until he sold, and even if they hung him and he sold," as though the first clause included a lesser form of coercion in the transaction itself, such as seizure of the object or overwhelming social pressure, without an express declaration. In truth, the principal ruling is that in such a case the sale is not valid until the seller says "I consent," as follows from the Gemara and the authorities. The Rif, Rambam, and Mechaber can also be read consistently with this: the opening clause speaks of a lesser but genuine independent coercion, such as monetary pressure or beating, and then adds that even severe danger to life through hanging does not invalidate the sale. But where the buyer merely seizes the object, everyone requires an express declaration of consent. This is the principal rule.
Even though he did not receive the money in the presence of witnesses. See Sema, seif katan 4, who explains that witnesses heard the seller acknowledge receipt and he later claims that he was compelled to make the acknowledgment. We do not accept that claim. Beit Yosef explains that Rambam understands the Gemara's distinction between "he showed the money" and "he did not show the money" as referring to witnesses. At first glance this is difficult. Bava Batra 48 records Rava's ruling that a coerced sale is valid, initially with distinctions between an unspecified field and a particular field, and regarding a particular field between a case where the money was visibly delivered and one where it was not. The final halachah, however, is that in all these cases the sale is valid, even with a particular field, as shown from Amemar's rule that a man who was coerced to betroth a woman effects valid kiddushin. According to Rambam's interpretation, what proof is there from kiddushin? Kiddushin ordinarily includes an actual transfer of money, where a mere acknowledgment would not substitute. Maharahash asks this as well. Tosafot's question there, s.v. VaAfilu BeSadeh Zo, is also difficult under this explanation, because regarding an acknowledgment why should there be any logical distinction between an unspecified field and a particular field? It appears that this can be resolved by another interpretation cited by Beit Yosef in the name of the Rif. The phrase "he showed the money" can mean that the buyer actually paid at once, while "he did not show the money" can mean that the buyer did not hand over the money immediately but told the seller, "come and take your money." Even in the latter case the sale can be valid, because once the sale itself creates a debt for the price, the buyer's obligation to pay is treated as sufficient consideration for purposes of the rule that coercion together with money produces consent. The two interpretations are linked. Those who say that an acknowledgment to a robber is ineffective because, had the seller not admitted, the robber would have exposed him to danger, cannot mean that every acknowledgment to a robber is void even to create an independent personal obligation. We have never heard such a rule, and the robber did not necessarily coerce him to make the acknowledgment. Rather, any acknowledgment that is itself necessary to uphold the coerced sale is suspect. The seller was coerced into making the sale, and even if the coercer demanded only other formal acts while the seller voluntarily supplied an acknowledgment, if the acknowledgment serves the same purpose of validating the sale we may say that it too was made because of the coercion. This resembles forced vows in Nedarim, where a person may be excused even for language extending beyond the exact words the coercer demanded. Thus, if validation of the coerced sale requires actual payment at the time of sale, the seller's acknowledgment that he received payment is part of establishing the sale and is tainted by the coercion. But if a coerced sale can be valid even though no cash is handed over immediately, because the buyer's resulting debt for the price is enough, then the sale is already complete once the valid kinyan is made. A later acknowledgment that the seller received the purchase money is no longer part of effecting the sale. It is merely an acknowledgment that an ordinary debt was paid. The coercion concerned the sale, not that later debt acknowledgment, and the acknowledgment is therefore effective. This explains Rava's language. At the earlier stage, "he showed the money" includes two requirements: that payment be actually made at the time of the coerced sale rather than merely become due, and that the payment be witnessed rather than established only by the seller's acknowledgment. The final halachah says that even without this form of immediate visible payment the sale is valid. That means both that the sale can stand where the buyer merely says "come take your money" and incurs the price obligation, and that an acknowledgment by the seller that he already received the money can be accepted. The proof from coerced kiddushin is then clear. Even kiddushin by deed can be effective under coercion because, as Tosafot explain, the husband's obligations of food and clothing that arise through marriage can function as the monetary benefit analogous to payment, even though he has not yet handed them to her. This establishes that actual contemporaneous cash is not indispensable. Similarly, if a field is sold through another valid kinyan, the buyer's immediate obligation to pay the purchase price can supply the monetary element of the rule. Since the sale becomes valid at once and the buyer merely owes money, the seller's acknowledgment that the debt has been paid is also effective, because it is no longer necessary to create the sale itself. Accordingly, Tosafot's difficulty about distinguishing an unspecified field from a particular field is also removed. Once we hold that a coerced sale can take effect without immediate payment, an acknowledgment regarding payment is no longer part of the coerced conveyance and is therefore effective. It follows, however, that where the sale cannot take effect without actual money, such as a sale by deed in a situation where money is specifically required, the Mechaber would agree that a mere acknowledgment of receipt is ineffective. There the acknowledgment remains necessary to complete the coerced acquisition itself, and the seller was coerced to complete that acquisition. I am also uncertain about a case where they hang him and he says, "go perform chazakah and acquire," and the buyer later performs chazakah outside the seller's presence. At the moment the seller gave permission no money was paid and the buyer had not yet become indebted, while the later chazakah was not performed before the seller. It requires study whether the principle that coercion together with payment produces genuine conveyance can operate in such a sequence.
But his acknowledgment alone is ineffective. If the seller writes a deed containing a warranty, the rule is not stated here explicitly. Perhaps the Mechaber relied on what he wrote above in siman 151:50 concerning one established as a robber, where some say that if the deed contains a property warranty the purchaser acquires. That case includes even a seller's acknowledgment. It appears, however, that this depends on the dispute of the Sema and Taz below in seif 3 concerning a gift deed containing a warranty. According to the Sema, only where it is known that the warranty clause itself was coerced is it ineffective; ordinarily the additional warranty demonstrates genuine consent. The same would apply here. According to the Taz, however, we ordinarily assume that the coercion extended to the warranty as well unless it is known that the warranty was written voluntarily. On that view a standard warranty clause would not help here either. Even according to the Taz, one must distinguish siman 151 from the present case. There the seller is not currently being coerced at all. He merely claims that because the person robbed him once and risked his life over the matter, he remained afraid. It is reasonable to say that writing an additional warranty, which is like undertaking liability beyond the specific field, is something he would not do merely because of that old fear. Here, by contrast, the coercer is presently forcing him and perhaps even physically restraining him. It is reasonable to presume that the coercion extended to the warranty too. The Taz there explains that where it is known that the warranty was not coerced and the seller wrote it voluntarily, even a prior moda'ah does not help, because the voluntary warranty demonstrates genuine consent.
And even if he possessed it for many years. See Shach, seif katan 3, who refers to the responsum of Mahari Sason stating that the possessor has the law of a robber. That is straightforward. However, his further assertion on logical grounds that if a defect is later found in the sale the robber must reimburse all expenditures even where they produced no improvement overlooks the explicit rule of the Tur and Mechaber in siman 232:14; see there.
And he returns the money. Although seif 10 rules that if the seller issued a moda'ah he need not return the money when his receipt of it was established only by his own acknowledgment, whereas here the Mechaber speaks of a case where he did not receive the money before witnesses and nevertheless implies that he returns it, Maharahash answers in his Kuntres HaOnes that seif 10 concerns one who issued a moda'ah not only against the sale but also against his acknowledgment. That is why the Mechaber there carefully says that he issued a moda'ah "concerning this," meaning concerning the acknowledgment itself. Here he issued a moda'ah only against the sale, not against the acknowledgment. When there is no moda'ah against the acknowledgment, it is enough that we know the sale itself was coerced. We need not separately know that the seller was specifically coerced into acknowledging receipt, and he need not return the money merely on the strength of that acknowledgment. According to the authorities cited by the Rama above who hold that an acknowledgment is ineffective even without a special moda'ah, no moda'ah against the acknowledgment is needed at all.
And the witnesses must know that he is selling because of the coercion. See Sema, seif katan 7, through his statement that the coercion must not have ceased from the seller. In my humble opinion his words are very difficult. Ramban, cited by Beit Yosef, does write that according to the view that proof of coercion alone can suffice without a moda'ah, the witnesses must know that the seller was still under coercion at the time of sale; otherwise perhaps he later consented. But when the seller has issued a moda'ah, Ramban explicitly writes that we no longer harbor this concern. The same is clear from Rashbam as cited by Beit Yosef, and Maharahash proves this in the Kuntres Moda'ah VeOnes. Maharahash notes that Rambam's language can perhaps be read to require knowledge that the coercion continued until the sale, but he also provides another explanation of Rambam and writes that all the other commentators disagree. Even if Rambam personally required this, his view would not override the many authorities who reject it. Maharik, cited by Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer 134, goes further. Even where the coercion was formally removed before the sale, as long as there is reason to say that the seller still fears that same coercer, the sale remains an act under coercion, unless the circumstances of the coercion materially changed, for example the coercer's power substantially weakened. Daily practice also validates moda'ah documents written before a later deed of sale. According to the Sema's literal formulation, those would be invalid because perhaps the seller freely consented at the later moment. On his approach, witnesses would also need to write in the moda'ah deed that they knew the coercion continued specifically at the moment of sale, as Ramban requires where the writing merely records the coercive facts and no moda'ah was actually delivered. Our practice is not so. It therefore appears that even the Sema's words, "that coercion did not cease from the seller," mean only that the underlying coercive situation did not materially weaken during the interval. See Shach, seif katan 4, who writes that Responsa Divrei Rivot, siman 311, contains a surprising ruling. There is a copying error in the Shach's quotation. It should read: "it is not a gift, since it was made under coercion," as the responsum itself has it. The case was a Jew who owed money to a gentile and could not pay. The gentile agreed to wait another year only if the Jew paid a specified amount of interest. Out of fear of the oppressive gentile, the Jew agreed. Later the Jew needed some favor from the gentile, who refused unless the Jew gave him a promissory note combining the principal and the interest. The gentile later sold the note to another Jew, who sought to collect both principal and interest. The responsum ruled that because the interest obligation was in substance a gratuitous undertaking and was accepted only under coercion, there was no binding obligation. At first glance this ruling appears entirely sound. The original debt did not include interest, and when the debtor later could not pay, the added interest was plainly imposed only because of coercion. A debtor who lacks funds has no preexisting obligation to add interest. Even apparent consent under coercion is like "they hang him and he gives a gift," which is ineffective. Perhaps the Shach called the ruling surprising because the later promissory note was issued in exchange for some separate benefit the gentile provided, making that later act resemble a sale for consideration. Alternatively, the Shach may have understood the responsum as saying that the original debt itself already bore interest and that the interest was a clear existing obligation. The Shach also refers to Responsa Alshekh, siman 71. The point there is that even if witnesses wrote, "we recognized his coercion," but the facts of coercion they recount do not legally amount to coercion, the witnesses can clarify the facts and we assess them legally. This does not constitute forbidden retraction from testimony, because witnesses are capable of mistakenly classifying a situation as legal coercion.
