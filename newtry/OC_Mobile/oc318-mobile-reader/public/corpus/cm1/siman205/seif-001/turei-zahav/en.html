(Seif 3, in the gloss: A gift in which it is written, etc.) The Rivash writes: “In our case as well, it is possible that since the gift includes a warranty, it is regarded as a sale, as we say in the chapter HaMekabel concerning the law of an adjoining landowner. Moreover, Shimon’s own claims mention that they threatened him only that he must sell the property to them, and we rule that where one was coerced and sold, the sale is valid so long as he did not deliver a declaration of coercion. Furthermore, he should not have accepted a warranty upon himself, as in the case of one who purchases property from a sicarius and then purchases it again from the owner, where if the owner wrote him a warranty, the purchase is valid.” End quote. The Sema, seif katan 10, asks from the law at the beginning of siman 242 and answers that there it is known that everything was done under coercion. This is difficult. It is obvious that a warranty written under coercion is ineffective. Furthermore, the language in siman 242 does not imply this, for the Tur writes there, “since it was under coercion,” and not, “if it was under coercion.” This implies that the reference is to the coercion of the gift itself. In addition, whence do we know to say here that it was truly a sale but was written as a gift as a subterfuge? On the contrary, perhaps he was compelled to write the warranty as well. The Rivash’s proof from the sicarius is also difficult, for the cases are dissimilar. There, the warranty is from the dispossessed owner to the purchaser, and the sicarius does not care about it. It therefore proves that the owner resolved to transfer the property. Here, however, the warranty benefits the coercing party himself, who certainly does care about it and would insist upon it. It appears to me that the Rivash deals with a case where the coercion concerned only the warranty, and this is known. This is evident from the language of the Rivash quoted above, that they threatened him only that he must sell it to them. This proves at least that they did not compel him to give a gift with a warranty, and the sale is therefore valid. The Rama here likewise speaks of such a case. In siman 242, by contrast, we do not know how the warranty came to be written, and we properly presume that just as the principal transfer was made under coercion, so too was the writing of the warranty. This appears entirely clear to me. In truth, the Sema’s difficulty is not a difficulty, for there the case is that we do not know whether the warranty was given under coercion. Rabbeinu Yonah teaches that we presume it was coerced, as is clear there in the Rivash. Independently, however, this gloss still requires resolution. The Rivash first writes, as the Rama writes here, that a gift is like a sale. His intent there concerns the delivery of a declaration of coercion, for which there must be knowledge of the coercion. Yet afterward, when the Rivash adds the further argument quoted above, he withdraws from that approach and concludes that because the instrument contains a warranty, the transaction remains fully valid even if there was coercion. He compares it to the case of a sicarius, where the warranty cancels the prior coercion. If so, here too, in a gift containing a warranty, the warranty cancels the declaration of coercion that was previously delivered. Why, then, does the Rama write here that the law of such a gift is like the law of a sale regarding a declaration of coercion and recognition of the coercion, which is the subject under discussion, just as he wrote immediately before this that a compromise has the law of a sale? In truth, here, since the gift contains a warranty, the transaction should remain fully valid even if a declaration of coercion was delivered, as explained. This requires resolution.
