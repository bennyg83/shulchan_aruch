Seif 1. Note: I find it very difficult that the early and later poskim do not state explicitly that under a clearly established threat of death, even “they hanged him until he gave” can constitute a valid gift without payment. Gittin 55b regarding sikarikon appears explicit. Rashi explains that because of the coercion, when the owner says, “Take this land and leave me alone,” he transfers it wholeheartedly, invoking the principle that a coerced sale is valid. Although Rashi cites the rule of a coerced sale, where payment is normally present, his point must be that whenever the circumstances prove that the owner fully resolves to transfer, the transfer is effective, and the same principle can extend to a gift under mortal coercion. The Shach in Yoreh De'ah writes that one should consider leniency where the threat of death is certain, because the coercion may cause him fully to transfer, as stated in the rulings of Mahari. I am surprised that he did not cite the explicit Gemara. I later saw in Mordechai, chapter HaNizakin, that there are differing approaches. Rabbeinu Tam appears at first to say broadly that mortal coercion produces full intent, like a coerced sale. The responsum of Maharam, however, first limits the rule to the original sikarikon decree, where the Jew knew with certainty that the oppressor would kill him if he refused. In ordinary threats, where the non-Jew may only be threatening without intending to act, the owner may not fully resolve to give. This would explain why the poskim omitted a broad rule. Maharam later gives another distinction: where the transfer requires no further collection or enforcement, mortal coercion can effect it; where further collection remains necessary, it does not. According to this, if the victim immediately gives the property to the robber and nothing remains to be collected, the robber acquires, and it is surprising that the poskim do not discuss this. One cannot say that the rule was omitted because it is simply the sikarikon law in siman 236. That law itself applies where the oppressor fears later legal consequences, as shown by the Gemara's distinction between periods in which murderers themselves were punished and periods in which they were not. Where a powerful official or ruler cannot realistically be sued or punished, the special sikarikon structure may not apply, and the gift under mortal coercion may therefore be valid. Perhaps the case was omitted because it is uncommon. There is also a reverse difficulty in the Shach. He suggests that certain mortal coercion produces full intent to transfer, yet the Gemara and siman 236 show that even fear of death does not necessarily create full intent where the victim expects later legal recovery. In such a case the law of sikarikon applies and, before the prescribed period, the purchaser must return the land without payment. This tension in the Shach requires study.
