If he brought them in with permission, he is exempt. See siman 398, where the Tur records a dispute among the Geonim. Ri and the Rosh rule like the Sages that when one gives another permission to bring produce into his courtyard, he thereby accepts responsibility for guarding the produce. The Rif and Rambam rule like Rabbi that permission to bring it in does not itself constitute acceptance of guarding. Even according to the Rif and Rambam, however, this dispute concerns whether the courtyard owner becomes liable for damage that his animal causes to the entrant's produce. Everyone agrees that the owner of the produce is not liable if the courtyard owner's ox is injured by that produce after it was brought in with permission. That is the law the Mechaber states here. It is therefore surprising that Rama adds that some disagree, referring to siman 398, because the dispute there does not concern the present exemption of the owner of the produce. One must explain with difficulty that Rama understood the Mechaber's continuation, "if the householder accepted responsibility to guard the produce, the householder is liable," as implying that in the preceding case of mere permission each party is exempt from damage to the other. According to that implication, the Mechaber would also exempt the courtyard owner if his animal damaged the entrant's property. Rama then notes that some disagree with that aspect. This interpretation is strained because the courtyard owner's liability had not been discussed earlier in the seif.
