Tied by their wings. See Sema, seif katan 45. He explains that there is no dovecote within fifty cubits, apparently because otherwise the birds would belong to the owner of the dovecote, as stated above in seif 8. Tosafos Yom Tov writes similarly. This is an unnecessary difficulty. On the contrary, if there were no dovecote within fifty cubits and the doubt were whether someone intentionally placed them there or whether they fell accidentally, there would be no need to establish the case as one of birds that hop. Rather, the case is where there is a dovecote within fifty cubits, so we do not suspect an accidental fall. Tosafos in Perek Lo Yachpor explains that where we can attribute the birds to a dovecote, we do not attribute them to a fall. This is also implied by Rav Ukva bar Chama's statement that the case concerns hopping birds, together with the rule that a hopping bird does not travel more than fifty cubits. Thus there is a dovecote within fifty cubits, yet because the birds are tied and found behind a fence or wall, there is also a possibility that a person intentionally concealed them there. Rashi explains likewise. Above, seif 8, concerns birds that are not tied. Nor can the Gemara simply establish the case where no dovecote lies within fifty cubits and attribute the find to a fall, because then the finder should take it and announce it: if someone intentionally concealed it, he can identify the place, while if it fell, the owner presumably despaired after noticing the loss. Ramban likewise explains that beyond fifty cubits the finder keeps the birds because the one from whom they fell despairs, since there is no identifying mark. Even if he had not yet noticed the fall, the finder should still take them rather than leave them indefinitely. The Gemara therefore had to establish the case as one of hopping birds.
If the item is guarded, etc. Meaning that it is in a guarded place, as stated by the Tur, Tosafos, and the Rosh. Since it is fully guarded, even if it has an identifying mark there is no obligation of returning a lost object, and therefore one should not touch it.
It is uncertain whether he placed it there, etc. The wording is difficult. Since the item has an identifying mark, the reason not to touch it is that it was intentionally placed in a guarded location. If so, this is a case of certain placement, not uncertain placement. The Rosh states the principle expressly: where it was placed in a guarded location, such as a garment or axe beside a fence, even if it has an identifying mark one does not touch it. The Tur's language also indicates this.
And in a place that is entirely unguarded, etc. The Rama also abbreviated too much here. It is obvious that where the owner certainly placed the item and it has no identifying mark, even in a wholly unguarded place it does not belong to the finder; it must remain until Eliyahu comes because this is despair without knowledge. The Tur and Rosh therefore explain the case as small bundles in the public domain without an identifying mark, which roll about under people's feet. Their owners originally put them down temporarily to lighten their load, forgot them, and then despaired because there is no identifying mark in the object or place. Only in such a case did the owners presumably despair before the finder took it, so it came lawfully into his hand. Otherwise, where the despair was without knowledge, it must remain until Eliyahu comes according to the poskim and the Rama. See below, siman 262, end of seif 12.
And if he took it, etc. The Nimukei Yosef, Ran, Ramban, Rashba, and Maggid Mishneh indicate explicitly that in such a case he may return it, provided he has not yet taken it home. If the owners intentionally placed it there, the location is sufficiently guarded for them and he may replace it there. If it arrived there accidentally, then because it has no identifying mark it belongs to the finder. Since he could lawfully take it home, there is no reason he may not return it to the place from which he took it. This appears to me the primary ruling, and the episode of Rabbi Abba bar Zavda in our Gemara appears to correspond to the Yerushalmi case.
