Bound by their wings. See Shach, seif katan 28. His words are initially difficult. He asks that if there is no dovecote within fifty cubits, why the Gemara needs to say that the birds can hop. But if they cannot hop, why would the finder acquire them after taking? And why should he not touch them, since if they fell there is no sign and if they were deliberately placed the location could be identified? Shach also asks why the Gemara does not simply establish the case where there is no nearby dovecote. Yet if the birds are bound and cannot move, the distance from a dovecote should make no difference, because they plainly did not arrive there by hopping from it. His intent appears to be this. If there were no nearby dovecote, the Gemara's concern that perhaps they "came from elsewhere" would mean a concern that they fell there. Then why mention hopping at all? Falling has nothing to do with the ability to hop. Shach therefore proves that we do not ordinarily suspect accidental falling where there is a plausible deliberate-placement explanation in a guarded location, just as Tosafot say we do not suspect falling when there is a nearby dovecote because falling is uncommon. Here too, where deliberate placement is plausible, falling is not the primary concern. Accordingly, the Gemara's statement "perhaps they came from elsewhere" means there is a nearby dovecote and perhaps the birds hopped from there. The question therefore properly depends on their ability to hop. The Gemara answers that because they are tied there is also a realistic possibility of deliberate placement, and therefore one should not take them. Shach then asks why the Gemara did not establish the case with no nearby dovecote and explain the doubt as accidental falling. His answer is that the Tanna cannot hold that falling is a meaningful concern against deliberate placement, because then the law should be: take and announce. If someone intentionally placed them, he can identify the place; if they fell, the finder should proceed under the ordinary lost-property rules. Therefore the Gemara properly focuses on hopping from a nearby dovecote rather than accidental falling.
In a place that is wholly unguarded. See Shach, seif katan 31, who questions this. There is no real difficulty. Even deliberate placement in a wholly unguarded place can be treated as intentional abandonment or as something the owner later forgot, as Tosafot and Shitah Mekubetzet explain. Maharsha at the beginning of Eilu Metziot writes explicitly that scattered fruit, whenever it is plausible the owner placed it there, can constitute conscious despair from the outset. Tur and Rosh were forced to speak specifically of small bundles only because they needed the location not to function as an identifying sign, since small bundles roll. Large bundles can retain a location sign. The cross reference in Shach to siman 262, seif 12, is a scribal error and should refer to seif 9, which discusses small bundles.
And if he took it. See Shach, seif katan 32, citing Nimukei Yosef and Rashba that he may return it. The dispute depends on whether we suspect that the owner deliberately placed the object and later forgot it. Tosafot hold that such forgetting is plausible in a partially guarded place, so the object can become an actual lost object and the finder becomes a lost-property custodian. Rashba and Ran hold that where the place is at least somewhat guarded we do not presume forgetfulness. The finder can therefore restore the item to the same place deliberately chosen by the owner.
