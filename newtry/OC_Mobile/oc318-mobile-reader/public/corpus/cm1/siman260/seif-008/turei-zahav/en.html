(Seif 9, whoever finds an article.) Rambam’s words initially appear very difficult. He writes that even where the object has an identifying mark, one should not touch it, whereas the Gemara implies that the rule not to take an article of doubtful placement applies specifically where it has no identifying mark, for the Gemara asks why the location or knot should not serve as an identifying mark. Beit Yosef cites this difficulty in the name of Maggid Mishneh and is forced to suggest that Rambam had a different text, but this is strained. Even according to that proposed text, what compelled the Gemara at the conclusion to include an object bearing an identifying mark? Initially the distinction between an object with and without an identifying mark was based on reason. Why should that reasoning disappear without being rejected? Beit Yosef’s explanation is even more forced. The central discussion in the Gemara rejects Rabbah’s reason that otherwise the owner would lose his money, yet according to Rambam’s statement that the finder acquires an object without an identifying mark, by what right does he acquire another’s money? Maggid Mishneh likewise raises this objection. The matter is doubtful whether the object fell, in which case the finder acquires an article without an identifying mark, or was intentionally placed there. This is also analogous to despair without knowledge. This is why the Tur writes that Rambam’s ruling is unpersuasive. Beit Yosef repairs Rambam by explaining that he holds that in a case of despair without knowledge the finder should not take the object, but if he did take it he acquires. I am astonished, for it nevertheless came into his hand unlawfully. This is precisely Abaye’s principle regarding despair without knowledge: even an object without an identifying mark is treated like one with a mark according to Rabbah, and although the owner later certainly despairs, the finder originally took it unlawfully. So too according to Abaye, an object without an identifying mark cannot be acquired retroactively unless it is known that the owner became aware of the loss before the finder found it. Knowledge of the loss of an object without a mark stands in the same place as despair regarding an object with a mark. This is straightforward. There is also an explicit Gemara in Bava Metzia 37, where the Gemara challenges the rule that in a doubtful placement, if one took the article he should not return it, from the case in which a depositary places the disputed property between the claimants and withdraws. The Gemara answers that he should set it aside. This explicitly shows that an article of doubtful placement must be set aside, since the two cases are compared. It is also difficult from the Gemara’s challenge to Abaye from the case of a thief who gave an object, and the like. According to Beit Yosef there is no difficulty, because one could say that Abaye concedes after the finder has taken it. Let us therefore set out Rambam’s actual sequence. He first writes the rule in this seif through “he must announce it.” He then writes: “What is considered intentional placement? If he found a donkey or cow grazing on the road by day, he should not touch it, as Scripture says, ‘wandering on the road,’” and likewise, “if he found a cloak or axe beside a fence, he should not touch it,” as quoted in the following seif. It appears to me that Rambam too holds that where an object has an identifying mark, the finder takes and announces it even in a case of doubtful placement, like the other authorities cited by Beit Yosef in the name of Maggid Mishneh. Rambam’s words may be understood as follows. He first states the law of definite intentional placement, as later clarified by “What is considered intentional placement?” In such a case the object has no status of a lost article at all, as the Gemara explicitly says, “This is not a lost article.” Rambam gives the reason from the verse “wandering on the road,” which applies to a lost article but not to an object safely placed. Since there is no lost article, he should not touch it even if it bears an identifying mark, because it is in a fully guarded place and is like property resting in its owner’s home. If he picked it up while still standing there, he may return it to that place. Certainly, if he carried it home he may not return it, because, as the Tur writes in the name of Rosh, perhaps the owner came and searched for it. Rambam does not discuss that case in this chapter, but only one who picked it up and remains there. He then states a second category: even if one is uncertain whether the object was lost or intentionally placed. Rambam later explains this through the case of tied fledglings, the Gemara’s case of doubtful placement. This concerns an object without an identifying mark, where there can be a right of acquisition if the owner knowingly despaired, such as valuable items whose loss a person ordinarily notices. In such a case we say that so long as it remains in place, the finder should not take it intending to keep it by virtue of its lacking a mark, because perhaps the owner placed it there. Valuable character therefore does not help, since if it was intentionally placed there the owner did not despair. If he nevertheless took it, he acquires whatever rights ordinarily attach to an object without a mark, because we may then rely on the possibility that it fell. One should not ask why in this doubt we rule leniently for the possessor, while in doubtful despair without knowledge, where we do not know whether the owner knew, we rule stringently until knowledge is certain. In doubtful despair without knowledge the possibilities of informed and uninformed despair are balanced. In doubtful placement, however, it is more probable that the article fell. The Gemara’s questioner would have permitted it entirely because it may have come from elsewhere, following the majority. The answer only makes it a doubt, so initially we are stringent and do not take it lest it was intentionally placed, but after the fact we rely on the majority that it fell. Rambam’s statement that if one took it he “acquired it” means that he acquired the same rights he would have in an object without an identifying mark found in a definite manner of falling. He does not mean that he acquires in every case, for he is certainly no better than one who finds an object that definitely fell, who acquires only when it is known that the owner became aware of the loss. Maggid Mishneh’s phrase, “if it is doubtful whether it fell, he acquired it under the law of one who finds an object without an identifying mark,” has precisely this meaning. Rambam’s words therefore read as follows: “Even if he is uncertain whether it was lost or placed, he should not touch it. If he transgressed and took it,” even if he is still there, “he may not return it.” Rambam then explains the distinction. If it is an object with no identifying mark whatsoever, neither in its location nor in its knot, only then does he acquire and need not return it. The repeated language teaches that only after the fact does he acquire and need not return it, but initially he may not take it. He concludes that any object with an identifying mark is not included in this restriction. Rather, even initially he takes and announces it. Although Rambam says only “he is obligated to announce it,” the meaning is the standard law of “obligated to announce” used by the Gemara and authorities. Rambam therefore includes doubtful placement together with definite falling and with an object found in the public thoroughfare, where one certainly takes and announces even if it was definitely placed, as he later writes concerning a highway. By this grouping Rambam teaches that one takes it initially for the purpose of announcing it. One should not infer from Rambam’s wording, “if it was an object without an identifying mark,” that the preceding rule “he should not touch it” referred to an object with a mark. Rambam later makes clear that an object with a mark should indeed be touched and announced. His wording is explanatory, indicating that the earlier case concerned specifically an object without a mark. If he may not return such an object because he did not acquire it under the law of informed despair, as where it was not a valuable item and the owner may not yet know of the loss, Rambam teaches that it must remain set aside until Eliyahu comes. The Gemara in HaMafkid therefore properly challenges from this rule. According to this explanation, Rambam and the Tur disagree only where one took an article of doubtful placement without a mark. Rambam holds that he may acquire it under the ordinary law of such an object, whereas the Tur holds that he gains no right and it remains as though still lying on the ground. This differs from the Rama’s explanation here. With God’s help, all of Rambam’s words are thus resolved.
