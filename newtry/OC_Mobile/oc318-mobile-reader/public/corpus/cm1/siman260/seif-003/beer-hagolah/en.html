Rambam likewise writes there, law 11, 'it appears to me,' etc. The Maggid Mishneh writes that the reason is sound. When they say that the objects belong to the homeowner, it is not because his courtyard acquires them for him, for if so even objects found from the midpoint outward would belong to him. Rather, his courtyard does not acquire them because a hole opening into the public domain is like an unguarded courtyard. When they say that these belong to the homeowner, it is because he himself presumably hid them there. Since the finder admits they are not his, a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. The sugya likewise appears to show that the wall itself does not acquire them for him at all.

There, in the cited statement, Rav Menashya bar Yaakov establishes the Mishnah that says 'these belong to him.' The Maggid Mishneh writes that Rambam did not rule according to Rav Nachman's explanation there, that even with three Jews, etc., perhaps because Rava disagrees with him on 26b. Rava is concerned when a sela fell from among three people even if it is worth only two perutot, because perhaps they are partners. The same applies to an inn rented by three Jews. The Gemara explicitly compares the two cases, and this is primary.
