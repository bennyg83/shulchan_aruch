The same applies to the last tenant. See Shach, seif katan 10, who explains that the case is one where the homeowner does not live together with the tenants. His words are difficult. If the homeowner now lives in the house after the tenants left, then even if he did not live with them earlier, the found item should belong to the homeowner. If the homeowner left together with the tenants, why should it belong to him more than to them? They previously lived there together and left together, so he cannot be called the last occupant. Tosafot's statement has a different intent. Their difficulty was why the Gemara asks that we follow the last occupant when the Mishnah concerns an old, rusted object, which cannot plausibly belong to the last occupant. They therefore explain that the initial assumption was a case where the homeowner lived together with the tenants and remained after they left. He is then truly the last occupant. Since tenants normally clean and search when leaving, while the homeowner who remained did not search the hidden places, the object can be presumed his, even if it is old and rusted, from something he hid earlier. The Gemara then answers that the house had been made an inn for three non Jews. When non Jews live there together with the homeowner, even an item originally belonging to the homeowner can become subject to despair because of their presence. The principal ruling therefore appears to be that Mechaber speaks where the homeowner does not live there and the last tenant still occupies the house. The item then belongs to that last tenant.
The last tenant. See Shach, seif katan 11, who limits this to an item without an identifying sign. At first glance this is difficult. If the legal presumption is that the earlier tenants cleaned the house and removed their property when leaving, and therefore the object certainly belongs to the current tenant, why should an identifying sign matter? It appears Shach follows Raavad, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet, that the reason we follow the last tenant is not that the object is certainly his. Rather, the earlier tenant, after searching and not finding it when he left, presumably despaired because another tenant entered. This rationale works where the item lacks an identifying sign, since he expects the next tenant will not know whose it is and will not announce it. If the item has an identifying sign, he may not despair because he expects the finder to announce it. If the object is rusted, however, the former owner may believe it was found long ago and not returned, and thus despair even if it had a sign. Still, Shach's distinction where the homeowner lived together with the tenants is difficult. If the item is rusted and despair rests on the belief that it was taken, what difference is there whether it has a sign? The primary rule in my view is this. If the object is not found in a hole or crevice, we presume with certainty that it belongs to the last occupant, even if it has an identifying sign, because prior tenants certainly searched when leaving. If it is rusted, and the homeowner lived there together with the tenants and remained after they left, we presume it is the homeowner's from something he hid earlier, even though he had despaired during the non-Jewish tenancy. After they left, his guarded courtyard reacquires it for him. If non Jews are still living there when it is found, it belongs to the finder even if the homeowner gives an identifying sign, because the homeowner despaired and his courtyard does not acquire for him while it is not guarded from them. If it was found after the non Jews left, the homeowner's courtyard can acquire it for him. If it is found in a hole or crevice, it can belong to one of the earlier occupants. If it lacks an identifying sign, those earlier occupants have despaired, and the last tenant acquires it when it lies in a guarded and ordinarily discoverable place. If it has a sign, it should be taken and announced, unless rust proves sufficiently old despair. If it lies in an unguarded place or in a place where it would not ordinarily be found, it belongs to the finder.
Three non Jews. See Shach, seif katan 12, who says the case is where they are still in the house. According to Tosafot, who explain that the homeowner lives with them, this is necessary. If they had already left and the homeowner remained, the object would belong to the homeowner. According to authorities who explain that the homeowner does not live there, once the non Jews leave and the house remains empty, an item may belong to the finder. Shach further says three non Jews are required so that there be a recognizable majority. Pnei Yehoshua correctly questions this, since the Mishnah says that with half Jews and half non Jews one must announce, implying that any majority should suffice. Pnei Yehoshua answers that three are needed for a different reason: if a Jew comes and gives an identifying sign, the law still treats him as having despaired because the place is one frequented by many. In my view the matter is simpler. Tosafot had to establish that the non Jews are still in the house because the Mishnah concerns a rusted item. Since rust shows the item existed there from before, if the non Jews had already left and the house were now a guarded courtyard, the homeowner's courtyard could reacquire the object after their departure. While the non Jews still live there, however, the courtyard is not guarded for him. Because the basis is despair, and the object cannot plausibly be attributed to the present non Jews when the rust predates them, three non Jews are required so that it is a place frequented by many, in which the owner reasonably despairs.
