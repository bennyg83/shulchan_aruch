(To three gentiles.) The Gemara states this rule. Rashi, Rosh, and the Tur explain that the same applies even to one gentile, provided he was the last occupant. Tosafot write: “Fewer than two do not constitute a majority, and the case is where they were still present, for otherwise we would attribute the article to the householder, who was the last occupant.” Rambam, whose ruling the Mechaber quotes, also appears to require three gentiles. The opinions may be reconciled. Tosafot and Rambam explain that the finder acquires because even if other people had been in the house, there is no concern that they lost the object. Even if they did, they despaired because the occupants were predominantly gentile. Three gentiles are therefore required, since two do not constitute a gentile majority sufficient to presume despair over a lost article. Rashi, Rosh, and the Tur discuss a case where there is no possibility that another person lost it in that house, because others were not found there. It must therefore have fallen from the person who occupied the house, and even one gentile is sufficient if he was the final occupant. Along these lines, the Tur writes that where two gentiles and one Jew were there together, we attribute the loss to the majority, meaning that it necessarily fell from one of the three present in the house. There is thus no dispute. The Sema, seif katan 5, explains Tosafot as referring to three people including the householder, but this is not satisfactory. Why establish the case that way when it could be established with one gentile who was the last occupant?
