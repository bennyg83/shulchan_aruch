Then if, etc. The case is one in which the homeowner does not reside with them. Otherwise, the object belongs to the homeowner, who is the last occupant, as Tosafot write. In practical law, Hagahot Ashri and the other authorities do not disagree with Tosafot, contrary to what appears from the Bach.
It belongs to the last tenant, etc. This applies specifically to an item without an identifying mark. An item with an identifying mark must be taken and proclaimed unless it has accumulated rust. So states Hagahot Ashri in the name of Or Zarua, in a case where the homeowner does not live there. If the house was rented to others while the homeowner lived with them, it appears that an item with an identifying mark must be taken and proclaimed even if rusted, because a homeowner does not forget his property for many days. If it has no identifying mark, however, the homeowner too despairs when others reside with him. Tosafot, folio 26a, proves all this. They write that even without a mark, the homeowner does not despair of what he loses in his house, expecting to find it today or tomorrow when no strangers reside with him; but where the house was made an inn for three people, he despairs even of an item with a mark. According to our ruling, three Jews do not cause despair of an item with a mark, as explained below in siman 262, seif 2; the rule applies where non Jews reside with him. In that case, even if the homeowner also resides there, an item with a mark that has not rusted belongs to the finder, since the homeowner despairs after making the house an inn for three non Jews. The Sema explains that 'the last tenant' means a Jew; if the last tenant is a non Jew, the object belongs to the finder. The Tur says this, and it follows from the Gemara according to Rashi and the Rosh. The prior Jewish tenants certainly swept, searched, and removed all their belongings when they left. If the object is found in a deep crack or hollow where sweeping may not have revealed it, however, we do not attribute it to the last tenant or homeowner. It may belong to the earlier tenants, who despaired after searching without finding it, and therefore belongs to the finder.
To three non Jews, etc. This concerns a case in which they are still in the house. Otherwise, according to Tosafot, it belongs to the homeowner, who is the last occupant. According to the authorities and the Mechaber, who hold that the homeowner does not reside with them, the same rule applies even if they are no longer there. The Sema's explanation that the three include the homeowner is certainly incorrect, for the Gemara speaks of three non Jews. Tosafot likewise ask why three Jews are needed, since two together with the homeowner would suffice, showing that the three Jews do not include him. All the more so, the three non Jews do not include him. Tosafot mean simply that fewer than three do not constitute a significant majority for lost property. Since the homeowner ordinarily resides there according to Tosafot, two non Jews are insufficient; a recognizable majority is required. This appears to be the view of the Rambam and Mechaber: three non Jews are needed so that no other person, including the resident homeowner, can claim the object. The Tur interprets the Gemara according to Rashi and the Rosh, that three is not exact regarding non Jews; even one suffices, provided he was the last occupant. According to this, if the Jew resides there, two non Jews suffice. One may reconcile the Tur with the Rambam and Mechaber by distinguishing an inn, where the non Jews' primary residence is elsewhere and three are required, from ordinary residents, where two may suffice against the Jewish homeowner. Maharsha suggests such a distinction, though his words are not compelling. The principal understanding of Tosafot is as I explained.
Even inside the house, etc. Tosafot write that the item must be found in circumstances indicating that it is lost property. This follows their view that the homeowner resides with them. According to the authorities and Mechaber, who hold that he does not reside with them, the object belongs to the finder even if it does not appear lost, because it is attributed to the non Jewish majority.
