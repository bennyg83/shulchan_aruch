They are like a courtyard belonging to both of them. Rosh was asked about Shimon, who lived in Reuven's house while paying rent and acquired a found object in the house. Does the object belong to Shimon or did Reuven's courtyard acquire it? Rosh answers that rental for its period resembles a sale of use, so the premises are like a jointly held courtyard. He cites the rule that a partners' courtyard can acquire and says that if a wounded deer entered the house the two should acquire it jointly, like two people who lift a found object together. A purchase from a thief is different because it is not ownerless property to which courtyard acquisition applies in the same way.
I am surprised by Rosh's proof from Perek HaSefinah. There the statement that a partners' courtyard acquires from one partner to the other is established specifically where the goods are measured into the purchaser's own vessel. The joint courtyard itself does not acquire from one partner to the other. Tosafot in Kiddushin says the same. Were it not for Rosh, I would say even a found object coming from outside is not automatically acquired by a jointly owned courtyard. Rather, whoever actually finds it acquires. The Jerusalem Talmud supports this. It discusses a wall between two properties that falls. One view says half belongs to each side, and a found object in each half follows that owner. The other says the whole wall is held jointly, and "the finder acquires." On the latter view the entire area is a partners' courtyard, yet the courtyard itself does not acquire the find for both. Conceptually, even when two people lift a found object, the Gemara initially asks why the object should not be treated as lying on the ground, with neither acquiring. It answers that each person's hand can acquire on behalf of the other. There are two hands, each helping the other. A jointly owned courtyard, however, is one legal hand shared by both. There is no separate hand of one partner acquiring for the other. The single shared hand therefore does not acquire for either, and the object remains as though on the ground until someone acquires it. A responsum of Maharam of Rothenburg supports this. Reuven, Shimon, and Levi lived in one house. Reuven and Shimon made a profitable acquisition from a gentile, and Levi claimed one third because he shared the house. Maharam ruled Levi did not acquire merely through the courtyard unless they had agreed beforehand to share profits. He adds that, following Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and the doctrine of bereirah, each partner can in a sense acquire in the courtyard as his own turn arises. This suggests that one of the partners who first acquires the found object can prevail through bereirah, while an outsider may not necessarily do so. The Jerusalem Talmud's phrase, "the finder acquires," can likewise mean one of the partners. At minimum, Maharam shows that a found object in a partners' courtyard is not automatically acquired jointly by all partners. This is difficult against Rosh and requires careful consideration.
