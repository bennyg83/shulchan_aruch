(Seif 5, and some say that money found upon the counter.) Their dispute concerns the Mishna’s statement: “If one found money in a shop, it belongs to him; between the counter and the shopkeeper, it belongs to the shopkeeper. If one found money before a money changer, it belongs to him; between the chair and the money changer, it belongs to the money changer. Rabbi Elazar says: even money found upon the table belongs to the finder.” The Gemara concludes that Rabbi Elazar’s proof is that if money upon the table belonged to the money changer, why did the Mishna specify money between the chair and the money changer? Even money upon the table would belong to him. Moreover, it should have taught, “one who found money in the money changer’s establishment,” just as the first clause says, “in a shop.” The second opinion, which is the Tur’s view, holds that since Rabbi Elazar stated specifically concerning a money changer that money upon the table belongs to the finder, it follows that money upon the shopkeeper’s counter belongs to the shopkeeper. Otherwise Rabbi Elazar should have stated the same rule in the first clause concerning a shopkeeper. Furthermore, his proof is from the fact that the Mishna did not say “in the money changer’s establishment” as it said “in a shop.” Since the first clause actually does say “in a shop,” there is no such inference there, and money upon the counter may properly be attributed to the shopkeeper. There is also a practical reason for the distinction. Others do not ordinarily place their belongings upon a shopkeeper’s counter, whereas those who come to exchange money commonly place their coins upon the money changer’s table. Rambam holds that the law is the same for a shopkeeper and a money changer, and that no distinction should be made. Rabbi Elazar stated the rule regarding a money changer because that clause is the source from which the principle is learned. The Mishna’s first clause says generally, “in a shop,” whereas its second clause says, “before the money changer.” This difference reveals that money upon the table belongs to the finder, and the same necessarily applies to money upon the shopkeeper’s counter. The second clause was taught to clarify the first. One cannot ask why the first clause did not likewise say, “before the shopkeeper,” for then we would not have noticed any discrepancy. The Tanna deliberately used differing formulations so that we would derive the rule from the money changer and apply the same reasoning to the shopkeeper. This appears correct.
