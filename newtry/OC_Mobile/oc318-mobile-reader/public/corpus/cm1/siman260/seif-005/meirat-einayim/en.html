Between the counter and the shopkeeper, etc. The shopkeeper ordinarily sits inside the shop, with the box containing the goods he sells in front of him and its opening facing where he stands. He takes items from it to sell. Customers stand on the outer side of the box, while the shopkeeper puts the money received from customers into the box. Therefore, an item found between him and the opening side of the box certainly fell from the shopkeeper and belongs to him, since he did not despair of it.
Even if it has an identifying mark. This is the view of Rabbeinu Yitzchak and the Rosh. The Rosh explains that the Jew who lost the money despairs, saying, "I must have lost it before the shopkeeper, and he will not return it to me. He surely intends to keep it, and I cannot successfully bring him to judgment because he can evade me and say, 'I did not find it; one of the many people entering and leaving found it.'" Rashi, however, holds that only an item without an identifying mark belongs to the finder, as the Tur cites him, and Rambam agrees, as is clear from the Mechaber's conclusion at the end of this seif that where the majority are Jews he must announce it because it has an identifying mark. The laws of a moneychanger and a shopkeeper are the same in this respect. Rama's gloss at the beginning of the seif, "even if it has an identifying mark," placed directly on the Mechaber's ruling, is liable to mislead readers. At the end, where the Mechaber says that if there is an identifying mark he must announce it, Rama should instead have written, "some say he need not announce it." That would teach both that this is a dispute and that the same dispute applies to both shopkeeper and moneychanger. This requires study.
Some say that what is found on the shopkeeper's box, etc. But everyone agrees that what is found on the moneychanger's seat belongs to the finder. So it is in the Gemara. The Tur explains the difference: those who come to exchange money ordinarily place their own money on the table, so what is there belongs to the finder. Customers in a shop, by contrast, do not ordinarily place their belongings on the shopkeeper's box. This requires study.
Between the seat and the moneychanger. The "seat" is the stand upon which the table rests and at which he exchanges money. It separates the moneychanger standing inside the shop from the customers outside, like the shopkeeper's box described above. Therefore what is found between the moneychanger and the seat belongs to the moneychanger, since it certainly fell from him and came there. What is on the table or farther outward belongs to the finder for the reasons explained above.
On the seat before the moneychanger. Meaning, even if he found it on the seat itself in front of the moneychanger, and all the more so beyond the seat toward the entrance of the shop. This is the Gemara's and Tur's wording.
Otherwise he must announce it, etc. I already wrote that Rabbeinu Yitzchak and the Rosh disagree for the reason explained above concerning the shopkeeper. The same rationale applies here. The Ir Shushan mixes the rulings and implies that in a shopkeeper's premises, even where most people are Jewish and the item has an identifying mark, it belongs to the finder without announcement. That is incorrect, as explained.
