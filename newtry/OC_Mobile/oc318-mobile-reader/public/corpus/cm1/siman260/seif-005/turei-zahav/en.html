(Seif 6: when they fall they become repulsive.) At first glance this is difficult. The Gemara uses this reasoning only according to Rava, who holds that despair without knowledge constitutes despair. Rava is challenged from the baraita that olives and carobs are prohibited, and answers that their appearance shows that they fell from a tree and the owner does not despair. The Gemara then asks why, according to the opening clause, dried figs and figs are permitted, and answers that figs are different because when they fall they become repulsive, so the owners abandon them in advance. But Abaye holds that despair without knowledge does not constitute despair, and we rule like Abaye. From where, then, do we derive this reasoning about becoming repulsive through the fall? One may answer that once the Gemara revealed the concept that the appearance of olives and carobs demonstrates their origin, the same difficulty exists according to Abaye regarding dried figs and figs. Although the owner knows that they fall because they are valuable, perhaps he still does not despair, since he knows a finder may refrain from taking them because their appearance shows where they came from. Therefore, even according to Abaye, we need the reasoning that figs become repulsive upon falling. It further appears from the Gemara's wording, which makes the fall itself the cause of their becoming repulsive, that dried figs already lying on the field do not fall under this reasoning. Concerning dried figs, there is no meaningful identifying appearance at all, because they are ordinarily dried on the field. The owner therefore despairs from the outset, and no additional reason of repulsiveness is necessary. This also appears from our master's wording.
