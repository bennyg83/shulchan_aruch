It was never, from ancient times, in the established ownership of the person now living there. Meaning, even if it is known to have been in this person's and his ancestors' possession for three or four generations, nevertheless, since it is known that it never originally belonged to them or their ancestors, and the lost object is covered with rust and found deep below as described later, the burden of proof rests on the claimant. The finder may say, "It did not belong to your ancestors; it belonged to the ancient non-Jewish inhabitants who lived here long ago." This is the Gemara's language. The same applies to other earlier non-Jewish inhabitants, according to the circumstances of each place. Even though such rust and sinking might possibly have occurred over two or three generations during which his ancestors lived there, since it was found in the manner of a cache and could have come there in ancient times from a non-Jew, the burden of proof rests on the claimant. The Tur likewise writes this.
It belongs to the finder. Meaning, even if it was found toward the inner side of the wall. Why do we not say that the courtyard of the present householder acquired it for him? In the Derishah I gave several reasons. The general reason applicable to many cases is what I wrote in the Perishah in the name of Mordechai citing Rabbeinu Baruch: a person's courtyard acquires for him without his knowledge only true ownerless property, not a lost item, even after despair, because if the owner had known where it was he would not have abandoned it. Responsa Rosh cited by the Tur in seif 3 appears similar, though it is not a conclusive proof, as I explained in the Perishah.
In the manner of all old caches. It is possible that when it was first placed there, it lay in a hole in the wall or within the handbreadth adjoining the ground. Later the wall sank and the surrounding ground level rose, as commonly happens, so that it ended up far below the surface.
Perhaps they were placed there. In the Perishah I wrote that there appears to be no distinction in this case whether it lies in the outer half or inner half of the wall. The old wall is taught together with a ruin, and such a wall is not ordinarily used. The concern is instead that an outsider made a cache there, similar to finding something in a refuse heap that is not cleared, discussed at the end of this siman. Therefore the ruling is not to touch it, as with that refuse heap, and it does not belong to the owner of the ruin or wall.
From the midpoint outward, etc. It appears that this applies even if it was found deep down in the manner of a cache. See the Perishah.
If it fills the entire wall, etc. Even if it is one long piece extending from one end to the other, since it is not evident whether it was placed there from the inside or outside, and there is no prior owner's chazakah, the rule in this kind of monetary doubt is that they divide.
