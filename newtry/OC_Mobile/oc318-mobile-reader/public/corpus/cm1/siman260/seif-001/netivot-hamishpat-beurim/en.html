It belongs to the finder. See Sema, seif katan 2, through his statement "but not an ordinary lost object." These words are not accepted halachah, because Rambam, Rosh, Ran, and Tosafot disagree. They each supplied other answers to the difficulty, showing that they do not accept this rule. Thus the majority in both authority and number disagree with this reasoning. Moreover, the rule is itself difficult. The reason the object belongs to the finder is that we presume it belonged to gentiles, and gentile property in this context is like wilderness property and fully ownerless. One would therefore have to say, following the Rosh, that the spoil of the Emorites was originally acquired by all Israel, who later despaired of recovering it, so it has the status of a Jewish lost object. The Mordechai's rule would then apply only in Eretz Yisrael and not outside it, yet all the authorities state without qualification the rule of one who finds an item in a ruin. They therefore do not accept the Mordechai's explanation. This is also clear from Shach, seif katan 18.
Covered with rust. See Shach, seif katan 1, through his statement that it is an intentional abandonment. His meaning is that we are concerned that the hiding place may be recent, meaning that someone hid the item there after the ruin was formed. Therefore, if the ruin is not normally cleared away and is thus guarded, one should not touch it. If it is not guarded because it is normally cleared away, leaving it there is intentional abandonment, as below in seif katan 11.
Very deep. See Shach, seif katan 2, who asks why both requirements are necessary. In my humble opinion, rust is required so that it be recognizable as an old hiding place, while great depth is required because that makes the item unlikely to be discovered, so the owner's courtyard does not acquire it, as Tosafot write. But if it lies where people's hands ordinarily reach, it is likely to be found and the courtyard therefore acquires it.
He should not touch it. See Shach, seif katan 5, who explains that the case is one of a Jewish majority. In my humble opinion, a gentile majority permits taking only an ordinary lost object or something that has the law of a lost object, such as an item placed in refuse that is destined to be cleared away, or where the owner later decided to clear it. But where an item is intentionally placed in a genuinely guarded location, as here in refuse that is not normally cleared, as Sema, seif katan 4, explains, it is certainly prohibited even if we know that it belongs to a gentile. This resembles stealing from a gentile, which is prohibited. Only a gentile's lost object is permitted. This is likewise explained below in siman 266, Sema, seif katan 4; see there.
From the midpoint outward. See Sema, seif katan 5, who writes that this applies even if the item is found very deep, and Shach, seif katan 7, says that this is a scribal error. In truth there is no scribal error. The Sema's intent is clear. Even if the item is very deep in the manner of a hiding place, so that one might suspect that a passerby intentionally concealed it and therefore should not touch it, as with refuse in seif 11, this concern does not apply in a wall. People ordinarily use holes in walls, so one does not normally choose them for secure concealment, fearing that another person who wishes to use the hole will find and take the item. We therefore do not suspect that it was deliberately hidden there. Rather, someone from the street certainly placed it there temporarily, forgot it, and subsequently despaired of recovering it. This is plainly the Sema's intent, as he writes in Perishah.
