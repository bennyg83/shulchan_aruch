He therefore implies that even if it is somewhat guarded, one may not touch it. In seif katan 47 he again appears to reject Tosafot and the other authorities who hold that in a partially guarded place, if the object has an identifying sign, one takes and announces it. Tur, however, indicates that Rambam does not disagree with the other poskim on this point. Maggid Mishneh was troubled by the sugya of an item found behind a fence, where the Gemara asks that the knot or place should serve as an identifying sign, proving that with a sign one takes and announces. He therefore suggests Rambam had a different text. Bach further proves from Rambam's own language, "Every object that has an identifying sign, whether it is a doubtful placement..." that Rambam agrees with Tosafot. In my view Rambam can be explained coherently. At first he speaks of something certainly placed deliberately in a fully guarded location. There he correctly says one should not touch it even if it has an identifying sign. Why alter the owner's deliberate arrangement and trouble him when the location itself is fully secure? He then writes, "Even if the matter is doubtful and one does not know..." meaning a place only partially guarded, and the item itself has no identifying sign. Here, contrary to Sema, the issue is precisely that the body of the object lacks a sign. Sema asks how Rambam can say that if one took it he must not return it, since Rambam concludes that the finder acquires it. There is no difficulty. In a doubtful placement, if it truly was deliberately placed, its location itself is an identifying sign and the finder did not acquire it. Once he has taken it away, he must not restore it because perhaps the owner has already returned, searched, and not found it, as the Yerushalmi cited by Tosafot explains. He therefore announces it, and the person who placed it can identify the place. Rambam then says that if it is an item with no identifying sign, meaning neither in its body nor in its place, the finder acquires it. The example in seif 10 is young birds that move about, for which even deliberate placement does not create a location sign. Finally Rambam says that every object having an identifying sign must be announced, meaning one should take and announce it in the usual sense. This is exactly the position of Tosafot. The reasoning is compelling. Where the object itself has a sign and the situation may be a true loss, failure to take it could violate "You may not hide yourself." The mere possibility that it was deliberately placed and taking it will inconvenience the owner cannot justify risking violation of that prohibition. Where the item has no identifying sign but the place is partially guarded, however, there is no duty to take it. If it fell, the finder would acquire it and the owner loses nothing by the finder leaving it. If it was deliberately placed, one should not disturb the owner's arrangement. Therefore one leaves it. Tosafot appear to differ only because they assume that even in a partially guarded location we can suspect the owner placed it temporarily and later forgot it. Then the object can become a true lost object and the location can serve as a sign, creating a duty to take and announce. Rambam can follow Ritva, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet, that we infer later forgetfulness only in a place entirely unguarded. In a partially guarded place we do not assume he forgot it. This also reconciles Rambam's ruling concerning bundles in a private domain, which is treated as unguarded and therefore is taken and announced. Mechaber's later phrase, "and likewise if they were fixed in place, he must announce," can be read as referring to the case where he already took them: then he must announce them. This may also explain the Gemara's question, "Let the place be an identifying sign." The Mishnah simply said "do not touch it" without explaining what happens if he did take it. The Gemara infers from the earlier rule that items without signs belong to the finder and asks that here the place should itself be a sign, requiring announcement after taking.
An item without an identifying sign, the finder acquires it. All the commentators question Rambam because this seems to be despair without knowledge. See Sema, seif katan 42, and Shach, who struggle with the matter. In my view Rambam holds that where the young birds can move from this partially guarded place to a wholly unguarded place, as birds can hop up to fifty cubits, we presume that after placing them the owner later forgot them and despaired, just as Rambam holds that deliberate placement in a fully unguarded place is treated as forgotten and despaired. Nevertheless, while the birds remain exactly in the partially guarded location where the owner placed them, they have not yet legally left his domain. Despair does not operate on property still in the owner's legal possession, as Ramban writes at the beginning of Eilu Metziot regarding an item taken before despair. The area is not a public domain, and a person's four cubits can acquire there. By placing the birds there, the space is temporarily associated with the owner of the item, similar to Tosafot in Kiddushin concerning a large animal. As long as the birds remain there, despair does not transfer them. Therefore one should not touch them. But once the finder has taken them, the law is that he may not return them even if he has not yet acquired them, because the case is one of doubtful deliberate placement and he has become a finder-custodian. Once he took them, the birds left the original owner's domain. The owner's earlier despair can now become effective. This is not ordinary despair without prior knowledge. The despair actually preceded the finder's acquisition; it simply could not operate while the object remained in the owner's legal domain. This resembles Tosafot in Sukkah concerning despair in land. The same follows from Shach, seif katan 31, and Maharsha: deliberate placement in a wholly unguarded location itself creates a presumption of conscious despair. Birds capable of hopping to such a location are treated similarly. Thus Rambam's language that the finder "acquires it" is exact: once he lawfully reaches the stage after taking, the prior despair can operate completely, even against later proof of ownership.
