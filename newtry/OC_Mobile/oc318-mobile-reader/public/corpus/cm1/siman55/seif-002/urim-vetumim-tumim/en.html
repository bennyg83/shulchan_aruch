And he collects nothing with it except from unencumbered property. Sema writes that Darkei Moshe states explicitly that the entire amount of the note may be collected from unencumbered property. This also appears from Shulchan Aruch. For if the phrase "and he collects from unencumbered property" referred only to the half that had not been repaid, why would the ruling depend on the rights having been deposited with a third party in a manner that avoids asmachta? Even if there were asmachta, the unpaid half would certainly still be collectible. This corresponds to Rosh's first explanation in Nedarim, where he says that the entire amount may be collected from unencumbered property because the document is acquired through delivery. Nevertheless, even the unpaid half cannot be collected from encumbered property, by decree lest the creditor collect the whole note from encumbered property, analogous to an antedated document. Sema objects that Rosh himself retracted that explanation and instead said the case is one where the debtor gave back as a gift the amount he had already repaid, rather than treating it as repayment. If it had been repayment, what would mere delivery of the note accomplish? Transfer of documentary rights requires writing together with delivery. Sema therefore explains that only the unpaid portion can be collected from unencumbered property, while the repaid portion cannot be collected even from free assets. Although above in siman 48, if the debtor redelivers the document before witnesses, it becomes effective, that case is different because he reborrowed on the same day. Therefore, says Sema, here the case must involve writing and delivery. Shach, seif katan 6, asks what writing and delivery can accomplish once the lien has been extinguished. He therefore forces a difficult interpretation into Rosh, saying in effect that writing and delivery would ordinarily be necessary but here cannot help because the lien has already been extinguished. This does not appear correct. In truth, Rosh's words are sound because, regarding unencumbered property, the lien is not considered extinguished at all, as I explained above in siman 48. In particular, according to what I explained there, the third party will return the note before delivery witnesses in accordance with the law governing a third party. That itself supplies effective delivery witnesses. The creditor can therefore certainly collect from free assets. Why, then, according to one explanation does Rosh require delivery, and according to another writing and delivery, when the note is already written in the name of this very creditor and the debtor is merely returning to him the note that records the debtor's own obligation? What further acquisition is necessary? I was initially perplexed by this until I searched and found Responsa Rashba, Torat HaBayit, responsum 99. Rashba writes explicitly that even if the debtor placed a note with a third party and said, "If I fail to do such-and-such, acquire this note for him from now," the creditor does not acquire merely through delivery. This is because the case is legally like one person conferring upon another ownership of a document, and documentary rights are not acquired by delivery alone. Even if the debtor himself physically hands the note to the creditor, Rashba says the creditor does not acquire until there is writing and delivery. Rashba states a general rule. If a person says to witnesses, "Write and give the note to me," or, "Keep it in your possession until I wish, and if the purchaser or creditor later agrees I will deliver the note to him," and later the other party agrees and the document is delivered, the recipient does not acquire until there is writing and delivery. The reason is that the document was not originally created as the recipient's document. It was created for the transferor, and only now is the transferor attempting to confer that paper upon the recipient. It therefore has the law of an ordinary document owned by one person and transferred to another. Rashba proves this from Bava Batra, Perek HaSefinah. The Gemara says that documentary rights require writing and delivery, and challenges from Rav's law concerning two forms of deed. One is the case where a seller writes a deed before the purchaser arrives, as permitted by the Mishnah. Once the purchaser acquires the land, the deed is acquired together with it wherever the deed may be. At first the Gemara assumes acquisition by agav is like delivery and asks why writing is unnecessary. It answers that agav is different because it is equivalent to writing and delivery. This proves that even a deed written in the name of the purchaser requires the formal acquisition of writing and delivery where it was not initially created directly as the purchaser's operative instrument. Rashba says he found support from Rabbenu Chananel as cited by Baal HaIttur concerning a person who deposited a note with a third party. According to R. Yehudah, even if the debtor later tells the third party to return it to the creditor, the creditor does not acquire until there is writing and delivery. Baal HaIttur, in Shaar HaShelishi, states this as well. Accordingly, even though in our case the document was originally written from this debtor to this creditor, after the debt was repaid and the debtor now wants to make the same paper into a renewed instrument, it becomes in legal effect a document belonging to the transferor that is now being newly conferred upon the recipient. Even though, for collection from free assets, we do not call the prior lien completely extinguished, as explained above in siman 48, the new delivery is still not better than a case where a seller instructed witnesses to write a full deed but did not authorize them to acquire it on behalf of the purchaser. Rashba requires writing and delivery there, and all the more so here. This is the position of Rabbenu Chananel and can readily be understood as Rosh's view in his commentary to Nedarim. Initially Rosh thought the Mishnah followed the view that documentary rights are acquired by delivery alone, and therefore wrote that the creditor acquires by delivery. He then retracted because the Mishnah must accord with our halachah that writing and delivery are required, whereas the Mishnah mentions only that the document was deposited with the third party and says nothing about formal writing and transfer. This is a clear explanation of Rosh, and Shach's objection was unnecessary. There is no complete extinguishment regarding free assets, yet writing and delivery are still required because the paper has become the transferor's document rather than an already-operative document of the creditor. All this is according to Rashba, who holds that even a deed written from seller to purchaser requires writing and delivery if it was not originally delivered as the purchaser's operative instrument. Tosafot Bava Batra 77b, s.v. "is acquired," disagree. They hold that a deed written in the name of the seller and purchaser is acquired by mere delivery and does not require a separate writing of transfer. Rosh rules the same way in his halachic decisions there: a deed written from seller to purchaser is certainly acquired through delivery alone. Regarding the Gemara's challenge from the case where the seller first wrote a deed, they explain it differently, but according to everyone on their approach, a document written in the name of the actual purchaser or lender needs only delivery. According to that view, our document likewise should not require writing and delivery at all. This suggests either that Rosh in his halachic decisions retracted from what he wrote in his commentary to Nedarim, or that the commentary to Nedarim attributed to Rosh is actually by a different Rosh, such as Rosh of Lunel. In any event, Rama above in siman 48 rules that when a note whose lien was extinguished is redelivered before delivery witnesses, it becomes effective, although Sema explains that case as reborrowing on the same day. But why should same-day reborrowing matter for the question whether the paper has become the transferor's document and therefore requires writing and delivery? Even a sale deed is normally delivered the same day the property is acquired, for otherwise it could become antedated, yet Rashba still requires writing and delivery. Therefore, if Rama accepted Rashba's rule, he should have required it there too. We must therefore say that Rama follows Tosafot and Rosh that a document written in the name of the actual creditor or purchaser is acquired by delivery alone. Indeed, the special Rashba rule requiring writing and delivery in such a case is not found codified by the other poskim or by Shulchan Aruch. If it were accepted practical halachah, it is difficult to understand why it was omitted. There is an even broader position in Baal HaMaor: if a note was written for one Yosef ben Shimon and sold to another person with the identical name, writing and delivery are unnecessary because the recipient's name matches the name in the document. Ramban in Milchamot disagrees, but only because the debtor never undertook an obligation to the second Yosef ben Shimon, and he asks by what mechanism the actual debt lien transferred. This implies that where the debtor and creditor are the same parties named in the original instrument, even Ramban agrees that the paper itself does not require writing and delivery merely because it had temporarily become the transferor's document. It is true that this analysis weakens one proof from the Gemara that agav works like writing and delivery for all documentary rights, because perhaps the Gemara's statement "agav is different" referred only to the special seller-to-purchaser deed where mere delivery ordinarily suffices, as Tosafot and Rosh say. Rama below in siman 66, seif 10, nevertheless rules in accordance with the authorities that agav transfers all kinds of documentary rights. I discuss that difficulty there. There is still another route to defend Rama even if we accept Rashba's basic distinction. Rashba holds that delivery witnesses do not effect monetary documents in the manner of R. Elazar, as he states in his chiddushim to the first chapter of Gittin. But according to our halachah, delivery witnesses do effect monetary documents. If the debtor now hands this note to the creditor before delivery witnesses, remove the original signatures entirely. Through this very delivery the note becomes an operative document in the creditor's hands. At the moment of delivery it is transformed into the recipient's document and the underlying debt or sale takes effect. What role would a separate writing of transfer play? The delivery itself both creates the document and completes the obligation. The Gemara's proof from agav is different because there the document was not delivered before witnesses at all. The seller merely said that acquisition of the land should carry the deed with it. Agav can therefore function as the acquisition mechanism for the paper, but it has nothing to do with R. Elazar's rule of delivery witnesses, because there was no witnessed delivery. But where the note is physically handed over before witnesses, and we rule that delivery witnesses create monetary documents, there is no need for writing and delivery. The delivery itself makes it the recipient's operative instrument. This also explains Rosh's Nedarim discussion. There he was commenting according to R. Yehudah and Rav, under a framework where delivery witnesses do not necessarily create monetary documents in the way we rule. That discussion need not control our practical halachah. There is, however, another serious issue. The present ruling says the debtor made a kinyan that if he failed to appear by a certain date, the creditor should receive the note for the full sum, in a manner that avoids asmachta. Above in siman 48 Rama rules that if the lien of a note was extinguished but the debtor makes a new kinyan, the old lien can be renewed and collection may proceed even from encumbered property. If so, why here does the creditor collect only from unencumbered property? What difference is there between an unconditional new kinyan and a kinyan subject to an effective condition? Since the condition is halachically valid and does not suffer from asmachta, the kinyan should itself create a new property lien. The difficulty is even greater according to those who hold that an ordinary kinyan automatically stands for writing. Even if the entire old note were thrown away as worthless paper, the new kinyan should create purchaser-facing liability. One must perhaps answer that this special kinyan is inferior. It does not contain an ordinary "from now" clause, and after the condition occurs the sudar has already returned to its owner. The kinyan is used only to remove the asmachta problem, and the authorities were lenient to treat it as sufficient for that purpose. I explained a similar point above in siman 40 from Responsa Talmidei HaRashba. See also Beit Yosef below in siman 207, where several authorities discuss the special structure of such a kinyan. Since it is not an ordinary full kinyan, it does not independently create a purchaser-facing lien without a valid document. Still, this cannot fully explain Rama's unqualified wording, because many authorities hold that a simple "from now" clause does not remove asmachta and that the entire effectiveness of the procedure rests on the fact that a genuine kinyan was made. For them the kinyan should be fully effective. Rama himself below in siman 207, seif 14, cites the opinion that "from now" alone does not suffice. According to that approach, why should this kinyan not create encumbered-property liability? It is difficult to make the law depend silently on one theory of asmachta. The better answer is the one I developed above. When the ruling says the creditor may collect from unencumbered property, that means only if the debtor admits that he has not paid. The document itself is still antedated as to the renewed obligation, and according to one opinion an antedated document is invalid even against the debtor absent admission. The novelty is only that the creditor has acquired a real claim and the paper is not a mere potsherd. Since the debtor cannot say, "there was never any obligation," the note has some force. But it cannot collect from purchasers because its date predates the renewed liability. This removes the difficulty from the new kinyan. A kinyan may recreate the obligation, but it does not cure the antedated date of the old document. Above in siman 48 the entire discussion concerned same-day reborrowing, where no antedating problem existed. This also resolves Sema and Shach's question why writing and delivery should be needed. Their question applies primarily to a note with no new kinyan. Where there is a valid new kinyan, a separate writing and transfer of the old paper is certainly unnecessary to create the obligation itself. Responsa Rashba expressly says that where the deposited arrangement included a kinyan, writing and delivery are unnecessary. The remaining issue is only the documentary date and purchaser-facing collection. Nevertheless, regarding Rosh's further rule that even the unpaid half cannot be collected from encumbered property because we decree lest the creditor collect the repaid half from purchasers, Shach's objection is strong. Shach asks: if a person borrowed one hundred and repaid fifty without taking a receipt because he trusted the creditor, would we ever say the remaining fifty cannot be collected from encumbered property merely because the creditor technically still holds a note for one hundred? Certainly not. Rosh's case differs only if we say the creditor is actually entitled to collect the repaid fifty from unencumbered property because of the renewed arrangement. Then there is a realistic concern he might improperly use the note for the entire amount against purchasers. Even so, where is the source for imposing such a sweeping rabbinic penalty? With an antedated document, the defect existed at the moment of writing and the instrument itself was wrongful because it could create an earlier lien. Chazal therefore penalized the note. Here the document was perfectly valid when written, and its lien attached lawfully. Only later did the borrower repay part of it and then enter a new arrangement creating possible misuse. Why should that later event retroactively cancel the previously valid lien on the unpaid half? In rabbinic decrees and penalties we do not extend beyond the clear enactment. Rosh's statement in his commentary to Nedarim stands largely alone on this point, as Shach notes. I therefore prefer to interpret Rama, despite the awkward wording, as saying that "nothing is collected except from unencumbered property" refers to the repaid portion that is being revived. The unpaid half remains collectible according to its original valid lien. This is the sounder practical law.
