The shalish is exempt. Responsa Masat Binyamin rules that this applies specifically where the shalish returned the document to the creditor. If he wrongfully returns an unpaid document to the borrower, he is like one who burns a debt instrument and is liable. The Shach cites this. Rama in siman 386 says that a scribe who mistakenly writes one hundred instead of two hundred is exempt and is not comparable to one who burns a document. The distinction is that one who burns an existing document destroys a right already possessed by the creditor and is therefore a direct causative tortfeasor. A scribe who errs never created the additional right in the first place. He merely failed to provide the creditor with the evidence that might have been created, which resembles one who refuses to help another recover a lost object or refuses to testify. Ramban writes that one who sees another intending to give a gift and prevents the gift is not liable, because he did not damage existing property but merely prevented a benefit from arising. The same applies to a scribe who writes one hundred instead of two hundred. A shalish who simply returns the document to the creditor likewise does not himself create the damage; the creditor's later use of the instrument creates it, much as one who hires false witnesses is exempt in human court. But if the shalish himself gives false testimony concerning the terms of the deposit and later admits he lied, he is like witnesses who testified falsely and is liable under the laws of direct causation. Even if his false testimony favored the creditor rather than the borrower, once he himself supplied the operative false evidence and thereby caused the loss, he must pay. The Tumim struggles with these comparisons, but the distinction between destroying an existing right, directly providing false operative evidence, and merely enabling another person's later wrongful act resolves the matter.
