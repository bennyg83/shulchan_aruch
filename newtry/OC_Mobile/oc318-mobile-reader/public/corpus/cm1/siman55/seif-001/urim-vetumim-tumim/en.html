The third party is exempt because this is indirect causation in damages. Shach, citing Masat Binyamin, writes that this exemption applies only if the third party returned the document to the creditor, because the creditor still has to collect through beit din; but if he returned it to the borrower, he is liable. I am astonished even in the case of return to the borrower and am uncertain whether this law is truly unanimous. Rama below, siman 386, seif 2, rules that a scribe who made an error and wrote one hundred instead of two hundred is exempt because the damage is only indirect. This is Nimukei Yosef's ruling in Perek Lo Yachpor regarding a town scribe, who is treated as already warned and nevertheless remains exempt for a writing error because it is only grama. Yet that error creates immediate loss: the creditor can now collect only one hundred. How is that different from returning to the borrower a document worth one hundred? Whether the scribe never wrote the full obligation or another person later returned the document to the borrower, the creditor has lost the same enforceable proof. It is strained to say Nimukei Yosef meant only an error in a receipt. Rama says "document," and Nimukei Yosef plainly discusses a scribe's mistake in a note. Conversely, if we decide that returning a paid note to the creditor is exempt because further collection is still required, then witnesses who knowingly signed a false note for the creditor should also be exempt under dinei de-garmi. Their false signatures do not themselves extract money; the creditor still must collect. What difference is there between witnesses who create a false note and a third party who returns a paid note to the creditor? In both cases the ultimate monetary loss requires later collection. Yet Tur and Mechaber above in siman 46 rule explicitly that witnesses who admit they signed falsely are believed against themselves and must pay under dinei de-garmi. I discussed this there. Sema says the difference is that in the false-witness case the damage is certain, whereas with a returned paid note it is not. I do not understand the distinction. False witnesses sign that the borrower owes one hundred when in truth he paid; a third party returns a note saying the borrower owes one hundred when in truth he paid. In each case the creditor still must collect through the false instrument, and collection may or may not actually occur. There is an additional difficulty from the law of a judge who errs in legal judgment and obligates an innocent party. According to Tosafot and Rosh, because we rule like R. Meir that dinei de-garmi are actionable, even where the legal ruling can theoretically be reversed, and even where the judge did not personally take and transfer the money, he can be liable if the litigant pays based on his erroneous ruling. See Tosafot Bava Kamma, Rosh in Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot, and what I wrote above in siman 25. Yet the damage from the judge's words is still incomplete until the prevailing party actually collects. Why is the judge's ruling different from returning a paid note to the creditor? If anything, Rif and Rambam are more lenient with judges because of the need not to discourage judicial service, yet even that discussion shows how close the cases are. One might distinguish, as Tosafot Bava Kamma 100 do according to the Sages who impose liability for declaring pure food impure but exempt one who burns another's note, by saying that speech can count as a direct act while a document is not the body of the money. But that distinction is made according to those who exempt burning a document altogether. According to R. Meir, whom we follow, burning a document is actionable as garmi. Moreover, where the judge's ruling can be reversed, his speech is not itself a completed physical act and yet Tosafot still impose garmi liability. I therefore see no principled distinction between that and returning a paid note. The better explanation is that the dispute here tracks the major dispute regarding judges. Tosafot and Rosh hold that even where a judgment can be reversed, if the litigant ultimately paid because of the judge's ruling, the judge can be liable under dinei de-garmi. According to them, witnesses who create a false note and a third party who returns a paid note to the creditor should likewise be liable once the borrower actually pays. All are forms of causing an innocent party to be charged through an instrument or ruling. Rashba and Nimukei Yosef, however, generally follow Rif's approach that liability for an erroneous judgment requires the judge to have personally taken and transferred the property. Ramban in Milchamot explains that where the judgment remains reversible, the damage is not sufficiently certain at the time of the erroneous ruling. Even if the litigant later pays based on it, the original act was not certain damage and therefore is merely grama. On that conceptual approach, returning a paid note to the creditor is no worse than an erroneous judgment without personal transfer. At the moment of return the note can still be challenged or beit din can refuse to collect on it. If loss later occurs, the original act was still not certain enough to qualify as garmi. The same is true of the scribe who wrote one hundred instead of two hundred: the witnesses can still correct their mistake, rewrite the proper document, or tear up the defective one. The damage is therefore not yet inevitable. This explains Rashba's exemption and Nimukei Yosef's ruling concerning the scribe. But according to Rosh and those who impose garmi liability even where the original ruling could be reversed, liability should follow here as well. Mechaber itself is not difficult. Above in siman 25, seif 1, he follows Rif that where the judgment can be reversed there is no garmi liability, and here he follows Rashba that the third party is exempt. In siman 46 he nevertheless rules that false-signing witnesses must pay. One can say he does not accept Ramban's reason that reversible harm is merely grama as a universal principle. Instead, with judges he adopts Rif's special policy that the law was relaxed so that people will agree to serve as judges, or because a judge who acts within the judicial role is treated as compelled by the Torah's command. I discussed that at length above. False witnesses receive no such policy protection. The real difficulty is Rama. In siman 25 he brings Rosh's view that even where the judgment can be reversed, the judge can be liable under garmi; in siman 46 he leaves standing the rule that false-signing witnesses are liable; yet here he adopts Rashba's exemption for a third party who returned the note. The positions appear inconsistent. Perhaps Rama intended the present case specifically where the paid note contains no special credibility clause. The borrower can then demand that the creditor swear that the debt was not paid. We presume Jews do not swear falsely. Therefore, when the third party returns the note, no certain damage exists at all: the creditor should be unable to collect unless he truthfully swears. If the borrower later chooses to pay anyway, the earlier return was only remote causation. This could distinguish the case from a false judgment or from a false note containing effective credibility. Rashba's responsum, however, states the exemption even where the document was delivered to a gentile creditor, where the oath protection may not exist. Perhaps Rama deliberately omitted that extension and intended to adopt Rashba only in the ordinary Jewish-creditor case lacking a credibility clause. Even so, the matter requires great study. The authorities' positions concerning judges, scribes, false witnesses, and third parties returning paid documents do not align simply, and Rama's formulations are particularly difficult to reconcile.
