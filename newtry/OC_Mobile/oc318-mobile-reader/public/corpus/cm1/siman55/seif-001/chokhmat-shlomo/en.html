Seif 1. One who paid part of his debt and deposited the document with a third party, etc. I was asked about a case in which the document was deposited under a valid arrangement that does not constitute asmachta: if the debtor does not pay a specified amount by a fixed date, he will become obligated in a larger sum. The date passed, but the lender did not demand payment on that day. Does the failure to demand payment count as cancellation of the condition, so that the debtor does not become liable for the larger amount? In my opinion it does not cancel the condition. In siman 73, seif 6, the Sema and Shach explain that even one who swore to pay on a given day is not obligated to pay until the creditor demands payment. If that is true of an oath, it is certainly true of an ordinary condition. The Taz, who disagrees there, does so only because an oath creates an independent prohibition requiring the person to fulfill what he swore even without a demand, like any oath to perform an act. Without an oath, even the Taz agrees that payment need not be made until demanded. I found the same principle in Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer 143, seif katan 14, that a condition is not treated as violated until demand is made. I also brought a proof in my responsa to Choshen Mishpat, third edition, siman 243. Even with respect to an oath, the primary ruling appears to follow the Sema and Shach. The Taz’s proof from the requirement that a document say “when he demands payment” is not decisive. There the issue is whether the debtor must pay the second claimant rather than the first. If the first oath expressly said “when he demands payment,” then while the first creditor has not demanded, paying him would not fulfill that oath, so the second oath may obligate payment to the second creditor. But if the first oath did not contain that phrase, although the debtor cannot be compelled to pay without demand, if he nevertheless pays voluntarily he has fulfilled his oath. Consequently one cannot compel him to pay the second creditor, because he can still pay the first and fulfill the oath. There is no basis to prefer one oath over the other. Moreover, ordinary vows are interpreted stringently as to their obligation but leniently as to ambiguous wording. If a person swore simply to pay, we cannot compel him without demand, for he may say he intended payment when demanded. Nevertheless, if he voluntarily pays, he has surely fulfilled the oath. There is therefore no contradiction. The principal point is that one who swears to pay generally undertakes no more than the obligation imposed by law. Just as one who swore to pay a debt is exempt if shemittah intervenes, because he did not undertake payment beyond his legal liability, so too one who ordinarily need not pay before demand is understood to have sworn only on that basis. The case in Yoreh Deah 238 is different because there he owed two creditors and apparently possessed funds sufficient for only one. Ordinarily the law would not require paying the full amount to either one, but division. By swearing to pay the first creditor in full, he demonstrated an intention to assume an obligation greater than the ordinary legal duty. Once we see that he intended to bind himself beyond the law, it is reasonable to understand that he also intended to pay without demand, unless he expressly limited his oath to payment upon demand. This distinction resolves the sources. Accordingly, even regarding an oath the ruling follows the Sema and Shach, and without an oath all authorities can be understood to agree. Certainly in monetary law, where there is doubt, we should not extract money on the basis of a disputed extension of the condition. In the same seif: provided that he was not prevented by circumstances beyond his control. If the obligor needed to obtain the cooperation of others in order to perform what he undertook, and he reasonably assumed he could persuade them but in fact could not, this is considered ones. That remains true even where, in theory, he could have persuaded them only by paying an extraordinarily large sum. The Gemara in Gittin indicates that having to give enormous sums is itself a form of compulsion. Therefore, wherever ones is legally effective, this too is ones. The same appears in Even HaEzer 50 in Beit Shmuel. There, seif 1, in the gloss: the third party is exempt because this is indirect damage. See what I wrote on Choshen Mishpat, fifth edition, siman 569, in a responsum to the community of Kalik, where I explained when the third party must pay and when he is exempt.
