Ketzot HaChoshen cites the dispute in Yoreh Deah 232 whether one who was prevented only on the final day of a period is considered compelled, and the view of the Agudah that this is not called compulsion. He sought proof from the law of houses in a walled city. In my view that proof does not stand. A house in a walled city is treated in Arakhin as a loan-like transaction, even though Rashba calls it a sale on condition. The correct structure is that if redemption money is given within the year, the sale is undone retroactively and the buyer's use is retrospectively treated like a loan, which is why the Gemara discusses interest. In an ordinary sale or gift conditioned on the transferor's own act, if he is prevented by an unforeseen compulsion from fulfilling the condition, the transfer can fail through the principle that the Torah exempts unavoidable circumstances. This differs from a condition imposed on the recipient, where his failure merely prevents him from acquiring. Nevertheless, the Agudah did not need the walled-city case to establish that final-day compulsion is sometimes insufficient, for Scripture itself requires that the seller actually acquire the means to redeem. If he has no money throughout the period, that is a strong form of inability and yet redemption expires. Still, I do not think the Agudah's rule should be extended from vows to monetary conditions, gittin, or kiddushin. In those fields, if a truly unusual and unforeseeable compulsion arose only on the last day, there is strong basis to say the party never intended the condition to fail through such a circumstance. In vows, by contrast, the Torah expressly released compelled vows. The Agudah can therefore hold that this special release applies only where the person was genuinely unable to perform at all. If he negligently delayed until the last day and only then became unable, he may still need formal release from the vow because the vow itself was not automatically annulled. Thus the Agudah's distinction belongs specifically to vows, which is why Shulchan Aruch brings it there and not in monetary law or gittin. In civil and marital conditions, a final-day unavoidable event can still negate the condition where the circumstances clearly show it was not contemplated.
