"He Who exacted payment," etc. See what I wrote below in siman 207 concerning the laws of asmachta, because that is the proper place for this discussion. See there.
Provided that he was not under unavoidable compulsion. This means an unusual but foreseeable compulsion, such as the melting of snow. But an ordinary and common circumstance, such as the interruption of a river crossing, should have been stipulated against. Since he did not stipulate, he accepted that risk, and it is not considered compulsion, Shach.
We place the third party under a ban, etc. This is where the lender cannot be compelled to return the document because he is absent, or because he denies having received it from the third party. But if the lender is present and admits the matter, we compel the lender rather than the third party, Sema in the name of Rashba.
That he accepts upon himself, etc. The same law applies to every case of indirect damage. Once the damage occurred he is exempt, but initially we compel him to remove the cause of damage, Sema.
Who returned it unlawfully, etc. This means where we know that the document had been paid. But where only a receipt or document was found and the matter is doubtful, we rule that it should not be returned. If he nevertheless returned it, see above in siman 65, seif 1, Sema.
The third party is exempt. This means where he returned the document to the lender, because collection still remains necessary, so the damage is only indirect causation and not actionable causation. But if he returned an unpaid document to the borrower, he is liable under the laws of actionable causation, because this is like burning another person's document, Shach in the name of Responsa Masas Binyamin. See Tumim, where I discussed this at length, because in my opinion it is a dispute among the poskim. According to Rosh, even returning it to the lender may create liability. If he returned it to a gentile, or to a Jew in a place where the document may be collected without an oath, it requires study whether he can be exempted. See there. It appears that Rama deliberately did not write the case of returning it to a gentile. See there.
