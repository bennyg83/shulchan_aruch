The third-party custodian is exempt. See Baer Heitev in the name of Masat Binyamin, who distinguishes that when the custodian merely returned the document to the creditor the resulting loss is indirect, as appears from a responsum of the Rosh. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 140, strongly questions this. If, according to the Rashba, a custodian who returns a document to the creditor causes only indirect damage and is exempt, how can the Gemara in Ketubot 85a, in the case of Avimi, hold an agent liable because 'I sent you to improve matters, not to impair them,' since he should have taken the document before paying? Had he taken the document and then returned it to the creditor, he would supposedly be exempt, yet because he never took it at all he is liable. Chatam Sofer therefore concludes that the laws distinguishing direct from indirect damage apply to an ordinary outsider who damages another, but one who received property as a custodian or agent is liable even for remote consequences of negligent custody or deviation from his agency. Many instances of custodian negligence and misperformance of agency are, upon analysis, only indirect causation, yet the custodian or agent is liable. Thus the Rosh correctly held a third-party custodian liable for returning Shimon's sale document to Shimon's father. Although documents are excluded from ordinary custodian liability, that exemption concerns a document lost through negligence; here he effectively destroyed the legal protection by his own act. The Rashba's case cited by the Rama at the beginning of siman 55 is different. There both borrower and creditor instructed the custodian that if payment were not made by a certain date he should return the document to the creditor. The law nevertheless forbade him to do so because the condition was an asmachta. If he violated the law and returned it, he is exempt because he did not violate the principals' instruction. His fault is therefore only that of an ordinary tortfeasor causing indirect loss. Based on this, Chatam Sofer ruled in a case where Reuven gave his agent a non-Jewish debt claim to collect and expressly warned him that if he could not collect it he was not to transfer it to Reuven's partner Shimon. The agent disobeyed, delivered it to Shimon, Shimon collected the entire debt, and then withheld Reuven's half because of a separate claim. Since the agent violated the principal's instruction, he must compensate Reuven for the loss. Once the agent is liable, Shimon cannot seize Reuven's money in a manner that shifts the loss to the agent; Reuven need only take a hesset oath regarding Shimon's claim. Chatam Sofer concludes that this is the true law. Pitchei Teshuva notes, however, that Netivot HaMishpat below, siman 58, seif katan 2, had already addressed the difficulty from Ketubot and answered well that there the agent changed the manner of payment against the sender's instruction, which constitutes negligence causing monetary loss and therefore creates liability. Also, the Rama's broad wording below in siman 386, seif 3, concerning a third-party custodian who returned a document, implies exemption in all cases. See the Shach there. The matter requires study.
