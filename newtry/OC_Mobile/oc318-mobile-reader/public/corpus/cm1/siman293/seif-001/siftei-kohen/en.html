Only in the place where it was deposited, etc. Abaye explains the baraita at the end of Perek HaGozel Batra to mean that a loan may be demanded anywhere, but a lost item or deposit may be demanded only in its place. A creditor may compel payment wherever the debtor has money or movable property, whereas the owner of a deposit cannot compel substitute payment when the deposited object itself is not there. The Bach explains likewise. The objection that this is obvious because a loan is given for expenditure while a deposit must be returned in kind is not compelling. The baraita teaches that the creditor has the advantage: although the debtor cannot compel the creditor to accept payment in the wilderness, the creditor may compel the debtor to pay there if he has money. It also teaches that even if the custodian has an identical object, ready money, or an identical utensil, the owner cannot compel substitution there. It appears, however, that if the custodian has the very coins deposited, the owner may compel their return even there. Although I wrote above, siman 292, seif 7, that a custodian of money need not return the identical coins before spending them when he has other money, here, if we do not compel him to return these coins, the depositor will receive nothing at that place, so he must return them. The Baal HaTerumot also discusses the debtor's obligation to swear that he cannot pay and the absence of a comparable oath regarding a deposit. That discussion states the practical law after rabbinic oaths were instituted; it is not the explanation of the baraita. Even if the owner claims with certainty that the deposit is present, the custodian would at most incur a heset oath, and in the time of the baraita that oath had not yet been enacted.
