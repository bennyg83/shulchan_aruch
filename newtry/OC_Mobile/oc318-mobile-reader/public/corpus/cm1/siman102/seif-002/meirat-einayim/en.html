What a song. It is written and the man you breathe in will be sent out to you, namely, from what you want to teach him from his own words, from which he gives him some of the curses that he will send to him, and that he will respond to his husbands, and that if he is paid in the land, he will give him some of his hands, and that he will end up with his own words
Give me a little more inferior land. The Gemara explains that land prices may be high in Nisan and low in Tishrei. The creditor can say to the borrower: Had my money been in my hand, I could have purchased land now at the present low price. Since my money is in your possession, if you insist upon reckoning the land according to the higher price expected later, I am entitled to receive a correspondingly larger quantity now. Rashi explains that buyers plow and prepare fields in the summer, and sow in Tishrei and Cheshvan. In Tishrei fields may be cheaper because it is already too late to prepare them sufficiently for that year’s sowing, and they will not be useful until the next season. Tosafot write that a comparable seasonal difference can apply to houses. Houses are expensive during the period when people normally rent them, and after most tenants have already rented, prices decline because fewer renters remain.
For they do not compel him, etc. The reason for this opinion is that the creditor’s right to these inferior fields is no stronger than it would be if they were intermediate land. If they were intermediate land, the debtor could choose which of his intermediate parcels to give. So too here the debtor may say: I do not wish to give you a larger amount of that inferior parcel because it is particularly desirable to me; instead I will give you a somewhat smaller amount of intermediate land. Only when the debtor has revealed that he is willing to give the creditor the inferior land, but wishes to value it at the higher future price, does the Gemara say that the creditor can answer, “Had my money been in my hand,” as explained above. The Ir Shushan confused these points and did not clearly state that according to the dissenting opinion the creditor is not heard unless the debtor wishes to give the inferior land at the higher future valuation. I do not know why, since the Rosh and Tur cite the first view and then reject it for the reason I have given. At minimum, the rule should be that the burden of proof rests upon the party seeking to extract property.
