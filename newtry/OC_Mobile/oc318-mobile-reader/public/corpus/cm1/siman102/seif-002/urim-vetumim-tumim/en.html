Give me a little more inferior land, etc. This comes from Bava Kamma 7b. The Gemara concludes that if an injured party asks for somewhat more intermediate land, or a creditor asks for somewhat more inferior land, we listen, because otherwise the injured party's position is weakened and the door is closed before borrowers. The Rif and Rambam omit this entirely, both for tort claims and debts, and this requires explanation. Even if they agree with the Rosh that the rule applies only where the tortfeasor or debtor first disclosed willingness to give the property at its future higher value, they should have stated that distinction. The Rosh asks that even if the debtor has two parcels of superior land, the tortfeasor ordinarily chooses which one to give. How then can the injured party compel him to give a particular intermediate parcel? Maharshal answers that the injured party indeed may choose among superior parcels, because if he specifically values one parcel it is effectively superior relative to the others. But Tosafot in Gittin 50 indicate that according to the view that grades are assessed against the world there is no concept of superior of the superior for this purpose. The Rif and Rambam rule that appraisal follows the world. Therefore the Gemara's rule about an injured party demanding more intermediate land may belong to the contrary view, and its omission is understandable. The omission regarding an ordinary creditor still needs explanation. There the Rosh's question remains, since a debtor can normally choose among intermediate parcels. The answer appears from the difference between Ulla's reason for giving a creditor intermediate land and the alternative reason in Gittin. According to Ulla, Torah law gives a creditor inferior land and the Sages raised him to intermediate land to avoid closing the door before borrowers. Under that theory the creditor has no right to a specific better parcel. According to the Gittin explanation, however, he should really have received superior land, but the Sages reduced him to intermediate so that lenders not target attractive property. On that approach, a creditor who asks for a particular intermediate parcel can say that the Sages curtailed his right only as necessary, and the debtor cannot freely divert him elsewhere. This distinction also fits the verse that the debtor brings out the pledged property. According to Ulla, that verse gives the debtor the choice. According to the alternate derivation, that verse is not the source of the creditor's land grade and does not necessarily grant that same choice. The Gemara's discussion allowing the demand for more inferior land is stated in the name of Abaye. Tosafot indicate elsewhere that Abaye does not hold the lien to be by Torah law in the same manner as Rava, while Rava follows Ulla. Thus Abaye's discussion can fit the alternate basis, while Rava, whom we follow, can maintain Ulla's rule that the debtor may choose the parcel. This also explains Rava's statement that a later purchaser of inferior land may say that he deliberately bought land unsuitable for the creditor. If the creditor could always say, "Give me extra inferior land," the land would not be unsuitable. The Rif and Rambam therefore correctly omit Abaye's rule and instead codify Rava's case. The Baal HaTerumot's organization supports this. At the beginning of Shaar 4 he first cites the reason from Gittin that the Sages did not give a creditor superior land lest he target attractive property, and immediately derives that if the creditor asks for somewhat more inferior land we listen. That sequence shows that the two rules depend upon one another. Later, where he discusses successive purchasers, he needs a distinct Geonic rationale allowing the second purchaser to say that he left the creditor a place to collect, rather than simply relying on the first purchaser's power to shift him among parcels. This is consistent with the view that under that approach the debtor cannot always divert the creditor from one intermediate parcel to another.
And some say that we do not compel, etc. This is the Rosh's opinion. The Yam Shel Shlomo cites glosses to the Rosh saying that Tosafot, Or Zarua, and Mahari Chalfon do not distinguish as the Rosh does, indicating disagreement. In truth there is no need for that gloss, because Tosafot and many others explicitly explain the passage concerning a purchaser of inferior land by saying that the creditor could otherwise demand somewhat more inferior land. Rashba, Nimukei Yosef, Ritva, and Ran say the same. This directly conflicts with the Rosh's requirement of prior disclosure. The Rosh's position faces a strong question. If a purchaser retained intermediate land, he could ordinarily say that the creditor should collect there and that the inferior parcel bought by the later purchaser is land unsuitable for him. If the creditor cannot demand extra inferior land without a prior disclosure, how does the Gemara's reasoning work? One could force the case into a situation where the first purchaser had offered the inferior land at its future price, but that is strained and would also undermine the ordinary rule that the first purchaser can transfer his defenses to the second purchaser. The Rosh may instead read the earlier case as one where the first purchaser no longer had intermediate land when collection occurred. When the second purchaser originally bought the inferior parcel, the first purchaser still owned intermediate land, but it later deteriorated and became inferior. The Rosh can hold that a debtor may shift a creditor from one intermediate parcel to another, but not necessarily from one inferior parcel to another when the creditor says that this particular tract is worth intermediate to him. The second purchaser can nevertheless answer that he originally bought land that was then unsuitable for the creditor because intermediate land remained elsewhere, and the later deterioration is the creditor's misfortune. This preserves the Rosh's reading. The many authorities who reject the Rosh must answer his question, namely why a creditor can force the debtor to give a particular inferior tract when among intermediate tracts the debtor has the choice. The distinction is that owners commonly value and retain particular ordinary fields, so even a cash buyer cannot assume that every intermediate parcel is available. There is therefore no closing of the door if the debtor chooses one intermediate field rather than another. Inferior land, however, is ordinarily marketable and people do not cling to a barren tract. If the debtor refuses to give it merely because the creditor now asks for it, a lender can fairly say that with cash he could have bought such inferior land almost anywhere. That would indeed discourage lending. The same logic does not apply to a tort claimant, which is why Maharshal needed his separate explanation regarding superior land. Now. Mordechai, in the chapter Shnei Dayanei Gezerot, cites Riva's question: why is the concern about closing the door necessary? A creditor should simply say that he declines the rabbinic benefit of intermediate land and reverts to Torah law, under which he receives inferior land. Maharam answers that the principle allowing one to decline a rabbinic enactment is not used to extract property. The Ran combines this reason with the concern about access to credit. At first glance the question itself is puzzling. Even if the creditor declines the enactment and reverts to Torah law, Torah law merely permits payment from inferior land. It does not normally let the creditor insist upon one particular inferior parcel if the debtor wants to give intermediate or superior land. The verse describes the debtor as bringing out what he wishes. Thus the closing of the door rationale is still needed to let the creditor choose this specific tract. Riaz, however, resolves the issue. He writes that if the injured party asks for intermediate or inferior land, or the creditor asks for inferior land, because that field is adjacent to his own, he may demand it. He evidently reads the Gemara as a case of adjoining property, where the law against conduct resembling Sodom permits the claimant to insist on a contiguous parcel. On that reading Riva's question is exact: once the creditor declines the rabbinic upgrade and returns to his Torah entitlement to inferior land, the adjoining parcel should be assigned to him. Thus Riva and Riaz likely share the same understanding. Although Maharshal therefore ruled against the Rosh because Tosafot, Rashba, Ritva, Ran, Nimukei Yosef, Mahari Chalfon, and Or Zarua disagree, practical law is less simple. The Rif and Rambam omitted the rule altogether, and I have explained a reason for their omission. Riva and Riaz also limit the creditor's power to the special adjoining case. Since the debtor is in possession, he can therefore say kim li in accordance with the Rif, Rambam, Rosh, Riva, and Riaz and refuse the inferior land unless there was the disclosure required by the Rosh.
Unless he wishes to give it to him. Meaning at the future higher value. This is the Rosh's resolution of his question that even if both parcels are intermediate, the debtor can normally choose which to give. The Rosh answers that the case is one where the debtor has already disclosed that he does not care about retaining the particular land and is willing to give it, but only at the future price. At that point refusing today's value creates the concern of closing the door before borrowers. It follows that the same disclosure matters among two intermediate parcels, and likewise between two superior parcels in a tort case. Otherwise the Rosh's original question would remain. This supports the Tur's ruling below in siman 419, adopted by the Mechaber, that although a tortfeasor may normally shift the injured party from one superior parcel to another, he cannot do so after disclosing a willingness to give the requested field at the future price. Ir Shushan questioned this, but the point is clear, as the Sema explains. The difficulty is that here the Mechaber does not follow the Rosh, but the authorities who hold that such prior disclosure is unnecessary. According to those authorities, disclosure makes no difference at all, because they answer the Rosh's question through other distinctions between choosing among equal grades and demanding a lower grade. How, then, can the Mechaber in siman 419 follow the Tur and make disclosure decisive between two superior parcels? This requires study.
