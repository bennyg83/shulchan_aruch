From whichever he wishes. If the debtor owns only inferior land, the creditor cannot force him to give whichever inferior field the creditor chooses. Rather, the debtor may give whichever inferior field he chooses. One should not compare this with the Shach's ruling in siman 119, seif katan 6, regarding a person who suffered damage, or one who stipulated for superior land, that if the debtor has only intermediate land the creditor may choose whichever intermediate field he wishes. There, even if the chosen field were superior to the other intermediate fields, the claimant would still be entitled to collect from it because his legal entitlement is to superior land. Here, by contrast, if the debtor has only inferior land, then even if the particular field were better than the others, the creditor would still ordinarily collect the worse field under our rule that land is graded relative to the debtor's holdings, and when he owns only intermediate and inferior land the creditor collects from the inferior. If the debtor owns superior and inferior land, one might initially think that the creditor can compel him to give whichever inferior field the creditor chooses, because even if that field were better it would still be within the class from which the creditor collects. Likewise, if he owns superior and intermediate land, one might initially think that the creditor can compel him to give whichever intermediate field he wants, since even if one intermediate field were actually better than the other, the debtor would still have to pay from intermediate land. This might have provided an explanation for Tosafot in Gittin, who infer from Rava's words that he grades land relative to the world. Their inference is difficult because it is not obvious where Rava indicates this. According to the initial approach, Tosafot's proof would be from Rava's statement that where the first purchaser leaves equivalent intermediate land with the debtor, the creditor cannot collect from the second purchaser because the first purchaser can say that he left equivalent property. Tosafot explain this by the principle that the first purchaser transferred to the second all rights he possessed. This appears to apply even where superior land was also left with the debtor. Tosafot therefore infer that Rava grades land relative to the world. If he graded relative to the debtor's holdings, the creditor could have forced the debtor to give those particular intermediate fields while they were still in the debtor's possession, whereas under world grading he could not have done so because even if those intermediate fields were better he was only entitled to ordinary intermediate land. Upon further consideration, however, this reasoning is incorrect. According to the rule that grading follows the debtor's own holdings, even where he owns intermediate and inferior land, the creditor cannot compel him to give whichever inferior field the creditor prefers. Since the grading is relative to the debtor's holdings, the inferior land is itself called intermediate for this purpose. The court appraises it as intermediate, and once the debtor provides the class to which the creditor is legally entitled, the creditor cannot compel him to give more than his entitlement. This differs from the Shach's case in siman 119, where the debtor originally had superior land and sold it. There, his remaining intermediate land is classified as intermediate, while the claimant's right is to superior land. Since the debtor is not giving the claimant the class to which he is entitled, the claimant can force a choice. Here, by contrast, the creditor seeks more than his legal entitlement. A proof is that even if the field he wanted were worse than the one offered, according to the Rosh and Rama the creditor could not force the debtor to give it absent a prior indication of preference. If he cannot force the debtor to give a worse field merely because he personally prefers it, certainly he cannot force him to give an equally valued or better one. So long as the debtor gives him his legal entitlement according to beit din's appraisal, the creditor has no power to say, “This field is better for me.” The matter depends on beit din, not on his subjective preference. A further proof is from Ketubot 110. The law that each creditor simply collects from the other and each ends where he began cannot be limited to cases where both hold identical grades. Even if Reuven owns superior and intermediate land and Shimon owns intermediate land, according to the view that grading follows the debtor's own holdings, the Gemara still does not create a case in which reciprocal collection changes the result. If a creditor could force his debtor to give whichever intermediate field he chooses, Shimon could insist specifically on Reuven's intermediate field because it adjoins his own, and by sequencing the collections could manipulate the outcome. Yet siman 75 states explicitly that each party remains with his own property. It follows that even where a debtor has superior and intermediate land, the creditor cannot force him to give a particular intermediate field, provided the debtor gives him his legal entitlement according to beit din.
