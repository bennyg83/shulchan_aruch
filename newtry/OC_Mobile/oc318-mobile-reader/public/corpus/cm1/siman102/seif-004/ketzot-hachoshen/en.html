Ulla derives from the verse, "the man to whom you lend shall bring the pledge out to you," that Torah law gives a creditor inferior land. Another opinion rejects Ulla. Rashi understands that view as holding that even rabbinically a creditor may collect only inferior land. Nimukei Yosef, citing Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia, explains the dispute in a case where the debtor originally had superior land and later sold it. According to Ulla, since Torah law is inferior land, we look to the time of collection. Land that is now medium relative to the remaining estate can be treated accordingly. The opposing view follows Rabbi Shimon, who holds that Torah law gives superior land and the Sages lowered it to medium land so that people would still lend. Under that view the classification follows the time of the loan. The Jerusalem Talmud has an opinion deriving medium land directly from the same verse. It reads "the man" as the beit din officer. If the creditor himself entered, he would naturally take superior land; if the borrower chose, he would offer inferior land. The court officer therefore takes medium land. Our Bavli, however, follows Shmuel that even a court officer may not enter the debtor's house and must wait outside. The borrower himself brings out the pledge. Under that reading the verse cannot support the Jerusalem Talmud's derivation of medium land, and Ulla's derivation of inferior land is more natural. This also answers the attempt to prove from Rif and Rosh's language, "Why did the Torah say medium land?" that they hold medium land is biblical. Talmudic language frequently calls rabbinic law "Torah" in a loose sense. Rashba himself paraphrases the rule as "Why did they say that a creditor collects medium land," because once the Sages changed the Torah baseline from inferior land to medium land, the question is why they stopped there instead of giving superior land. Thus the accepted law remains that Torah law is inferior land and the Sages instituted medium land.
