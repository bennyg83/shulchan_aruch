He had superior land when he borrowed and later sold it. Since the debtor had superior land at the time of the loan, the creditor's lien already attached to the intermediate land under its halachic classification. Specifically in this case, because the field already bore the status of intermediate land when the loan was made, that status does not disappear merely because the superior land was later sold. But if at the time of the loan the debtor owned only intermediate land, so the lien attached to it, and afterward he acquired inferior land, the creditor collects from the inferior. The reason is that at the time of the loan the original field did not yet bear the halachic designation “intermediate land” from which the Sages ordained collection, because under the rule that grading follows the debtor's own holdings it was then his superior land. Once he later acquires property that fits the rabbinically assigned class, namely intermediate land relative to his holdings, the creditor collects accordingly. A proof is from Ketubot 109, which discusses one party with intermediate land and the other with inferior land and states that if the inferior land is collected first it may then be collected back by the other party according to the view that grading follows the debtor's own holdings. Why should this be so if at the time of the loan the debtor owned only intermediate land? It necessarily follows as I have explained. If the debtor originally had superior land and it was later destroyed, it likewise appears that the creditor then collects from the inferior land. Only where the superior land was sold do we say that the debtor cannot intentionally impair the creditor's earlier lien. Moreover, even after sale the superior land remains subject to the lien, because if the other assets are destroyed the creditor may return to the superior land sold to purchasers. That reasoning does not apply where the superior land itself was destroyed.
