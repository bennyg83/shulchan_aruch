That appraisal is according to his own property. The Rambam in chapter 19 of Hilchot Malveh rules that if a debtor owns intermediate and inferior land, the creditor collects from the intermediate land. From this the Maggid Mishneh and Ran inferred that the Rambam holds that land grades are assessed relative to the world generally, for if they were assessed only relative to the debtor's holdings, the intermediate land would itself be treated as superior and the creditor would receive only the inferior land. Yet in chapter 24, regarding two parties who each hold a note against the other, the Rambam writes that if one owns superior and intermediate land while the other owns inferior land, one collects inferior and the other intermediate. This seems to show that he assesses according to the debtor's own holdings. If grades were assessed according to the world, the owner of the intermediate land should collect it first and the other should then collect the same intermediate land from him. The Gemara at the end of Ketubot explicitly ties Rabbi Sheshet's view that reciprocal claims are simply offset to appraisal according to the world. The Kesef Mishneh therefore understands the Rambam to follow the majority view that appraisal is relative to the debtor's own property. His ruling in chapter 19, where intermediate and inferior land exist and the creditor receives the intermediate, must concern one who originally had superior land and sold it. In such a case everyone agrees that the intermediate land remains the creditor's grade. The Giddulei Terumah calls this strained because the Rambam should have stated that the superior land had been sold. I would defend the Kesef Mishneh from the wording of the baraitot in Bava Kamma 8a. One baraita says that if the debtor has intermediate and inferior land the creditor takes intermediate, while another says inferior. One reading of the Gemara resolves them by saying that his intermediate land is equal to the world's superior land and that the dispute is whether appraisal follows his property or the world's. Rashi explains that he had superior land and sold it. The reason this additional fact is necessary is that if the debtor never possessed superior land and appraisal is only according to his own holdings, who ever gave the remaining parcels the names intermediate and inferior? Relative to him they are superior and inferior. The fact that the baraita calls one parcel intermediate indicates that the designation had already attached when he possessed superior land. Thus the Rambam's bare phrase "he has intermediate and inferior land" can itself imply that he had superior land and sold it. This gives a reasonable defense of the Kesef Mishneh. The Maggid Mishneh, however, holds that the Rambam assesses according to the world. He explains the chapter 24 case by saying that the owner of the intermediate land collects first and therefore receives inferior land, after which the other collects intermediate. Rabbi Sheshet disagrees about which claimant proceeds first. At first glance this is difficult, because the Gemara itself rejects an explanation based merely on who sued first, saying that ultimately both come to collect together. The Ran and Kesef Mishneh raise this objection. The Maggid Mishneh therefore suggests that the Rambam may have had a different text. A defense is possible. In the Gemara's initial approach, where appraisal is relative to each debtor, if the owner of inferior land comes first to collect intermediate land, beit din should tell him to wait until the other first collects inferior land from him. His haste is an obvious device to obtain intermediate land and later pay inferior land. Beit din need not assist such manipulation. But if appraisal is according to the world and the owner of the intermediate claim comes first, he knows that by proceeding first he will receive inferior land, whereas by waiting he could preserve his own intermediate land. If he nevertheless insists on immediate collection, perhaps he has some concern that the other will preempt him. Beit din need not protect him from the consequence of his own choice. Thus he collects inferior land and the second party later collects intermediate. Rabbi Sheshet may instead follow the principle discussed above in siman 85 that where each has lent to the other, the second document functions merely as a receipt rather than an independent claim. Each then remains with what he already has. This can explain the disagreement without contradiction. Another explanation of the Maggid Mishneh is that because both claims ultimately arrive before beit din together, there is uncertainty about which collection should be effected first. By Torah law a creditor takes inferior land, while intermediate land is a rabbinic enactment. A general rule is that uncertainty within an enactment is resolved by reverting to Torah law. Thus the first claimant whose collection is uncertain receives inferior land. The second claimant, whose entitlement is certain in either sequence, receives intermediate land under the enactment. This is conceptually sound. Still, the Gemara's wording that the dispute concerns whether appraisal is relative to one's own property remains difficult. The Rambam may be reconciled differently. Ketubot 109 states the argument, "If you remain silent, fine; if not, I will return the deed to its original owner." The Rosh and other authorities rule that this argument works even to diminish the claimant's rights, and the other party cannot say, "When you actually return it, then I will respond." Ramban likewise explains that this means making a new transfer, not undoing the prior one. It follows that "to its owner" is not literal. The deed could equally be sold to another person. One might ask why, if a debtor owns intermediate and inferior land and the creditor seeks the intermediate, the debtor cannot say, "Take the inferior land, or I will sell the intermediate land to another and leave you to pursue it there." The answer is either that once the creditor's lien has attached to the intermediate land this device cannot be used merely to uproot the lien, or, more fundamentally, that even if the land were sold the creditor would by strict law still collect it. The Sages only enacted that one does not collect from encumbered property when free property remains. They did not enact that fictional return or transfer should defeat the very rabbinic right to intermediate land, particularly where no concern of tikkun olam is served. In the reciprocal debt case, however, the owner of the inferior land can say to the other, "If you remain silent, fine; if not, I will sell my note against you to a third party with the required written assignment." It is then as though the note were already transferred. That third party would collect intermediate land from you. Even if you first collect inferior land from me, the intermediate land later collected from you would belong to the third party, and you could not recover it from him. Thus one side takes inferior and the other intermediate, even though grades are assessed according to the world. Why, then, did the Gemara itself not say that Rabbi Nachman and Rabbi Sheshet dispute this principle rather than the method of appraisal? Because the Gemara elsewhere indicates that Rabbi Sheshet does not accept the "if you remain silent" argument where it diminishes the other party's position. It would be strained to make that collateral rule the central dispute here when the Mishnah's structure naturally points to the appraisal issue. Moreover, in the Gemara's initial reading the Mishnah was thought to teach precisely the rule of appraisal according to one's own property. But at the final interpretation, where the loans are for ten years and mature on the same day, one can say that Rabbi Nachman also accepts appraisal according to the world, yet reciprocal collection remains because of the "if you remain silent" argument. The Rambam can therefore be coherent. A further resolution may be drawn from the law of property classified as ra'ui. Most authorities hold, as discussed above in siman 111 and below in siman 104, that a creditor does not have the same lien on property that comes to the debtor only later, and Mahari Weil calls even such a receivable during the debtor's lifetime ra'ui. Thus the intermediate land that the owner of inferior land will later collect may have only a weaker lien in favor of the first creditor. If the first debtor already owns other property, his creditor's primary lien is not on this later acquired intermediate parcel. It is therefore reasonable that the owner of the intermediate claim first receives inferior land, even if the other later acquires intermediate land. This is especially so where beit din is uncertain which collection should be done first. This explanation, however, works according to our conclusion in Bava Batra 125b that a loan is ra'ui whether ultimately collected in land or money. Rabbi Nachman there, according to Rabbenu Tam's reading in Tosafot, treats such a debt as presently possessed, and a firstborn may take a double portion. For Rabbi Nachman himself the later acquired intermediate land would therefore be fully subject to the creditor's lien, and the Gemara's objection that both collections arrive together is understandable. Our practical law, which does not follow Rabbi Nachman on that point, can nevertheless follow the Rambam while assessing according to the world.
However. All these explanations leave another question. Why does the Rambam formulate the case as one party having superior and intermediate land and the other inferior land? He could simply have said one has intermediate and the other inferior, and still ruled that one collects intermediate and the other inferior. According to the Maggid Mishneh's explanation that the owner of the intermediate land collects first, the superior land appears entirely unnecessary. The Lechem Mishneh already noted this. The most satisfactory explanation is based on the conclusion in Bava Kamma regarding the contradictory baraitot. The Gemara says they dispute Ulla's enactment. The Rif explains that the baraita giving a creditor intermediate land when the debtor owns intermediate and inferior land follows Ulla's enactment, while the other baraita, which gives him inferior land, rejects it. From this the Rif and Rambam rule that appraisal is according to the world, because we rule like Ulla. Yet in monetary matters we also rule like Rabbi Nachman, who in the reciprocal debt passage appears to assess according to one's own holdings. The Rambam can reconcile the rulings by observing that even the baraita rejecting Ulla, and therefore giving inferior land where the debtor owns only intermediate and inferior, agrees that if the debtor also owns superior land the creditor receives intermediate. Without Ulla, the reason for intermediate collection need not be the familiar concern that borrowers will be unable to obtain loans. It may instead be that payment from inferior land is not full equivalent payment, because a note collectible only from inferior land is worth less than its face value. Where the debtor owns all three grades, intermediate land is therefore appropriate even without Ulla's enactment. But where he owns only intermediate and inferior land, the alternate baraita did not extend the rule so far. In reciprocal lending there is no ordinary concern of closing the door before borrowers. The second creditor chose to lend to someone who was already his creditor rather than simply paying his own debt and cancelling or obtaining a receipt. If he did so precisely to improve the grade he would later collect, that is no reason for a special enactment. The situation is also uncommon. Therefore Ulla's enactment does not govern, and we fall back on the alternate rule. The party who owns superior and intermediate land must pay intermediate because even without Ulla that grade remains proper, while the party who owns only intermediate and inferior pays inferior. This precisely explains why the Rambam specified superior and intermediate land on one side. On this reading, in this reciprocal debt case appraisal effectively follows the debtor's own holdings, yet the general law still follows appraisal according to the world. One could have suggested that the disagreement about Ulla depends on whether liens are by Torah law. If they are, there is greater reason to establish a uniform world based intermediate grade. If liens are only rabbinic, one might limit the enactment and assess only the debtor's property. This might also affect after acquired property, which under the view that one cannot convey something not yet in existence is encumbered only by enactment. But this would revive the Lechem Mishneh's question why the Rambam needed to mention superior land. I therefore return to the prior explanation as the correct and conceptually sound one. Tosafot in Gittin 50 also infer from Rava's statement in Bava Kamma concerning Reuven who sold fields to Shimon that Rava assesses according to the world. This appears difficult because we rule like both Rava and Rabbi Nachman. The preceding explanation reconciles them. Why Tosafot saw this implication in Rava can also be understood from the phrase that the buyer purchased "land unsuitable for you." Even if only superior land remained with the first purchaser while inferior land was sold, the sold inferior land can be called unsuitable because a creditor would naturally prefer to collect from superior property rather than an undesirable inferior tract. If grades were assessed only relative to that owner, however, the supposedly inferior tract might in fact equal the world's intermediate land. It could no longer be described categorically as undesirable.
Therefore. The simplest reading is that Tosafot adopt the Ritva's explanation: inferior land is called unsuitable because no sensible creditor would choose a barren inferior tract when he can collect intermediate land. That description makes sense only if appraisal is according to the world. If appraisal were according to the debtor's own property, his inferior tract might be perfectly ordinary intermediate land by general market standards. The Gemara could not simply say it is unsuitable. This supports the world based appraisal, while the reciprocal debt case is reconciled as explained above.
