He had superior land, etc., and stipulated, etc. Yam Shel Shlomo objects that if the superior land still exists, the debtor must fulfill his condition. The Shach answers that the Tur's rule is explicit in Bava Metzia 66b. A seller promised that if the purchased field were seized he would compensate the buyer from the very best of his property. The court said that the absolute best would not be given, only superior land. A flood then destroyed the very best land, and Rav Chisda ruled that what had previously been merely superior now stood in the place of the very best. One might argue that the case included an express exclusion, "I will not pay you from the very best," but the ordinary wording of the Gemara does not suggest that this is essential. The Taz nevertheless says that the distinction is not strained. If the debtor expressly disclosed that he refuses the very best parcel, then when that parcel disappears the remaining superior land can be treated differently. But if he simply promised superior land, perhaps his intent was straightforwardly to give superior land, and an unexpressed motive involving a still better parcel is merely an intention of the heart. Still, this is not enough to reject the Tur. In his abridged rulings he states the rule simply where the debtor stipulated superior land and possessed an even better grade, without recording any express exclusion. This shows that he considered the exclusion immaterial. Why then did the Rif and Rambam omit the law? The Ritva already wondered about the Rif. The answer appears in Shitah Mekubetzet and Ritva in the name of Rabbenu Pinchas. The case is not that the very best land vanished completely. Rather, it deteriorated until it became extremely inferior. The former superior land thereby became the new very best, the former intermediate became superior, and the former inferior became intermediate. The claimant who was promised superior land therefore receives what is now intermediate, because the condition is measured by the debtor's current grades. This explanation assumes that appraisal is according to the debtor's own property. According to the view that grades are measured against the world, the former superior land remains superior in objective quality and the rule does not follow. The Rif and Rambam, who assess against the world, therefore omitted the case. The Ritva also accepts this explanation. One might object from Rashi in Gittin, who says that when superior land is destroyed the creditor collects intermediate even though according to appraisal relative to the debtor the intermediate has now become superior. The distinction is that an ordinary creditor receives intermediate by rabbinic enactment to prevent discouraging loans. Once the intermediate grade was fixed when the lien arose, that enactment does not disappear merely because the debtor's superior land later vanished. Otherwise the very concern behind the enactment would return. A contractual condition for superior land is different. It depends on the terms of the condition at the time of actual collection, since no lending policy requires freezing the original grade. Thus current relative grading can govern the condition. The rule as stated by the Tur therefore requires study in light of the Rif and Rambam, whose interpretation has strong support. Yam Shel Shlomo also cites a variant text in the Tur: if the debtor promised payment from the very best and that very best was destroyed, the creditor cannot take ordinary superior land but receives intermediate like other creditors. He objects that this follows Rashi in Gittin, while Tosafot and the Rosh reject Rashi because it would weaken the injured party's right to superior land. But that objection is not decisive. Tosafot's criticism applies to a tort claimant whose superior grade is a Torah entitlement. Destruction of one field does not erase his right to another superior field. An ordinary creditor who seeks superior land only by condition is different. If the precise grade contemplated by the condition disappeared, the condition need not extend to a different parcel, especially where he never stipulated regarding the remaining superior field. Even Tosafot do not necessarily dispute Rashi's application to a creditor. Thus where the contractual grade disappears, the creditor returns to his ordinary intermediate entitlement. The law is sound.
