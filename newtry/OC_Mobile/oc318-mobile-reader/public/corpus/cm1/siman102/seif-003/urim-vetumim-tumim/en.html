Give me superior land, etc. The Tur records three opinions. The Raavad holds that if a creditor asks for a smaller amount of superior land, the debtor cannot be compelled to give it even at the future higher valuation. This must mean even where the debtor previously disclosed willingness to give the superior land at that future price. Without such disclosure there is no possible dispute, since the Mishnah plainly gives an ordinary creditor intermediate land and no one can force the debtor to give superior land merely by offering a favorable valuation. Thus the Raavad regards the earlier statement as nonbinding words. The Beit Yosef likewise understands the dispute this way. A second opinion says that after such disclosure the debtor must give the superior land at today's lower price. A third says he must give it only at the future higher price, and the Tur adopts the third view. Several authorities wondered how the Tur could rule so simply against his father the Rosh, whom they understood as requiring today's price even for superior land. But a careful reading of the Rosh removes the difficulty. The Rosh writes that if a creditor asks for somewhat more inferior land according to one textual reading, or somewhat less superior land according to another reading, then because otherwise the door would be closed before borrowers he receives it at today's price. The words "according to one reading" and "according to the other reading" are crucial. The Raavad and Rashba had a text of the Gemara that included both inferior land and superior land. On that text, the Gemara's objection about closing the door necessarily applies to superior land as well, for otherwise there was no need to abandon that interpretation. Tosafot, however, did not read the superior land clause. On their text the objection concerns only inferior land, and one may hold that superior land is different because no concern of closing the door applies. The Rosh did not decide between the textual traditions. He was explaining that whichever version one reads, prior disclosure is required for the Gemara's argument. The Tur, with his own judgment, followed Tosafot's text and therefore held that superior land need not be given at today's price. Yet he learned from the Rosh's treatment of a ketubah that when the debtor has disclosed willingness to give a better grade, the claimant may at least take it at the future valuation. Thus the Tur's ruling is fully consistent with his father. One might still ask why the Gemara ultimately establishes Abaye's statement with a ketubah claimant who asks for intermediate land at the future value, rather than with an ordinary creditor asking for superior land on the same terms. Tosafot say the Gemara could indeed have done so, but that is somewhat strained after the discussion began with an ordinary creditor. The deeper distinction is this. One explanation in Gittin says that a creditor ought by basic law to take superior land, but the Sages reduced him to intermediate so that a person not lend money merely to target another's attractive field. Under that theory, if the debtor himself discloses willingness to give superior land at the future valuation, the original concern falls away. The creditor did not know at the time of the loan that the debtor would later make such an offer, and ordinary superior land is readily purchasable. Therefore the creditor may revert to the underlying entitlement. Ulla, however, holds that by Torah law the creditor gets inferior land and the Sages raised him to intermediate solely to facilitate lending. On that theory there is no basis to elevate him further to superior land, even after disclosure. The Gemara therefore did not wish to make the final explanation of Abaye depend upon the dispute whether Ulla's enactment is accepted. It used the ketubah case, which works according to all views. The earlier phrase "or superior land" can be understood as belonging to the opinion that the creditor's Torah right is superior land. This explains the textual traditions and the Tur's ruling. For practical law, Bedek HaBayit prefers the first view, that even at the future valuation the debtor need not give superior land, attributing it to Tosafot. I do not see a proof from Tosafot's omission of the superior clause. Their point is only that the Gemara's concern about closing the door does not require today's low price for superior land. It does not address whether prior disclosure binds the debtor at the future price. Indeed, Tosafot say that the Gemara could have established the final case with a creditor and superior land, which leans toward the Rosh and Tur. In any event, without prior disclosure everyone agrees that the creditor cannot demand superior land at the future price, because his legal grade is intermediate. Tosafot's discussion of a seller who retained intermediate land confirms that superior land is not within the creditor's ordinary choice. The remaining dispute is only after disclosure. That issue follows the broader dispute reflected in the Mechaber and Rama in the preceding seif. According to the Mechaber, who does not treat disclosure as binding there, it is ineffective here as well. According to the Rama, who does, it is effective here too. This is the correct practical distinction.
