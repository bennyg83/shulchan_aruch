But if money does not acquire at all, why should mere words create that consequence? The answer is that words accompanied by money can create it. At first glance, once the Sages removed the acquisition effect of money, why should Rabbi Yochanan's view be stronger than Reish Lakish's for this purpose? The answer is that according to Rabbi Yochanan, money still acquires by Torah law, and the Sages did not remove the seller's right in the money itself. He may use it. Therefore the seller has actually received and acquired the buyer's money, and even though the sale is not yet complete rabbinically, retraction deserves the curse. This is no weaker than situmta, a commercial act that can generate the curse even without a complete acquisition. According to Reish Lakish, however, money does not acquire even by Torah law. The seller cannot use it before meshichah and holds it only like a deposit. If so, one might think there is no meaningful payment at all, only words. The Gemara therefore teaches that even words accompanied by money can be enough for the curse. For practical halachah, however, we follow Rabbi Yochanan. Accordingly, the curse applies where there is actual money that the seller acquires and may use. A pledge is different. Under the accepted rule, "there is no maneh here and no pledge here," the seller acquires no right in the pledge when no personal debt has first arisen. The pledge remains the buyer's property. There is therefore no payment at all and no basis for "Who exacted payment." This also explains the case of an advance payment for a future dowry. Even though the merchandise did not yet belong to the seller, the money was advanced precisely so that the seller might use it during the interval. He therefore acquires the money, and the curse can apply even though the goods themselves were not yet present. According to Ramban and Rashba, who hold that a pledge can become legally encumbered even though it does not itself effect the principal acquisition, one could argue that the curse should apply there too. Our practical law, however, follows the view that no lien in the pledge arises at all without a personal obligation, and therefore the curse does not apply.
