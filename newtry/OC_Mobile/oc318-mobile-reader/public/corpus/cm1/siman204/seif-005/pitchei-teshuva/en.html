Because he fears losing the entire purchase. See Sema, seif katan 5, who precisely notes the phrase "the entire purchase," excluding a case where he fears only partial loss, such as where he purchased wine and fears that it will sour. Shaar Mishpat writes that Rabbeinu Yerucham, cited by Beit Yosef, implies that the same distinction applies even after deterioration has already occurred. If the merchandise was totally lost, he does not incur Mi Shepara; if only part of its value was lost, such as wine that soured, he does incur Mi Shepara. It appears that this is specifically where the wine soured, for the wine itself remains in existence. If, however, part of the merchandise was lost through an unavoidable event and that portion is no longer present, even though much of the merchandise remains, he does not incur Mi Shepara with respect to the missing part. Since that portion was entirely destroyed and no longer exists, it is treated like a total loss. This differs from a price increase or decrease, and from wine that soured, where the object remains but has deteriorated. A physical diminution of the merchandise is more serious.
