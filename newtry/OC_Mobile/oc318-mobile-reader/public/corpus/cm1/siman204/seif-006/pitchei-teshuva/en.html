See Baer Heitev and Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 170, concerning Reuven, who gave Shimon one hundred gold coins as earnest money toward a purchase of ten measures of wool at fifty gold coins per measure, which was the market price. When the time for delivery arrived, Shimon gave him ten measures for a total price of five hundred gold coins. Reuven paid Shimon the full five hundred and did not deduct the original one hundred, saying that he wished to leave that one hundred as earnest money toward potash that Shimon would cook during the coming summer. When the time for the potash arrived, Shimon sold it to another at a higher price. Reuven now claimed the profit, arguing that Shimon had sold merchandise that already belonged to him and therefore the profit was his. The question was whether Reuven had acquired the potash through the one hundred gold coins, and if not, whether Shimon, if he nevertheless wished to give Reuven the profit, would thereby violate the prohibition of interest. Chatam Sofer replies that it is very clear that no complete kinyan occurred in any manner. Whichever way one analyzes the original one hundred gold coins, the result is the same. If those first one hundred are treated as payment toward the wool, then when Reuven later paid five hundred, the first four hundred of that later payment completed the five hundred due for the wool, and the last one hundred became the earnest money toward the potash. In that event the potash was purchased with money itself, which creates only the obligation of Mi Shepara and not a complete acquisition. If, instead, the first one hundred was removed from the wool transaction, became a debt owed by Shimon, and was then applied as earnest money toward the potash, most poskim hold that a debt does not effect acquisition at all, while according to Rambam it creates only Mi Shepara, not a complete kinyan, because this debt did not arise from a sale. Concerning the second question, if Shimon wishes to keep faith with Reuven and say that he sold Reuven's potash and therefore wishes to give him his profit, Chatam Sofer discusses at length the sugya of "wine that he does not have" in Bava Metzia 62b, the views of Tosafos and Nimukei Yosef there, and the words of Pnei Yehoshua. He concludes that even if we assume that the five hundred paid later was entirely for the wool and the original debt of one hundred was then applied as earnest money toward the potash, so that according to most poskim Reuven did not acquire even to the extent of Mi Shepara, nevertheless, if Shimon wishes to keep faith and give him the profit corresponding to five hundred gold coins' worth of potash, Reuven may accept it. He may rely upon the poskim who hold that where the seller possesses sufficient money, that is equivalent to possessing the commodity itself. Since Shimon at least had five hundred gold coins in cash when the price was fixed, and presumably the market price for potash had already been established at that time, for otherwise the questioner would not have been uncertain, one may rely upon Nimukei Yosef in the name of Rashba, Rokeach, and Rav Hai Gaon. According to them, in such a case we lower the standard one level: where the seller has the means, even acquisition through an existing debt that did not arise from a sale or forgiveness of a loan is effective sufficiently that there is no rabbinic interest. Any profit beyond the amount corresponding to five hundred gold coins depends upon whether Shimon can truthfully say that at that time he had surplus money sufficient to purchase and set aside additional potash.
